## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies methods for improving independence tests between random variables, focusing on high-dimensional data. It introduces two approaches: (1) tests based on variational mutual information estimators (InfoNCE, NWJ), and (2) tests based on the Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC) with learned representations. The authors establish a close relationship between variational MI-based and HSIC-based tests, showing that learning a variational bound is related to learning kernels for HSIC. They propose a new method that learns representations to maximize the asymptotic power of an HSIC test, provide theoretical guarantees, and demonstrate empirically that HSIC-based tests generally outperform variational ones on synthetic problems of structured dependence. Reviewers agree the paper is technically sound and well-presented, with interesting ideas and extensive synthetic experiments. However, concerns are raised about clarity of contributions, lack of real-world evaluation, missing theoretical convergence rates, insufficient guidance on kernel/architecture selection, incomplete related work, and the need for ablation studies comparing power-maximization vs. standard objective minimization.

### Strengths
- The paper is technically well presented, with claims well supported theoretically and experimentally (R1, R2, R4).
- The idea of deriving an optimizer for independence testing directly from hypothesis test error (minimizing the SNR / maximizing power) is interesting and makes the motivation more explicit than indirect information-theoretic methods; the authors show that variational MI bounds are not designed for power minimization (R2, R3).
- The paper provides formal guarantees (Theorem 4) and connects the proposed method to variational bounds of mutual information (e.g., InfoNCE, NWJ), which are popular in the community (R3, R4).
- It is the first to construct a valid independence test based on variational MI estimators in high dimensions, and gives theoretical insights into the HSIC-MI connection (R4).
- The authors present an intuitive permutation invariance interpretation of the neural estimation expression (R3).
- The proposed HSIC-based tests, with representations learned to maximize asymptotic power, show nontrivial performance improvements over baselines, especially in high-dimensional settings (R2, R4).
- The experimental validation is abundant with various synthetic settings (R1, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity of contributions and focus**: The paper claims to present two approaches, but the conclusion suggests it only studies one (R1). The core contribution – minimizing the SNR (statistical power) instead of the test statistic itself – is not sufficiently highlighted; the InfoNCE and permutation-test adaptation are not novel, and the experiments do not compare the power-maximizing objective against a baseline that simply minimizes the lower bound (or test statistic) without power optimization (R2). Incorporating such a comparison would strengthen the contribution (R2).
- **Lack of real-world evaluation**: All experiments are on synthetic data. At least one experiment on real high-dimensional data is expected given the use of neural network optimization (R3). Several reviewers request real-world validation, e.g., on causal benchmark datasets (R4). If real experiments are not feasible, the authors should explain why (R4).
- **Kernel/architecture selection and generalization**: The choice of kernel significantly influences HSIC test power, but the paper does not address practical challenges such as selecting the number of layers and hidden units in an MLP, or how these choices affect test power. More guidance or an acknowledgment of this limitation is needed (R1). The paper should also discuss when a unique maximizer of the kernel exists and the reasonableness of the assumptions in Theorem 4 (R3).
- **Sample efficiency and scaling analysis**: Although sample efficiency is improved, the methods still require many samples to detect subtle dependencies in very high-dimensional data. The paper does not provide an analysis of how required sample size scales with dimensionality or dependency complexity, making it difficult to assess practical applicability (R1).
- **Theoretical convergence rates and asymptotic properties**: The paper lacks theoretical rates for convergence of test statistics and does not discuss asymptotic validity of power in the main content. No guarantees on convergence rate to asymptotic distributions are given, which hinders assessment of statistical consistency (R1). Tightness of Proposition 3 beyond asymptotics is questioned (R3).
- **Missing related work**: The paper does not mention independence tests based on optimal transport (e.g., [1],[2]) (R2). It also overlooks classical methods like canonical correlation analysis, the Hirschfeld-Gebelein-Renyi coefficient, and recent work on max-sliced mutual information (Tsur 24) that connects to constrained variational MI estimation (R3).
- **Permutation test limitations**: Permutation tests, while straightforward, may be less effective and computationally costly. Is it possible to derive a limiting distribution for the test statistics? The computational complexity of Phase Two in Algorithm 1 should be discussed (R2). Also, the choice of \(n_{\text{perm}}\) in Algorithm 1 is not guided; what are best empirical strategies and how does this choice impact convergence rate? (R1)
- **Ablation and design choices**: No ablation studies are performed; it would be informative to see if the network size should indeed be linear in the input dimension (R3). The definition of \(K\) in InfoNCE notation should be clarified (R3, R4).
- **Presentation and organizational issues**: The paper lacks a bottom-line summary in Section 2, which feels like it stops in the middle (R3). Some crucial parts, such as the assumptions of the main theorem, are relegated to the appendix, making it harder for readers to grasp contributions in a bird’s-eye view (R3). Specific and very technical details in Section 5 do not benefit presentation (R3). Typos and undefined acronyms (InfoNCE, NWJ, DV) appear (R3). Lines with errors: line 218 “If \(X \perp Y\)” should be “if \(X \not\perp Y\)”; line 223 “When HSIC = 0 the statistic is not mean zero”; lines 417 and 419 missing \(\lambda\) in \(J_\lambda\) (R2). Also, line 101 “method method”, line 136 “feature-features”, line 147 “by by” (R3). Citation format is questioned (R3).
- **Scope limited to independence testing**: The paper only addresses (unconditional) independence tests, which is considerably easier than conditional independence tests. If the authors consider extending to conditional independence, what specific challenges do they foresee? (R4) The intro would benefit from a short discussion of cases where standard kernels (e.g., Gaussian with unit bandwidth) perform extremely poorly, as this motivates the work (R3). Additionally, a paragraph distinguishing traditional independence tests from those for high-dimensional settings would help readers (R4).
