## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a cross-lingual unsupervised ASR method that leverages a supervised source-language acoustic model (AM) and a target-language n-gram language model (LM) to generate pseudo-labels for target audio, then iteratively refines the target AM via iterative pseudo-labeling (IPL). It assumes unpaired target audio and text, but no labeled target speech. Experiments on Common Voice and LJSpeech cover European and African languages across families, using English, Spanish, German, French, Kinyarwanda, and others as sources. Key results: English→Swahili achieves 18% WER; the method outperforms character-based wav2vec-U 2.0 by 15% absolute WER on LJSpeech using 800h of labeled German data instead of 60k unlabeled English data. The paper also explores transliteration for different scripts. Reviewer scores range from 3 to 6; one reviewer (score 3) is significantly more critical, while the others (score 6) are generally positive.

### Strengths
- Addresses a practical underexplored problem (unsupervised ASR for low-resource languages without labeled target audio).
- The approach is simple, using well-understood off-the-shelf techniques (CTC, n-gram LM, self-training/iterative pseudo-labeling) and is more straightforward than GAN-based alternatives (e.g., wav2vec-U).
- Comprehensive experimental design covering multiple language pairs, language families (Indo-European, African), and cross-family transfer (e.g., Indo-European→African).
- Comparisons with zero-shot baselines (with/without target LM) and fully supervised training demonstrate effectiveness; the method achieves competitive WERs (e.g., 18% for en→sw) and significantly outperforms wav2vec-U 2.0 (15% absolute improvement on LJSpeech).
- Using a multilingual source AM further enhances performance.
- Intensive analysis across dimensions: amount of labeled source data, unlabeled target data, different source/target combinations, and transliteration for non-Latin scripts.
- Paper is well-written and straightforward to understand.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty (all reviewers):** The core ideas—cross-lingual transfer using a source AM and target LM, and self-training—are not new. One reviewer (R2) notes very similar prior work (e.g., Hasegawa-Johnson et al. 2016, “ASR for under-resourced languages from probabilistic transcription”) and argues the paper does not sufficiently highlight differences. Another reviewer (R1) states the contribution is limited in cross-lingual scenarios. R2 further argues the approach is specific to ASR (CTC architecture) and not clearly connected to general ML problems; it does not seem applicable to other architectures (attention, RNN-T, HMM) without justification. The paper does not demonstrate sufficient novelty for an ML conference, according to R2 and R1.
- **Reliance on romanization/script compatibility:** The method requires character sets to be aligned via the unidecode package. Reviewers raise several concerns:
  - Performance is measured in romanized script, not the original script (R3). It is unclear if unidecode was inverted before CER calculation.
  - Discarding characters that fail conversion from the reference text may invalidate comparison with other reports (R2).
  - The approach is primarily applicable to languages using Latin or romanizable scripts; ideogram-based languages (e.g., Chinese, Japanese) would be difficult to handle without a reliable transliteration system (R2).
  - Section 5.6 uses “Alphabets” but not all writing systems are alphabets; extension to abugida, abjad, or logographic systems is unclear (R3).
- **Language coverage and generalization concerns:**
  - One reviewer (R4) finds the results encouraging only for specific pairs (*→sw and be→cs) and questions generalization to other low-resource languages.
  - R4 notes it would be more valuable to show one source AM applied to many targets rather than many sources for one target.
  - R3 suggests comparing on FLEURS (used by prior unsupervised ASR work) would enable clearer comparisons; the current language set is more limited.
  - No systematic analysis of which acoustic or linguistic properties make a source language suitable for transfer to a given target (R3).
- **Baseline comparisons are limited:** R1 points out that the core validation (Section 5.2) only includes zero-shot with/without LM and fully supervised training; it would be more informative to compare with other unsupervised methods (e.g., wav2vec-U, other cross-lingual approaches). The comparison in Section 5.7 (800h labeled German vs. 60k unlabeled English) is difficult to interpret because the resources differ drastically (R2).
- **Expected findings:** Many experimental results are trivial (e.g., more data helps) and unsurprising (R2). The method’s simplicity means few unexpected or novel insights (R2).
- **Missing analyses and unanswered questions:**
  - Is the correlation between LM perplexity and final WER tested? (R1)
  - How is the Kinyarwanda AM trained if text data are not available in Common Crawl?
  - Table 3 shows degradation when LM decoding is applied (de→sw); is there any analysis to explain this? (R4)
  - Why was CTC specifically chosen? Could the method work with other architectures (attention, RNN-T)? (R2)
  - Could self-supervised learning (SSL) features be combined with this method? (R2)
  - What specific similarities (acoustic, lexical, other) are critical for performance? (R3) The paper does not answer this.
  - Is the example in Figure 1 real or idealized? (R3)
- **Presentation issues:**
  - Typos (e.g., Section 5.2 duplicated phrase) and missing inline pointers to tables/figures (R1).
  - The equation parameter \(\alpha > 0\) appears unexpectedly in Section 2.2, without context (R2).
  - Experimental setups in Section 4 are diverse and difficult to follow; the design intention should be clearer (R2).
  - Figure 3 is too small; Figure 4 would benefit from consistent y-axes across subplots (R2, R3).
- **Minority reviewer’s more negative view:** Reviewer 2 (score 3, confidence 5) considers the technical novelty insufficient for an ML conference, the methodology not new, most findings expected, and the scope narrow (CTC-specific). While other reviewers found the work practical and effective, R2’s assessment highlights concerns about generality and contribution level that the other reviewers did not fully share.
