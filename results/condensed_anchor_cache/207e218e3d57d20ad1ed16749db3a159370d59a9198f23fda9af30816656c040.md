## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Prolate Spheroidal Wave Function-based Implicit Neural Representations (PIN), which uses the optimal space-frequency domain concentration of PSWFs as a nonlinear activation in INRs. PIN is evaluated on image reconstruction, image inpainting, occupancy fields, NeRF, novel view synthesis, edge detection, and denoising. The authors provide theoretical proofs of localization and expressivity, and extensive experiments demonstrate that PIN outperforms existing INR baselines.

### Strengths
- The proposed PIN outperforms other INR baselines across multiple tasks, including image reconstruction, image inpainting, occupancy field, NeRF, novel view synthesis, edge detection, and image denoising.
- Detailed ablation studies are conducted.
- The paper clearly explains limitations of current INRs and proposes a novel activation based on PSWFs to overcome them.
- Theoretical proofs establishing the localization and expressivity properties of PIN are provided.
- The motivation is compelling, with an insightful critique of prior activation functions (Gaussian, Gabor wavelets).
- Experiments cover a diverse range of tasks, enhancing the validity of the findings.

### Weaknesses
- The evaluation is limited to small datasets. For image reconstruction, only the Kodak Lossless True Color Image Dataset (24 images) is used; larger-scale evaluations (e.g., DIV2K with a thousand images) are needed.
- The exact implementation of the numerical estimation of the PSWF is unclear: the approximation order is not stated, the discretization method (finite difference, spectral, etc.) is not specified, and the practical handling of the PSWF’s infinite support (truncation method and its impact) is not discussed. The impact of approximation order on computational complexity and model performance is not analyzed.
- The effect of the finite-order approximation on the theoretical properties of the PSWF (e.g., infinite support) is not discussed.
- The activation appears computationally heavy; runtime (training and inference time) and trade-off between quality and speed are not reported. A quantitative comparison of decoding speed for different INR methods is missing.
- The paper does not report the specific parameters used for each baseline method (e.g., frequency parameters in WIRE, ω in SIREN, σ in GAUSS). Without this, it is unclear whether performance gains are solely due to PSWF or to parameter tuning for each baseline. 
- For Occupancy Field and NeRF experiments, only a subset of the dataset is used; the number of objects and selection criteria are not specified, and comparisons with state-of-the-art methods for those specific tasks are lacking.
- Figure 4 was replaced from a previous submission with a scene showing better results, raising the question of whether these results were obtained by tuning parameters per scene. The authors should clarify how these new results were obtained and why they were not included in the earlier version.
- Several recent INRs (FINER, FINER++, H-SIREN) have been proposed since the initial submission but are neither cited nor compared.
- The paper does not evaluate PIN on image super-resolution, leaving its general applicability to other tasks unclear.
- The image inpainting experimental setup is ambiguous: are missing pixels fed as black or are those coordinates masked out during training? Are pixel masks provided to the INR?
- Guidance on selecting the bandwidth parameter \(c\) is missing, especially for continuous domain tasks like NeRF where there may be no known frequency bounds.
- Presentation quality could be improved: figures lack visual clarity, vector graphics should be used, and citation formatting (e.g., \autoref{}) should be consistent.

*Note: One reviewer expressed lower confidence (2) and suggested larger-scale evaluations and runtime analysis; another noted that the paper could be strengthened by applying PSWF to newer INR training/input strategies. These concerns are incorporated above.*
