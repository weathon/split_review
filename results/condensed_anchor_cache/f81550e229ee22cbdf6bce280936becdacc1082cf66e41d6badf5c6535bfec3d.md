## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes using LLMs (specifically GPT-3.5) as a high-level decision-maker for autonomous driving, with MPC as a low-level controller. The system uses a chain-of-thought prompt describing the road scene, the LLM outputs a high-level decision (e.g., turn left, wait), which is translated into parameter matrices for MPC. Techniques include attention allocation, situational awareness, and multi-vehicle joint control. Experiments are conducted in the IdSim simulation environment across multiple driving scenarios (5 main scenarios, ~25 total). The method is compared against RL and MPC baselines and claims superiority in both single-vehicle and multi-vehicle tasks, as well as ability to regulate behavior via text input.

### Strengths
- **Timely and relevant topic**: Using LLMs for autonomous driving is a popular and promising direction (Reviewers 2,4).
- **Practical hybrid architecture**: Combining LLM (high-level reasoning, interpretability) with MPC (low-level control with strong priors) is a smart approach that leverages strengths of both (Reviewers 2,4).
- **Useful cognitive pathways**: Proposed techniques (attention allocation, situational awareness) are simple, practical, and help structure LLM reasoning for driving (Reviewer 4).
- **Clear presentation**: Paper is well-written; Figure 2 provides a clear illustration of the pipeline; diagrams and examples clearly demonstrate methodology and superiority over baselines (Reviewers 2,4). Reviewer 3 also finds the motivation and interpretability justification clear.
- **Full pipeline demonstration**: Shows a complete system from observations to control, and ability to regulate driving behavior via text input (Reviewers 1,4).
- **Potential for long-tail problems**: LLM common sense reasoning is a promising way to address rare events (Reviewer 2), though this potential is not demonstrated with sufficient evidence (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of reproducibility and missing details** (Reviewers 1,3):
  - No information on how LLM output is translated into MPC parameters, details of predefined weight matrices, or parameter values (even missing from supplementary).
  - Code is not provided; reproducibility is difficult.
  - It is unclear whether the discrete actions shown in the prompts are the only possible actions, and whether the LLM chooses a vehicle other than the ego (Appendix B mentions “vehicle 15”).
  - What “predefined situations” does the MPC rely on? How many situations are designed (e.g., how many \(X^t\))?

- **Insufficient and weak baselines** (Reviewers 2,3,4):
  - Only one unspecified RL algorithm and one MPC are compared; no modern trajectory planning (e.g., Wayformer) or traffic simulation (e.g., BITS) baselines.
  - No details on RL implementation or whether the RL agent receives the same input as the LLM.
  - Comparatively weak baselines makes it hard to assess practical usefulness.

- **Toy simulation environment limits significance** (Reviewers 1,2,3):
  - IdSim is overly simplistic; real-world driving requires visual/spatial cues, not just language.
  - Only ~25 scenarios are used, with no explanation of how they were selected; the paper focuses mainly on left-turn tasks, limiting generalization.
  - One specific long-tail case (breakdown vehicle in roundabout, Figure 1) is shown, but no broad evidence that LLM solves long-tail problems across many scenarios.

- **Trustworthiness and safety concerns** (Reviewer 2):
  - GPT-3.5/GPT-4 have uncontrollable randomness even at temperature=0; impact on results and safety is not addressed.
  - No discussion on how to ensure LLM always outputs reasonable decisions across model version changes.

- **Running frequency and latency** (Reviewer 2):
  - Language processing may be too slow for real-time driving; no running time reported for any method.

- **Language-only input is insufficient** (Reviewer 3):
  - Driving heavily relies on visual and spatial information; language alone is unlikely to be sufficient outside this toy setup.
  - To be realistic, a multi-modal LLM would be required; the paper does not discuss how to incorporate visual cues.

- **Limited reasoning capability of LLMs** (Reviewer 3):
  - It is unclear whether the tested scenarios actually require logical reasoning; they appear simple (e.g., turning left).
  - The paper does not test different types of reasoning abilities.

- **Manually designed situations limit scalability** (Reviewer 4):
  - The method relies on a fixed set of manually predefined situations; this may not scale to diverse real-world driving.

- **Missing discussion of related work** (Reviewer 4):
  - Relevant papers on language-controlled traffic generation (Zhong et al.) and LLM-controlled driving (DriveGPT4, GPT-Driver, DiLu) are not cited or discussed.
  - Limitations of the proposed method are not discussed.

- **Writing and clarity issues** (Reviewers 1,3):
  - Figures are not fully self-explanatory: Figure 1 is unclear whether “common sense” is fed or emergent; Figure 2 uses terms “judge”, “select” without definition.
  - Notations are confusing (e.g., \(env^t\) dependent on time when environment is static).
  - The example in the introduction about yielding does not clearly motivate the need for LLMs; simple MPC cost functions might achieve the same behavior.
  - Interpretability claim: all collision avoidance algorithms can provide a reason for decisions; it is not clear why LLM interpretability is more trustworthy.

- **Metrics and scenario details missing** (Reviewers 1,2,4):
  - Details on metrics used in Table 2 are not provided.
  - It is unclear whether other agents are reactive or replaying fixed trajectories; scenario durations and possible actions are not specified.
  - Out of 5 scenarios, only 1 is described in detail (roundabout with breakdown).
