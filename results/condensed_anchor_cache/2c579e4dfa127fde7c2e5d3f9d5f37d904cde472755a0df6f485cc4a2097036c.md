- Scores: 8, 6, 6, 6, 8

## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers largely agree the paper presents a novel defense against model extraction using a dynamic multi-exit (Early-Exit) neural network. Attack queries (assumed out-of-distribution) exit early with uninformative outputs, while benign queries (in-distribution) exit later to preserve utility, simultaneously protecting functionality, architecture, and improving efficiency. The approach is grounded in information bottleneck theory and evaluated against SOTA defenses. However, major concerns are raised: unclear assumptions about ID/OOD separation, overstated architecture protection claims, insufficient worst-case adaptive attack evaluation, limited experiments on architecture theft, and poor presentation. Ratings (8,6,6,6,8) reflect a mix of strong support and significant reservations; minority reviewers (1,5) are more positive.

### Strengths
- Novelty and motivation: First application of multi-exit neural networks to defend model extraction (Reviewer 1, 2, 4, 5). Simultaneously protects functionality, architecture, and improves inference efficiency (Reviewer 3).
- Adaptable: Easily integrates with architectures like ResNets and ViTs (Reviewer 1).
- Sound theoretical foundation: Use of entropy and information bottleneck theory is well-suited to reducing extractable information (Reviewer 1, 3). Innovative loss function design using IB theory (Reviewer 3).
- Extensive evaluation: Covers various scenarios, models, datasets, with comparisons to SOTA defenses (Reviewer 1, 2). Includes adaptive attacks (Reviewer 2) and thorough ablation study (Reviewer 1).
- Promising results: Achieves 2× efficiency improvement and up to 12% reduction in clone model accuracy (Reviewer 1, 5).
- Clear organization: Good progression, Figure 1 aids clarity (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- Presentation and readability: Related work poorly organized (e.g., ActiveThief should be in functionality stealing section; data-based vs data-free distinction unclear) (Reviewer 1). Methodology (Section 4) hard to follow (Reviewer 1, 2, 3); steps after OOD generator not coherent without Algorithm 1 (Reviewer 1). Language errors: “covert” for “convert,” misspelled “label” in tables, repeated line in Appendix D (Reviewer 1). Math hampers readability; need a notation table (Reviewer 4). Repetitive notation (e.g., ‘r’) (Reviewer 3).
- Unclear ID/OOD definitions and assumptions: Assumption that attack data are OOD is weakly justified; benign queries may also be OOD, reducing utility (Reviewer 2). No formal definition of ID vs OOD provided (Reviewer 2, 3). Rationale for loss terms separating ID/OOD unclear (Reviewer 3). Claim that approach is prevention but it functions as detection relying on OOD classification; insufficient results support this (Reviewer 1).
- Architecture protection claims overstated: Early-exit only protects unexecuted layers; attackers could still steal the majority of the network if they execute it (Reviewer 3). No demonstration of how attackers would exploit full execution to steal architecture (Reviewer 3). Experiments on architecture theft limited – only Carlini et al. considered, missing earlier reverse-engineering work (ICLR 2018) on ImageNet (Reviewer 1). Additional metrics and experiments needed (Reviewer 3). A “Limitations” section recommended (Reviewer 3).
- Impact on benign queries and utility: 90% of ID samples exit in first three exits, producing less informative outputs; impact must be understood (Reviewer 5). Minimizing mutual information (X_id, Z_id) in Eq. (3) may compromise accuracy; need explanation why it does not (Reviewer 3). Table 12 shows ID >90% exit at Exit2 but OOD 75% – inconsistency with loss objectives; analyze exit patterns (Reviewer 3). If OOD still 35% after last two blocks, what is defense effect? (Reviewer 3). Training on OOD data uses original network labels; what is accuracy on OOD data? In hard-label setting, attacker might still get correct labels with some errors (Reviewer 1).
- Weak worst-case adaptive attack evaluation: Adaptive attacks should assume attacker knows training data distribution (e.g., public domain data for malware detection) (Reviewer 5). Currently, no evaluation against worst-case with partial ID knowledge (Reviewer 5). For DBME setting, random sampling is weak; uncertainty-based sampling (ActiveThief) is more effective (Reviewer 1).
- Limited attack comparisons and baselines: For DFME, clone model architecture should match victim for fair comparison (Reviewer 1). DNF not better than MeCo on CIFAR-10; discuss (Reviewer 1). For in-distribution defense, JBDA is obsolete; use ActiveThief or Knockoff nets (Reviewer 1). No comparison with other information-theory defenses (e.g., adversarial training) or dynamic architectures (e.g., BranchyNet) (Reviewer 4).
- Poor theoretical justification of MI optimization: Choice of mutual information optimization method lacks deeper theoretical and intuitive justification (Reviewer 3).
- Limited applicability: All datasets are classification; effectiveness for object detection or NLP unvalidated (Reviewer 3, 4). Translation to multi-modal architectures (e.g., VLMs) or multi-pipeline methods unclear (Reviewer 4).
- Insufficient hyperparameter and ablation analysis: Choice of thresholds barely discussed; need ablation of hyperparameters, exit thresholds, entropy weights (Reviewer 4). Exit point analysis should report per-exit accuracy and exact counts of ID/OOD samples, not “over half” (Reviewer 1). Graph showing accuracy vs. exit points for ID and OOD needed (Reviewer 3).
- Vulnerability to attacks on multi-exit networks: Adversary may design stronger attacks exploiting multi-exit structure (Reviewer 2). Prior work on attacking multi-exit networks should be discussed (e.g., CCS 2022 auditing leaks, arXiv 2023 model stealing, INFOCOM 2023 backdoor, Usenix Security 2023 bit-flip, CCS 2023 prediction privacy) (Reviewer 2).
