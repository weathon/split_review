## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Deep Concept Removal, a method to remove undesired concepts from neural network representations by penalizing the norm of Concept Activation Vectors (CAV) learned from a concept dataset. The approach incorporates adversarial probing classifiers at multiple layers using an adversarial penalty based on TCAV. Experiments are conducted on synthetic MNIST, subpopulation robustness benchmarks (Waterbirds, CelebA), and fairness tasks. The method decouples model training from concept erasure, allowing post-hoc removal using a concept dataset.

### Strengths
- The proposed concept removal setting is novel and extends TCAV intuitively.
- The paper provides a comprehensive literature review and explains necessary background in an easy-to-follow manner.
- The method decouples model training from concept erasure, requiring concept labels only from a separate concept dataset and enabling post-hoc removal.
- The analysis of how concept removal effectiveness varies across network layers (e.g., applying adversarial CAV before contraction is most effective) is insightful.
- The work is original in leveraging adversarial linear classifiers for out-of-distribution robustness in the concept removal task.
- The appendix contains extensive implementation and mathematical details.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of comparison with established baselines.** The paper does not compare against relevant concept removal methods such as LEACE, kernel-space concept erasure, adversarial concept removal, or other methods that explicitly disentangle representations or remove specific attributes. This omission makes it difficult to assess the novelty and relative effectiveness of the proposed approach. The paper also lacks a dedicated related work section.
- **Performance concerns on core benchmarks.** Table 2 shows the method performs worse than methods without concept annotation in some cases (e.g., Waterbirds, where it performs significantly worse than ERM). The disparity between datasets where adversarial CAV performs poorly and where it outperforms baselines is not adequately addressed. The method does not work on Waterbirds; the authors attribute this to higher-level vs. lower-level features, but this distinction is arbitrary and lacks a clear definition, making the explanation unconvincing. Subpopulation robustness may be a questionable evaluation task because concept dataset images with/without the concept may differ in many other ways, potentially leading to a spurious CAV.
- **Insufficient evaluation on harder tasks and models.** The main synthetic experiment (S4) is limited to MNIST, which is not representative of modern computer vision. Demonstrations on harder tasks (CIFAR-10/100) and larger models (ResNet50, ViTs) are missing. The OOD concept dataset experiment uses EMNIST, which is too similar to MNIST; a more nuanced understanding of when OOD concept datasets fail (degree of dissimilarity) is needed.
- **Unsubstantiated claims of OOD generalization.** The only OOD result is on Celeb-A (blond males as unseen domain), but these images are drawn from the same support as the original data, not a true covariate shift. Providing the model with prior knowledge about the desired invariant feature (gender) makes the setup artificial.
- **Lack of theoretical justification and ablation studies.** The regularization term (Eq. 3.2) is purely heuristic; there is no theoretical justification for why penalizing the norm of the CAV effectively removes concepts. The paper lacks ablation studies on the training loss modules, such as the effect of the penalty term in Eq. 3.2 and different values of λ in Eq. 3.1.
- **Incomplete analysis of results.** The results in Figure 7 are not adequately explained: concept accuracy for "young" and "glasses" remains relatively unchanged while "gender" is significantly reduced. The distinction between lower-level and higher-level features is not supported. Table 1 shows test accuracies at 100% while training accuracies are much lower, but this is not addressed. The counterproductive reconstruction in Figure 3 (stripes appear in images without stripes) is not discussed.
- **Limited scope of downstream tasks.** The method is validated only on classification tasks. It is unclear whether it can be applied to object detection, image generation, or other tasks where the concept might be encoded differently or the objective is not classification. This also relates to the open question of adaptation to generative models.
- **Practical scalability concerns.** The concept dataset formulation (appendix C.4.1) to avoid correlations between subgroups may imply combinatorial explosion as the number of concepts increases. The method's sample efficiency (RQ3) is not explored.
- **Presentation issues.** Figures lack axis labels and have small legends; the writing in the second half (experiments, detail, setup) can be significantly improved.
