## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a model-based reinforcement learning approach that models the reward function using a dual-Hawkes process (combining Hawkes and Cox processes) to capture disease progression and recovery intensities. The reward is defined as the difference between these intensities. A GAN is used to learn history embeddings and simulate the clinical environment for policy training. Experiments are conducted on a simulated environment and the MIMIC-III dataset.

### Strengths
*   **Novel combination of RL and survival analysis:** Integrating a dual-Hawkes process to model disease and recovery dynamics for the reward function in a treatment optimization context is novel (Reviewers 1, 2).
*   **Important problem:** The paper addresses a meaningful problem: learning dynamic treatment strategies from time-series event data (Reviewer 3).
*   **Effective recovery of simulated intensity function:** The dual-Hawkes model appears to accurately recover the simulated intensity function on synthetic and semi-synthetic data (Reviewers 2, 3).
*   **Experiments on both simulated and real data:** The evaluation includes both a synthetic environment and the real-world MIMIC-III dataset (Reviewer 2). *(Note: Reviewer 1 strongly criticized the experimental design and fairness of these experiments, see Weaknesses.)*

### Weaknesses
*   **Unclear main contribution and insufficient justification of modeling choices:** The paper presents two components (dual-Hawkes reward and GAN history embeddings) without clearly stating which is the main contribution; in the MIMIC-III experiment, the GAN is only used as a simulator for off-policy evaluation (Reviewer 2). Several modeling choices lack motivation and ablation: the decision to use an RNN vs. a Transformer for \(g(\cdot)\), training with or without the GAN objective, the choice of the Gaussian kernel in Equation 2, and the separate modeling of disease and recovery processes despite only a sparse reward signal (Reviewers 2, 3). The disease/recovery threshold on the SOFA score (L388) is arbitrary without justification or sensitivity analysis (Reviewer 2). The order of training (whether disease/recovery processes are learned first then used as rewards via their difference as mentioned at L66, or jointly) is not described (Reviewer 2).
*   **Weak experimental validation of the RL policy:** For a non-theoretical paper, the experiments are insufficient (Reviewer 1). The actual policy evaluation occupies less than half a page (L489–525), with most space dedicated to transition dynamics model evaluation (Sec 5.1–5.3) and a literature review (Sec 5.4). The paper does not assess whether using dual-Hawkes reward modeling enhances policy optimization relative to alternative reward functions (Reviewer 1). The comparison to DQN and CQL is unfair: DQN and CQL are trained on clinical risk scores (L458–463), while the proposed method is trained on the integral values of modeled recovering and illness intensities, and *all* methods are evaluated on those same integral values. This setup treats the simulator as the ground truth, making the proposed method an *online* RL algorithm compared against offline methods, and does not prove that the proposed model-based+policy-optimization design is better than CQL/DQN for offline data, nor that the sensitivity of existing RL methods (claimed L514) is absent in the proposed method (Reviewer 1). Additional weaknesses in experimental reporting: Figure 4 does not specify what "agent" and "behavioral" approaches are or how the ground truth reward is simulated (Reviewer 3); Figure 5 lacks definition of error bars (Reviewer 2); Figure 1 is never referenced or explained (Reviewer 2).
*   **Missing competitive baselines for event sequence modeling:** The paper does not discuss or benchmark the dual-Hawkes approach against related time-series event modeling works, such as Hawkes Process with Flexible Triggering Kernels (Yamac et al., 2023), Transformer Hawkes Process (Simiao et al., 2020), and Intensity-free learning of temporal point processes (Oleksandr et al., 2020), making it difficult to assess its significance (Reviewer 3).
*   **No accounting for time-varying confounding:** The proposed RL approach for treatment recommendation from observational data does not account for time-varying confounding, which could bias treatment assignments (Reviewer 3).
*   **Lack of justification for offline RL design choice:** The paper uses model-based RL with policy optimization purely from offline data without online updates. This means the "virtual environment" is in-distribution to the offline data, inheriting the drawbacks of offline RL that the paper itself acknowledges (L215–217, L472–479). No analytical or experimental evidence is provided to justify shifting away from established offline RL methods (e.g., CQL, importance-weight-based methods) to this design without further adjustments (Reviewer 1).
*   **Poor clarity and missing details:** Several important functions (\(f(\cdot)\), \(\pi(\cdot)\), \(\phi(\cdot)\)) are left unspecified; how the reward function for the MIMIC-III experiment is simulated and how the model is trained end-to-end is unclear (Reviewer 3). There is notation overload: \(\phi(\cdot)\) is used for both the intensity triggering kernel and the GAN generator (Reviewer 3). Section 5.4 is out of place in the experiment section (Reviewer 1). Typos: "Hwakes" should be "Hawkes", "dual-Hawkes" inconsistent capitalization (Reviewer 3). L451–457 shifts to describing the GAN without clear relation to baselines (Reviewer 1). L326–328's relevance is unclear (Reviewer 1). L297 advocates maximizing accumulated illness risk, which seems contradictory (Reviewer 2).
*   **Unsubstantiated medical claims:** Section 5.5 makes claims such as "By reducing vasopressor usage and moderately increasing IV fluids, our method notably enhanced patient recovering" without supporting medical references or specifying which patient types these changes apply to (Reviewer 2).
*   **Inadequate evaluation metrics and requested ablations:** To convincingly evaluate the method, the paper should: evaluate on a simulation dataset where the simulator is only used for generating training data and final evaluation (not intermediate interactions); conduct ablations comparing model-based+policy-optimization RL with CQL and DQN using the *same* reward function; and compare dual-Hawkes reward vs. clinical risk score reward on the same RL model using an evaluation metric that is not identical to either reward function (Reviewer 1). Additionally, more detailed descriptions (equations, loss functions) of the RL training procedure should be provided, potentially by reducing Section 5.4 (Reviewer 1). The claim that prior work tends to assume Markovian dynamics (L204, L225–226) requires citations (Reviewer 1).
