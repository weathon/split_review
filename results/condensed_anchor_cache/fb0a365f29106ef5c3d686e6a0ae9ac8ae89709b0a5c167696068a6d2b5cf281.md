## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies untrained neural networks (Deep Image Prior) for accelerated MRI reconstruction. It analyzes how architectural components affect overfitting and proposes two architecture-agnostic remedies: low‑pass filtering the white‑noise input and penalizing the network’s Lipschitz constant. Experiments on fastMRI (4× acceleration) show that these simple modifications reduce the performance gap between well‑designed and poorly‑designed architectures.

### Strengths
- Investigating untrained neural networks (Deep Image Prior) for MRI reconstruction is a new and interesting direction (R1).
- The paper provides a systematic and comprehensive analysis of architectural influences on reconstruction quality, which has not been done this way before (R2; R3 also acknowledges it as a comprehensive investigation).
- The proposed remedies are simple, practical, and computationally efficient; they can be implemented with minor code changes and are easily reproducible (R1, R2).
- Extensive experiments (e.g., Fig. 4, Fig. 6, Tab. 4) demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed methods in lifting the performance of under‑performing models (R2, R4).
- The paper is well‑structured and clearly communicates its methodology, findings, and contributions, making it accessible (R2, R3, R4).
- The core idea of low‑pass filtering the input noise to regularize the smoothness‑artifact trade‑off is impressive and effective (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing comparisons to important baselines.**  
  The paper does not compare against self‑validation based early stopping, a popular non‑architectural regularization (R4). The comparison to Self‑Validation (Yaman et al., 2021) is included but the reported performance is significantly worse than originally reported, raising concerns about the implementation or experimental setup (R3). Moreover, no comparison is made with end‑to‑end supervised learning methods such as UNet or deep unfolding approaches like VarNet (R3). One reviewer notes that on fastMRI 4×, classical TV methods yield comparable PSNR/SSIM to the proposed method, and the UNet baseline outperforms it by a significant margin – this situation limits the practical impact (R3, minority negative opinion).

- **Limited generalizability beyond the studied setup.**  
  The conclusions are drawn from a narrow context: fastMRI with 4× acceleration and a specific set of architectures. It is unclear whether the architectural insights (e.g., importance of low‑pass filtering) transfer to other MRI datasets, different acceleration factors (e.g., 8×), or non‑MRI tasks like natural image denoising/super‑resolution (R2, R3). One reviewer questions whether the proposed regularization can help reduce the performance gap at higher acceleration factors where untrained networks typically fall further behind trained ones (R4).

- **Overclaim and insufficient theoretical grounding.**  
  The paper claims an optimal architecture based on observations from a single data setup, yet this claim lacks theoretical justification and has not been validated across diverse data configurations; furthermore, comparison to a fully‑sampled ground truth is impractical in real‑world applications (R3, minority strongly negative). Another reviewer notes that Section 4 is presented as the first systematic study on architectural dependencies but is largely redundant with the investigations already reported in Darestani & Heckel (2021, Appendix B) (R4).

- **Incomplete architectural and data‑acquisition analysis.**  
  The hyper‑parameter insights were derived from a baseline CNN; a more comprehensive study that includes transformer or NAS architectures would strengthen the paper (R1). The effect of data‑acquisition parameters (e.g., undersampling factor) on the observed conclusions is not explored; it is therefore unclear whether the same architectural remedies remain effective under more aggressive undersampling (R2). Additionally, the best model reported in Table 2 uses a 2‑layer 256‑channel network, which has 4× more parameters than an 8‑layer 64‑channel network – this may explain its higher overfitting propensity regardless of architecture depth (R4).

- **Hyperparameter selection is not adequately discussed.**  
  There is limited guidance on how to choose hyperparameters such as the sigma of the Gaussian blur; it is unknown whether these parameters depend on the undersampling factor or the network architecture. Without clear guidelines, practical applicability is diminished (R2, R4).

- **Experimental design and metric issues.**  
  Figure 3 claims deeper/narrower networks are less prone to overfitting, but the metric used (absolute masked SSIM curves) is not fair; one should measure the slope of the fall or the convergence value. It is also unclear whether Fig. 3 is averaged over multiple examples or just one sample, making broad conclusions difficult (R4). The dataset setup for Section 4.1 (which also contains experiments) is omitted, even though untrained networks require different architectures for each anatomy (R4). The “Brain setup” in Table 6 is never explained in the paper (R3).

- **Practical impact and novelty concerns.**  
  The need to optimize each image separately (per‑image optimization time) versus a one‑time training budget makes the DIP approach less motivated for clinical use; the paper should report optimization runtime or show that DIP outperforms supervised methods (R1). One reviewer (R3) argues that incorporating input coordinates and Lipschitz regularization, while possibly novel for DIP‑MRI, does not offer a significant advantage for the broader MRI reconstruction community, given the already saturated performance on fastMRI benchmarks.

- **Minor presentation issues.**  
  Missing spaces before parentheses throughout the paper (R4). Table 2’s labels (Depth, Width, Kernel size, Skip connections) are confusingly placed (R4).
