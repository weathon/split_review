- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 5, 6, 3, 1

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes augmenting LLMs with a differentiable interpreter and a pre-compiled library of programs to improve algorithmic reasoning. Reviewers find the motivation important and the approach original, but the experimental evaluation is too limited and unconvincing. Weaknesses include poor presentation (unclear methodology, lack of baseline comparisons, missing experimental details), low sorting accuracy (33–40% with the ground-truth algorithm already in the library), no analysis of length generalization, and no discussion of training difficulties or differentiable interpreter limitations. Reviewer 4 further questions the novelty relative to existing LLM tool-use methods and argues the program search problem is trivial. Reviewer 2 is more positive about the potential but stresses the need for more experiments and public datasets. Overall, the paper is seen as an initial proof-of-concept that is not yet ready for a main conference.

### Strengths
- Addresses the important and well-motivated problem of LLMs’ inability to perform robust algorithmic reasoning (all reviewers).
- Original proposal to equip LLMs with a differentiable interpreter and a library of neurally compiled programs, rather than relying on non-differentiable external tools (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- Background section is comprehensive and an interesting read (Reviewer 1).
- Provides an analysis of the impact of recursion depth on trainability in the Fibonacci dataset (Reviewer 2).
- The approach is clearly outlined and could be invaluable once refined and scaled (Reviewer 3).
- The paper targets a significant problem of integrating programs with neural networks (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Poor presentation and unclear methodology**: The introduction devotes most space to motivation and only one sentence to methodology (Reviewer 1). Section 3 does not clearly separate prior work from new contributions; most components (Differentiable Registers, Probabilistic Execution, Differentiable Interpreter) lack citations or explicit attribution (Reviewers 1, 2). Section 3.4 (probabilistic compilation) is too brief; e.g., it is unclear whether the model selects a single program or runs multiple programs simultaneously during training (Reviewer 1). The descriptions of library components (just arithmetic operators, or also sorting, basic logic?) and neural architectures for experiments 4.1 and 4.2 are missing, as are definitions of inputs, outputs, and the difference between “circuits” and “tables” (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- **Missing baselines**: No purely neural baselines are provided. Section 4.2 fine-tunes LLaMA without showing LLaMA’s performance alone; Figures/Tables lack comparisons with/without the differentiable modules (Reviewers 1, 2). Questions about the performance of standard LLM tool-use approaches are unanswered (Reviewer 4). Without baselines, the contribution cannot be assessed.
- **Unconvincing results**: Sorting accuracy on fixed-length lists is only 33–40% *despite* the ground-truth insertion sort algorithm being already in the library, which the authors misleadingly call “decent performance” (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). The authors claim the differentiable circuit ALU generalizes beyond lookup tables, yet Figure 3 shows *lower* accuracy for the differentiable circuit (Reviewer 3). The results do not support the claimed properties of being “universally expressive, adaptive, and interpretable” (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing analysis of length generalization**: No experiments evaluate generalization to longer sequences (e.g., training on lists of size 8, testing on size 12), which is the core problem for demonstrating true algorithmic reasoning rather than pattern matching (Reviewer 3). Sorting experiments are limited to fixed-length lists, undermining claims of algorithmic capability (Reviewer 3).
- **Insufficient exploration of training difficulties and limitations**: The paper does not discuss potential issues with the differentiable interpreter, such as numerical instability, gradient vanishing, or long noisy gradient paths (Reviewers 2, 3). The authors note that gradient-descent learning struggles even when the ideal algorithm is present, but provide no details on what did or did not work (Reviewer 2). The poor sorting performance is attributed to difficulty parsing natural language, but no ablation or evidence (e.g., appendix analysis) is given to support this claim (Reviewer 3).
- **Questions overlapping with criticisms** (all reviewers): (a) How does the model differentiate between program selections – are all programs run at once, or is learning from output error only? (Reviewers 1, 4). (b) What is the exact set of algorithms in the library for each experiment, and how are they compiled into differentiable form before training? (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). (c) Why use differentiable register machines for sorting instead of other techniques that might provide better gradient estimates? (Reviewer 4). (d) For a parse/unparse model, why is differentiability needed when discrete search works? (Reviewer 4). (e) What are the training hyperparameters used in 4.2? (Reviewer 3). (f) What is the difference between “instruction counter” and “program counter” and do formulae (3)–(4) assume broadcasting? (Reviewer 2).
- **Contrast between reviewers**: Reviewers 1 and 3 (scores 5, 3) and especially Reviewer 4 (score 1) find the evaluation too weak to support the paper’s claims. Reviewer 2 (score 6) is more optimistic about the approach but also stresses insufficient experimentation. Reviewer 4 additionally argues that the approach is not novel—it reduces to LLM tool argumentation with parsing/unparsing—and that the program search problem is trivial, differentiating it from the other reviewers who saw originality.
