## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Discriminator-Guided Action Optimization (DGAP), a framework that uses a trained discriminator to optimize LLM-generated action plans for embodied tasks. The discriminator learns a score function from a limited set of demonstrations to assess alignment between LLM actions and optimal actions at each step. LLMs are then prompted to generate actions that maximize this score, using historical action-score trajectories. The method is theoretically connected to critic-regularized optimization in RL. Experiments on ScienceWorld and VirtualHome with GPT-4 and Llama3-70B show superior performance and efficiency over prior methods.

### Strengths

- The framework achieves superior results on the studied benchmarks, outperforming baselines by a large margin (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- The integration of a discriminator to guide LLMs in generating higher-quality action plans is a creative and promising approach (Reviewer 2; one reviewer (score 8) was particularly positive about this contribution and the theoretical derivation).
- The paper provides a detailed theoretical derivation linking DGAP to critic-regularized optimization, offering insights into embodied planning optimization (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- The use of a score function to quantitatively assess planning effectiveness is more reasonable than previous outcome-supervised approaches (Reviewer 3).
- Experiments are thorough and include comparisons with a wide range of baselines, including reasoning-based and search-based approaches (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow (Reviewers 1, 2; though one reviewer noted room for improvement, see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty and motivation**: A reviewer with high confidence (5/5) questioned the novelty relative to prior language grounding work (e.g., Saycan) and found the fundamental differences unclear (Reviewer 1). Another reviewer noted the need to clearly emphasize the main contribution (Reviewer 4).
- **Generalizability and scalability**: Multiple reviewers raised concerns about generalizing the method beyond text-based environments. The current evaluation is on relatively constrained simulators; it is unclear how the discriminator would perform in real-world settings with continuous action spaces, high-dimensional state spaces, and more intricate sensor data (Reviewers 1, 2). The reliance on a limited set of demonstrations may limit generalization; the paper does not analyze sensitivity to demonstration quantity/diversity or adaptation to unseen tasks with different distributions (Reviewers 2, 4). More proofs of discriminator generalization are needed (Reviewer 4).
- **Benchmark choice**: The paper lacks experiments on widely-used embodied planning benchmarks like ALFRED (https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.01734), which includes both LLM-based and RL-based methods for comparison (Reviewers 1, 3). Reviewers suggested discussing differences with such benchmarks and comparing with explicitly stored, search-based methods to better demonstrate the discriminator’s advantages (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Performance on short vs long sequences**: The method does not achieve optimal performance on short-sequence tasks; its advantages are primarily evident in long-sequence tasks (Reviewer 3). More analysis of performance differences between short and long horizons is needed, as generalization to both types is important (Reviewer 4).
- **Groundtruth score quality**: The groundtruth score for offline data collection is evaluated by a sentence embedding model. The quality and potential noise of this embedding model could significantly affect the groundtruth and thus model performance (Reviewer 4).
- **Presentation**: While two reviewers praised the writing, one reviewer found it unclear regarding the main contribution and suggested adding qualitative planning results (e.g., visualizations) to provide more intuition of the benefits (Reviewer 4).

*Note on reviewer disagreement*: One reviewer (score 8) was notably more positive, rating the paper’s contribution and soundness higher than the majority (score 6), particularly praising the theoretical derivation and the score function approach. Their criticisms (e.g., missing ALFRED, short-sequence performance) are included above.
