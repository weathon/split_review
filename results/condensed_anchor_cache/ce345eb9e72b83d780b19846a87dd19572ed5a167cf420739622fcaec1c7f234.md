## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Neuron to Graph (N2G), an automated method that interprets individual neurons in LLMs by constructing a token graph/trie from the top-20 most activating dataset samples. The pipeline includes pruning (retaining substrings where the last token’s activation exceeds half of its full-context activation), saliency-based augmentation (replacing salient tokens with DistilBERT predictions), and trie construction. Evaluation is performed on a 6-layer SoLU transformer (The Pile dataset) against token-lookup and n-gram baselines. Reviewers’ scores range from 1 to 6, with major disagreements on novelty, rigor, and generalizability: some found it a practical, scalable tool (scores 5,6), while others considered it an ad-hoc post-hoc analysis lacking external validation and theoretical grounding (scores 1,3).

### Strengths
1. **Novel automated interpretation approach.** N2G provides an automatic method to explain neurons via a graph of activating sequences, enabling visualization and identification of neuron behaviors (Reviewers 1, 5). The use of a trie as a simpler model is praised for simplifying evaluation (Reviewer 5).
2. **Scalability.** The method scales to all 18,432 neurons of a 6-layer model using a single Tesla T4 GPU, making it practical for broader interpretability research (Reviewers 1, 4).
3. **Evaluation against baselines.** N2G outperforms token-lookup and n-gram baselines in predicting neuron activations, with precision-recall analysis that reveals interesting trends (Reviewers 1, 4, 5). However, some reviewers note the improvement is not significant (Reviewer 4) and overall performance is not great (Reviewer 5).
4. **Discovery of neuron behaviors.** The method identified neurons related to repetition detection, activation trajectories, and in-context learning (Reviewer 5). (Contrast: Reviewer 3 questioned the evidence for the in-context learning claim.)
5. **Clarity and organization.** The paper is well-written, motivated, and easy to follow (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited to one model and activation function.** N2G is tested only on a 6-layer SoLU transformer; it does not address popular SwishGLU-based LLMs, limiting practical relevance (Reviewers 1, 2, 5). Several reviewers emphasize that explaining widely-used models is more important (Reviewers 2, 3).
2. **Ad-hoc design with arbitrary hyperparameters.** The choice of top-20 samples, pruning threshold, saliency threshold, and augmentation threshold is made without strong justification (Reviewers 2, 3). The process lacks a rigorous definition of “explanation” and is closer to post-hoc analysis than a principled method (Reviewer 2).
3. **No external validation.** Activation prediction is evaluated on the same dataset used to construct the trie; there is no test of out-of-distribution generalization (Reviewer 3). This is a major limitation.
4. **Augmentation does not address the illusion of interpretability.** The paper acknowledges that looking at top-activating samples can create an illusion of understanding, but augmenting with DistilBERT merely rephrases tokens without introducing true distributional diversity, and this is not quantified (Reviewer 3).
5. **Resulting graph structure is limited.** The graph is essentially a pivot token with context tokens; it is not a rich or complex graph (Reviewer 2). The claim of “at scale” in the title is not clearly reflected in the method or results (Reviewer 2).
6. **Quantitative evaluation insufficient.** There is no comparison with state-of-the-art interpretability methods (e.g., [1] Bills et al.), and the improvement over baselines is marginal (Reviewer 4). The evaluation covers only one target model and dataset (Reviewer 5).
7. **Missing examples of poor explanations.** The paper notes that interpretability is weaker for deep-layer neurons but provides no concrete examples of poorly explained neurons (Reviewer 1).
8. **Unclear dataset description and missing details.** The Neuroscope dataset is not clearly described (Reviewer 4). The minimum activation level for selecting top-20 samples is not specified; some neurons may have no significantly activating samples (Reviewer 3). The x-axis label (“Relative Token Index”) is missing in Figure 2 (Reviewer 5).
9. **Unsupported claim about in-context learning.** The paper claims to have discovered “in-context learning neurons” based on repetition detection, but this jump is not supported by evidence (Reviewer 3).
10. **Unjustified assumptions.** The method assumes that neuron function is captured solely by high-activation samples, ignoring possible roles of low or moderate activation (Reviewer 5). It also assumes that token-level positions carry meaning to the last layer, while later-layer neurons may compose more abstract concepts (Reviewer 5).
11. **Missing ablations and justifications for steps.** Questions remain about why exactly 20 samples are used (Reviewer 1), how the pruning parameter e_i is determined (Reviewer 2), and whether the graph structure itself affects performance (Reviewer 4). The inspiration for the four-fold model is unclear (Reviewer 4).
