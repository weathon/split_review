## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes CollabUIAgents, a two-stage multi-agent learning framework for LLM-based agents in interactive environments (e.g., mobile operations, web browsing). Stage 1 uses curriculum learning on automatically synthesized multi-level instruction data to adapt the base model. Stage 2 applies a process reward decomposition strategy during multi-agent reinforcement learning, distributing rewards at both the agent and conversation-round levels without predefined roles. The framework aims to address sparse rewards, rigid role assignments, and poor cross-task/environment generalization. Experiments show improvements over open-source models and performance on par with closed-source models.

### Strengths
- Addresses important problems: sparse rewards in multi-step interactive tasks and rigid role assignments in multi-agent systems. The two-stage learning enhances adaptability and cross-task generalization. (R1)
- The method enables automated data generation, reducing need for manual annotation. (R1)
- Comprehensive experimental design with extensive comparisons to state-of-the-art models and thorough ablation studies provides strong empirical support. Results show impressive performance improvements. (R1)
- Some hypotheses are insightful, e.g., the explanation for randomly generated edges on communication graphs. (R2)
- Motivation is well clarified; methodology and experimental setups are well stated; experimental analysis is comprehensive and reasonable to justify importance of each module. (R2)
- Investigates an important direction in multi-agent LLMs and poses a promising avenue for future research. (R3)
- The problem of cross-platform generalization is important. (R4)
- The idea of assessing each agent’s contributions via a critic is interesting. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty.** The paper mainly combines existing techniques (data synthesis, curriculum learning, SFT, DPO, MARL) in an unsurprising way; no novel methodology. (R2, echoed by R1/R3/R4)
- **Clarity and reproducibility issues.**
  - Experimental setup lacks clarity on configuration and evaluation of different agent setups, impacting transparency and reproducibility. (R1)
  - The organization needs improvement, especially the "MARL with Edge Updates" section. (R4)
  - Task formulation: concepts of "agent" and "policy" need clarification. There is inconsistency between a_t in Eq(1) and Eq(3) – it is unclear whether a_t is a single agent action, a joint action, or an aggregated action. (R3)
  - Multiple unclear character definitions and grammatical errors: line 271 should be “each agent π_i” not “all agents π_i”; line 132 should be “the number of agents” not “the number agents”; transition function and maximum step both denoted T. (R3)
  - Equation 9 needs more explanation. (R4)
- **Scalability concerns.** The framework is demonstrated with a small number of agents. As the number grows, managing an undirected communication graph may become computationally expensive. (R1)
- **Process Reward Decomposition not well-justified.**
  - Skeptical about using a critic agent to directly generate temporal and structural credit assignments. (R4)
  - The nature of reward decomposition (stage 2) is unclear: is it an artificial prior or adaptive learning? If artificial, it is no different from role allocation in form and does not reflect adaptive learning advantages. (R3)
  - Claim that “the critic agent might find it simpler to identify whether a single decision is wrong than to judge long decision chains” seems to contradict RL literature (single-step rewards are usually less accurate than long-term rewards); needs evidence. (R3)
- **Missing multi-agent model definition.** Stage 2 lacks a formal model (e.g., Markov Game, Dec-POMDP). It should be explained how it relates to or differs from standard models. (R3)
- **Collaboration mechanisms unclear.** In stage 2, each agent seemingly learns independently; there is no explanation of how multi-agent collaboration is ensured or promoted. (R3)
- **Missing comparisons.** Lack of comparison with related work on multi-agent LLMs, e.g., Zhuge et al. (2024) "Language agents as optimizable graphs." (R4)
- **Data faithfulness and harms.** No discussion on the faithfulness of automatically generated data to realistic situations; hallucinations or societal harm from training on such data are not addressed. (R2)
- **Unclear design choices.**
  - Why is the curriculum divided into three parts? What is the insight? (R3)
  - In majority voting (Eq(5)), how are ties handled (random selection?) and does that cause performance fluctuation? (R2)
  - The link between local memory size and diversity of decision making (line 171-172) is not clearly explained. (R2)
  - The strategy of SFT followed by DPO is stated without reasoning. (R2)
- **Presentation issues.**
  - Figures could benefit from clearer annotations and layout to convey framework structure. (R1)
  - Some figures are unclear (e.g., annotations). (R1)
- **Weak connection to MARL.** The paper is "not strongly connected to MARL" (R4).
