## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes Automatic Discount Scheduling (ADS), a mechanism for imitation learning from observation (ILfO) that adaptively alters the discount factor during RL training to address “progress dependency” tasks—where proxy rewards for later steps hinder learning of earlier behaviors. ADS prioritizes early rewards initially and gradually involves later rewards. Experiments on nine Meta-World tasks show ADS significantly outperforms state-of-the-art ILfO methods (e.g., optimal transport based proxy rewards) and solves tasks unsolvable by baselines. The idea is simple, well-motivated, and shows strong empirical gains across tasks with abundant ablations (e.g., comparisons to fixed and exponential discount schedules). However, several reviewers note that the method, while effective, lacks theoretical analysis (especially regarding non-stationarity introduced by changing the discount factor in off-policy RL), and the evaluation does not compare against other curriculum learning or temporal-slicing baselines. There is also disagreement on whether ADS is inherently a generic RL technique or tightly tied to ILfO, and whether aspects of the problem (e.g., visual encoder quality) are fully diagnosed.

### Strengths

- The paper identifies a critical and previously unaddressed obstacle in ILfO—the progress dependency property—and provides a clear motivating example (Basketball task in Meta-World). The solution (ADS) seizes a key cause of the issue and is algorithmically intuitive and reasonable. [R2, R3, R1]
- The method is novel, simple, and effective. Changing the discount factor adaptively is a heuristic that strongly improves performance over fixed and exponential discount schedules. [R1, R4]
- Experiments are comprehensive: results on nine Meta-World tasks (varying complexity), demonstrable gains over state-of-the-art ILfO baselines, and comparisons to fixed discounts and exponential scheduling. Ablation studies are abundant, and reproduction details are provided. [R2, R4]
- The paper is well-written and clearly presented. The problem motivation, the challenge of progress dependency, and the ADS mechanism are articulated lucidly. [R4, R2, R3]
- The research is significant for robotic imitation learning: it broadens understanding of ILfO limitations and introduces a solution that can tackle tasks previously unsolvable. [R3]
- Reviewer 2 is confident in the contribution (rating 8), noting strong empirical performance and clear reasoning. [R2]

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of theoretical analysis**: The paper does not provide formal analysis of how the discount factor scheduling affects convergence, especially when used with off-policy algorithms like SAC. Changing the discount factor introduces non-stationarity in the target Q values for all data in the replay buffer; this is not well addressed and the paper seems incomplete without such analysis. [R1]
- **Missing comparisons against curriculum learning baselines**: The main comparison (Figure 3) only includes vanilla proxy-reward methods; it should also compare against existing curriculum learning approaches for imitation learning (e.g., gradually increasing the length of the demonstration considered, or dividing the sequence into temporal slices and imitating each in order). Without this, the claim that ADS is better than methods that do not use curriculum learning is weak. [R1, R3, R4]
  - Specific missing baseline: simply truncating the expert trajectory (e.g., maximize OT with first 25%, then 50%, then 75%, then 100% of the demonstration). [R1, R4]
  - Another missing baseline: temporally slicing the demonstration and imitating each slice sequentially. [R3]
- **Unclear problem scope**: The paper implies the problem lies with “any proxy-reward-based method,” but the motivating example (Basketball) may stem from the specific use of optimal transport (OT) with a frozen pre-trained ResNet visual encoder that does not capture task details well (e.g., high visual similarity in end frames). Would using a more task-specific or fine-tuned visual encoder mitigate the problem? The paper should clarify whether the issue is (1) proxy-reward methods in general, (2) OT specifically, (3) OT with an insufficient encoder, or (4) OT over partial observations. [R4]
- **Motivation of longer increasing subsequence (LIS) heuristic**: What are the advantages of LIS over OT for measuring progress alignment? If LIS is good at measuring macroscopic progress, could it be used directly as the reward function instead of OT? This choice needs better justification. [R4]
- **Relationship to existing RL challenges**: The progress dependency problem seems similar to catastrophic forgetting or training instability in RL. The paper should discuss connections to these classic issues and how methods like replay buffers might address them. [R2]
- **Model-based planning alternative**: Would a model-based planning approach also alleviate ILfO’s progress dependency? How does it compare with ADS? This is not addressed. [R2]
- **Generic RL implications**: Scheduling the discount factor is a technique applicable to all RL problems, not just ILfO. The paper should at least discuss potential challenges and benefits of applying ADS to general RL (e.g., convergence properties), even without experiments. [R1]
- **Comparison to goal-based reward RL**: The paper does not compare against RL with a goal-based reward formulation. Such a comparison would help situate ADS relative to a common alternative. [R3]
- **Intuitiveness of the idea**: At its core, the key proposition is intuitive—given the goal is to imitate a sequence, a dependency between actions is somewhat expected. The approach may be seen as a direct response to an oversight in the original problem formulation; more sophisticated reward designs or distance measures might address the challenge more naturally. [R3]
