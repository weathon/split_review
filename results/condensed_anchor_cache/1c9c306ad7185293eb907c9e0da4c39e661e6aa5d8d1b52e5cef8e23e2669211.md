## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces GraphFilter, a method for selecting high-quality and diverse subsets of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) data by representing the dataset as a bipartite graph linking sentences to their n-grams. A priority function multiplicatively combines a quality metric (SUPERFILTER) with a diversity metric (TF-IDF on n-grams), and the algorithm greedily selects sentences, removes covered n-grams, and recalculates priorities. Experiments across three model backbones (GEMMA-2-2B, MISTRAL-7B-V0.3, LLAMA-3-8B) and six benchmarks show that GraphFilter outperforms nine baselines in both performance and computational efficiency (e.g., up to 61× faster than AlpaGasus on CPU). Ablations validate that using both quality and diversity together is better than either alone, and that quality matters more with smaller budgets while diversity becomes more important with larger budgets.

### Strengths
- **Significance**: The paper targets the important and practical problem of data selection for SFT of LLMs. The method is scalable, efficient (CPU-only), and open-sourced, making it accessible for resource-constrained environments.
- **Originality**: Using a bipartite graph to represent sentence–n-gram relationships and iteratively removing covered n-grams is a novel approach to jointly optimize for quality and diversity. The multiplicative priority function combining SUPERFILTER quality with TF-IDF diversity provides a data-driven selection mechanism.
- **Quality**: The methodology is thorough, with comprehensive experiments across three model architectures (GEMMA-2-2B, MISTRAL-7B-V0.3, LLAMA-3-8B), six benchmarks, and nine baseline approaches. Ablation studies examine the impact of different n-gram combinations, priority functions, and the role of quality vs. diversity.
- **Clarity**: The paper is well-organized and communicates complex ideas effectively. Figure 2 and Algorithm 1 clearly illustrate the selection procedure. Tables present results and ablations in an easy-to-read format.
- **Support for main claim**: The experiments convincingly show that combining quality and diversity yields better subsets than using either alone. The finding that quality is most valuable under small budgets while diversity dominates at larger budgets is insightful.

### Weaknesses
1. **Related work gaps and scope overclaim**: The paper divides existing methods into quality- and diversity-focused, but many consider additional criteria (e.g., verbosity, complexity, helpfulness [3,4]). Methods like S2L [1] and DiverseEvol [2] (though task-specific) should be discussed; otherwise the claim of solving SFT data selection should be narrowed. The motivation for a SFT data budget is not well-justified—what controls the budget? Could downstream accuracy suffer on low-quality data? Is filtering for SFT as important as for pretraining?

2. **Reliance on SUPERFILTER quality metric**: The method depends heavily on SUPERFILTER, which may not generalize to domains or languages where perplexity-based metrics are suboptimal. No mechanism is provided to plug in alternative quality indicators. An experiment comparing SUPERFILTER with other quality metrics (e.g., per-task rewards) would test adaptability.

3. **Confusion and lack of ablation on TF-IDF diversity metric**: The use of TF-IDF is problematic. TF appears to be global term frequency, not within-document, so for many terms TF and DF are similar, and the diversity score reduces to \(x \log(1/x)\). Diversity actually arises from removing covered n-grams, not from the TF-IDF formula. No ablation tests whether using TF-IDF provides any benefit over simpler counts (e.g., frequency-based or uniform scores). Some reviewers found this unclear enough to question the method’s publishability.

4. **Overselling of claims**: The introduction claims GraphFilter “captures the complex relationships between sentences and n-grams,” but it only captures whether a sentence CONTAINS an n-gram. The claim “up to 61× faster” cherry-picks a single baseline (AlpaGasus) and may refer to a mode not using the priority function \(\phi(u)\), which is untested in the main results. The performance gains “up to +2.37, +3.02, +3.38” are measured against LONGEST, which is worse than RANDOM—not a competitive baseline. This misleads readers into expecting both higher accuracy and massive speedup jointly.

5. **n-gram removal and adaptability**: n-gram nodes are visited only once and then removed. This may hinder learning specific knowledge or capabilities that require repeated exposure. The method does not achieve best performance on all tasks, supporting this concern. No experiment explores visiting n-grams multiple times or using a soft removal.

6. **Limited error analysis**: No in-depth examination of cases where GraphFilter underperforms or selects less useful data (e.g., when quality and diversity conflict). The analysis focuses on aggregate metrics; a breakdown of why certain selected examples hurt performance is missing.

7. **Evaluation restricted to standard benchmarks**: The method is only tested on standard benchmarks (legal, biomedical, social media, etc.). Real-world scenarios with noisy, low-resource, or domain-specific data are not evaluated. Extending to multilingual or low-resource languages would strengthen generality.

8. **Computational efficiency analysis is minimal**: While runtime is reported, there is no breakdown of costs for graph updates, priority re-ranking, or TF-IDF computation. Scalability to substantially larger datasets is unclear.

9. **Lack of fine-grained control over n-gram diversity**: All n-grams are treated equally; there is no way to weight specific n-gram types (e.g., named entities, domain-specific terms). Overemphasis on broad n-gram diversity may include noisy or irrelevant patterns.

10. **Missing ablations and comparisons**:  
    - No test of n-gram lengths beyond trigrams (e.g., 4-grams, 5-grams) to see where diversity plateaus.  
    - The optimal combination of quality and diversity is untested: why Quality × Diversity and not Quality + Diversity, or other functional forms?  
    - When applying GraphFilter to instructions only, it is not compared against SOTA methods (DEITA, ARMORM) applied to instructions only—those methods were originally applied to both instructions and responses. A fair comparison would test each method under the same input type.  
    - Section 5.4 shows that as budget approaches the full 300K sentences, \(\Delta_{\text{all}}\) tends to zero; this should be explicitly noted.  
    - Variance/error bars are missing; re-running multiple selections is needed to assess stability.  
    - In Table 4, “Without TFIDF nor SuperFilter” yields 45.61, while random in Table 1 is 45.51 – is this a typo or re-run? It should be clarified.  
    - Comparison with DEITA in the same setting as Section 5.2 would isolate improvements from GraphFilter’s n-gram approach.  

   (The above points combine reviewer questions that overlap with missing experiments or ablations into Weaknesses.)

11. **Minor presentation issues**: Figure 1 is not very useful; it could better illustrate the algorithm.
