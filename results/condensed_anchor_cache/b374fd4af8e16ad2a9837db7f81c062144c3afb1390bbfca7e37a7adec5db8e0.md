## Merged Review

### Summary
Domain Shift Tuning (DST) is a framework that treats domain gaps as differences in knowledge subnetworks within PLMs. It introduces two components: a lightweight Knowledge Steering Layer (KSL) affixed after the last transformer layer, which predicts a topic and selects a weight to project the final hidden state into vocabulary; and Knowledge Distribution Modeling (KDM) as an auxiliary loss to align topic/knowledge latent representations with textual similarity. The PLM is frozen while the KSL is fine-tuned. DST is tested on encoder models for topic clustering and decoder LMs for text generation, aiming to achieve domain adaptation with lower computational cost.

### Strengths
- By framing domain adaptation as knowledge distribution alignment, DST minimizes computational overhead and avoids catastrophic forgetting, making it beneficial for limited-resource settings (R1, R3).
- The method is tested against an impressive number of baselines from domain adaptation and other PEFTs, and it outperforms several of them (R1, R2).
- The use of rKSL is instructive; it shows values much larger than zero, revealing how much more knowledge than the residual is being used (R2).
- DST is model-agnostic, strengthening its applicability beyond language and Transformers (R2).
- The subnetwork motivation and integration with the knowledge steering layer are interesting and intuitive (R2, R3).
- DST is tested on both clustering and text generation, which is valuable for both embedding models and LMs (R2).
- KSL provides a lightweight mechanism for representing domain-specific knowledge without changes to the underlying PLM architecture (R3).

### Weaknesses
- The motivation in the introduction is cursory and lacks logical connections. The claim that dataset size discrepancy can lead to catastrophic forgetting and poor generalization is not convincingly supported by the cited references, especially in the era of LLMs. The transition to the diversification of PLM applications is abrupt and missing clear logic (R1, R3).
- The foundational hypothesis that PLMs encapsulate multiple pieces of knowledge as subnetworks (lines 38–40) lacks supporting references or verification experiments. Representing domain gaps by differences in model parameters between source and target domains is insufficiently justified; it is unclear whether these parameter differences capture meaningful domain-specific knowledge rather than noise (R1).
- Table 4 is missing performance metrics for base methods such as PEFT on LLMs, and would benefit from a more diverse set of directly comparable baselines at similar computational cost (R1). Additionally, the baseline parameter settings may not be fair; they should be scaled to match DST’s parameter budget or throughput, or compared at similar computational cost (R2).
- Most previous work and baselines compared are already two years old; the paper needs more recent baselines and discussion of how DST compares to state-of-the-art methods (R3).
- In Table 4, the application of DST on Llama and BLOOM yields only negligible and inconsistent improvements, questioning its effectiveness and robustness for modern LLMs (R3). Furthermore, these LLM experiments lack comparison to few-shot or zero-shot prompting baselines, which is a significant omission given the models’ in-context learning capabilities (R2).
- The L_KDL loss term is not ablated to demonstrate its usefulness (R2).
- The paper references VAEs as inspiration for the latent vector \(z\), but this connection is never formally introduced nor detailed. The relationship between the continuous latent space in VAEs and the discrete knowledge components in DST should be explicitly defined, including how the training objectives differ (R2).
- The notation and writing are unclear in several places. The latent “knowledge” vector \(z\) is not clearly defined, nor is its length \(K\); it is unclear if \(K\) is fixed or variable per input sequence, and whether \(z\) is a scalar or vector across sequence indices. The notion of “knowledge” is redefined multiple times (e.g., “latent relative concept”, “co-occurrence pattern of tokens with similar semantics”) without consistency (R2). The paper states that “KSL considers knowledge as a quantized sample of the underlying token distribution” (like vector quantization?), but this is not clarified (R2).
- The selection process for the \(W_{az}\) matrices and how the minimum operation in KDM is differentiated are not described; the paper lacks code or pseudocode for KSL/KDM implementation, making reproduction difficult (R2).
- The KSL may introduce a speed reduction despite low parameter count, due to its additional layer with \(K\) subcomponents. A detailed analysis of training and inference time compared to baselines is needed (R2).
- The number of fine-tuning steps is missing, which is important for defining linear decay and understanding the method’s cost (R2).
- The rationale for using KL divergence for \(\text{SIM}_z\) and cosine similarity for \(\text{SIM}_{TID}\) is not explained. If \(z\) vectors are softmaxed probability distributions, this needs clarification and justification of how these different functions affect the minimization term in KDM (R2).
- The paper minimizes \(\min(\text{SIM}_z - \text{SIM}_{TID})\); a justification for this choice over a minimax formulation (maximizing the minimum) is needed (R2).
- Writing issues: Figures and tables (e.g., Figure 1 left side) appear cluttered, reducing clarity. The citation style disrupts readability; author names would be clearer within parentheses. Equation (6) has an incorrect symbol following “else”. Citation formatting should use \citep instead of \cite if following ICLR template guidelines (R1, R3).
