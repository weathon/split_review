## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper presents a comparative study of discrete prompt inversion methods (PEZ, GCG, AutoDAN, Random Search, PRISM, BLIP2 captioner) for text-to-image generation using Stable Diffusion 2.1 on 100 prompts (10 images each). Reviewers unanimously agree the work is clearly written and easy to follow, with a broad coverage of baselines and evaluation metrics (CLIP similarity, FID, KID, cosine similarity). However, reviewers have major reservations about the limited scope and impact. While some reviewers (R1, R2) find the study well-contextualized and a useful starting point, others (R3, R4) deem the experimental effort minimal, the results lacking deep interpretation, and the contribution insufficient for a top-tier venue. Key criticisms include: reliance solely on CLIP-space optimization and evaluation (biased, ignoring diffusion-loss methods), lack of human evaluation or analysis by image type, only one generative model tested, a single qualitative example, and no proposed improvements or novel insights beyond confirming captioning as a simple alternative. There is disagreement on the paper’s soundness (ratings from 1 to 3) and contribution (ratings from 1 to 2). The consensus score of 3 reflects average quality but with strong concerns about scope and impact.

### Strengths

- The paper is well-written, clear, and self-contained; methodologies are explained well and experimental setup is easy to understand and replicate.
- Broad coverage of six prompt recovery methodologies (PEZ, GCG, AutoDAN, Random Search, PRISM, BLIP2 captioner), adapting discrete optimization methods from language models to text-to-image.
- Quantitative analysis includes multiple evaluation types: image:image (FID, KID), text:image (CLIP similarity), and text:text (cosine similarity between inverted and original prompts).
- The qualitative analysis, though limited to one example, is thorough and highlights connections between related concepts in prompts and term rarity/imagination.
- The work is well-motivated and contextualized among prior works, defining why prompt inversion techniques are useful—some reviewers found this aspect strong (R1). The attempt at a more thorough benchmarking of prompt-inversion methods is appreciated (R4).
- The paper is easy to read and the experiments are presented in a way that facilitates reproducibility.

### Weaknesses

- **Limited scope and generalization**: The study uses only Stable Diffusion 2.1, which has a fixed CLIP-based text encoder. This biases results toward methods that minimize CLIP objectives (e.g., PEZ has better CLIP-scores) and limits insights to a single model. Reviewers question whether findings generalize to other generative models (e.g., DALL·E) or models that do not use CLIP as a text encoder (R1, R2, R4).
- **Focus on CLIP-based inversion is restrictive**: Nearly all benchmarked methods optimize in CLIP space and are evaluated using the same CLIP model. This makes the finding that CLIP similarity is a poor proxy for image reconstruction potentially an artifact of adversarial solutions rather than a general limitation. Using a different CLIP model (e.g., OpenCLIP) or more robust consistency metrics (e.g., DSG, VQAScore) could disambiguate this (R1, R4). EditBench (Wang et al., CVPR 2023) arrives at opposite conclusions about CLIP.
- **Missing comparisons with diffusion-loss-based methods**: Prompt inversion directly via the diffusion loss (e.g., Mahajan et al., CVPR 2024; Textual Inversion, Null-Text Inversion, Custom Diffusion, Imagic, Reversion) is not considered. These approaches use a stronger signal aligned with the diffusion distribution and can outperform PEZ across the board. Their inclusion would reveal how different families of approaches compare and whether results change when not using CLIP-based optimization (R2, R4).
- **Insufficient experimental depth**: Despite the benchmarking claim, the evaluation is basic: only 100 prompts from one model, no analysis of performance by image type (style reference, dense scene, single vs. multiple subjects), no separate treatment of variability across images from the same prompt, and no human evaluation to validate quantitative findings even at a small scale (R3, R4). The paper could easily be extended with more experiments; the current effort is minimal.
- **Lack of actionable insights or novel contributions**: Results are presented without deep interpretation; the Discussion section is rudimentary, simply stating that captioning is a good alternative and re-assessing known issues. There is no study or even specific proposal for improving existing methods based on the findings. The contribution is limited to reporting observations that are already partly known in the literature (R3). Reviewer 4 strongly disputes the motivation for focusing on discrete prompts (L138), noting that soft prompting is extensively used and cited much more often, making the paper's premise questionable.
- **Qualitative analysis is too limited**: Only a single image example is discussed, limiting utility and generality. No reconstructed images are shown for any method (R1, R4). The patterns observed may not hold for other prompt styles or concepts.
- **Captioning-based conclusion requires nuance**: The conclusion that captioning models yield better prompts is not necessarily true; the vocabulary distribution of a captioning model may not align with that of the text-to-image diffusion model. Moreover, the paper does not consider whether prompt fluency matters for generation quality (R2).
- **Minor issues**: 
  - The formatting does not follow the ICLR template (R2).
  - Section 3 (detailed method descriptions) could be condensed to allow space for experiments (R3).
  - No consideration of variability across images from the same prompt, which is included but not analyzed separately (R3).
