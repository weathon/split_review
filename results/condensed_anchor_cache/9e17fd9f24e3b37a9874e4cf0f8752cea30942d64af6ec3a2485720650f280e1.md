## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an adaptive training approach for Vision Transformers (ViTs) that changes the model’s shape (patch size and/or width) during training, guided by neural scaling laws. By traversing the scaling laws adaptively, the method aims to reduce total FLOPs required to reach a target performance compared to static architectures. The work leverages the flexible architecture FlexVIT and schedules either patch size or width (one at a time) using the gradient of the fitted scaling law to determine when to change. Experiments show FLOPs reductions against fixed baselines and FlexVIT schedulers.

### Strengths
- **Motivation and reasoning**: The paper addresses an important and timely problem—reducing training compute for large models—and provides good intuitive motivation by challenging the assumption that a static architecture is compute-optimal (Reviewers 2, 3, 4). The grounding in scaling laws gives a principled basis for the adaptive schedule (Reviewer 4).
- **Results**: Substantial FLOPs improvements are demonstrated over static baselines and the FlexVIT scheduling baseline, and the adaptive model surpasses its static counterpart for most patch sizes (Reviewers 3, 4). Reviewer 3 rates the results as “excellent.”
- **Presentation**: The paper is well-presented with helpful visualizations (e.g., Figure 3) that aid understanding (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Relevance**: The topic is highly relevant given the increasing computational demands of state-of-the-art models (Reviewer 4).
- **Related work**: The study of previous work is new and very related to the topic (Reviewer 1).

- **Minority positive reviewer (Reviewer 3, score 6)**: Unlike the other reviewers (all score 3), Reviewer 3 is considerably more positive, praising the “excellent results,” “good motivation,” and “adequate evaluation.” This reviewer found the method sound and the presentation excellent.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and related work**: The concept of adaptive models that change shape during training is very similar to Neural Architecture Search (NAS), yet the paper does not discuss NAS in the related work or compare against any NAS methods. A comprehensive overview of NAS (e.g., Elsken et al.) should be cited and at least a baseline comparison provided (Reviewer 4). The three claimed contributions could be summarized as one point—an adaptive strategy to change patch size and width (Reviewer 1).
- **Theoretical depth**: The paper lacks theoretical connections and relies almost entirely on empirical results. If the focus is empirical, more varied experiments and applications are needed (Reviewer 1).
- **Incomplete exploration of shape parameters**:
  - Only patch size and width are considered; other architectural choices (e.g., depth, number of heads) are not explored. Reviewer 2 and Reviewer 4 request ablations on other transformer hyperparameters.
  - The method schedules only one shape parameter at a time (either patch size or width). It is unclear whether the results hold when two shape parameters are scheduled together (Reviewers 1, 3). Cross-adapting experiments (e.g., changing both patch size and width) would strengthen the argument (Reviewer 1).
  - Only vision tasks are tested. An experiment on other modalities (text, audio) would demonstrate generality (Reviewer 3).
- **Computational cost analysis**: The paper reports only FLOPs, which do not fully characterize computational cost (e.g., training time, carbon emissions). A discussion or estimate of carbon emissions and the end-to-end cost of the method (including the greedy search to determine optimal hyperparameters) is missing (Reviewer 3).
- **Complexity and robustness of the scheduling**: The method introduces additional training dynamics and hyperparameter tuning. The paper lacks a detailed analysis of how the patch-size transition schedule is determined and whether the schedule is robust across different datasets and model architectures. The current approach uses a pre-defined schedule derived from scaling laws, but the practical implications of this choice require further exploration (Reviewer 4). Some limitations mentioned in the paper could be incorporated into the main experiments (Reviewer 1).
- **Overfitting concerns**: Altering the model shape during training could lead to overfitting. The paper does not provide train/test gap analysis, especially around the points where patch size or width changes. It is crucial to verify that these transitions do not cause sudden degradation in generalization performance (Reviewer 4).
- **Ambiguities**: There are ambiguities in figures/paragraphs; for example, the arrows in Figure 3 are not explained (Reviewer 1).
