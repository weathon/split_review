## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces Multiple-Frequencies Population-Based Training (MF-PBT), a hyperparameter optimization algorithm that mitigates the greediness of standard PBT by employing sub-populations evolving at distinct frequencies, with an asymmetric migration process to balance short- and long-term optimization. Experiments on Brax RL locomotion tasks with PPO show improved sample efficiency and long-term performance.

### Strengths

1. The idea of using multiple evolutionary frequencies to address PBT’s greediness is novel, well-motivated, and fits naturally into the existing literature on population-based RL methods. (R1, R2, R3)
2. The asymmetric migration process (separating parameter migration from hyperparameter migration to prevent hyperparameter collapse) is a practical insight that could benefit other population-based methods (e.g., PB2). (R1, R2)
3. The empirical validation shows benefits over standard PBT and a smart use of random search to confirm that greediness is reduced. (R2)
4. The experimental setup and ablation studies (e.g., on frequency spread, migration design) are motivated and detailed. (R2, R3)
5. The work clearly communicates the root cause of PBT’s shortcomings and uses it to motivate the proposed changes. (R3)

### Weaknesses

**Limited scope of evaluation and missing baselines**
- All experiments are confined to locomotion tasks in Brax with PPO; this is considered insufficient for a general HPO method. The paper should include more diverse domains (e.g., tasks from PB2 and PB2-Mix) and other RL algorithms beyond PPO (value-based, policy gradient). (R1, R2, R3)
- Recommended comparisons include Bayesian optimization (R1), PB2, BG-PBT, multi-fidelity HPO methods (Shala et al. 2022 & 2024, Eimer et al. 2023, Becktepe et al. 2024), and PBT-BT (Zhang et al. 2021) which uses backtracking as a form of migration across time. (R1, R3)
- Only seven random seeds are used; given Brax speedups, this is too low for reliable conclusions. (R3)

**Doubt about whether the improvement is due to the sub-population scheme vs. simply tuning PBT hyperparameters**
- Reviewer 3 argues that the paper ignores interaction effects of population size and selection criteria. Standard PBT with a larger population (e.g., 80 members, as originally recommended) or a reduced truncation fraction (e.g., 10% instead of 25%) can achieve similar diversity without explicit multi-frequency. The paper uses population size 32, which likely underperforms. (R3)
- The ablation on `t_ready` (evolution frequency) shows that standard PBT can reach similar performance to MF-PBT when its own frequency is tuned, casting doubt on whether MF-PBT’s sub-population structure is necessary. (R3)
- Reviewer 2 notes that better early performance might be due to lucky initial hyperparameter assignments rather than the frequency of evolution; a controlled experiment with randomized initial hyperparameters across frequencies is needed.

**Lack of thorough analysis of MF-PBT’s own hyperparameters**
- Only two frequency spreads are tested (in supplementary); more systematic exploration (uniform and non-uniform spreads) is needed to understand sensitivity and provide guidelines. (R2, R3)
- No analysis of sensitivity to other hyperparameters (e.g., population size, migration rate). Larger population sizes than 32 were found harmful, but no deeper investigation. (R3)
- The paper does not explore adaptive frequency selection instead of fixed frequencies. (R2)
- The computational overhead of managing multiple frequencies (especially asymmetry between cost of evolutionary updates and gradient updates) is not compared to standard PBT. (R2)

**Questionable experimental choices**
- The evaluation uses 512 validation episodes to compute fitness, which is unusually high and may be unrealistic in practical settings. (R3)
- The paper evaluates on excessively long training horizons far beyond the recommended Brax regime; on standard regimes the improvements are small (Table 1). (R3)
- The performance metric “anytime performance” is not rigorously defined; it is unclear whether MF-PBT is consistently better at all training stages or just faster initially. A more formal definition (e.g., integral of performance curve) is needed. (R2)
- Results section organization is confusing: hyperparameter analysis is presented before main performance results. (R2)
- The motivation and practical implications of anytime performance (e.g., reduced training time) could be better developed. (R2)

**Minor factual claim in abstract**
- Reviewer 3 notes that the claim that PBT generates hyperparameter schedules in a single training run is misleading because PBT requires vast parallel resources (as pointed out in the cited SEARL paper). (R3)

**Reviewer disagreement**
- Reviewer 2 is strongly positive (score 8) about empirical validation and insight; Reviewer 3 is strongly critical (score 6, recommends reject) asserting that the experiments unfairly treat PBT and do not convincingly show improvement. This contrast remains unresolved. (R2 vs. R3)
