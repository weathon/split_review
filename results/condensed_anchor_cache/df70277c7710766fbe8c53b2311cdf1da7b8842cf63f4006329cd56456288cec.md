## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes LRRL, a meta-learning approach that uses a multi-armed bandit algorithm (Exp3) to dynamically select the learning rate during deep RL training. The approach is evaluated with DQN on several Atari games and compared to fixed learning rates and schedulers. Reviewers find the problem important and the idea conceptually straightforward, but raise major concerns about experimental scope (only DQN, limited environments), sensitivity to the chosen set of learning rates (hyperparameter tuning is merely shifted), lack of comparison to existing meta-gradient methods or other hyperparameter optimization tools, and weaknesses in the bandit formulation (e.g., ignoring relationships between learning rates, inability to encode long‑term exploration/exploitation trade‑offs). The reported metrics and statistical rigor are also criticized.

### Strengths
- The problem of adjusting the learning rate during training is important, and the proposed bandit-based approach is conceptually straightforward and easy to implement. (Reviewers 1, 4)
- The motivation is clearly presented. (Reviewer 1)
- The bandit formulation yields insights into which learning rates are favored in different environments, as shown in Fig. 2. (Reviewer 1)
- LRRL achieves competitive performance with DQN+Adam on several Atari games compared to fixed baselines and schedulers. (Reviewer 2)
- In principle, the approach could reduce the need for hyperparameter tuning, though this is disputed. (Reviewer 2)
- The writing is clear and easy to follow; experimental details are well described in the main body and Appendix B. (Reviewer 3)

### Weaknesses
- **Choice of learning rate set remains a critical hyperparameter.** Many configurations of LRRL (e.g., different sets of arms) show no benefit over a fixed baseline; simply using the largest set 𝒦(5) does not guarantee improvement. The performance varies drastically—K_sparse(3) far outperforms other choices (Table 1). This contradicts the original motivation of reducing learning‑rate sensitivity and merely shifts hyperparameter tuning to the set of arms. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)
- **Experimental scope is too narrow for the claims made.** The paper only tests a single RL algorithm (DQN) on a limited set of Atari games. Claims about “deep Reinforcement Learning” are overstated. Reviewers suggest testing on PPO, SAC, A3C, TD3, DDQN, and continuous control tasks, or scaling back the claims to DQN specifically. (Reviewers 3, 4)
- **Lack of comparison to existing methods.** The paper does not compare LRRL to meta‑gradient methods (e.g., Flennerhag et al.) that already address learning‑parameter adaptation, nor to common hyperparameter optimization tools like Optuna. Such comparisons are needed to demonstrate advantage. (Reviewers 3, 4)
- **Bandit formulation ignores continuity of learning rates.** Learning rates are continuous and similar values have similar optimization properties, yet LRRL treats them as independent discrete arms. A linear bandit or contextual bandit formulation could exploit this structure. (Reviewer 1; also noted by Reviewer 4)
- **Inability to encode long‑term learning‑rate decay intuition.** A standard decay schedule sacrifices early reward for higher later reward (large steps first, fine‑tuning later). The bandit’s per‑window reward cannot capture such delayed benefits; this is a fundamental drawback. (Reviewer 2)
- **Sparse rewards can mislead the bandit.** If a feedback window falls entirely in a no‑reward part of an episode, the chosen learning rate is falsely penalized. This may explain why in Pong (sparse reward) the agent selects learning rates nearly uniformly. (Reviewer 1)
- **Reporting and statistical rigor are insufficient.** Max average return (Tables 1, 2) is noisy and poor practice (see Henderson et al.). Only 5 independent runs are used; the paper should report bootstrapped confidence intervals or tolerance intervals. The meaning of the shaded region in Fig. 1 is not specified (assumed std dev). (Reviewers 1, 3)
- **Missing experimental details.** Section 5.1 does not state which optimizer is used with DQN (RMSProp, Adam, or another?). In Fig. 1 the x‑axis is labeled “# iterations” – the total number of training steps is not given. It is also unclear why Pong is swapped for Video PinBall in Section 5.2, and how the specific Atari games were chosen – the paper should clarify whether only favorable results are reported. (Reviewers 1, 3)
- **Novelty is questioned.** Using bandits for hyperparameter selection in RL has been explored before; meta‑gradient methods already adapt learning parameters. The paper’s contribution is therefore limited. (Reviewer 4)
- **Limited range of learning rates tested.** All five learning rates are within one order of magnitude (a known good range for DQN). Experiments with a wider range (e.g., 10⁻¹ to 10⁻⁶) or randomly sampled sets are missing, making it hard to assess general applicability. (Reviewer 3)
- **Theoretical discussion of Exp3 is incomplete.** The paper’s claim that adversarial bandits handle nonstationarity is imprecise (it requires a sequence of changing best actions, not just any nonstationary setting; see Lattimore & Szepesvári). The paper also lacks analysis of alternative bandit algorithms (e.g., linear contextual bandits). (Reviewers 1, 4)
