## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper reconsiders and critiques the key claim of Feldman & Zhang (2020) (F&Z) that memorization is necessary for generalization. Three methodological issues are identified: (1) the memorization definition includes an arbitrary threshold that affects conclusions; (2) the approximation algorithm for leave-one-out memorization scores has a high upward bias; (3) the marginal utility calculation induces a distribution shift when removing points. The authors conduct experiments on 19 model/dataset/optimization combinations showing a strong negative correlation (Pearson -0.997) between memorization and generalization, contradicting F&Z. Reviewers appreciate the critical re-evaluation but raise concerns about conceptual framing, experimental rigor, missing comparisons, and overconfident assertions.

### Strengths
- Undertaking a careful analysis of a high-impact work (426 citations) is worthwhile and uncommon (R1, R2, R5).
- The paper is generally well-written, clear, and self-contained (R1, R3).
- Experiments are technically solid with settings and hyperparameters clearly stated (R2).
- The revisionist perspective – examining sub-population shifts and approximation errors – is novel and practically relevant (R4).
- The paper provides a valuable empirical contribution via Leave-One-Out experiments to reveal conditions under which the original approximation yields incorrect results (R4).
- The arguments aid intuition and attempt to collect empirical evidence (R5).

### Weaknesses
**Conceptual & framing issues**
- The paper’s original contribution to the memorization–generalization question itself is unclear; it refutes F&Z’s specific claims but does not directly provide evidence on whether the “necessary condition” holds (R2).
- Both the current paper and F&Z use an ambiguous, misquantified definition of memorization, so both suffer from potential oversimplification (R3). An alternative, more quantifiable definition would strengthen the paper (R3).
- The claim that F&Z’s experiments fail a falsifiability test (Line 231) is too strong; adopting a specific threshold is testable, if not terribly compelling (R1).
- The argument about removing memorized points with high vs. low scores (Line 243) is confusing and should be clarified or removed (R1).
- The claim that subpopulations are “most vulnerable to overestimation” (Line 265) needs tightening: if examples are dropped at random, the fraction of instances in a small population is statistically the same as in a large one – though a variance or sublinearity argument might apply (R1).
- The F&Z conclusion is specifically about long-tailed distributions; the cat example in this paper does not represent a long-tail setting and is misleading. A more appropriate example (e.g., 100 cat colors with few examples per rare color) should be used (R5).
- It is unclear whether the modified CIFAR-10/100 datasets still follow a long-tailed distribution (R5). The authors should provide quantitative analysis (e.g., samples per class, Gini coefficient).

**Experimental rigor & evidence**
- Since F&Z used a more powerful architecture, the validity of comparing 88% test accuracy on CIFAR-10 (moderate) vs. F&Z’s results should be acknowledged (R1).
- The Leave-One-Out estimate based on 20 models only has an accuracy of ±5% (1/20). Reporting absolute approximation error rather than relative error would be fairer to the original method (R1).
- The statistical test for the claim that “for most thresholds, the drop in accuracy is not statistically significant” (Line 399) is insufficient; simply overlapping ±1SD does not indicate significance. An ANOVA for main effect of item type (random vs. memorized) and interaction with threshold would be appropriate (R1).
- The assertion (Lines 307–308) that small sub-population points are “not memorized” is overly confident and lacks rigorous evaluation. The paper needs to provide a distribution of memorization scores for points within those sub-populations rather than a blanket statement, and confirm that the approximation algorithm overestimates their scores (R5).
- The identification of sub-populations lacks a formal quantitative definition. For example, “check if multiple memorized points impact the same test set point” needs a specific metric, and the visual check step is subjective. Granularity matters: two points both impacting the same test point may have very different magnitude impacts; grouping them together could be problematic (R5).
- The criterion for classifying sub-populations as “small” vs. “large” is not quantified. A clear metric and justification for any threshold are needed (R5).

**Reproducibility & missing details**
- The paper lacks detailed descriptions of experimental setup: data set segmentation, specific threshold settings in Figure 2 and Table 1, and the criteria for selecting the “influential 500 points” for the LOO experiments are unclear. The method for calculating influence scores is not specified (R4).
- The authors should provide a side-by-side comparison of the original equations and their modified ones to clarify definitional differences and the error introduced (R4).
- The meaning of “SPP” (Line 300) should be explained (R4).
- Sharing the data used for memorization and generalization analyses would support reproducibility (R4).

**Missing comparisons & alternate works**
- The discussion of related work focuses heavily on privacy-adjacent domains, ignoring other papers showing similar conclusions without the criticized flaws (e.g., Cheng et al. 2022, PMLR; another NeurIPS 2023 paper showing explicit memorization boosts test performance) (R2).
- To strengthen the critique, the authors should apply the original F&Z method to their modified CIFAR-10/100 and show that it still produces a misleading gap between curves (green vs. yellow) that falsely supports memorization necessity (R5).

**Minor issues**
- Line 68: “but due to common ML oversight” is redundant – it refers to ML oversight twice (R1).
- Line 72: “we disprove the conclusion” should be softened to “we obtain an alternative or weaker conclusion” (R1).
- Figure 1 caption is ungrammatical (R1).
- Line 244: “Fix” is a weird paragraph heading; suggest “Our solution” (R1).
- Line 251: “leave one out (LOO)” should be defined on first use (R1).
- Jiang et al. (2021) could be cited regarding subpopulation size effects (R1).
