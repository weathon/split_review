## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a large-scale dataset (FormL4) and a Process-Driven Autoformalization (PDA) framework for autoformalization in Lean 4, aiming to convert natural‑language mathematics into formal statements and proofs. The dataset includes both statements and proofs in natural and formal languages, and the framework uses Lean 4 compiler feedback to iteratively improve LLM performance. Reviewer opinions are strongly divergent: one reviewer (Reviewer 4, score 8) found no significant weaknesses, while another (Reviewer 3, score 1) considered the work severely flawed, and two others (Reviewers 1 and 2, scores 5 each) had moderate concerns. The following summary captures the main points raised across all reviews.

### Strengths
- Autoformalization is an important and timely problem; a good benchmark for Lean 4 has been lacking, so the dataset fills a gap.
- Incorporating compiler execution feedback during autoformalization is a novel and valuable idea.
- The proposed PDA framework (including the Process‑Supervised Verifier and iterative refinement) shows strong performance improvements compared to off‑the‑shelf models.
- The dataset is curated with a combination of automation and manual checking, and includes three meaningful test sets (random, basic, real‑world).
- The methodology for preventing formal terms from appearing in autoinformalized descriptions (Appendix C) is well done and well documented.
- The approach to compiler‑feedback integration is technically sound in concept.
- The code and data will be made public, supporting reproducibility.
- The paper’s overall structure is logical, and the motivation and potential impact are clearly articulated.
- The process‑driven verification direction could be valuable beyond this specific application, potentially reducing the burden of formal verification.

### Weaknesses
*Note: Reviewer 4 stated that they see no significant weaknesses. The following list combines the substantial concerns raised by the other three reviewers, with their specific evidence.*

**Dataset construction and quality concerns**
- The dataset was built by LLM‑based informalization, but only a small fraction of samples received manual checking (≈ 3% of the data); the manual success rate was only about 72%, which is insufficient for a benchmark meant to be reliable.
- The claim of “extensive manual verification” (L256–257) is misleading given the low coverage and 72% accuracy; using “verification” in this context is at odds with the usual standards of formal verification.
- The paper systematically obfuscates dataset quality:
    - L201–203 states that FormL4 does not guarantee strict semantic alignment between formal and natural‑language proofs, yet the abstract and introduction claim the dataset is “rigorously quality‑checked manually”.
    - L205–208 says that “the quality of FormL4 … does not pertain to whether the natural‑language proof perfectly corresponds to the formal proof”; this is contradictory – semantic correspondence is directly relevant to dataset quality.
    - L213–215 claims that perfect translation of formal proofs to natural language is infeasible, but almost all tactics have understandable natural‑language descriptions; the paper should simply permit elision of details, not allow incorrect steps.
- No effort is made to deduplicate lemmas that are essentially the same (e.g., proving `x=y` and `y=x`) across train/test splits (L166–167).
- The role of proofs in the dataset is unclear: does it contain incorrect proofs? If so, how are they useful? (Reviewer 1, L201–208)

**Evaluation and baseline issues**
- The evaluation (Table 4) does not measure appropriate baselines or rule out data contamination. A proper baseline would compare with an autoformalizer trained on randomized mislabeled data from FormL4 (unrelated informal‑formal pairs). The high results may simply reflect homogeneity between train and test splits.
- Contamination concern: correct answers in the dataset are directly copied from Mathlib; models trained on Mathlib have an unfair advantage, potentially invalidating the benchmark results.
- The final evaluation metric relies on whether generated code compiles, but code that compiles is not necessarily correct; this metric can be misleading. Even when both statement and proof compile, the success rate is only 75%, and 50% when the proof fails to compile. It is unclear how the evaluation handles multiple valid formalizations of the same statement or whether all statements are compatible with all proofs.
- Table 14 (in Appendix N) shows extremely low accuracy on MATH (0.65%) and GSM8K (8.16%) even with a fine‑tuned Mistral model. This suggests that high results on FormL4 are due to overfitting or limited difficulty range, yet this is buried in the appendix. The final baseline model’s performance line is missing from that table (L1566–1569).
- The “informalization success” rate of 0.72 is claimed to indicate high‑quality performance (L259) without any benchmark or comparison to justify that threshold.
- What about the 20% of samples where Gemini failed during informalization? (L245–247)

**Generalizability and methodological gaps**
- The claim that PDA generalizes to other formal languages (L103) is unsupported; the framework is narrowly focused on Lean 4 and no evidence or discussion is provided for adapting it to Coq, Isabelle, or Agda, which have different proof styles and compiler outputs.
- It is not adequately explained why proof steps are necessary for compiler‑based evaluation: the Lean 4 compiler can check a theorem statement alone (with empty proof). The advantage of using statement‑proof pairs should be clarified.
- The Process‑Supervised Verifier (PSV) in Figure 1 only appears to indicate which statement failed, not utilizing the error message from the proof assistant. Is that correct? (Reviewer 3 question)
- The terms RFT and VEA are only referenced, not explained (L418–419).
- There is confusion in L512–515 about the difference between “proof validity” (whether the sample passes the Lean 4 compiler) and “autoformalization performance” (whether the generated proof is faithful to the informal sketch).

**Qualitative analysis and failure modes**
- Qualitative evaluation of failure modes is inadequate. The explanation for why some theorems are hard to informalize (L1284–1289) is vague – what “topics” or “settings”? No concrete examples of challenging cases or failure modes are given.
- The link between the claim that providing proof steps improves informalization quality and the results in Table 6 is not clearly justified (Reviewer 1, L194).
- Individual subjectivity in determining condition constraints is mentioned, but it is not stated whether most human‑checked informalizations were merely incomplete rather than incorrect; separate numbers for correctness vs. completeness would be helpful.

**Other specific issues**
- Several reviewers noted missing statistical significance tests (e.g., for Table 4).
- Minor wording, grammar, and formatting errors: line 141 (tense mismatch), line 176–177 (capitalization), line 190–191 (missing letter), line 194–195 (“preceded” → “preceding”), line 326 (figure specifier `[ht]` in equation), line 491 (“falls short of even empowered”), line 493 (“syntactically true” → “semantically valid”), line 519–520 (missing word), line 533 (“drive” → “driven”), line 534–536 (grammatical error), inconsistent use of “Lean 4” vs “Lean4”, and several instances of wrong quotation marks.
- The paper should use “verifier model” instead of “verifier” to avoid confusion between the Lean compiler and the PSV model.
- The end of Section 5 (“Factorial Design”) is poorly worded and could be confused with the factorial function; the term “Dataset Split” should be consistent with “Test Set Categories”.
- Section 6 needs rewriting; the second sentence confuses the dataset with the PDA method.
- Table 14 suggestion: add `\texorpdfstring{\textsc{FormL}4}{FᴏʀᴍL4}` for proper small‑caps in PDF bookmarks.
