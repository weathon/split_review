## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a reward-based policy that uses only the history of rewards and actions (no observations) to achieve zero-shot transfer across environments with different observation spaces. The method trains an LSTM policy with PPO and an offline behavior cloning loss on demonstration data. Evaluations are conducted in Pointmass, Cartpole, and Car Racing, with a 2D-to-3D transfer demonstration in a reconfigured AirSim environment.

### Strengths
- **Well-written and easy to follow** (R1, R2).
- **Principled and intuitive approach**: The idea of conditioning on reward history is clearly motivated and explained (R1, R4). In particular, the authors convincingly argue why reward-based learning can be effective in navigation tasks (R4).
- **Thorough challenge analysis**: The paper provides an in-depth examination of difficulties inherent to reward-based policies, including poor observability, difficulty estimating value functions, and limited exploration. It also highlights the necessity of dense rewards and shows how the method struggles as dimensionality increases (R3).
- **Expert guidance ablation**: An ablation study on the behavior cloning loss demonstrates its importance, especially in the more complex Car Racing task (R2, R3).
- **Simplicity and comprehensibility**: The method is straightforward with minimal components and no additional tunable hyperparameters, making implementation easy (R3).
- **Robustness to observation quality**: Once trained, the model is independent of observation quality, allowing it to perform well even with noisy or degraded visual observations (R3).
- **Effective reward estimation for transfer**: Section 4.4 shows that estimating rewards from images allows the reward-based policy to perform well even without access to the true reward at inference. Transferring from a 1D reward signal is easier and more reliable than from high-dimensional observations, requiring minimal data and proving more resilient than state estimation (R3).
- **Ablations and statistical significance**: The authors perform ablations and report results with statistical significance over 50 trials (R1).
- **Novel perspective**: The work offers a fresh approach to policy learning by using rewards as the primary signal (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Unrealistic assumptions and limited applicability** (R1, R2, R3, R4):
  - The method assumes dense and accurate rewards with no analysis of how sparse rewards can be before training fails (R3 Q6, Q7). In practice, reward signals are often noisy, delayed, or human-generated, and it is unclear how the method would handle such imperfections (R3).
  - The source and target environments must share identical transition dynamics, action spaces, observation spaces, and reward structures. The paper does not discuss realistic scenarios where these conditions hold, leaving practical feasibility unexplored (R1, R2, R3 Q8, R4 Q2 on generalization to varied MDPs).
  - Expert guidance (behavior cloning from an observation-based expert) significantly boosts performance, especially in complex tasks, undermining the claimed “reward-only” approach. Training an expert adds computational overhead and requires online inference from that expert (R3 Q9).
  - The method essentially treats the RL problem as a bandit-like setting, ignoring that actions change the state distribution. This theoretical mismatch raises doubts about whether the approach can work in general RL problems (R1).
  - Because the policy does not depend on observations, it is obvious that it will generalize across observation shifts. The experiments are therefore unsurprising (R2). The color palette shift is a trivial change that the method ignores by design (R3 Q4).

- **Loss of spatial context**: By relying solely on rewards and actions, the method lacks spatial awareness critical for tasks like navigation (e.g., distinguishing similar corridors) and manipulation (e.g., reaching objects at different positions). In Car Racing, the ability to keep the car centered on the track depends on an overly-engineered reward function, which is impractical in real-world applications (R3 Q10). This limits the method to simple tasks where positioning does not play a role (R3, R4).

- **Lack of methodological novelty** (R1, R3):
  - The approach is a straightforward combination of an LSTM, behavior cloning, and PPO, all well-established techniques. No new theoretical insights or scaled experiments are provided (R1, R3).
  - The explanation of the method is sparse: Figure 3 and its caption are unclear, e.g., not evident that the “options” in the testing phase depict modes where rewards are true versus learned (R3).

- **Weak experimental evaluation** (R1, R3, R4):
  - Experiments are limited to simple environments (Pointmass, Cartpole, Car Racing). The authors note that more complex environments with higher state dimensions or sparse rewards are “impossible” for this method, raising questions about practical utility (R3, R4). It is unclear whether reward-based policies can handle complex locomotion, manipulation, or humanoid control (R4).
  - No comparisons are made with other established RL techniques (e.g., domain randomization, domain adaptation, model-based methods (R3 Q1)) that could address observation shifts, offering no context for the method’s relative performance (R1, R3 Q11).
  - The number of seeds used for training is not specified, making it difficult to assess robustness (R3). Evaluation uses 50 trials but training seeds matter.
  - No ablation is performed on the LSTM component: stacking previous rewards and actions is not explored, leaving it unclear whether the recurrent network is necessary or if simpler methods could suffice (R3 Q5).
  - The demonstration dataset size and the balancing of losses between RL and supervised learning are not reported (R1 Q1, Q2).

- **Transfer limitations** (R2, R3):
  - The 2D-to-3D transfer (Car Racing to AirSim) manually reimplements kinematics to match the source environment, circumventing realistic challenges. This undermines the notion of true zero-shot transfer, as manual alignment is rarely viable in real-world applications (R3).
  - No quantitative results are provided for the 3D transfer; only a claim of “reasonable driving performance” (R3 Q12). The authors dismiss dynamics shifts as outside focus, but adaptation to dynamic differences is unavoidable in 2D-to-3D transfer (R3).
  - The reward estimation section (Section 4.4) requires access to a state-based policy that solves the task, which is unrealistic if the problem is a POMDP where states are not known (R2).

- **Scalability concerns**: The method’s effectiveness in more complex tasks remains uncertain, and it may struggle to learn necessary behaviors before transfer (R4).

- **Minor issues** (R2, R3, R4):
  - The POMDP definition is missing the observation function (R2).
  - The definition of the reward-based policy as $R\times A \to A$ is incorrect; the method uses a full history of rewards and actions, not just the most recent pair (R2).
  - The illustrative example in Figure 1 (real-road transfer) is missing from the paper, which may confuse readers (R4).
  - Grammatical errors with “et al.” citations (R2).
  - Incorrect capitalization in line 248 (R2).
  - The second hypothesis in line 253 is validated by definition, so it is not a meaningful hypothesis (R2).
