## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates the roles of data representation and selection algorithms in data pruning for NLP fine-tuning. The authors categorize methods by representation (training dynamics, hidden states, gradients) and selection objective (difficulty, diversity, validation performance). Through theoretical analysis, synthetic simulations, and experiments on 3 NLP tasks (DeBERTaV3, OPT), they find that gradient-based methods generally outperform cheaper alternatives, and that representation choice often impacts the pruned set more than the selection algorithm. The study is timely and well-positioned, but reviewers raise significant concerns about conceptual clarity, flawed theoretical reasoning, fairness of method comparisons, limited novelty, and insufficient generalizability. All four reviewers gave a rating of 3, reflecting a consensus that the work has value but suffers from critical weaknesses that limit its contribution at a top venue.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-positioned and investigates a crucial and timely problem: disentangling the roles of representation and selection in data pruning (R1, R3, R4).
- It provides a broad, organized review of existing data pruning methods, covering training dynamics, hidden states, and gradients as representations, and difficulty, diversity, and validation performance as selection objectives (R1, R3, R4).
- The related work section is extensive, clearly written, and self-contained, offering a compact overview of the field (R1, R2).
- The paper combines theoretical analysis, synthetic simulations, and empirical studies on standard NLP benchmarks, giving the work a multi-faceted structure (R1, R3, R4).
- It introduces a previously unrecognized perspective: many pruning methods can be disentangled into separate choices for representation and scoring/selection rule (R4).
- The theory suggests similarities between different representation choices (gradients, hidden states, training dynamics), offering a unifying view (R4).
- The experiments compare several existing methods and also explore new combinations of representation and scoring that had not been systematically compared before (R4).
- A key practical insight is provided: LESS failed on the CAD dataset due to label imbalance, and a simple label-balancing fix restores its performance (R2).
- The paper emphasizes that representation often plays a more fundamental role than the selection algorithm, providing practical guidance for practitioners (R3, R4).
- The findings, while not universally surprising, are in the right direction and could inform future research (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Conceptual ambiguities and inconsistent terminology:** The paper uses “data pruning” and “data selection” interchangeably, but these have important distinctions (e.g., one-shot removal of redundant data vs. multi-round selection or coreset selection). The authors also do not clearly define key terms or discuss how their findings might differ across these settings (R1). This confusion extends to the title “for fine-tuning,” which is not discussed in the paper beyond the experimental setting; data pruning for fine-tuning (e.g., instruction-tuning) is a different problem with different considerations, and none of the baseline methods were proposed for such use cases (R1, R3).
- **Flawed theoretical reasoning:** In Section 3.2 (lines 243–244), the argument that if \(|\sigma(x) - \sigma(y)|\) is small then \(|x-y|\) must be small (due to sigmoid monotonicity) is incorrect. Because the sigmoid flattens at \(\pm\infty\), the difference in sigmoids can be arbitrarily small while the argument difference is arbitrarily large. This undermines a core part of the theoretical analysis (R2). Moreover, the motivation for the theoretical analysis is unclear: the quantities studied (differences in output probabilities or gradients) are not directly used by any pruning method, and the connection to the methods under study is weak (R2, R4). The theory is also described as not providing in-depth insights beyond general similarity observations (R1).
- **Conclusions are not surprising or overly ambiguous:** Several main conclusions are either trivial or too vague. For instance, the finding that “methods with the same objective select different datapoints depending on their representation/scoring” is unsurprising because “difficulty” is not a fixed property but a human-defined measurement that varies across methods; the claim that “data pruning methods may not be effective” is too general without specifying conditions (R1, R2, R3, R4). The simulated experiments in Section 3.3 are described as sanity checks rather than sources of new insights (R4). The paper does not sufficiently quantify when methods work and when they fail, nor does it provide specific conditions for effectiveness (R1, R3, R4).
- **Methods taken out of their intended contexts, leading to unfair comparisons:** The paper evaluates methods in a uniform single-task fine-tuning setting, but many of these methods were originally designed for different scenarios (e.g., S2L for domain-specific fine-tuning, LESS for downstream task-specific fine-tuning with billion-parameter models). Swapping their representation components and applying them in a setting not aligned with their original intent may diverge from the methods’ design principles and undermines the validity of the conclusions (R1, R3). For example, S2L’s representation (training trajectories) was specially designed for domain-specific fine-tuning; labeling it “representation-agnostic” (Section 3.3) is a misinterpretation (R3). Similarly, the claim that Pruthi et al. use influence functions (lines 117–118) and that Feldman & Zhang use gradients (lines 125–127) are not accurate representations of those works; the former compares to influence functions, the latter uses prediction differences after retraining, not gradients (R4).
- **Experiments are limited in scope and scale:** The experiments are confined to NLP fine-tuning tasks (3 tasks) with models up to 350M parameters, while many baseline methods (e.g., LESS, S2L) were originally tested on billion-parameter models. The small scale and limited task variety restrict the generalizability of the findings (R1, R3). The paper does not test on larger models (e.g., LLaMA-7B, Pythia-1B) or broader contexts (e.g., instruction tuning, multi-task learning) (R3). The study also focuses on task-specific fine-tuning and excludes general instruction fine-tuning scenarios, raising questions about whether the conclusions extend beyond the specific setting (R1, R3).
- **Lack of novelty and no new methods proposed:** The paper purely evaluates existing approaches; no new data pruning methods or frameworks are proposed. While a comprehensive comparison is valuable, the contribution is limited by the absence of novel algorithmic insights or improvements (R3, R4). The paper does not derive new useful combinations of representation and scoring that consistently outperform existing methods, nor does it provide clear guidance on which specific implementations to use in practice (R4).
- **Presentation and structure issues:** The overview of methods in Section 3.1 reads as a laundry list without enough structure; the paper would benefit from tables clearly mapping each method’s representation and scoring approach, and from providing concrete examples (e.g., distance to cluster centroid) to clarify the second stage (R4). Section 3.2 mixes elaboration and derivations; presenting theoretical results as formal theorems with remarks would improve rigor and clarity (R1).
- **Specific experimental gaps and unclear choices:** The paper does not explain why LESS and memorization produce relatively similar scores in Fig. 2a-b (R4). LESS-balanced is shown in Fig. 2d without showing balanced counterparts for other methods, and LESS-OOD is used in Fig. 2e without showing the normal LESS usage (R4). There is an extra point on the x-axis for LESS in Fig. 2a-b (R4). The text mentions Spearman correlation but does not check whether Pearson correlation yields consistent results (R4). The assumption that representations from the training model are available in a one-shot setting is strong and not discussed (R1).
- **Missing systematic review of method scenarios:** The paper focuses only on the content of methods (representation and selection) while overlooking the specific scenarios (e.g., training from scratch vs. fine-tuning, domain adaptation) each method was designed for. This narrow focus makes the attempt to disentangle components appear artificial and limits the credibility of universal conclusions (R3).
