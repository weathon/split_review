## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes SMILING (Score-Matching Imitation LearnING), an online imitation learning (IL) method that replaces the traditional adversarial discriminator with a diffusion-model-based score function. The key idea is to minimize the Diffusion Score Divergence (DS divergence) between expert and learner state distributions by training score functions via simple regression on noise. The paper provides theoretical first- and second-order instance-dependent bounds with linear scaling in horizon, and compares SMILING against BC and DAC on continuous control tasks (including humanoid walk, sit, crawl, obstacle navigation), showing improved performance. The work is motivated as an alternative to GAN-style IL (e.g., GAIL) that avoids adversarial training instability and mode collapse.

### Strengths
- **Clear motivation and intuition**: Using diffusion model score functions for distribution matching is a well-motivated alternative to adversarial discriminators, leveraging the strong distribution modeling capability of diffusion models. (Reviewers 2,4,5)
- **Solid theoretical contributions**: The paper provides well-defined assumptions, lemmas, and proofs for first- and second-order instance-dependent bounds, sample efficiency, and comparisons between score functions and discriminators. Section 5.1 intuitively explains why score functions can be more expressive than discriminators in linear models, and this is validated by experiments in Section 6.3. (Reviewers 1,2,3,5)
- **Impressive experimental results on challenging tasks**: The method outperforms baselines (BC and DAC) on several continuous control benchmarks, particularly on complex humanoid tasks (e.g., Humanoid Walk). Reviewers 4 and 5 noted these results as strong evidence of the benefit of avoiding GAN training. (Reviewers 4,5; Reviewer 1 also notes good performance)
- **Theoretical grounding for data efficiency**: The analysis (e.g., line 368) suggests that SMILING can achieve enhanced data efficiency compared to traditional f-divergence or IPM matching methods, especially near-deterministic expert cost. (Reviewer 1)
- **Clarity of exposition**: The writing is clear overall, with both theoretical and intuitive explanations; notations, formulations, and theorems are easy to follow. (Reviewers 2,4,5)

### Weaknesses
*Note: Items marked with † indicate that the minority (score 8) reviewers were less critical of this point, while items marked with ‡ indicate that the majority (score 6) reviewers were more critical.*

- **Insufficient comparison to other diffusion-based and recent IL methods** (†Reviewers 1,2,3 strongly criticize; Reviewers 4,5 only mention missing comparison in a few prior works):  
  - The paper only compares against BC and DAC, which are considered weak or obsolete baselines.  
  - Missing comparisons to recent adversarial IL methods such as ValueDICE [E], IQlearn [F], and especially diffusion-based IL methods: DiffAIL [A], Diffusion-KDE [B], DBC [C], DRAIL [D].  
  - Missing comparison to offline imitation learning methods using diffusion models (e.g., as policies) to show the benefit of online demonstrations.  
  - Without these comparisons, the paper cannot convincingly demonstrate that SMILING is competitive or novel.  
- **Lack of experimental validation of theoretical claims** (Reviewers 1,2):  
  - The theoretical results on data efficiency (Section 5) are not backed by experiments quantifying the relationship between number of expert trajectories and converged normalized score.  
  - No experiments are provided for both stochastic and deterministic cost scenarios to substantiate the theoretical analysis.  
- **Missing experimental details** (Reviewers 2,3):  
  - The performance and training details of the expert policy (RL algorithm, reward function, number of environment steps, specific values of expert and random policy performance) are not reported, making it impossible to assess the quality of demonstrations or sample efficiency claims.  
  - For example, if the expert policy required ~10^6 steps, directly applying RL to learn the policy might be simpler.  
- **Novelty concerns and insufficient distinction from prior work** (Reviewers 1,3,4):  
  - Several prior works have applied diffusion models to IL (DiffAIL, DRAIL, etc.) and energy-based models for score matching in IL (e.g., arxiv 2004.09395, OpenReview pdf). The paper does not clearly articulate the technical differences and advantages over these methods.  
  - The framing as "LfO" (learning from observations) is not fully leveraged; the objective could readily apply to state-action matching, as shown in Figure 4. The specific considerations for LfO are not demonstraed.  
  - Reviewer 3 notes that the connection to inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) in Section 2 is confusing; the method does not infer a reward function but directly uses score matching as a cost, appearing to be pure IL without discriminator. Clarify whether a reward function is inferred.  
- **Clarifications needed in the algorithm description** (Reviewers 4,5):  
  - In Algorithm 1, the cost function \(c^{(k)}\) is not explicitly defined; its relationship to the score functions and Eq.3 should be clearly stated.  
  - The phrase "mixture of preceding policies" in line 3 should be precisely defined: does it mean averaging state distributions from previous iterations, or some other combination? A concrete example or diagram would help.  
  - Clarification requested for the sentence at line 081 about fitting score functions to the mixture of preceding policies.  
- **Time complexity of cost function evaluation** (Reviewer 5, also noted by Reviewer 2 indirectly):  
  - Evaluating \(c^k(s)\) in Eq.3 requires 5,000 DDPM steps (model evaluations) per state, which could be prohibitive for high-dimensional problems.  
  - If a single randomly sampled time step is used to estimate Eq.3, the variance of this estimate should be incorporated into the theoretical analysis but is not addressed.  
- **Data aggregation choice not justified** (Reviewer 5):  
  - The algorithm uses a mixture of all preceding policies \(\pi^{(1:k-1)}\) for score matching, but the necessity over just using the most recent policy \(\pi^{k-1}\) is not justified theoretically or empirically. An ablation comparing these two choices is needed.  
- **Section 6.3 interpretation unclear** (Reviewer 3):  
  - The experiment supposedly shows score functions are more expressive than discriminators, but the results may simply reflect that adversarial training suffers from mode collapse. The claim of "expressivity" versus "robustness to mode collapse" needs clarification.  
- **Assumption (c) and \(\epsilon_{RL}\) in discrete state spaces** (Reviewer 5):  
  - The rate of \(\epsilon_{RL}\) is discussed only for continuous spaces; score matching is typically applied in continuous spaces, and extending to discrete spaces is not straightforward. The paper should discuss limitations or possible adaptations.  
- **Minor issues**:  
  - Lines 267-269 mention SAC/DreamerV3, but these are not used in experiments; this appears to be leftover from a previous draft (Reviewer 3).  
  - Reviewer 1’s question about whether the theory applies to state-action pairs (Q1) and whether score-matching can circumvent the LobsDICE issue (Q2) are not addressed; these relate to missing theoretical discussion or experiments.
