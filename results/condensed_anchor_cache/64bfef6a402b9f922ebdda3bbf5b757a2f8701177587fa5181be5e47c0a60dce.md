## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes FIG, an algorithm that uses pre-trained flow matching or diffusion models to solve linear inverse problems. FIG guides the reverse-time sampling by conditioning on “measurement interpolants” instead of the direct measurement. The authors provide theoretical justification showing that for specific interpolant choices the relationship between signal and measurement interpolants retains the same linear model with adjusted noise. The algorithm is simple to implement as a modification to existing diffusion/flow matching samplers. Experiments on several inverse problems (super-resolution ×4/×16, Gaussian/motion deblurring, inpainting) using both flow matching and diffusion models show state-of-the-art results, especially in challenging settings.

### Strengths
- The idea of measurement interpolants is intuitively appealing and theoretically justified, with proofs that Condition 1 and Assumption 1 guarantee the validity of the sampling scheme.
- The method is conceptually simple and easy to implement as a change to existing pre-trained diffusion or flow matching models.
- The theoretical framework is well‑presented with mathematical formulations and clear pseudocode (Algorithm 1).
- Evaluation covers a variety of tasks and datasets with strong experimental results; FIG surpasses prior baselines (e.g., DDNM⁺) particularly in challenging inverse problems.
- The algorithm works for both diffusion and flow matching generative models.
- The paper is clearly written and well‑structured, explaining core concepts and providing a detailed derivation.
- Reviewers agree the method is sound and the empirical results are solid, though opinions on novelty vary.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty of guidance design.** The use of a gradient of a reconstruction function to guide generation has been widely explored in prior work. While implementation details differ, the core approach appears to be an adaptation of reconstruction guidance to flow matching with only minor adjustments to weighting terms (R2, R4). One reviewer (R2) notes that the proof of Theorem 1 is already present in earlier work (cites [2],[3],[4]) and suggests moving it to the appendix; another reviewer (R3) considers the approach novel.
- **Missing related work and comparisons.** The paper omits acknowledgment of highly related work [4] which specifically addresses efficiency in inverse problems using flow matching and diffusion (R2). Comparisons should include additional recent methods such as GDP (R4) and, if possible, results with more recent base models (Flux 1.0, SD3, SDXL, DiTs) (R4). R3 also requests comparisons against additional methods within the same domain.
- **Insufficient motivation and explanation.** The paper does not clearly explain what limitations of existing methods are addressed by measurement interpolants or why conditioning on interpolants is advantageous (R1). A dedicated subsection explaining these issues, with experimental evidence, would improve clarity. Also, the practical implications of each theorem should be briefly stated after each theorem or in a discussion subsection (R1).
- **Efficiency claims not fully supported.** The algorithm adds an inner loop of $K$ gradient steps per ODE step (Algorithm 1, lines 7‑8); for $K>1$ this increases inference time. The paper does not analyze computational cost for different $K$ or transformation types, nor does it benchmark Number of Function Evaluations (NFE) against state‑of‑the‑art efficient methods (e.g., NFE=5) (R2). The claim that prior works struggle with efficiency needs better justification (R2).
- **Inadequate evaluation metrics.** Only PSNR and SSIM are reported. For generative models, FID and KID are standard and more aligned with human perception; these should be included (R2).
- **Limited scope: non‑linear, latent, and blind scenarios.** FIG is not directly applicable to non‑linear inverse problems or generative models with latent encodings (R3, R4). No discussion is given on how to extend the method to such cases (R3). All degradation settings are fixed and known; real‑world blind scenarios are not evaluated (R4). R3 also asks about the risk of overfitting and how to mitigate it.
- **Lack of ablation on measurement interpolants.** The effectiveness of the measurement interpolation itself is not verified through ablation studies (R4). The ablation on the variance rescaling weight $w$ is minimal; visualizations showing the effect of different $w$ on the trade‑off between measurement fidelity and the prior, as well as interaction with noise level, are missing (R3, R4).
