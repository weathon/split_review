## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies causal reasoning in LLMs through counterfactual question answering. It proposes new metrics—necessity inconsistency rate (N-IR), sufficiency inconsistency rate (S-IR), absent necessity inconsistency rate (AN-IR), absent sufficiency inconsistency rate (AS-IR), and average inconsistency rate (Avg-IR)—to assess factual-counterfactual consistency. It also introduces three fine-tuning methods (supervised, preference-based DPO, and causal consistency feedback) and evaluates on several generalization modes (common-effect, common-cause, inductive, deductive) using Phi-3 mini (3.8B parameters). The fine-tuning approaches show improvement on the proposed metrics, particularly for inductive reasoning. One reviewer (score 8) finds the paper highly original, well-structured, and significant for causal reasoning research. Other reviewers (scores 6, 5, 6) raise concerns about experimental design, model choice, clarity, and motivation.

### Strengths
- Proposed metrics (N-IR, S-IR, AN-IR, AS-IR, Avg-IR) provide a fine-grained evaluation of causal consistency, addressing the limitation of accuracy-only evaluation of LLM reasoning.
- Classification of generalization into four types (common-effect, common-cause, inductive, deductive) offers a structured framework for analyzing reasoning transfer.
- Fine-tuning approaches (supervised, preference-based, causal consistency feedback) are relevant, original, and well integrated; inclusion of both factual and counterfactual information during training aligns with human causal inference processes.
- Paper is well-organized, comprehensive, and significant for researchers in causal reasoning (with or without LLMs).
- Evaluations show that the fine-tuning methods improve performance on the proposed metrics compared to baseline models.

### Weaknesses
- **Model choice**: Only Phi-3 mini (3.8B parameters) is used, which has limited reasoning capabilities; conclusions may not transfer to stronger models. Reviewer 1 requests architecture details and performance on common reasoning benchmarks to contextualize limitations.
- **Experimental design and fairness**: The performance gain from training on both factual and counterfactual examples could be due to increased number of training examples rather than the method itself. A properly controlled experiment with equal numbers of factual and counterfactual examples (randomly sampled) is missing. A model tuned on both may simply overfit causal patterns and perform better on combined metrics.
- **Limited scope and generalizability**: Experiments are restricted to one model and one synthetically generated dataset; results are difficult to generalize across models and datasets. Section 6.3 (real-world data) lacks training/test splits, sample sizes, and clarifications on which generalization modes were tested per problem; a summary table of datasets, sizes, sources, and tested modes is requested.
- **Lack of error analysis**: No breakdown of errors by task type, complexity, or reasoning challenge (e.g., false sufficiency vs. false necessity) is provided. Deeper analysis of where fine-tuning fails and how preference tuning addresses specific error types is needed (reviewer 4 suggests error examples/visualizations).
- **Comparison with Chain-of-Thought (CoT)**: The paper claims (in a footnote) that CoT effectiveness may be due to imitation, but does not compare its methods to CoT prompting. Such a comparison is needed to test that claim.
- **Motivation and novelty**: The studied examples are very direct—LLMs can translate them to a formal representation and call a dedicated solver, achieving near 100% accuracy. The advantage of the proposed approach over using a formal representation and solver is unclear. Related work (e.g., arXiv:2307.02477, LREC 2022, arXiv:2309.12284) already explores counterfactual reasoning for training; the novelty lies in the metrics, but the weak experiments make the contribution unclear.
- **Clarity and presentation**: Formal definitions and method descriptions are difficult to follow (reviewer 1). Placement of related work is odd (reviewer 3 suggests moving to end). There is a typo in line 144 (“interesting” → “interested”).
- **Generalization analysis**: The analysis and discussion of generalization results could be more in-depth; in particular, the difference between inductive and deductive reasoning performance is not explained. Reviewer 2 points to confusion over what distinguishes in-domain evaluation from the type of generalization studied (e.g., multiplication learning analogy). Clarification is needed.
- **Incomplete conclusion**: The limitations section does not discuss how the method could adapt to non-binary representations of causes and effects; reviewer 3 requests hints on such adaptation.
