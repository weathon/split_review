## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a framework that decomposes pointwise risk into Bayes risk (aleatoric uncertainty) and excess risk (epistemic uncertainty). By applying Bayesian methods and proper scoring rules, it derives various existing uncertainty measures (e.g., Mutual Information, Expected Pairwise KL Divergence) as special cases. Experiments on image classification datasets evaluate these measures on out-of-distribution and misclassification detection using AUROC.

### Strengths
- The paper is well written and clear.
- It presents a solid theoretical contribution by unifying different predictive uncertainty measures under a Bayesian risk decomposition framework, connecting known UQ methods (e.g., Mutual Information, Expected Pairwise KL Divergence) with a common theoretical foundation.
- It covers a lot of ground in a limited span, discussing risk, proper scoring rules, different ways for risk estimation, and a substantial amount of experimental results, providing a nice overview of different uncertainty measures and how they relate.
- Combining pieces that have appeared in some form before (pointwise risk, proper scoring rules, Bayesian risk estimation) is a novel idea; the paper shows how various uncertainty measures naturally arise and correspond to different ways of estimating risk for a proper scoring rule, with a deeper investigation of relationships.
- The paper addresses a cogent problem in the literature: the existence of many aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty measures and how they relate to one another.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and prior overlap (R1, R2 vs. R3, R4):** Two reviewers (R1, R2) raise concerns that parts of the framework are not entirely novel. Schweighofer et al. (2023a) already describe EPKL as a deviation from Mutual Information that does not assume the BMA to be the true model and discuss the relation to reverse Mutual Information, disagreeing with the paper’s problem statement (Line 45) that relationships are unclear. Results similar to Table 1 are already given by Hofman et al. (R2). The paper should explicitly clarify which parts of the generalization are novel and which are established. R1 also notes that experimental conclusions (e.g., excess risk suitable for soft OOD but not hard OOD) are not novel and replicate findings in previous works [1,2]. Conversely, R3 and R4 find the combination novel and the paper addresses a cogent problem.
- **Limited experimental scope and weak motivation (R1, R2, R3):** Experiments are limited to image classification; regression tasks are not included. No simulations under controlled conditions (known true η) to test estimators under various distribution shapes, misspecification, or approximate posterior errors (R3). The experimental evaluation is narrowly focused on OOD and misclassification detection, but the foundational assumption (misclassification relates to aleatoric uncertainty, OOD to epistemic) is largely untestable (R3). Differences between Total, Bayes, and Excess risks are negligible across datasets, questioning meaningfulness (R3). Only experiments 6.2 and 6.3 come with a hypothesis, but those are about UQ in general, not about the proposed generalization, limiting the paper’s impact (R2).
- **Lack of baseline comparisons (R1, R3):** The paper does not compare its measures to strong baselines or modern UQ methods (e.g., normalizing flows, VAEs). R1 notes the authors explicitly state they are not aiming for state-of-the-art, but without comparison to existing approaches (e.g., [3,4] from R1), it is hard to assess practical utility and relative performance (R1, R3).
- **Model misspecification and definitional issues (R1, R2, R3, R4):** The framework assumes the true data distribution can be well approximated; impact of model misspecification on derived uncertainty measures is not addressed (R1, R3). The Bayesian route has a fundamental drawback: epistemic uncertainty at the predictive level is washed away by taking the expectation over the posterior; this needs discussion (R4, citing Caprio et al., Hullermeier and Waegeman). The effect of prior misspecification (e.g., overly informative prior) on underestimation of epistemic uncertainty is not discussed (R3). Definitions of aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty are vague and ignore model misspecification and input uncertainty (R2); a more consistent definition is needed (R2, citing a source).
- **Limited practical utility (R3, R4):** Computing the proposed uncertainty measures requires access to the posterior p(θ|X,Y), limiting applicability to Bayesian models (R3). The paper should discuss implications and potential ways to address this limitation (R3). R4’s Bayesian averaging critique reinforces this limitation.
- **Lack of training and implementation details (R1, R3):** Important training details are missing from Appendix H: what loss function was used to train the models? Is it the same as the loss used for uncertainty quantification? How were ensembles trained? (independent networks or shared components? any regularization for diversity?) (R1). R3 also asks: How are the UQ measures computed given the outputs of a deep ensemble? If only posterior samples are available, does the framework resort to regular Monte Carlo? (R3)
- **Presentation and clarity issues (R2, R3, R4):** Notation R_Exc^{(1,3)} is hard to track; consider renaming “Excess risk” to epistemic risk and “Bayes risk” to aleatoric risk, or substituting indices with abbreviations (R2). Line 456 overstates: “all instances of excess risk should perform worse [than Bayes/Total risk on misclassification detection]” is not true in general; add “typically” or “usually” (R2). Lines 104-107 are repetitive with Lines 36-38 (R2). The section on energy-based models seems irrelevant; either clarify its relevance or remove (R2). Table 5 mentioned on Line 475 is in the appendix but the text presents it as in the main body (R2). Section 6.4 needs clear pointers to where evidence for claims can be found (table, row, column), especially Lines 477-482 (R2). Tables should clarify whether ± indicates standard deviation, variance, or standard error, and may use bold to highlight conclusions (R2). Typo on Line 198: should be η_{θ|D_tr} instead of η_θ|D_tr (R4). The paper leaves many choices (4 proper scoring rules × 9 estimates for Excess risk, 12 for Bayes risk) without guidance on when to use which; are there general or domain-specific recommendations? Can bounds be derived? (R3). The meaning of “5 groups of ensembles” in Section 6 is confusing; do all have the same architecture? (R3).
