## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates the oversmoothing phenomenon in Transformers through the lens of low/high-pass filters. It reparameterizes the product of output and value projection matrices to avoid oversmoothing. The authors analyze conditions under which Transformers are not low-pass filters by examining the eigenspectrum of attention and weight matrices. They show that oversmoothing depends on which eigenvalue dominates and that residual connections and the matrix \(H\) can counteract it. A sufficient condition for counteracting oversmoothing is proposed via constraining the eigenspectrum of \(H\) to \([-1, 0)\). Empirical validations on Vision Transformers (ViT) demonstrate that the new parameterization improves robustness and data efficiency, and that existing pre-trained networks do not necessarily oversmooth.

### Strengths
- The paper provides a good analysis of when and how to avoid oversmoothing, which is an important problem since Transformers often consist of many layers (R1, R2).
- The investigation is comprehensive, combining both theoretical and empirical analyses (R2, R3).
- A novel reparameterization technique is introduced to mitigate oversmoothing (R2, R3, R4).
- The findings challenge the prevailing view that Transformers are always low-pass filters, showing that oversmoothing is not inevitable and depends on the eigenspectrum (R4).
- New insights are given on the eigenspectrum of both attention and weight matrices (R4).
- Empirical evaluations validate effectiveness, demonstrating the ability to deepen Transformers and enhance robustness, including performance under severe corruption and low-data regimes (R2, R4).
- The paper is well-motivated (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited evaluation scope**: Experiments are conducted only on computer vision tasks (ViT for image classification) (R2, R4). The method is not tested on NLP, multimodal, or sequence-based tasks, raising concerns about general applicability (R2). Furthermore, oversmoothing is less problematic in image classification because labels are sparse and some oversmoothing in deeper layers is expected; a task requiring a prediction per token would be a stronger test (R4). A minority reviewer (R1) notes that the explanation for why the reparameterized ViT improves robustness and data efficiency is insufficient—these benefits are more nuanced than simply selecting higher-frequency features.
- **Insufficient comparison with prior work**: The theoretical contribution is not clearly differentiated from Wang et al. (R3, R4). Specific comparisons with other mitigation techniques such as AttnScale and FeatScale (Wang et al.), residual scaling (Noci et al.), and alternative initializations (e.g., Trockman & Kolter) are missing (R3, R4). The paper should discuss how its preventive measures differ from constraining \(\|W_V\|_2\) or using AttnScale/FeatScale (R3).
- **Theoretical assumptions limit generality**: The analysis assumes a 1-head attention layer, identical weights across layers, no LayerNorm, and fixed attention matrices (R4). Pre-LN architectures, which counteract oversmoothing, are not accounted for; it is unclear how findings generalize to architectures with different weights per layer or with Pre-LN layers (R4). The link between eigenvalue distributions (Table 1) and actual feature behavior is not demonstrated, especially given the fixed-attention assumption that is violated in practice (R1). While one reviewer (R3) found the paper easy to follow, others (R1, R4) found the contributions hard to digest due to unclear presentation; specifically, Theorem 1 is hard to read because of redundant cases (R1), and several definitions are missing (dominating eigenvalues (R1), \(Q\) in Theorem 2 (R3), asymmetry metric (R3), metrics for Tables 2 and 3 (R3)).
- **Symmetry assumption of \(H\)**: The proposed method parameterizes \(H\) (the product of output and value projections) as symmetric, based on the claim that \(H\) becomes more symmetric during training. However, evidence from Figure 2 is not conclusive: the asymmetry metric drops to \(\sim 0.7\) from an initial \(0.95\), but whether \(0.7\) is small enough to justify forcing symmetry is questionable (R1, R3). The presence of outliers in off-diagonal entries and the relationship to the condition number are not discussed (R3). If the optimal \(H\) is asymmetric, forcing symmetry could restrict expressive power, potentially leading to sub-optimal performance (R1 – minority more critical).
- **Missing analyses and experiments**: The paper does not report computational costs (R2). It lacks an analysis of how high-frequency / low-frequency components evolve during training (R3). The distribution of dominating eigenvalues is shown for CIFAR10 but not for ImageNet (R1). No runtime comparison with other methods is given (R1). The motivation for the low-data and corruption experiments is less clear; it would be more relevant to show if the new parameterization enables training in severe oversmoothing (e.g., very deep networks) or to analyze what happens during early training (R4). The stability of the parameterization, especially in early training, is not discussed (R4).
- **Unresolved questions about practical application**: Can the method handle an exceptionally high number of layers? (R2). How does it perform on NLP tasks? (R2). How do findings change with different weights per layer and with Pre-LN layers? (R4). Does preventing low-pass filtering guarantee that meaningful signal is preserved and that feature learning can take place? (R4). These questions remain unanswered.
- **Clarifications needed**: Several technical details are missing: how \(\Theta\) is updated in \(QR(\Theta) = [V_H, R]\) (whether QR decomposition is performed after every gradient step or \(\Theta R^{-1} = V_H\) is used with backpropagation through \(\Theta\)) (R3). Why \(V_H^{-1}\) appears after the clipping operation (R3). The correct sign in \(\text{diag}(\Lambda_H)\): it should be \(+(\psi^2)\), not \(-(\psi^2)\) (R1, R3, R4). The asymmetry definition is missing norm bars (“||”) (R3).
