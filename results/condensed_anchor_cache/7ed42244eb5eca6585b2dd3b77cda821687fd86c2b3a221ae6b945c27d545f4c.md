## Merged Review

### Summary

SkipDecode is a token-level early exit method for autoregressive LLMs that uses a fixed schedule to skip lower layers, designed for batching and KV caching. The reviewers generally find the idea simple and practical, but note several limitations: the method is only evaluated on OPT models (not other architectures like encoder-decoder or LLaMA), comparisons are limited to CALM (only two datasets with single metrics), missing real-world speedup details (hardware, batch size, metric), and the fixed schedule may be less adaptive than context-based alternatives. There is disagreement among reviewers: two (scores 5) are more critical, citing insufficient experimental rigor and missing related work; two (scores 6) are more positive, highlighting promising speedups and clear presentation.

### Strengths

- The method is conceptually simple and directly applicable without requiring extensive low-level implementation (R1, R3, R4).
- It addresses a practical limitation of prior early-exit methods (incompatibility with batching and KV caching) and is designed for real-world serving (R2, R1).
- Empirical results show 2x to 5x speedups with negligible performance degradation on the evaluated tasks, using OPT-1.3B and 6.7B (R1, R4).
- The paper is well-written with informative illustrations, and its limitations are clearly stated (R1, R4).
- The method is novel and compatible with existing inference optimization techniques (R4).

### Weaknesses

- Limited model architecture evaluation: experiments only on OPT (1.3B and 6.7B). It is unclear whether findings transfer to other architectures like LLaMA (with multi-query attention) or encoder-decoder models (R1, R4). Some reviewers considered this a significant gap.
- Only the fine-tuning setting is considered; the method’s applicability to zero-shot in-context learning (a key LLM use case) is not explored (R2).
- Comparisons are limited: only CALM as baseline, and only on two datasets with a single metric each (R4). No real-world speedup comparison with CALM-DEC (R1). More comprehensive evaluation (e.g., machine translation from CALM) is missing (R1).
- Missing experimental details for real speedups: hardware, batch size, whether speedups include prompt computation, and the metric used (latency/throughput) are not reported (R1, R4).
- Choice of datasets is narrow (summarization and E2E); the strong performance on E2E is not explained (R2). Additional tasks with varied input-output relations would strengthen the evaluation (R1).
- The fixed exit schedule (based on sequence position) is criticized as potentially less adaptive than a context-dependent approach. The claim that later tokens are easier (embedded saturation) may not justify forcing all tokens to skip the same layers (R3, and R1 questioned transferability to larger models).
- The paper does not demonstrate that KV cache generation is a massive bottleneck with an analysis (R3). This claim is central to the motivation.
- The viability of fine-tuning SkipDecode for each dataset is questioned, especially as model sizes grow; creating representative real-world datasets is a challenge (R3).
- Table 4 is unclear: no labels or description of what the numbers mean, and how to achieve the stated speedup (R3).
- Missing information: how are min_exit_layer and max_exit_layer chosen? (R4). What is the degradation from using batch-wise exit vs per-example? (R4). How is the gain of skipping compared to early-termination? (R4). How does the sequence-length hyperparameter (median) affect quality and speedup, and how would this work for variable-length generation like chatbots? (R4).
- Typos: “saturatedSchuster”, “Wile our method”, “SkipDecodemodels”, inconsistent capitalization (R1, R4). Minor: citation formatting makes the PDF harder to read (R2).
- Related work on LLM compression/inference (AWQ, SmoothQuant, GPTQ, FlexGen, Deja Vu, H2O, Scissorhands) is not discussed (R2).
