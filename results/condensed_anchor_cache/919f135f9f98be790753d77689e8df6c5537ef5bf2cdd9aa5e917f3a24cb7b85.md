## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a fairness metric called Explanation Disparity (ED), which measures whether a protected attribute Z can be inferred from the distribution of explanation values (e.g., Shapley values) of a model f. The authors argue that Demographic Parity (DP) does not capture whether a model actually “looks at” the protected attribute, and introduce a two-sample test based on AUC to detect ED. The paper includes theoretical analysis and experiments on synthetic and natural data. The four reviewers gave scores 3, 6, 6, 3, with two reviewers (R2, R3) strongly positive and two (R1, R4) strongly negative, reflecting sharp disagreement on novelty, clarity, and statistical rigor.

### Strengths

- (R1) Raises a compelling point: even when DP = 0, the model may implement different decision rules for different groups (Example 4.1), motivating a different approach.  
- (R1, R2, R3) The idea of using explanation distributions (based on Shapley values) to test independence of protected attributes is novel and elegant. Reviewer 3 calls it “a standard fairness metric.” Reviewer 2 considers the proposal of “explanation distribution” and Definitions 3.1/3.2 novel and intuitive. Reviewer 4 agrees that measuring fairness via explanations makes sense and captures causal properties beyond simple metrics.  
- (R2) The paper is well-motivated with real-world political connections.  
- (R1, R2) Theoretical analysis is rigorous, using simple statistical techniques (two-sample tests) cleverly.  
- (R3) ED addresses limitations of both equal opportunity (prevents use of proxies for protected attributes) and demographic parity (prevents using protected attributes to achieve same outcomes).  
- (R2) Shapley value is a credible and well-known concept for this task.

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of novelty disputed (R4)**: Using Shapley values to detect differential treatment has been done before (reference [1] and citations therein). Reviewer 4 considers this a major drawback, in direct contrast to Reviewer 3 who calls the idea “elegant and natural.” The minority reviewer (R4) strongly contests novelty.  
- **Motivation and justification of explanation choice insufficient (R1)**: It is not well argued why the explanation S(f(X)) should be independent of Z rather than any other function of the model. Why not measure variance of f(X) with respect to Z (as in a referenced NeurIPS 2023 paper on variance decomposition)? Counterfactual fairness notions might also address Example 4.1 more directly; the paper does not compare to them. Reviewer 1 would raise score if these comparisons are made.  
- **Computational cost of Shapley values not addressed (R2)**: The method relies on expensive Shapley values; the paper notes this for prior work but does not argue that the cost is absent in this framework.  
- **Reliance solely on Shapley values without comparison to other explanations (R4)**: The paper uses only Shapley values and does not assess whether they are good representatives of model behavior. To draw serious conclusions about bias, comparisons with other explanation methods should be performed. Conclusions about model behavior from explanation-based metrics must be drawn with caution, which the paper fails to do.  
- **No proper statistical test (R4)**: Contrary to claims (lines 256-257), no valid statistical test is provided. The null hypothesis is independence equivalent to AUC = 1/2, but there is no study of the test’s level, power, or theoretical supporting results. (Contrast: Reviewer 1 praises the use of two-sample tests, but does not evaluate the statistical rigor.)  
- **Presentation and clarity (R3, R1, R4)**:  
  - Definition of ED appears too late (page 5); introduction, philosophical foundation, and prior work should be shortened/relocated (R3).  
  - Section 4 should be split into measurement and theoretical properties; examples 4.1 and 4.2 are unclear on their takeaway (R3).  
  - Writing is sloppy: mixes expectation and probability distribution (lines 322, 325, 1062, 1092); the expression P(Z=1|W) requires careful handling when mixing continuous/discrete attributes (R4).  
  - Figure 2 and Figure 3 are not adequately explained (R3).  
- **Mischaracterization of counterfactual fairness (R4)**: The paper claims demographic parity is equivalent to counterfactual fairness, but counterfactual fairness is stronger (reference [3]).  
- **Missing experiment pinpointing proxy variables (R3)**: The most compelling use-case – identifying exactly which variables correlated with protected attributes are used by a model that satisfies DP or equal opportunity – is not demonstrated. Reviewer 3 would like this experiment.  
- **Questions indicating lack of clarity (R1)**:  
  - Lines 56-57: what does “DP may indicate fairness” mean if DP is a measure? What is meant by “when groups are being treated differently”? The sentence is confusing.  
  - Example 4.2: what does it imply? Even if we could measure dependence between X and Z, isn’t it more important that the model does not discriminate based on Z directly?
