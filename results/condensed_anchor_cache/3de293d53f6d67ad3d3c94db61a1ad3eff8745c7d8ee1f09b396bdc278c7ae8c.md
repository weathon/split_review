## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper presents a structured binarization method for LLMs by combining N:M sparsity, residual approximation, block-wise error compensation, and a layer-wise dynamic N:M allocation. A new Standardized Importance (SI) metric is introduced to identify significant weights without second-order computations. Non-salient weights are partitioned into three groups (sparse, intermediate, dense) for separate binarization. A specialized CUDA kernel is designed to leverage NVIDIA’s sparse tensor cores. Experiments on LLaMA-1/2/3, OPT, and Mistral show that STBLLM outperforms BiLLM under sub‑1‑bit budgets and achieves up to 17.85× speedup over ABQ‑LLM’s 2‑bit implementation. Reviewer 3 is notably more positive, praising the well‑organized presentation and rigorous validation, while the other reviewers generally find the novelty incremental and the evaluation lacking clarity.

### Strengths

- The analysis on flipping non‑salient binarized weights is intriguing (R1).
- The proposed method achieves the lowest perplexity among all compared methods in the sub‑1‑bit regime (R1).
- A dedicated CUDA kernel, leveraging Ampere GPU sparse tensor cores, obtains a 17.85× speedup over ABQ‑LLM’s 2‑bit implementation (R1, R2, R4). R4 additionally notes that actual runtime performance was measured using this custom kernel, providing a practical speedup assessment.
- 1‑bit weight quantization is important for accelerating LLM inference (R2).
- The paper is well‑organized and easy to follow, with a clearly stated problem (R3).
- The SI metric avoids expensive second‑order gradient computations and mitigates the impact of extreme values (R3).
- The separate binarization for non‑salient weights retains crucial information in that segment, enhancing model performance (R3). R4 also notes that grouping weights and binarizing within each group reduces quantization error.
- The approach is logical and rigorous, discussing the method from various perspectives and fully validating its effectiveness through comprehensive experiments (R3).
- The integration of sparsity with quantization achieves a significantly higher compression ratio, and sparsity opens up additional acceleration opportunities (R4).

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty concerns (R1, R2, R4)**: The proposed method is a combination of existing techniques (N:M sparsity, residual approximation, block‑wise error compensation, trisection search). R2 specifically notes that the SI metric is very similar to Wanda (differing only by additional data normalization) and that the binary quantization is quite similar to BiLLM (using Hessian to divide weights and residual approximation; the only difference is processing non‑salient weights into three parts instead of two). R4 describes the performance improvements over BiLLM as incremental. R1 suggests highlighting the main novelty and providing ablation studies on (a) the importance of residual approximation, (b) the impact of the trisection search and why two groups are used, and (c) which techniques contribute most to efficiency versus accuracy.
- **Mismatch between motivation and methodology (R2)**: The paper’s motivation (Section 3.1, Fig. 1) is that some binarized weights can be flipped without significant degradation, suggesting pruning on the binarized model. Yet the method adopts a “prune‑then‑quantize” approach. The paper does not justify the ordering or discuss how changing the order of pruning and binarization would affect results.
- **Trisection partition motivation unclear (R2)**: The paper aims to balance bits and performance (Section 3.4), but evaluations show that the improved compression ratio and performance come from N:M pruning, not the non‑salient weight processing. Why partition into three parts? Why not four or five? What do “dense”, “intermediate”, and “sparse” mean? R1 further asks how trisection search specifically improves over binary search and what the exact innovation is.
- **Practicality of N:M sparsity (R1)**: Benchmark results use various N:M configurations, but NVIDIA GPUs mainly support 2:4. The authors should discuss how practical the method is on NVIDIA hardware.
- **Flipping ratio analysis (R1)**: The analysis on flipping non‑salient weights is interesting; the authors are asked what would happen if the flipping ratio were increased from 0.15 to 0.5.
- **Unclear source of accuracy improvements (R2, R4)**: The experimental settings in the ablation study are confusing. Table 5 examines the SI method in N:M pruning, but the results seem to represent 4:8 pruning plus binarization – what binarization method is used in baselines? Table 8 compares STBLLM with BiLLM to illustrate trisection partitioning, yet the pruning methods differ (SI vs. Wanda). A detailed, step‑by‑step breakdown of each technique’s effectiveness is needed. R4 notes that Table 5 appears redundant with a subset of Table 7’s Wikitext2 results, and the manuscript lacks discussion of Table 7’s results, particularly why SI performs worse than SparseGPT on PTB and C4 datasets and what factors cause performance variations across datasets.
- **17× performance improvement source (R2)**: Where does the 17× speedup come from when reducing 2‑bit weights to 1 bit?
- **Clarity on zero‑shot tasks and Figure 3 (R3)**: The paper mentions seven zero‑shot tasks; it would be helpful to include a brief description of each. In Figure 3(b), after structured pruning, why are zeros assigned to the empty parts? Also, structured pruning usually does not achieve weight‑wise pruning; what does “structured” mean in this context?
- **Average bit calculation and overhead (R4)**: The manuscript needs a more comprehensive explanation of the average bit count calculation: a step‑by‑step breakdown, a concrete example, and clarification of whether indexing overhead from sparsity is factored in.
- **Algorithm 1 ambiguity (R4)**: The “Semi‑Structured” function is not explained – is it performing sparsification based on SI? Also, the abbreviation “OBC” needs a full reference and explanation.
- **Computational requirements (R4)**: An estimate of computational time for the 65B model (e.g., via theoretical scaling analysis) would be useful.
- **Figure 3 weight distribution overlap (R4)**: In Figure 3, there is an overlap between Salient Weight and Non‑salient Weight distributions. The reasons for this overlap and its effect on performance should be explained.
