## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces ArrAttack, an automatic framework for generating robust jailbreak prompts targeting defended LLMs. It combines a rewriting-based prompt generation technique with a separately trained robustness judgment model. Reviewers acknowledge the approach’s timeliness and the comprehensive evaluation across multiple models and defenses, but raise concerns about limited novelty, missing comparisons with prior works (PAIR, TAP, AutoDAN), incomplete testing on model-level defenses, reliance on a single evaluation metric (GPTFuzz), insufficient training and design justifications, and poor writing structure. A minority reviewer (R2, score 5) is notably more critical of novelty and evaluation, while the other two (R1, R3, both score 8) are positive but note specific weaknesses.

### Strengths
- Addresses a practical gap: existing jailbreak attacks fail against defended LLMs; ArrAttack’s combination of a robustness judgment model and rewriting-based generation is a unique response (R1, R2 acknowledged; R2 notes similar concepts in PAIR/TAP but still positive).
- Paper is well-structured, with clear problem formulation, method description, and evaluation criteria (R1, R2).
- Extensive experiments over multiple LLMs (white‑box and black‑box, including GPT‑4 and Claude‑3), diverse defense mechanisms, and metrics (ASR, semantic similarity, perplexity) (R1, R3).
- Table 1 shows high attack success rate and good perplexity scores (R3).
- Generated jailbreak prompts exhibit strong transferability across models and defenses (R1, R3).
- Soundness of the approach is good (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns and insufficient differentiation**: Core rewriting‑based attack methodology is similar to PAIR and TAP; the paper does not clearly distinguish its specific rewriting techniques or mechanisms for achieving success, making the contribution appear incremental (R2).  
- **Missing comparisons with relevant baselines**: No direct comparison with PAIR, TAP, or zero‑shot methods like AutoDAN, making it impossible to assess whether improvements stem from ArrAttack’s design or from a different evaluation setup (R2, R3). Comparison on established benchmarks (e.g., harmbench) is also lacking (R3).  
- **Limited evaluation of defense types**: Only input‑level system defenses (e.g., SmoothLLM, DPP, RPO, PAR) are tested; model‑level defenses such as safety training, unlearning, adversarial fine‑tuning, or stronger defenses (SafeDecoding, PAT, RAIN) are not considered, which limits generalizability (R1, R2, R3).  
- **Evaluation metric concerns**: Reliance on the GPTFuzz model for ASR evaluation is questionable due to potential bias and limited generalizability in complex scenarios; more robust and diverse metrics are needed (R2). Additional details on how GPTFuzz works (since ASR is sensitive to settings) are requested (R3).  
- **Writing and presentation issues**: Method and experimental sections are difficult to follow; Section 3 should focus on motivation and pipeline rather than hyperparameter details, and Section 4 should divide results into clearer subsections (R3).  
- **Training data and computational cost**: ArrAttack requires large training data (combining three datasets) whereas existing attacks (AutoDAN) are zero‑shot; ablations on limited data are missing (R3). Training a generation model incurs extra computational cost compared to flexible GPT‑4‑based attacks like PAIR/TAP (R3).  
- **Insufficient training details and justification**: Ablation studies (Tables 4, 5) show the robustness judgment model and prompt generation model are critical, but the training methods, parameter choices, and rationale are only briefly mentioned (R1). The choice of SmoothLLM as the single defense for training the judgment model, and how that ensures transferability across defenses, is not justified (R1’s question).  
- **Lack of detailed case studies**: More examples of generated prompts in defended scenarios, along with analysis of the robustness judgment model’s outputs, are needed (R1).  
- **Missing analysis of adaptability**: Questions remain about whether the robustness judgment model can be adapted to non‑English languages or real‑time defenses that evolve via user feedback or system updates (R2’s questions).
