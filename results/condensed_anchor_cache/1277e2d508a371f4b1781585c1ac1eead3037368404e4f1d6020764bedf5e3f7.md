## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates the impact of problem-solving data, synthetic data generation methods, and training stages (CPT vs. SFT) on mathematical reasoning in LLMs. The authors find that problem-solving data is more effective than general mathematical corpora during CPT; among four data synthesis methods, tutorship amplification performs best; and CPT (on problem-solving data) yields stronger reasoning than SFT (on the same data), particularly on complex multi-step problems. These insights are used to train JiuZhang-8B, a model that achieves competitive results with fewer training tokens. Reviews are mixed: several reviewers (1,4,5) rate it highly (8) for thoroughness and practical value, while others (2,3) find it lacking in novelty, theoretical depth, and rigor (scores 1 and 3). The majority agree on the paper’s clarity and empirical breadth, but identify significant weaknesses in analysis depth, reproducibility, and generalization.

### Strengths
- The research questions (effectiveness of problem-solving data, data synthesis methods, CPT vs. SFT) are clearly stated and systematically addressed, with results organized along each question for readability.
- The paper provides an in-depth comparison of four data synthesis methods (response diversification, query expansion, retrospective enhancement, tutorship amplification), with tutorship amplification identified as particularly effective – a practical insight for the community.
- The comparison between CPT and SFT is well-documented, including difficulty-level analysis showing CPT’s advantage on harder problems, and the attempt to explain why (e.g., SFT’s poor learning capacity for hard multi-step data).
- The level of detail on datasets, data mixture ratios, hyperparameters, and training procedures is very good, aiding reproducibility (though proprietary data limits full reproducibility).
- The final model JiuZhang-8B achieves competitive performance on standard math benchmarks against larger models, validating the proposed techniques and demonstrating efficient training (fewer tokens).
- The paper explores the impact of different data distributions (middle vs. high school, OOD vs. IND) and difficulty levels, providing useful insights into data composition.
- The experiments are extensive, covering many important cases, and the datasets used are publicly available (e.g., NuminaMath, Lila).
- The synthetic data generation techniques (especially tutorship amplification) can improve robustness to question modifications, a valuable contribution.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of deeper mechanistic understanding**: Several reviewers note that the paper offers strong empirical results but limited theoretical backing or analysis of *why* certain methods work (e.g., why tutorship amplification is better, why CPT outperforms SFT). Reviewer 2 finds the methodology insufficiently rigorous and the analysis superficial; reviewer 4 agrees that advancing understanding beyond observations is needed.
- **Limited novelty and impact**: Reviewer 3 states the paper does not represent a major conceptual advance and feels like “engineering mixtures of LLM research.” The contribution is seen as solid but not intellectually exciting. Reviewer 1’s critique that Result 8 (simpler data learned first) is trivial reinforces this.
- **Redundancy between Results**: Reviewer 1 points out that Result 4 (SFT less effective than CPT for learning math skills) and Result 6 (SFT’s in-domain learning weaker than CPT’s) appear to convey the same conclusion. The authors should clarify the specific nuances (e.g., overall vs. in-domain).
- **Insufficient analysis of data distribution (Section 5.2)**: Multiple reviewers (1,2) find this section confusing. Specific issues include:
  - The overlap between knowledge points in different datasets (e.g., GAOKAO, MATH, ZHONGKAO) is not quantified; a similarity analysis (e.g., cosine similarity on TF-IDF or knowledge point embeddings) is missing.
  - The claim that GAOKAO’s capabilities subsume MATH while ZHONGKAO requires higher general knowledge than MATH is at odds with results in Table 2 (middle school data outperforms high-school data on MATH). This raises questions about how OOD is defined and whether knowledge point labels capture true concept overlap.
  - No explanation is given for why high school-trained models perform worse on ZHONGKAO than middle school-trained models; analysis of the general knowledge types required by ZHONGKAO is needed.
  - Difficulty-level breakdown (reasoning steps) for middle and high school datasets is missing; such granularity would contextualize results, as done in Section 5.3.
- **Reproducibility concerns**: Reviewer 1 notes that most problem-solving data is proprietary (~24M out of ~25M pieces), limiting reproducibility and the ability to build upon the work. The authors should release a subset or provide more details on collection/annotation. Reviewer 2 also highlights vague dataset descriptions and lack of discussion on data cleansing.
- **Weaknesses in CPT vs. SFT analysis**: 
  - Reviewer 2 points out a contradiction: the abstract says CPT improvements are often less significant than SFT, yet Result 4 says SFT has a clear disadvantage compared to CPT. The authors need to reconcile this.
  - Reviewer 5 and 2 ask for more clarity on why SFT underperforms CPT: is it due to data volume limitations, catastrophic forgetting, or other factors? A detailed error analysis is missing.
  - Reviewer 2 questions whether these results could be influenced by specific model architecture or training configurations; theoretical justification is lacking.
- **Missing experiments and analyses**:
  - Ablation of data mixture ratios (Reviewer 6): Figure 1 only shows specific mixtures; extreme cases (e.g., 2:8, 1:9, or 100% problem-solving data) are not tested, leaving open whether the mixture is crucial.
  - Generalization to other domains (Reviewers 5,6,7): The paper focuses solely on math; tests on interdisciplinary tasks (e.g., physics, logic) are absent. Reviewers question whether techniques like tutorship amplification work in non-math reasoning domains.
  - Scaling to larger models (Reviewer 7): Experiments are limited to 8B models; the effectiveness on 13B, 32B, or 70B models is unknown. Scale-up could require more data, providing new insights on data quality vs. quantity.
  - Error analysis (Reviewers 1,2,5): No detailed error analysis on hard problems is provided; analyzing specific error types (e.g., incorrect reasoning steps) is requested.
  - Inference-time techniques (Reviewer 6): Results (e.g., Table 2) might change with best-of-N sampling or reward models; this is not explored.
- **Data synthesis details** (Reviewers 2,5,6,7): 
  - For response diversification, there is no check for incorrect logic leading to the correct answer (Reviewer 6).
  - Retrospective enhancement’s poor performance is not adequately explained (Reviewer 7).
  - Concrete examples of synthetic data, prompts, and human verification are missing (Reviewer 5).
- **Practical examples and real-world applicability** (Reviewers 2,5,7): The paper lacks specific examples of problem-solving data showing how it differs from general corpora. The real-world significance of JiuZhang-8B is not discussed, nor its use in tutoring systems.
- **Minor writing issues**: Reviewer 2 notes bolded “Result X” is hard to read; suggests bulleted contributions. Reviewer 2 also flags a missing space in a citation. Reviewer 1 suggests moving less important details (e.g., batch size) to appendix and adding a main pipeline figure.
- **Contradictory reviewer opinions on soundness**: Reviewer 2 rates soundness 1, citing insufficient rigor, while Reviewer 4 rates soundness 4 and finds experiments sound. This disagreement reflects the need for stronger justification and analysis.
