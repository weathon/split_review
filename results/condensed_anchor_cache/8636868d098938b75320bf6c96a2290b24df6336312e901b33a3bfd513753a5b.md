- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 1, 3, 5, 3, 6

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a multi-level conditional diffusion model that generates protein sequence, backbone, and all-atom representations simultaneously, guided by functional annotations. It also introduces Protein-MMD, a metric based on Maximum Mean Discrepancy applied to protein embeddings, to evaluate conditional consistency. Reviewers are highly divided (scores: 1, 3, 5, 3, 6). While the multi-level approach is seen as conceptually interesting and the problem timely, the majority of reviewers find the work fatally lacking in technical clarity, novelty, proper baselines, and rigorous evaluation. The absence of code and incomplete experimental details make the work unreproducible. Protein-MMD is described by some as a promising idea, but multiple reviewers note that MMD-based metrics for proteins using pLM embeddings are not new and the validation is insufficient. Baseline comparisons are inappropriate (most are non-conditional models) and missing key SOTA methods. Several critical experiments (unconditional generation, structural quality, ablation of conditioning, overfitting checks) are absent. Most reviewers recommend rejection or major revision.

### Strengths
- **Relevance and timeliness** (R1, R2): The task of generating proteins guided by functional descriptions is highly relevant for de novo design, drug discovery, and enzyme engineering.
- **Conceptually appealing multi-level approach** (R1, R3, R4, R5): The hierarchical generation at amino acid, backbone, and atom levels, with information passing between levels, is biologically motivated and brings a new perspective to protein design.
- **Interesting conditional flow mechanism** (R3): The specific mechanism of passing information from atom level to sequence is noted as innovative.
- **Protein-MMD as a potential evaluation tool** (R3, R5): One reviewer (R5) finds it original and impactful; another (R3) believes it could be a good contribution to the evaluation toolbox if properly validated.
- **Ablation studies performed** (R2, R5): Some ablation is conducted to assess components of the architecture.
- **Good presentation quality according to one reviewer** (R2, R5): R2 found the paper easy to read; R5 praised organization, diagrams, and mathematical detail.
- **SE(3)-invariant preprocessing** (R5): Acknowledged as a robust methodological choice.

### Weaknesses
- **Fundamental lack of technical clarity and reproducibility** (R1, R2, R3, R4, all): Critical implementation details are missing: no code provided, training hyperparameters incomplete, dataset preparation and splitting not described, architectural specifics (e.g., what is \(h_i\) – learned or assigned? what atoms are represented at the atom level? what are backbone edges? “first four torsion angles” not standard nomenclature – phi/psi are backbone torsions only). The Protein VAE is mentioned but not explained nor shown in Figure 1 (R2 Q6, R1 Q3). No description of how conditional information (GO annotations) is tokenized or encoded (R4). The mathematical encoding of atomic-level features is absent (R4 Q1, R4). The process for decoding from latent space to full structure (shown in Fig. 1) is missing (R4 Q2). The SE(3)-invariant preprocessing statement “iteratively applied to all amino acids” is unclear (R1 Q1). Proof of SE(3)-invariance within the model is not provided (R2 Q10). Several reviewers (R1, R2, R3, R4) note the paper is unreproducible in its current state.
- **Poor baseline selection and adaptation** (R1, R2, R3, R4): Four of five baselines (ESM2, ProtT5/ProstT5, ProteinMPNN, LatentDiff) are not conditional generative models; no explanation of how they were adapted for the conditional task is given. Models are pretrained on different datasets, making comparisons invalid. Missing critical conditional baselines: ProGen (R1), RFDiffusion (R3), Multiflow (R4), ProteinGenerator, ProtGPT2 (R4 Q5). ESM2 should be included as an ablation since the proposed model itself uses ESM2 embeddings (R3). Typo: “ProstT5” should be “ProtT5” (R3).
- **Insufficient validation of Protein-MMD metric** (R1, R2, R3, R4, partly R5): Multiple reviewers note that applying MMD to protein embeddings is not novel (R1, R2; see references [1],[2]). The experimental setup to validate Protein-MMD is unclear (R1): e.g., how are the two sets of sequences in Table 1 generated? What is the “MMD baseline” – what does “considering only sequence statistics” mean? How are accuracy, MRR, MNR calculated for FID, since FID is not a class-based metric (R3)? Missing fundamental sanity checks: performance on true known sequences, variation with different embeddings (e.g., ProtT5), random class assignment (R3). No justification for using ESM embeddings specifically or comparative analysis across ESM versions or other pLMs (R2 Q1, R3). The metric is not squared like classic MMD (R2 Q3). The statement that “the quality of generated samples improve with \(i \rightarrow \infty\)” is unsupported and the quality metric is not defined (R2 Q4, Q5). The interpretability of Protein-MMD in biological terms is limited (R5), and it is unclear which features drive functional adherence.
- **Inappropriate evaluation metrics overall** (R1, R2, R3, R4): No protein quality metrics (pLDDT, perplexity) are reported (R2, R1). Diversity metrics (TM-score, RMSD, Seq.ID) are computed only within generated samples, not against ground truth per class, so their scale is meaningless – random samples also achieve diversity (R1, R2). MMD and FID values also need a scale (e.g., distances between random subsets of the reference dataset) to be interpretable (R1). It is unclear against which set TM-score/RMSD are calculated (R1). Seq.ID is not defined (R1). The paper compares Protein-MMD to FID, but they are fundamentally different; F1 micro/macro scores would be more appropriate (R3). Missing traditional diffusion metrics: designability, diversity, novelty (R4).
- **Missing critical experiments and ablations** (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5): No unconditional generation results (R1, R4 Q3). No ablation on conditioning mechanisms (R1). No experiment showing that the conditioning function satisfies \(d(x^i, C^k) < \min_j d(x^i, C^j)\) for the EC dataset (R2). No experiment analyzing condition incorporation vs. sequence length (the claim about longer sequences helping is unsupported; R2 Q9). The claim that existing works use max length 128 is inaccurate (e.g., RFDiffusion, ProGen, GPT-like models; R2 Q8). Missing inverse folding ablation: generate structures then decode sequences for comparison (R3 Q5). No analysis of per-level contribution to performance (R5 Q4). No test on longer/complex proteins or multi-domain proteins (R5 Q2). No demonstration of practical benefits of SE(3)-invariance (robustness, structural accuracy) (R5 Q5). No discussion of latent space challenges like clashing atoms after reconstruction (R4). No overfitting analysis despite small datasets (~15k EC, ~32k GO) and large models (3 transformer diffusion blocks, each 12 blocks, plus 3 transformer decoders) (R1).
- **Conditional generation focus vs. claimed full-atom design** (R4, R2 Q1): The paper claims to design full-atom proteins, but experiments only evaluate sequence generation under GO/EC conditions; the model’s capacity to generate structure is not tested. The use of a “molecular encoder” is questioned – why not a protein-specific encoder? (R2 Q2). The structural examples in Figure 6 appear helix-dominant, raising concern about structural diversity (R3 Q6). The GO dataset details (hierarchy level, sample count, split) are missing (R2 Q7).
- **SE(3) invariance vs. equivariance** (R4): The paper focuses on proving SE(3)-invariance via preprocessing, but neglects the more desirable property of equivariance in protein modeling. Aligning all proteins to a common frame is not optimal.
- **Presentation mixed** (all): While R2 and R5 praised clarity, R1, R3, R4 gave presentation scores of 1 or 2 due to missing details, incomplete methodology, and lack of clear experimental setup.
