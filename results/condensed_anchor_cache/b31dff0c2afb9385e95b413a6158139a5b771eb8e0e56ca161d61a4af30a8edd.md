## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes “echo embeddings,” a simple method to improve text embeddings from causal LLMs: the input is repeated twice in context, and embeddings are extracted from the second occurrence. This allows early tokens to attend to later tokens without architectural changes or fine-tuning. On MTEB, echo embeddings outperform classical embeddings by over 9% zero-shot and by around 0.7% when fine-tuned. The method is evaluated on multiple models and scales, and compute-matched experiments show it still outperforms classical embeddings even when adjusting for doubled input length. A synthetic dataset is used to analyze the limitation of causal attention for embeddings.

### Strengths
1. **Simplicity and effectiveness**: The echo embedding method is straightforward, requires no architectural changes to the backbone, and works in both zero-shot and fine-tuned settings. Previous work showed repetition benefits reasoning and recurrent LMs; this paper demonstrates it is also effective for causal LM embeddings (R1, R2, R4).
2. **Impressive zero-shot gains**: On MTEB, echo embeddings achieve a substantial performance boost over classical embeddings (e.g., over 9% zero-shot) without any fine-tuning or backbone modification (R1, R3, R4). Results are consistent across multiple models and scales (R4).
3. **Compelling toy experiment**: The synthetic dataset clearly illustrates the limitation of classical embeddings (mean pooling / last token) and the advantage of echo embeddings, supporting the paper’s claim with controlled conditions (R1, R2, R3).
4. **Competitive with bidirectional-converted models**: Echo embeddings match or approach the performance of models like LLM2Vec that require backbone changes and unsupervised fine-tuning (R2, R3).
5. **Compute-matched results**: Even when the training steps are halved to match the computational cost of the doubled input length, echo embeddings still outperform classical embeddings (R4).
6. **Extensive evaluation**: The paper covers many MTEB tasks (classification, clustering, reranking, retrieval, sentence similarity) and multiple models (R4).
7. **Clear writing**: The paper is well-structured and easy to follow (R1).

### Weaknesses
1. **Baseline alignment concerns**: The PromptEOL baseline setting does not exactly match the original paper. In the original work, PromptEOL achieved an average of 72.10 on seven STS tasks using OPT-6.7B, while this paper reports 67.14 on ten STS tasks. The prompt used also differs from the original; the authors should report performance with the exact same prompt (R2).
2. **Trade-offs of doubled input length**: Echo embeddings double the input length, which increases compute quadratically (O(n²) due to self-attention). The paper only explores halving the training steps when doubling input length, not the scenario where training steps are kept constant (which would substantially raise cost). This leaves an incomplete picture of practical trade-offs (R1). Additionally, the method may fail when important information lies in the latter half of the input (the S2 case in Section 3.1) (R1).
3. **“Halve input” setting is unclear and potentially unfair**: For a prompt like “rewrite S, rewritten S’,” does halving mean reducing both S and S’? If so, the improvement may be an artifact of the problem rather than an advantage of echo embeddings. The authors should also halve the input for the PromptEOL baseline (R2).
4. **Motivation regarding causal attention**: The paper argues causal attention is a limitation, yet LLM2Vec uses causal attention and still works well for embeddings. This weakens the claimed motivation (R3).
5. **Minor fine-tuning advantage vs. cost**: After fine-tuning, the performance gap between echo embeddings and other models is minor, yet echo embeddings require the input to be repeated, increasing computational costs. This restricts the method mainly to zero-shot settings (R3).
6. **Potential dataset bias**: The specific tasks and datasets in the evaluation may favor echo embeddings; further validation on additional types of data and tasks (e.g., generation tasks) would strengthen general applicability (R4). Are there benefits for generation tasks when echoing important information in the prompt? (R4)
7. **Missing ablations**:  
   - Impact of repeating the input more than twice (tripling, etc.) (R1, R3).  
   - Sensitivity of results to prompt structure variations (R4).  
   - Performance on a wider range of autoregressive models, especially non-GPT architectures (R4).
8. **Minor presentation issues**: Typos at line 122 (“encode can” → “can encode”) and line 283 (“embedding 4” → “embedding in Table 4”). An illustrative example should be in the main article, not only in the appendix (R3).
