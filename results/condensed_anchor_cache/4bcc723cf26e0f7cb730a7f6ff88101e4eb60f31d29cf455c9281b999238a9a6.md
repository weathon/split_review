## Merged Review

### Summary
InnateCoder uses foundation models to generate candidate programs (options) in a domain-specific language, then applies stochastic hill-climbing to search for a policy combining these options. The paper evaluates in MicroRTS and Karel, claiming improved sample efficiency over baselines. Reviewers gave scores of 5, 6, 6, 6; one reviewer is notably more critical, while the others see promise but raise concerns.

### Strengths
- Novel method that leverages foundation models to provide a corpus of knowledge rather than solving the problem directly, leaving lower-level operations to options (R1, R4).
- Evaluated in two domains used in prior work, showing nontrivial improvement over baselines in many tasks and matching performance in others (R1).
- Authors made an effort to eliminate data leakage as a factor during evaluation (R1).
- Algorithm is explained well; DSL for each environment and exact prompts are provided (R2, R4).
- Results appear very good and claim SOTA, compared to competition winners in MicroRTS (R3).
- Related discussion is extensive and informative for the community (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Presentation and clarity issues**: The paper is confusing for readers not familiar with prior work, e.g., Section 2 discusses pros/cons of DSL before defining it (R1). The writing is at times difficult to follow; “2L” (line 283) is introduced without expansion (R3). Section 3.1 should introduce options informally before the formal definition (R4). Minor issues: Figure 1 appears after Figure 2; 3.2.1 is the only subsection under 3.2; typos at L208 and L230 (R1). Figure 4 is pixelated and hard to read (R3).
- **Lack of concrete examples**: The paper does not provide a single complete example of a programmatic policy or an option for any environment, making it hard to gauge complexity (R1, R2, R4). Authors suggest adding examples in the appendix (R1, R2).
- **Unclear differences from LISS**: The paper does not specify the exact differences between InnateCoder and LISS beyond the option source (R1).
- **Limited applicability due to DSL-dependency**: The method requires all discrete actions to be captured in a hand-crafted grammar, demanding significant domain expertise to design a DSL that is both expressive and amenable to hill-climbing search. The assumption that all tasks can be solved within such a DSL may not hold in many real-world scenarios (R1). It is unclear why machine learning is needed for these settings when the action space is already small and regular software engineering could learn the sequence of actions (R2).
- **Small contribution relative to alternative uses of LLMs**: A more compelling approach would be to learn a policy that outputs short programs conditioned on game history, allowing the foundation model to adapt. The authors argue this is expensive, but applying the current method from scratch is also costly due to DSL design (R2). One reviewer (R1) is more critical about this limitation, while others also note the small contribution.
- **Lack of transparency on prompt engineering**: The exact prompts are provided, but there is no discussion of how much prompt tuning was required (R2). The detailed “Program writing guidelines” and “list of tasks” suggest significant effort (R2).
- **Fairness of comparison in MicroRTS**: The definition of winrate (line 341) is unclear: what is the “set of opponent policies” and are they equally sampled (COAC, Mayari, RAISocketAI)? It is also unclear whether each method is trained exclusively on self-play and then evaluated against fixed reference opponents, or whether it interacts with those opponents during training (R3). If IC is trained against fixed opponent policies while other methods are trained differently, the comparison may be unfair (R3). One reviewer (R3) is not fully convinced that the method outperforms all known alternatives and would increase score if convinced.
- **Missing details on training procedure**: The paper should elaborate on the exact workflow—how the policy is improved, the round-robin tournament among 30 seeds, what data is used for improvement, and how/when evaluation occurs (R3). The explanation of SHC acquiring the initial programmatic component is lacking; does it use enumeration of the DSL? (R4).
- **Missing baselines**: Regular DRL algorithms should be evaluated on Karel as a baseline, since it does not seem overwhelmingly difficult (R3).
- **Unexplained results**: The combination of Llama 3.1 + GPT-4o performs worse on Mayari than either alone—this is not explained (R4).
- **Missing analysis on option filtering**: The paper does not provide specific numbers on the percentage of adopted options for each environment (R4).
- **Lack of discussion on ε in SHC**: The paper notes (line 371) that the semantic space can be less conducive to search depending on the quality of options, but does not discuss how varying ε could improve results (R4).
- **Missing analysis on option count groups**: Why is there a clear performance difference between agents with ≤1400 options and those with ≥5000 options? Agents with ≥5000 options show sharp early improvement while ≤1400 agents do not; the cut-off is unexplained (R1).
- **Missing ablation on search type**: There should be results comparing the importance of syntax search versus semantic search (R2).
- **Multi-modal information**: The prompts use only plain text; it is unclear whether providing images or animations (e.g., game states) could improve the quality of generated programs (R4).
- **Determinism and evaluation methodology**: Are the policies produced by InnateCoder deterministic? Are other baselines also deterministic? How are the numbers in Table 1 produced—by enumeration or simulation? If simulation, how many games? (R1). The difference between ε and V̂^n (line 210) is unclear (R1).
