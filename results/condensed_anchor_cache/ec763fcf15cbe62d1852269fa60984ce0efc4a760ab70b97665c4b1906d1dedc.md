## Merged Review

### Summary

Four reviewers assessed this paper on robust conformal prediction. The paper identifies a practical flaw in the prior RSCP method (its Monte Carlo non-conformity score approximation invalidates the certificate), corrects it with RSCP+ (using a Hoeffding bound), and introduces two techniques (PTT and RCT) to improve prediction set efficiency. Reviewers find the problem important and the overall contribution solid, but raise concerns about significance of the flaw (RSCP still works in practice), missing literature, incomplete ablation of PTT/RCT, unclear hyperparameter selection, and several experimental omissions. Scores range from 6 to 8, with some disagreement on the overall contribution and clarity.

### Strengths

- **Problem importance and novelty:** The paper tackles uncertainty quantification via conformal prediction under adversarial perturbations, an important and timely subject (R2, R3, R1). The identification of a missing high-probability bound in RSCP’s certification is a real issue, and RSCP+ provides a sound correction using Hoeffding’s inequality (R2, R4, R1). The overall framework is considered novel and theoretically grounded (R1, R3).

- **Technically sound contributions:** The paper’s technical content is solid; the appendix is detailed (R2). The PTT mechanism (Ranking and Sigmoid transformations) is theoretically motivated to minimize the efficiency gap \(\alpha_{gap}\) by reducing the slope of \(\Phi_{\tilde{S}}(\tau)\), and it is computationally efficient (R1, R4). RCT, while requiring retraining, is an interesting training-stage enhancement (R1, R3).

- **Empirical validation on multiple benchmarks:** Results are verified on CIFAR10, CIFAR100, and ImageNet with various network architectures (R2). The proposed PTT+RCT combination boosts efficiency significantly (e.g., up to \(4.36\times\), \(5.46\times\), \(16.9\times\) on respective datasets) compared to the trivial all-class sets produced by the baseline RSCP+ (R1, R2, R3). RSCP+ is more efficient than the original RSCP (R3).

- **Clear problem background and well-written (for the most part):** The motivation and background are well illustrated (R3). Despite some structural issues, the paper is generally well written (R2).

### Weaknesses

- **Fundamental motivation unclear: why does flawed RSCP still work?** If the original RSCP certificate is invalid due to Monte Carlo estimation, the paper should explain—preferably in the main text—why it still performs well in practice (R2). Appendix A implies catastrophic failures, but empirical results do not show obvious coverage violations (R1). This missing explanation weakens the paper’s core motivation.

- **RSCP+ baseline is trivial without PTT/RCT:** On CIFAR10, CIFAR100, and ImageNet, the vanilla RSCP+ (without efficiency techniques) yields prediction sets that include all classes, i.e., no informative reduction (R1). Even with PTT, sets on ImageNet remain relatively large, limiting practical application (R1). Reviewers question whether this conservativeness is truly inherent to RSCP or an artifact of the model/setup (R1 points out that RSCP itself produces non-trivial sets, e.g., average size 2.751 on CIFAR10 with APS, so calling RSCP+ “conservative” needs stronger justification) (R1). On clean (unperturbed) data, it would be useful to know if the prediction sets also become trivial (R2).

- **RCT contribution is not clearly demonstrated:** RCT requires retraining the base model, but experiments do not convincingly show that it adds much beyond PTT alone (R4). The added complexity may distract from the main contributions (R4). Results on ImageNet with PTT+RCT were requested but not provided (R1). The paper should better isolate the benefit of RCT.

- **Missing comparisons and incomplete literature review:** Several closely related works are omitted: [1], [2], and [3] (reviewer R3 lists references; author should identify them). Empirical comparisons to these methods should be added (R3). The paper only discusses Gendler et al. (2021) as prior work (R3).

- **Hyperparameter and implementation ambiguities:**
    - The optimal value for the temperature \(T\) in the sigmoid transformation is unclear; it must be fixed ex-ante to preserve i.i.d., but there is no guidance on how to choose it, nor what prevents setting \(T = \infty\) (R1, R4).
    - The ranking transformation: is it a novel contribution or borrowed from prior work? If the latter, a citation is missing (R3).
    - For the ranking transformation, the transformed scores become uniformly distributed, but the calibration and test scores are no longer independent—how does RSCP+ validity still hold? (R3).
    - The sigmoid transformation assumes uniformly distributed scores; why is that necessary? (R3).
    - Adversarial example construction is not specified (PGD? SmoothAdv?) (R2).

- **Limited ablation and sensitivity studies:**
    - The impact of the number of Monte Carlo samples (\(N\)) on RSCP+ is only tested on CIFAR10; additional experiments on other datasets are needed (R1, also Q3).
    - It is ambiguous whether PTT can ever *harm* efficiency (e.g., if the adversarial noise is crafted against the sigmoid transformation) (R4). The optimality proof relies on a uniform distribution assumption; a failure-mode analysis is missing (R4).
    - The experimental setup for PTT requires an additional held-out set. To ensure a fair comparison with vanilla RSCP+, the baseline should use the combined holdout+calibration set as its calibration set (R2). The paper should clarify or adjust the protocol.
    - Section D.2 appears to contain inconsistencies between maximizing/minimizing; this needs correction (R4).
    - The paper does not discuss regression problems—only classification is considered (R3).

- **Presentation and structure issues:** The three main techniques (RSCP+, PTT, RCT) are largely disconnected, making the paper hard to follow; jumping between them degrades readability (R2). The paper relies heavily on the appendix, which interrupts the flow (R2). The key differences between RSCP+ and RSCP should be summarized earlier, ideally in the Introduction, rather than appearing only on page 4 (R3).
