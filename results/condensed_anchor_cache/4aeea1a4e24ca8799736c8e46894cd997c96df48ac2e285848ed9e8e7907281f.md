## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper compares search for programmatic policies directly in the programmatic space (induced by the DSL) against search in latent spaces learned by LEAPS/HPRL. Empirical evaluations on Karel tasks show that direct programmatic-space search converges faster and achieves higher episodic returns, and the authors analyze the optimization topology to explain the difference. Reviewer scores are 3, 3, 5, indicating a split where one reviewer is more positive but still identifies substantial weaknesses.

### Strengths
- The paper is very welcome for questioning claims about latent-space advantages over simpler, more straightforward approaches (R1).
- The empirical evaluation is well-planned, conducted, and visualized; the background and related work review is well-organized and accessible (R1).
- A simple method that uses no deep neural networks outperforms the neural-network-based baselines on Karel (R2).
- The motivation for synthesizing programs as interpretable and generalizable policies is convincing (R3).
- Experimental analysis is comprehensive, using multiple topology-based metrics: ρ‑similarity, behavior‑similarity, identity‑rate, convergence‑rate (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Fundamental omission of the role of latent spaces:**  
  The paper misses the main point of using a latent space: not to improve search accuracy, but to make search possible when the observable space is intractable. If the programmatic space can be efficiently sampled (small programs, limited branching), showing that direct search beats latent search is a trivial result. The paper does not report running times, rejection rates, or how performance scales with program length or branching factor, which are needed to assess whether the result generalizes to larger problems (R1).  
- **Environment simplicity limits generality:**  
  The experiments are only on Karel, a simple grid-world environment. Using domain knowledge of this environment reduces the strength of the claimed conclusion (R2).  
- **Performance mismatch between Table 1 and Figure 5:**  
  For example, HC achieves episodic return 0.84 on DoorKey (Table 1) yet the convergence rate in Figure 5 for returns beyond 0.5 is 0.0; a similar contradiction appears for Snake. This suggests the Table 1 result may come from rare cases, making the convergence analysis misleading (R2, R3).  
- **Lack of detail on search in the programmatic space:**  
  - The maximum program length, the exact number of uses of the production rule `s := s; s`, and the maximum AST height are not specified. The paper claims they are “similar” to LEAPS, but the exact numbers and whether they are identical across all experiments are missing (R3).  
  - How the initial candidate program is sampled (length, probability distribution) is not described. This matters because the initial candidate dominates the search result in a non‑continuous space (R3).  
  - The method for breaking ties when all candidates have the same episodic return (e.g., 0.0) during Hill Climbing is not explained (R3).  
- **Ambiguity about randomness and convergence:**  
  - The paper uses fixed random seeds but does not test whether the programmatic search works properly under different seeds or whether HC converges in all tasks under different seeds (R2, R3).  
  - Section 5.2 states that if the algorithm fails to converge within budget, the initial program is re‑sampled and the search restarts. No failure rate for HC or CEBS is reported per task, so it is unclear how often this occurs. With a budget of 10⁶ steps, frequent restarts would turn the search into a brute‑force paradigm (R3).  
- **Reproducibility gaps:**  
  - The number of random seeds used for each algorithm in Table 1 and Figures 3–5 is not reported (R3).  
  - No pseudocode or figure is given for the Hill Climbing and Cross‑Entropy Beam Search implementations in the Karel environment (R3).  
  - No example program found by the direct programmatic-space search is shown, making it hard to judge the method’s efficacy (R3).  
- **Missing justification for why the programmatic space escapes local maxima:**  
  The paper claims the algorithm “is able to escape such local maxima” as a property of the search space itself, but provides neither theoretical nor numerical evidence to support this (R2).  
- **Overselling of unclear claims:**  
  The paper makes vague promises that are not concrete or trivially feasible. For example, the promise about restarting upon failure is not backed with data on how often failure occurs, and the high budget (10⁶) suggests the method could degenerate into brute‑force if the failure rate is high (R3).  
- **Overall novelty:**  
  While the effort is appreciated, the work does not contain enough novel insight to be a strong contribution (R3).
