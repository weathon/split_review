## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents OptiBench, a benchmark with 816 LP/MILP word problems across 16 problem classes, using a model-data separation format and a novel equivalence detection method based on the Weisfeiler-Lehman graph isomorphism test. The authors benchmark various LLMs and find GPT-4o achieves highest accuracy (49.39%) but varies widely across problem classes. Reviewers note the dataset diversity and evaluation metric as strengths, but raise concerns about dataset generation bias from GPT-4o, limited scope of the equivalence test, insufficient problem details, lack of reproducibility, and writing quality. One reviewer (score 3, confidence 5) is more critical on the theoretical limitations and dataset generation, while others (scores 6, 6) are more positive but still share concerns.

### Strengths
1. **Diverse and comprehensive dataset**: The benchmark covers 16 problem classes and 816 problems across over 80 practical domains, representative of both textbook problems and real-world complexity (R1 S1, R2 S2, R3 S2, R4 S1). R1 notes ambitious coverage, R2 appreciates comprehensive range of data types, R3 mentions diversity representative of textbook problems with varying difficulty, R4 highlights wide variety.
2. **Novel evaluation metric**: The Weisfeiler-Lehman graph isomorphism test for equivalence detection is interesting, theory-supported (with correctness guarantees), and addresses the need for automated evaluation in optimization modeling (R1 S2, R2 S1, R4 S2). R1 finds the algorithmic/theoretical contribution very interesting. R2 calls it an interesting and theory-supported approach. R4 notes the novel algorithm for detecting equivalence.
3. **Insightful empirical results**: The evaluation reveals significant performance differences across problem classes (GPT-4o over 90% on knapsack but below 5% on TSP) and that GPT-4o outperforms other models including o1-preview/mini and LLM-based agents (R1 S3, R3 S1, S3). R1 says findings are interesting, R3 notes that breakdown helps identify areas for novel research.
4. **Excellent positioning relative to prior work**: R1 S4 praises the positioning despite concurrent work in this fast-evolving space.
5. **Well-written paper**: R3 S1 notes the paper is well written (contrast with Weakness 1).

### Weaknesses
1. **Writing and presentation quality issues**: Multiple reviewers note problems including broken references (lines 243, 1151, R1, R2, R4), typos ("Instanc" in Fig. 2, "MLLP" on page 3), missing spaces before parentheses and citations (e.g., "Table2"), incorrect quotation mark usage (R2), small/illegible figure font (R3), and an unclosed parenthesis (line 473). R1 states "writing and presentation quality has a ways to go" (W1). R3 disagrees on overall quality (calls it well-written), but the specific errors are acknowledged by all.
2. **Insufficient detail and transparency about benchmark problems**: The paper only lists 8 LP and 8 MILP types, all standard textbook problems (R1 W2). The claimed diversity (over 80 practical domains) is not illustrated; only the knapsack problem is described in detail (R4 W2). Readers cannot assess the complexity or real-world relevance of problems. R1 specifically asks how complex problems are from a modeling perspective. R4 requests more examples, especially for complex problems. R2's question about why data is separated from the model and what aspects of LLM capabilities each dataset tests is also unanswered.
3. **Bias from using GPT-4o in dataset generation**: The benchmark problems were generated using GPT-4o to propose optimization models, create .json files, and translate solver code into world problem descriptions. Multiple reviewers (R1 W3, R3 W1, R4 W1) argue this creates a bias favoring GPT-4o over other LLMs, potentially explaining why GPT-4o outperforms even o1 models. R1 notes insufficient detail on the claimed quality control process (expert operations researchers reviewing problems) to trust benchmark quality; also suggests using established known problem benchmarks (e.g., TSP variants, scheduling, maritime inventory routing) instead. R3 questions whether the dataset is representative of scenarios where GPT-4o lacks knowledge. R4 suggests repeating generation with other LLMs for some instances to eliminate bias.
4. **Limited generality of the equivalence test**: The graph isomorphism test only compares variable-constraint graphs, ignoring logical equivalence of feasible regions and objective functions (R1 W4). Specifically, it cannot detect equivalence between weak and strong formulations (e.g., facility location problems), or reformulations that add/remove constraints/variables through presolve or cuts (R3 W2). R1 notes this is crucial for MILP and that the test likely cannot handle it; R1 explicitly says this needs to be addressed and that any test for this problem will struggle. R3 asks about efficiency of formulations (e.g., cuts, auxiliary variables) and notes such improvements would be considered incorrect. R2's question about applying to MILP generation also touches on these formulation concerns.
5. **Lack of reproducibility**: Datasets and benchmark results are not publicly available (R2 W1). As a benchmark paper, this is a key weakness for evaluating usability, effectiveness, and reliability.
6. **Potential licensing issues**: Use of Gurobi commercial solver in the pipeline raises concerns about data licensing and whether extending the work requires a Gurobi license (R4 question).
7. **Concerns about strict inequality constraints**: Equation (1) includes strict inequality constraints, which cannot be handled by standard LP/MILP solvers (R1 minor comment).
