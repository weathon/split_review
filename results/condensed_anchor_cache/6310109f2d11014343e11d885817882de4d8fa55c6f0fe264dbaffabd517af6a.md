## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes XTransplant, a method that transplants feed-forward activations from a source-language input to a target-language input during the first new token generation, aiming to probe the upper bound of multilingual capability (En→non-En) and cultural adaptability (non-En→En) in English-centric LLMs. It performs a greedy search over all N² source–target layer pairs to maximize the probability of the gold answer token. Experiments are conducted on XNLI, XQuAD, and XCOPA with LLaMA-2-7B-Chat, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, Qwen2-7B-Instruct, and Chinese-Alpaca-2-7B. The method yields large improvements (e.g., +80% relative growth in multilingual tasks), but multiple reviewers raised serious concerns about test–set leakage, missing baselines, computational cost, and limited practical applicability.

### Strengths
- The idea of cross-lingual activation transplantation is novel and interesting (R1, R3, R4). One reviewer (score 6) considered it well‑motivated; another (score 3) noted it is simple yet achieves significant gains in some settings.
- The paper provides an in‑depth discussion of the method, including analysis across tasks and cross‑cultural examples (R2).
- The experimental scope is broad, covering multiple tasks (XNLI, XQuAD, XCOPA), multiple LLMs (including a Chinese‑centric model, Chinese‑Alpaca‑2‑7B), and both directions En→non‑En and non‑En→En (R1, R2, R4).
- The method achieves large improvements over baselines in the reported “upper bound” setting (R1, R2, R4). One reviewer (score 5) highlighted that the method works for both English‑ and Chinese‑centric LLMs (R1).
- All possible source–target layer pairs are systematically investigated (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Test‑set leakage / unfair upper‑bound selection**: The main criticism across reviewers is that the “upper bound” is obtained by using the test set to select the best layer pair per instance or globally. This is considered unfair and invalidates the reported improvements as a valid evaluation.  
  - R1: Comparing the best combination of layers against the baseline is unreasonable; the authors should report average or median performance (e.g., from Figure 5) instead, and select layer pairs based on a validation set.  
  - R2: Strongly suspects that the improvements come from “lurking into the gold answer” because the test set is used for tuning. The large improvement in the En→En setting (which should not benefit from cross‑lingual transfer) supports this suspicion. The method should be tested with constant layer pairs per language, determined on a devset.  
  - R3 (question): The English XNLI score jumps from 60 to 94 in Table 1 – what experimental conditions caused this?  
  - R4: The N×N search is extremely expensive and there is no theoretical proof that transplanting feed‑forward activations gives the true upper bound; transplanting self‑attention outputs might also be considered.
- **Missing baselines**: Several natural baselines are absent, which could provide alternative upper bounds or fairer comparisons.  
  - Machine translation (translate prompt with a tool, then infer) is not included; instead the paper uses PIM (concatenating languages) (R1).  
  - Supervised fine‑tuning and translation‑then‑inference are not compared (R3).  
  - No comparisons across different model sizes (R3).  
  - Results for base (non‑instruct) models are missing; one reviewer (R2) guessed that base models could be less influenced by prompt language.
- **Practical limitations**: The method requires parallel bilingual inputs (a non‑English sentence and its English translation) and an expensive N×N layer search per prompt, severely limiting real‑world applicability (R1, R3, R4). The computational cost of the greedy search (O(N²) additional predictions) is not reported (R1).  
- **Lack of justification and clarity on key design choices**:  
  - Why is the transplantation applied only when generating the *first new token*? (R1, R4)  
  - Why is En→non‑En used for cross‑lingual tasks and non‑En→En for cross‑cultural tasks? (R2)  
  - In XQuAD, when transplanting non‑En→En, is the output in English or the non‑English language? (R3)  
  - How are layers chosen for the transplantation in Table 1 and Figure 5? (R3)  
  - The definition of “language consistency” in Section 5.1 is unclear; XTransplant is expected to hurt consistency, yet results suggest otherwise – can the authors explain and give examples? (R4)  
  - Why only feed‑forward activations and not hidden states or self‑attention outputs? Is there any theoretical grounding for this choice? (R3, R4)  
  - The paper does not explain how the findings can be used to improve multilingual LLMs or inform continued training strategies (R3).
- **Small test set size**: Each language subset contains only 50 instances; variance is not accounted for (R1).
- **Minor issues**: Figure 1 is unclear (R4); the phrase “curse of multilinguality” and “negative interference” could be condensed (R4); line 74 should specify “a question that requires knowledge learned in non‑English texts” (R4); line 145 should state that “another version” is the translation (R4); line 161 intuition for only first token unclear (R4); Section 3.2 “bi‑directional” is not accurate – “Mutual” or “Parallel” would be better (R4); line 203‑204 needs rephrasing (R4); typo “we we performed” on line 205 (R4). One reviewer (R2) noted a suspicious 0.0 accuracy on the English subset of XCOPA for Qwen‑2 and asked for verification.
