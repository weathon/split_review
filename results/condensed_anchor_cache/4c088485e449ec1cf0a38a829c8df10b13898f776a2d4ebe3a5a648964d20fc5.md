## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies whether a mediator can steer no-regret-learning agents to play desirable equilibria via nonnegative payments. It shows that steering is impossible with a finite total budget but possible with vanishing average payments, across normal-form and extensive-form games under full feedback and bandit feedback. For mixed equilibria, the paper expands the game with a mediator action that effectively implements centralized advice. Theoretical results are supplemented with experiments in large games.

### Strengths
- The problem is original, interesting, and has potential applications in mechanism design and federated learning (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 4).
- Good technical grasp; expertly leverages recent reductions (Zhang et al. 2023) to reduce mixed equilibrium problems to pure equilibrium problems (Reviewer 2).
- Provides a broad set of theoretical results across multiple settings and solution concepts (Reviewer 2).
- Mathematical parts are generally well articulated (Reviewer 2).
- Writing is clear and techniques are sophisticated; enjoyable to read (Reviewer 4). (However, Reviewer 1 found the paper inaccessible and lacking precision, see Weaknesses.)
- The paper gives a fairly satisfying and complete first investigation of the model (Reviewer 3).
- Experiments complement the theory, and the paper relates the framework to optimal mechanism design and information design (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Inaccessible presentation and lack of precision:** The paper is hard to follow; central notion “optimal equilibrium” is not well-defined (Pareto-optimal? welfare-maximizing?). “Full feedback” is not defined. “Directness gap” is not explained. The description of the learning setting (time iterations, repeated play) is unclear (Reviewer 1).
- **Steering problem defined via pure Nash equilibria** is odd because pure Nash equilibria are not guaranteed to exist. It is unclear how much theory applies to mixed Nash equilibria (Reviewer 1).
- **Experiments seem unrelated to theory:** The experiments focus on pure Nash vs. correlated equilibria, which does not align with the theoretical framing (Reviewer 1).
- **Major criticism (mixed equilibria):** For non-pure Nash equilibria, the paper does not solve the problem advertised in the title/abstract. The mediator is much more powerful: it can broadcast advice, and players are not standard no-regret algorithms but “learning with advice” algorithms that have a special option to follow centralized advice. This is significantly easier than steering via payments alone. The paper fails to mention that related work (Balcan et al.) uses more realistic broadcasting devices reaching only a small fraction of agents. This is a critical model difference (Reviewer 2).
- **Pure Nash equilibria may not be hard to steer:** Pure/strict Nash equilibria are locally asymptotically stable for all standard no-regret algorithms (citing [2-4]); finite payments should suffice. The lower bound in the paper relies on a specific, unnatural no-regret behavior. The experiment with Exp3 shows finite money works; the authors should provide a negative example where unbounded money is needed for Exp3; otherwise the \(T^\alpha\) unbounded payoffs are too loose compared to real-world lower bounds (Reviewer 2).
- **Motivation concerns:** The assumption that agents willingly accept being directed needs justification. Once steered to equilibrium, should agents be compensated for utility loss? An alternative is a one-step model with one-time compensation. Payment schemes raise fairness concerns (Reviewer 3).
- **Assumption that agents play no-regret while aware of steering** raises questions about bias and preferential treatment. The validity of this behavior model and application domains are not justified (Reviewer 3).
- **Technical difficulty for mixed strategies:** In the mediator-augmented game, solving Eq. (2) for mixed strategies involves an infinite \(X_i\) and non-convex optimization, which is unclear how to handle (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing comparison:** The difference between this work and Deng et al. “Strategizing against no-regret learners” is not extensively discussed; the models and results should be clarified (Reviewer 3).
- **Minority positive view:** One reviewer (Reviewer 4) found no weaknesses, but the above criticisms are raised by the other three reviewers, indicating significant disagreement on clarity, model realism, and technical feasibility.
