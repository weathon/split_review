## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper evaluates OpenAI’s o1 model in medicine across understanding, reasoning, and multilinguality using six tasks from 37 datasets (including two new benchmarks, LancetQA and NEJMQA). Results show o1 surpasses GPT-4 by 6.2% and 6.6% average accuracy on 19 datasets and the two new QA tasks. While one reviewer (score 5) praises this as the first systematic evaluation providing valuable first-hand data, the other two (scores 3 each) raise major methodological and interpretational concerns, with one calling the paper “simply testing GPT o1 on several benchmarks and reporting the scores.”

### Strengths
- **Timely topic and first systematic evaluation of o1 in medicine** – provides a reference framework for the medical LLM community and first-hand data insights (Reviewer 1).
- **Broad coverage** – uses 37 datasets covering concept recognition, clinical decision support, and knowledge QA, with multiple metrics (accuracy, F1, BLEU/ROUGE, AlignScore). This demonstrates o1’s improvements are not limited to a few benchmarks (Reviewers 2, 3).
- **Introduction of two new challenging QA datasets (LancetQA, NEJMQA)** with higher clinical relevance than standard benchmarks (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- **Valuable analysis in Section 4.3 and the discussion** on the limitations of current evaluation metrics (especially BLEU/ROUGE for free-form text) and the need for new methodologies – considered the paper’s most significant contribution by Reviewer 3.
- **Detailed experimental analysis** highlighting inconsistency in performance rankings across different metrics for open-ended QA, and that o1 still benefits from CoT and remains constrained by hallucinations (Reviewer 1).
- **Useful appendix** with prompts, decoding times, and case studies adding transparency (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited technological and data contribution** – no attempts to mitigate any of the identified issues (hallucination, metric discrepancies, prompt design); the paper raises problems but does not solve them (Reviewer 1). The two new datasets lack construction details (scraping, processing, proofreading, number of items) and it is unclear if they will be open-sourced (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). For other collected datasets, no cross-experiments or secondary processing are performed, yielding limited insights beyond o1 itself (Reviewer 1).
- **Methodological flaws in evaluation pipeline**:
  - Use of BLEU/ROUGE for summarization is questionable (n-gram overlap); Tables 3 and 4 show no correlation with AlignScore, raising doubts about result interpretation (Reviewer 3).
  - Categorization of MedQA, MedMCQA, PubMedQA under “Reasoning” is disputed – these tasks primarily assess knowledge recall rather than reasoning. The paper does not evaluate the reasoning process, only answer accuracy (Reviewers 2, 3).
- **Incomplete and inconsistent experimental comparisons**:
  - Results for Meditron and Llama are taken from prior papers, leading to evaluation discrepancies (e.g., PMC-Patient: o1 76.4 vs Llama3-8b 96.0). No direct experiments with these models (Reviewer 2).
  - Missing comparisons with competitive models like Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Gemini, or larger variants (Llama3-405b). Comparing o1 to Llama3-8b is questionable; Meditron-70b is compared but not Llama2-70b (Reviewers 2, 3).
  - Table 2 has incomplete results for Meditron and Llama3 (missing MedBullets, LancetQA, NEJMQA, MedCalc-Bench) (Reviewer 3).
- **Serious result inconsistencies**:
  - GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 scores on PubMedQA (52.8%, 25.4%) are far below previously published zero-shot results (75.2%, 71.6%) (Reviewer 3).
  - MedQA and MedMCQA results appear inverted between Table 2 and Table 6 (Reviewer 3).
  - NEJMQA results are not whole numbers despite 100 questions, puzzling (Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of statistical rigor** – no confidence intervals or significance tests. For NEJMQA (100 samples), a t-test yields p>0.05 between o1 and GPT-4, indicating no significant difference (Reviewer 3).
- **Insufficient support for conclusions**:
  - The claim “WE ARE ONE STEP CLOSER TO AN AI DOCTOR” is unsupported; the paper does not correlate benchmark performance with real medical proficiency. Modern clinical reasoning models involve more than internal cognition (Reviewer 3).
  - Statements about advanced prompting affecting o1 (e.g., self-consistency, reflexion) are based solely on LancetQA, insufficient to generalize (Reviewer 2).
  - No evidence that multilinguality relates to real-world physician needs (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing examples and analysis** – the surprising findings that “o1 still benefits from CoT” and “o1 hallucination remains serious” lack concrete examples or statistical data to help readers understand (Reviewer 1). The hallucination issue is not analyzed for randomness or prompt/parameter sensitivity (Reviewer 1).
- **Misleading case study (Figure 6)** – the NEJM question was originally an image challenge; models were not given images. GPT-4 mentions missing images; o1 may have hallucinated “isolated ulnar fracture” and reached the correct answer for incorrect reasons (Reviewer 3).
- **Presentation and reporting issues** (Reviewer 2): Abstract says 37 datasets, but Figure 2 averages only 19 datasets. Line 162 mentions asterisks in tables, but none appear. No reason given for excluding Llama3-70b while including Meditron-70b.
- **One reviewer (score 5) found this paper more valuable** than the other two (scores 3 each), particularly praising it as the first systematic evaluation and providing reference framework. However, the majority view is that the experiments and explanations are insufficient relative to the paper’s claims.
