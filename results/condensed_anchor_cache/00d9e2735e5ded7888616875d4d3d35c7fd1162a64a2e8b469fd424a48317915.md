## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a feature-centric approach to analyze Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), focusing on node features rather than graph topology. It introduces the *feature centroid simplex*, a geometric structure formed by class-based feature centroids, and uses coarse geometry to study phenomena like heterophily, oversmoothing, and feature reshuffling. The framework provides insights into dataset difficulty and suggests simple, graph-independent tricks (feature normalization, feature shuffling, early stopping) to improve node classification performance, especially on heterophilic datasets. Empirical validation is conducted on datasets including Actor, Chameleon, and Squirrel.

### Strengths
- **Novel feature-centric perspective**: Shifts the analysis from topology to node features, providing a new way to study GNN performance based on intrinsic feature properties rather than graph structure. (All reviewers)
- **Clear geometric tool**: The feature centroid simplex (a (K-1)-simplex in \(\mathbb{R}^m\)) and mapping from the probability simplex offer an interpretable representation of class relationships and capture clustering behavior of node embeddings per class. (R1, R2, R3)
- **Theoretical grounding**: Solid mathematical support with proofs in the appendix, applying coarse geometry (quasi-isometry) to explain heterophily, oversmoothing, and feature aggregation. (R1, R2, R4)
- **Practical recommendations**: Simple tricks (early stopping, feature normalization, adding homophilic edges) are empirically shown to improve GNN performance on challenging heterophilic datasets, enhancing model interpretability. (R1, R2, R4)
- **Visual presentation**: Figures and appendices (notation table, proofs, experiments) effectively support the main text. (R2)
- **Insightful mapping**: Linking the probability simplex to the feature centroid simplex reveals additional learning opportunities within the feature space. (R3)

### Weaknesses
**Presentation and clarity**  
- The paper is unnecessarily hard to read due to heavy notation, technicalities, and frequent references to the appendix, which obscure the main ideas. (R1, R3, R4)  
- Definitions are distributed throughout rather than consolidated early; e.g., initial formal definitions in Section 2.1 introduce \(m\) as feature dimension but then use \(m_1,m_2\) as unrelated constants, causing confusion. (R4)  
- The phrase "for each index \(i\)" instead of "for each node \(v_i\)" complicates reading; variable \(\gamma\) is introduced without clear definition. (R4)  
- The distinction between individual feature components in \([0,1]\) and feature vectors \(x^i \in [0,1]^m\) is not immediately clear. (R4)  
- The probabilistic node feature model is not motivated; it is unclear what it means for features to be "convex" and whether this occurs in practice (only a reference to the appendix). (R3, R4)  
- Theorems in Sections 2 and 3 lack an extensive description of meaning and importance; e.g., Lemma 1 relating the feature centroid simplex to the probability simplex is difficult to justify without explicit assumptions on the feature space and how large enough \(m\) guarantees the relationship. (R3, R4)  
- Lemma 2 is ambiguous due to the term \(e_c\); a focus on the geometric simplex would help. (R3)  
- The proposed feature normalization is described informally—unclear whether each feature dimension across nodes or each node’s vector is normalized (the former being a standard trick, so likely the latter). (R4)  
- The feature-shuffling trick is not well-explained (though well-motivated); it is unclear how it would be applied in real-world scenarios with only 5–10% labeled nodes. (R4)  
- Line 226: \(G\) is undefined; Line 220: the GCN model used (without \(\sqrt{d}\)) should be explicitly stated as a variant; Line 406: how does this differ from the spectral result?; Line 472: the list of applied tricks is missing from the main paper; Line 527: \(\ell_v\) should be redefined; Line 537: "many" should be made specific. (R4)

**Assumptions and theoretical rigor**  
- Some assumptions are optimistic: e.g., Lemma 1 expects convex positioning that likely overlaps in reality (though understood as a starting point). (R1)  
- To prove lemmas and theorems, restrictive assumptions about feature distribution and convexity are required; these may not hold across all graph data, limiting generalizability. (R2, R3)  
- The validity of the probabilistic claims is not fully convincing without a detailed analysis of the bounds on the number of sample points needed for convex positioning or to justifiably use the geometric centroid of a class. (R2)  
- The framework’s reliance on convexity and node classes having well-defined centroids may fail when features are not easily separable. (R2)

**Applicability and generalizability**  
- The approach mainly focuses on node classification; it is unclear whether insights extend to graph classification. (R1)  
- When graph structure itself is crucial (e.g., social networks, molecular graphs), the feature-centric perspective may have limited applicability. (R2)  
- The tricks are primarily beneficial for heterophilic datasets; for homophilic graphs, where existing GNNs already perform well, the proposed tricks offer no significant improvement. (R2)  
- A thorough discussion on scalability is lacking: e.g., what happens for small graphs or low-dimensional features? (R1)  
- There is no dedicated limitations section. (R1, R2)  
- The practical implications for real-world deployment are not clarified (e.g., applying feature shuffling with limited labeled data). (R2)

**Experimental evaluation and comparisons**  
- Empirical comparisons focus on baseline models and lack analysis with more advanced GNNs having adaptive architectures or attention mechanisms. (R2)  
- The paper would benefit from evaluation on datasets with low label informativeness, such as those from Platonov et al. ("A critical look at the evaluation of GNNs under heterophily"). (R2)  
- Missing comparative analysis with other topological/geometric approaches: e.g., persistence landscapes/images (Adams et al.), topological deep learning (Papillon et al.), feature expansion (Sun et al.), geometric graph neural networks (Han, Cen, Wu et al.). These should be discussed in background/related works and experimentally compared. (R2)  
- The oversmoothing analysis could be strengthened by tracking geometric quantities across epochs (e.g., volume of the feature centroid simplex decreasing with more layers), requiring precise definitions of such quantities in high-dimensional space. (R3)  
- The distinction of homophily/heterophily via centroid differences could be quantified more rigorously (e.g., using diameter, simplex volume, or dispersion of node embeddings within each class). (R3)  
- Figure 5 should include the class centroid; in Figure 1, using Squirrel as an example would be informative. (R1)

**Novelty and impact**  
- The feature-centric perspective is novel, but the overall contribution is somewhat limited: it builds on well-known challenges (oversmoothing, heterophily) without proposing new GNN architectures or fundamentally altering them. (R2)  
- The recommended tricks (early stopping, feature normalization) are standard machine learning techniques; their application to GNNs does not represent a significant advance. (R2, R3)  
- Minor reviewer disagreement: Two reviewers (R1, R4; scores 8) find the theoretical contributions and practical strategies sufficiently novel and well-supported, while two reviewers (R2, R3; scores 6) question the degree of innovation and the clarity of contributions.
