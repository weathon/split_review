## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces a new graph similarity measure, Graph Foster Distance (GFD), based on optimal transport between probability distributions derived from edge resistance distances (Foster distribution). The Foster distribution normalizes resistance distances so they sum to n-1, yielding a distribution over edges. GFD then compares two graphs via the Wasserstein distance between their Foster distributions. A variant for node-attributed graphs is also proposed. Experiments on synthetic modular graphs and five benchmark classification datasets are reported. The reviews are split: three reviewers gave score 3 (negative), one gave score 5 (positive). The positive reviewer found the paper well-written with modest accuracy and efficiency gains, while the negative reviewers raise major concerns about experimental depth, missing comparisons, theoretical gaps, and methodological issues.

### Strengths

- The Foster distribution concept—normalizing resistance distances via Foster’s theorem to obtain a probability distribution on edges—is novel and simple, offering a new way to encode a graph (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- The representation shifts from node-based distributions (common in OT graph methods) to edge-based distributions, enabling edge alignment instead of node alignment (R4).
- The approach is reminiscent of Wasserstein/Gromov-Wasserstein distances but introduces a fresh coding perspective (R2).
- Some synthetic examples (four modular graphs) are favorable (R2).
- The proposed method shows computational efficiency on the benchmark datasets, especially for sparse graphs, and yields slightly better classification accuracy compared to some baselines (R3).  
  *One reviewer (R3) specifically states “achieves slightly better classification accuracy than other graph similarity measures” and “computationally efficient”, though others deem the experimental support insufficient.*
- A variant handling node attributes (GFD with labels) is proposed (R4).

### Weaknesses

- **Experiments are too limited and incomplete:**
  - Only four toy graphs and five benchmark datasets are used; the datasets are small and tend toward performance saturation (R1, R2, R3).
  - Table 3 is incomplete: missing standard deviations, and baseline numbers (e.g., fGOT) appear lower than in the original publication (R1).
  - Section 5.1.2 samples only 100 graphs; why and how they were sampled is not explained (R4).
  - No experiments compare graphs of widely different sizes, despite the cost design being sensitive to scale (R4).  Larger-scale graphs and datasets are not tested (R2).
- **Missing comparisons and references:**
  - No comparison to graph-edit distance (GED), spectral distances, or other OT-based distances beyond those included (e.g., COPT, Nagai et al., Graph Diffusion Wasserstein, Sobolev Transport) (R2).
  - The connection between GFD and Gromov-Wasserstein / Fused Gromov-Wasserstein distances is unclear; the linear form in Eq. (8) vs. the quadratic form of GW is not discussed (R4).
- **Methodological concerns:**
  - The claim that GFD circumvents the node alignment problem is not properly justified; it is based only on four toy examples and seems to follow from general OT properties rather than the paper’s novelty (R1, R4).
  - The transport cost design \(C_{ij} = (f_1(i) - f_2(j))^2\) is sensitive to the scale of the graphs (e.g., \(G_1\) with 10 nodes vs. \(G_2\) with 1000 nodes) because the mass values \(f_1(i), f_2(j)\) themselves depend on graph size—this is undesirable (R4).
  - The GFD with labels solves node and edge OT couplings separately (two independent OT problems), which can lead to contradictory solutions: high node alignment but low edge alignment.  FGW optimizes both simultaneously and thus is stronger (R4).
- **Theoretical gaps:**
  - There is no study of whether two non-isomorphic graphs can have identical Foster distributions, i.e., whether the mapping is injective (R2).  Questions about equality cases are raised repeatedly (R2, R4).
  - The computational cost of computing the pseudo-inverse of the Laplacian (needed for resistance distances) and the stability of that step are not addressed (R2).
- **Presentation and depth:**
  - The paper is “half-baked” and lacks depth; it does not explore insights from the new distance (R1).  One reviewer notes it is “not really dense” and does not give readers a reason to adopt GFD over existing methods (R2).
  - Writing issue: Section 3.3 uses symbol \(A\) both for adjacency matrix and node attributes; notation should be changed (R4).
  - The positive reviewer (R3) finds the paper well-written and easy to read, but other reviewers critique its depth and missing details (R1, R4).  Additionally, R3 acknowledges that GFD is a “direct application of optimal transport on the Foster distributions” and that improvement over FGW (both in time and accuracy) is “very marginal”—a complaint echoed by others regarding limited experimental gains.
