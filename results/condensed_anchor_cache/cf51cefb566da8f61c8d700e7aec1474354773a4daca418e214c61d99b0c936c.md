## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes OVTR, the first end-to-end Transformer for open-vocabulary multiple object tracking (OVMOT), extending MOTR with a Category Information Propagation (CIP) strategy and a dual-branch vision-language alignment structure. It achieves modest improvements on the TAO open-vocabulary benchmark (about +1 point Novel TETA) and shows zero-shot transfer ability on KITTI. Reviewers agree on the novelty of the end-to-end approach, but strongly disagree on the clarity of exposition, the strength of experimental results, and the completeness of the paper. One reviewer (score 3) finds the paper incomplete and the results weak, while a high-scoring reviewer (score 8) finds it a solid first solution.

### Strengths
1. **First end-to-end open-vocabulary MOT solution.** OVTR jointly models motion, appearance, and category in a single Transformer, a difficult problem due to lack of diverse video data and the challenge of balancing detection, tracking, and classification tasks. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
2. **Static image augmentation for end-to-end training.** Using only static images to train the tracker (rare in end-to-end solutions for open-vocabulary) helps overcome the scarcity of diverse video data. (R1)
3. **Strong zero-shot association on KITTI.** OVTR achieves a significant improvement in IDF1 over OVTrack on the KITTI dataset, demonstrating transferability to unseen domains. (R1; note that R2 contests the suitability of KITTI – see Weaknesses)
4. **Reasonable motivation and design.** The CIP strategy for propagating category information across frames and the dual-branch structure that uses ground truth rather than proposals with unknown categories reduce training burden and are well-motivated. (R2, R3, R4)
5. **Rigorous experimental comparison.** The paper compares OVTR against state-of-the-art methods on TAO and KITTI, providing a strong empirical basis for the claims. (R4)

### Weaknesses
1. **Poor writing and lack of detail.** The method section is difficult to follow, lacking critical descriptions of training and inference processes. (R1, R2, R3, R4) Specific omissions noted:
   - No explanation of how inference is conducted (complete pipeline, feature fusion, integration of multiple features). (R3)
   - Unclear architecture representation: Figure 2 (5 outputs) vs Figure 3 (3 outputs), the distinction between Position Part and Content Part, and the input nature of Content Part. (R3)
   - In Section 3.2, it is not explained how static images are turned into “image sequences” via augmentation. (R3)
   - Section 3.3: the matrix I filter for self-attention, the role of O_txt, and the Content Isolation mechanism are insufficiently described. (R3)
   - Section 3.4: the origins of matrices P and O in the Dual-Branch Decoder are not shown, and the use of the propagated C^(t+1) is never explained. (R3)
   - Section 3.5: the introduction of P in the sequential loss computation and the relation between L in Eq.6 and Eq.5 are unclear. (R3)
   - A typo in Section 4.3: “trained solely on novel categories” should likely be “solely on base categories”. (R3)
2. **Marginal experimental improvements.** The gains over the baseline OVTrack are very small (~1 point Novel TETA), and on the validation set Base TETA is actually lower than OVTrack. (R1, R2, R3, R4) Ablation studies (Tables 3,4) show only marginal improvements (category isolation: +0.6 ClsA_b, +0.1 ClsA_n; content isolation: +0.1 AssocA), which are not convincingly explained. (R1)
3. **Ablation studies not convincing.** Performed on only a 10,000-image subset due to resource constraints. Metrics on this subset are generally low (especially Base ClsA) and lack discriminative power across ablation settings. The experiments could have been run on the full dataset. (R2, R3)
4. **Missing comparisons with recent works.** The paper only compares with OVTrack. Important related works not discussed: MASA (CVPR2024) and possibly SLAck (ECCV2024, concurrent). Additionally, differences with previous end-to-end closed-set MOT frameworks (TransTrack, TrackFormer, MOTR) are not explained. (R1, R4)
5. **Claims of mutual benefits unsupported.** The claim that tracking and classification mutually benefit is not convincingly demonstrated: on the validation set, only ClsA improves for Novel (LocA decreases by 1 point), and on the test set Novel classification improvement is negligible, with asymmetry between validation/test results. (R3)
6. **Weak localization accuracy.** Reported localization accuracy lags behind typical closed-set MOT performance, raising concerns about bounding box precision in open-vocabulary scenarios. (R4)
7. **Lack of analysis on speed, model size, and computational cost.** The dual-branch structure with multiple attention mechanisms increases complexity; no analysis of tracking speed, parameter count, or suitability for resource-constrained platforms is provided. (R2, R4) R4 specifically asks about real-time applicability.
8. **Inadequate justification for using only Base proposals.** The method uses only base-category proposals, yet shows no improvement on base categories. Previous methods that use unlabeled proposals achieve superior results without extra annotation; the apparent advantage is only computational efficiency. (R3)
9. **KITTI transfer experiment is not convincing for open-vocabulary.** KITTI contains only a few categories (e.g., Car) and is not diverse enough to properly evaluate open-vocabulary tracking. The single-category comparison with OVTrack is not a strong validation. (R2; contrasted with R1’s positive view)
10. **Missing visual evidence.** No clear visualizations are provided to demonstrate tracking under rapid motion, partial occlusion, or diverse appearances. The supplementary visualizations are problematic (pole case unrepresentative, elephant case ambiguous, comparison with OVTrack unclear; dense boxes hinder quality assessment). (R3, R4)
11. **Code and implementation details not released.** Reproducibility is hindered by the lack of source code, specifics on data augmentation, loss functions, and training procedures. (R1, R4)
12. **Minority (score 3) reviewer considers the paper incomplete**, citing fragmented descriptions, no inference explanation, and a failure to convincingly show the claimed mutual benefits. This reviewer also notes that the only apparent advantage is computational efficiency, and that the experimental results are weak overall.
