## Merged Review

### Summary
This work proposes pFedGT, a personalized federated learning (PFL) method based on a game-theoretic approach using target interpolation. Each client minimizes a weighted average of local loss and a second-order approximation of the global loss. The algorithm is framed as a game where clients minimize local problems, and the paper proves existence and uniqueness of a Nash equilibrium and linear convergence under strong convexity. Experiments on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and HAR show 2.6%, 3.0%, and 3.17% higher accuracy than baselines.

### Strengths
- **New PFL model**: The target interpolation formulation (weighted combination of local and global losses) is a promising modeling approach, complementing ideas like model interpolation (Mansour et al.).
- **Strong empirical performance**: pFedGT consistently outperforms leading baselines on multiple datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, HAR), as shown in detailed experiments.
- **Theoretical guarantees**: Existence and uniqueness of a Nash equilibrium are proved, and a linear convergence rate is established under standard assumptions.
- **Complete story**: The paper provides a full pipeline including problem formulation, algorithm pseudo-code, theoretical convergence, and thorough experiments.

### Weaknesses
- **Game-theoretic framework is not essential or well-justified**: Several reviewers question why a game-theoretic formulation is needed. The updating dynamics closely resemble FedAvg, and the linear combination of local and global losses appears to enhance collaboration rather than model strategic non-cooperation. The algorithm is essentially a new regularization technique; only a single sentence at the end of Section 3.1 mentions Nash equilibrium. The paper lacks concrete game-theoretic analysis (e.g., what happens if agents strategically vary their personalization parameters γ_i and only optimize local loss). It is unclear how the game perspective provides intuition or analytical clarity over a standard optimization interpretation.
- **Hyperparameter burden and lack of sensitivity analysis**: The method introduces multiple hyperparameters (μ, L, γ_i, ρ). There is no systematic study of how γ affects convergence rate and final per-client performance, especially under heterogeneous data. It is unclear whether a single γ value is optimal for all clients or if personalized γ values are needed. More generally, guidelines for hyperparameter selection in different scenarios are missing.
- **Major discrepancy between theory and experiments on ρ**: The theoretical results require ρ > L (or ρ ≥ max_i (L·L_{F_i}) in Theorem 2) to guarantee strong convexity and convergence. However, experiments (Figure 10) show that ρ=0 not only works but consistently yields favorable results, often outperforming nonzero ρ. This mismatch between the theoretical necessity and practical effectiveness is not addressed, undermining the practical relevance of the theoretical analysis.
- **Questionable theoretical assumptions and results**:
    - The regularizer (ρ/2)||w_i||² is added primarily for theoretical convenience to ensure strong convexity. This assumption is uncommon in deep learning (non-convex), and under Lipschitz continuity and strong convexity, uniqueness and convergence are relatively straightforward – diminishing the novelty of the proof.
    - Assumption 2 is not standard in PFL and lacks clear connection to real-world federated settings.
    - Theorem 2 indicates that when γ=1 (no collaboration), the algorithm achieves the most favorable convergence results, raising concerns about the practical utility of collaboration.
    - Theorem 2 appears to consider only one local step, not the multiple local updates typical in FL; its relevance to the proposed multi-step method is questionable.
    - The theoretical analysis does not explore how the aggregation interval and partial participation schemes impact convergence.
- **Privacy concern**: Unlike gradient-only methods, pFedGT transmits c_i = gradient - μ w_i to the server. When the model converges, c_i ≈ μ w_i, effectively sending the user’s model parameters to the server. This reduces user model privacy and may be unacceptable for many users.
- **Communication overhead**: In addition to the model, the algorithm requires exchanging an extra vector c^t between server and clients in each round, increasing communication cost in resource-constrained environments.
- **Presentation issues**: 
    - The heterogeneity level α in Figure 1 is not clearly defined; its relationship to the α in Theorem 2 is unclear.
    - The discussion of ‘c’ on page 4 is confusing; explicit reference to Algorithm 2 and further elaboration are needed.
    - The paper lacks a comparative table outlining settings, assumptions, solution guarantees, and convergence results of related PFL works, making it hard to see the distinct contributions.
- **Insufficient experimental details**: The experiments need more details on settings and should include a larger number of clients (e.g., beyond the current setup).
