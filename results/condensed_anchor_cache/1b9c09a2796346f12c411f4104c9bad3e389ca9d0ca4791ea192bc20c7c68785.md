## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes CellPLM, a pre‑trained model for single‑cell transcriptomic data. Unlike previous models that treat genes as tokens and cells as sentences, CellPLM treats cells as tokens and tissues as sentences, enabling learning of cell‑cell relationships. It uses a VAE‑like architecture with a Gaussian mixture prior and incorporates spatially‑resolved transcriptomic data during pre‑training. Experiments show consistent outperformance over existing pre‑trained and non‑pre‑trained models on several downstream tasks, along with substantially higher inference speed for generating cell‑level embeddings.

### Strengths

- The paper is well‑written, clear, and provides sufficient background for readers without deep domain expertise (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- It effectively addresses a key limitation of previous single‑cell pre‑trained models by explicitly modeling cell‑cell relationships (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- The novel paradigm – cells as tokens, tissues as sentences – is well‑motivated and offers a new direction for single‑cell foundation models (Reviewers 3, 4; Reviewer 4 considers this a significant contribution to the community).
- Experimental results consistently show CellPLM outperforming both pre‑trained (e.g., scGPT, scBERT) and non‑pre‑trained baselines across diverse downstream tasks (Reviewers 1, 2).
- The model achieves faster inference speed when generating cell‑level embeddings compared to gene‑level alternatives (Reviewers 1, 3; the claim’s framing is further discussed in Weaknesses).
- The integration of spatial transcriptomic data with large‑language‑model techniques is a strength (Reviewer 1).
- The model learns a smooth latent space, beneficial for representation learning (Reviewer 1).

*Contrast:* While some reviewers (e.g., Reviewer 4) highlight the novelty and potential impact, others (e.g., Reviewer 3) express skepticism about the source of performance gains and fairness of comparisons, as detailed in Weaknesses.

### Weaknesses

- **Unclear source of performance gains and lack of ablation studies.** Several reviewers note that the paper does not include ablation studies to isolate the contribution of each component. Specifically, it is unclear how much the performance improvement stems from: the Gaussian mixture prior, the transformer encoder architecture, the cell‑cell relationship modeling, the VAE‑like architecture, the use of spatial data, or the tokenization scheme. The observation that even a randomly initialized version of CellPLM outperforms most pre‑trained baselines raises the concern that the gains may be largely due to the Transformer architecture itself rather than the specific proposed designs (Reviewers 2, 3, 4; also noted by Reviewer 1 as “benefit of pre‑training is unclear”). Targeted ablations are needed to disentangle these factors.

- **Insufficient validation of the Gaussian mixture prior.** The choice of a Gaussian mixture prior is not well‑justified, and its advantage over a simpler standard Gaussian prior is not empirically demonstrated. The paper does not clarify how the number of mixture components is chosen, whether this choice is robust, or how the prior’s parameters are optimized (Reviewers 2, 4).

- **Potential unfairness in the speed comparison.** The claimed 500×/100× inference speed improvement is a direct consequence of aggregating gene‑level information into cell‑level tokens, which dramatically reduces input sequence length. The comparison against models that operate at the gene level (e.g., scGPT, scBERT) is therefore not apple‑to‑apple; the claim should be restricted to “higher inference speed when generating cell embeddings” (Reviewer 3).

- **Missing details on data preprocessing and gene set.** The paper restricts the gene vocabulary to 13,500 genes but does not specify which genes are selected (e.g., protein‑coding vs. non‑protein‑coding) or the exact preprocessing pipeline for the datasets. This hampers reproducibility (Reviewer 1).

- **Ambiguity in the cell masking strategy.** If masking is applied at the gene level and cells are sampled from the same batch, other cells in the batch could provide sufficient information to impute the masked cell, potentially leading to cheating. The paper does not clarify whether masking is at the cell or gene level or how information leakage is prevented (Reviewer 1).

- **Handling of single‑cell samples.** It is not explained how the model would operate when only a single cell is available (e.g., feeding a single cell token) (Reviewer 1).

- **Impact of sequence length and positional encoding.** The effect of varying the number of cells per “tissue” (sequence length) is not studied. It is also unclear whether the positional encoding used to incorporate spatial information significantly improves representation quality; an ablation removing it would be needed (Reviewer 1).

- **Missing baseline comparisons.** The paper does not compare against scVI or simpler baselines such as highly variable gene (HVG) selection, making it difficult to assess the true added value of pre‑training (Reviewer 1).

- **Gene‑level interaction analysis.** By feeding cell‑level representations, it is unclear whether the model can capture gene‑level interactions. How such interactions can be extracted or evaluated is not addressed (Reviewer 1).

- **Batch token handling.** It is not specified whether the batch embedding is learnable, nor how the model should be fine‑tuned when transferred to a dataset with a new batch label (Reviewers 1, 4).

- **Lack of discussion on paradigm trade‑offs.** The paper presents the cells‑as‑tokens vs. genes‑as‑tokens approach as an improvement but does not thoroughly discuss the pros and cons of each paradigm. A more detailed comparison of when one might be preferred over the other across different downstream tasks is needed (Reviewer 4).

- **Unclear motivation for the VAE‑like architecture.** The necessity of the variational autoencoder framework is not adequately justified when the primary goal is representation learning, not generative modeling. The added complexity (including the Gaussian mixture prior) raises the question of whether a simpler transformer with a direct head would achieve similar quality (Reviewer 4). This point overlaps with the first weakness but is a distinct architectural concern.

- **No direct evidence that the model learns cell‑cell relationships.** The paper claims that the model captures relationships between cells, but no analysis (e.g., attention‑pattern inspection, perturbation of cell order) is provided to verify this claim. The performance gains could instead result from higher model capacity or specific hyperparameter tuning (Reviewer 2).
