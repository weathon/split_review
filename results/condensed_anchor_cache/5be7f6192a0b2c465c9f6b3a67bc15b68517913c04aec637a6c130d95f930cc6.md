## Merged Review

### Summary
The authors construct a new parallel dataset of human-generated text (from StackOverflow, Yahoo Answers, and YouTube) and ChatGPT-generated text (ChatGPT-3.5, ChatGPT-4, GPT-3 Davinci-3) on the same questions. Using multiple analytic axes — part-of-speech tag distributions, stopword usage, BLEU scores, semantic similarity via BERT/Sentence-BERT embeddings, word neighborhood analysis via MIT Embedding Comparator, Roget’s thesaurus category chi-square tests, and ML classifiers (SVM, Naive Bayes, XGB, BERT with TF-IDF) — they compare human and GPT texts. Results show high semantic similarity, similar POS/stopword distributions, but significant differences in word usage patterns, Roget categories, and neighbor words. ML classifiers distinguish the two with decent F1 scores. The authors attribute disparities and parallels to conceptual, contextual, or lexicographic factors.

### Strengths
- **Dataset contribution** (Reviewers 1,3,4): Novel parallel datasets of human vs. ChatGPT texts across multiple domains and tasks, potentially useful for future research on text generation and evaluation.
- **Multi-axis analysis** (Reviewers 1,3,4): A systematic and comprehensive set of evaluation metrics (lexical, syntactic, semantic, thematic, ML classification) provides a broad surface-level comparison. One reviewer (R2) called it a “systematic study”; another (R3) found it intuitive and easy to follow.
- **Potential for specific communities** (R4): Insights from stylistic/linguistic variables may benefit relevant research areas.
- **Minority positive view** (R2, score 5/5 with low confidence) found the study systematic; others (R1, R3, R4) rated 1-3, citing major weaknesses.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of depth and explanatory analysis** (R1, R3, R4): The paper reports differences/similarities but does not dig deeper to explain *why* they occur, nor provides in-depth, significant features. Results are generic; the term “non-trivial” is used without definition or contrast to prior work (e.g., arXiv:2306.07799, arXiv:2204.05185). One reviewer (R1) noted similar existing works have more rigorous insights.
- **Unclear / unmotivated analysis** (R2, R4): The motivation is vague — even two humans would differ. No clear objective; analyses (e.g., stopword distribution) lack justification for what they reveal about human vs. machine generation. The introduction uses overgeneralized claims (“LLMs have been propping up ever since OpenAI revealed ChatGPT”; “all aspects of life”) and is not technical/scientific. Terminology: “ChatGPT-4” should be “GPT-4” (the model integrated into ChatGPT).
- **Insufficient data description** (R4): Details are missing on data language, length, word count, context/genre/domain, and question types (e.g., single-hop vs. multi-hop StackOverflow questions). Why not also use existing prompt-ready QA datasets?
- **Missing comparisons and baselines** (R1, R4): No discussion or comparison with current SoTA AI-generated content detection systems (e.g., DetectGPT, GPT-Zero). No comparison of patterns between two different LLMs (e.g., ChatGPT vs. GPT-4) or between top-2 human answers (R2). No ablation: classification uses only TF-IDF features; why not combine the other linguistic features explored earlier? (R4)
- **Specific analysis issues** (R1, R3, R4):
    - Figure 1: unclear whether human/chatgpt corpora are separate or combined; adds little value (could be appendix).
    - Figure 4: finding that YA-D3 vs YA-H have least semantic similarity is interesting but not further investigated (no human evaluation, no follow-up).
    - Figure 5 (t-SNE): better visualization methods exist (UMAP, Deep Aligned Clustering) to show topic overlap.
    - Figure 6 (word neighborhoods): colors not explained; uses only two examples and a “lesser-used metric”, insufficient to demonstrate differences.
    - Roget’s thesaurus thematic analysis: why not use more modern topic models (LDA, BERTopic, Contextual Topic Models) that might yield better results? (R4)
- **Classification details insufficient** (R3, R4): Section 4.1 does not clearly explain how BERT is used — are BERT embeddings input directly to a classifier, and if so which classifier? No exploration or ablation of using other linguistic features as inputs.
- **Not meeting conference rigor** (R1, R4): Overall the study reads as a mix-and-match of available tools without proposing novel approach or shedding new insights for ICLR; contribution and scientific soundness rated poor by multiple reviewers. One reviewer (R2) suggested it might be a better fit for linguistics conferences.
