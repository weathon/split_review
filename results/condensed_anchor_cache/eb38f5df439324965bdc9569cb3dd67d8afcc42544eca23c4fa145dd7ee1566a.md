## Merged Review

### Summary

HELENE extends MeZO with three components – an annealed momentum update, an A‑GNB estimator of the Hessian diagonal, and layer‑wise clipping – to accelerate zeroth‑order fine‑tuning of large language models. The paper reports theoretical convergence improvements (scaling with the largest layer dimension rather than total parameters) and experimental gains on RoBERTa‑large and OPT‑1.3B (up to 20× speedup over MeZO, 1.5% average accuracy improvement). Reviewers recognize the method’s design elegance and promising results against MeZO, but raise critical concerns about a mismatch between the theory (first‑order gradient assumptions) and the actual zeroth‑order implementation, insufficient memory overhead analysis, missing ablations and comparisons, and the practical utility of the optimizer in low‑memory settings.

### Strengths

-   **Design awareness and elegance** – The integration of annealed A‑GNB gradients and layer‑wise clipping shows clear awareness of the computational nuances of fine‑tuning LLMs; the discussion of EMA and related motivation is well justified (Reviewers 1, 2).
-   **A‑GNB estimator** – Removes the need to sample labels from the model, simplifying Hessian computation (Reviewer 3).
-   **Memory‑saving layer‑wise approach** – A solid step to conserve memory in large models (Reviewer 1).
-   **Convergence analysis** – The theoretical analysis of convergence steps is welcome and provides useful insights (Reviewer 2).
-   **Experimental improvements over MeZO** – Results on RoBERTa‑large and OPT‑1.3B across multiple tasks demonstrate tangible gains in speed and accuracy (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).

### Weaknesses

-   **Theory‑practice mismatch** – The theoretical proof (Lemma 10) is derived for exact first‑order gradients and clipped Hessian diagonals, but HELENE uses zeroth‑order SPSA‑estimated gradients throughout. Transferring first‑ or second‑order convergence rates to zeroth‑order is nontrivial and typically unsupported. The annealing momentum and A‑GNB estimator are not used in the main lemma. This weakness is considered critical by one reviewer (Reviewers 1, 3).
-   **Memory overhead and practical utility** – Storing momentum and the diagonal Hessian brings HELENE’s memory cost close to that of full parameter fine‑tuning, exceeding PEFT methods like LoRA. One reviewer calculates that OPT‑1.3B tuning uses ~16 GB, corresponding to 3×32 bits per parameter, while AdamW with 16‑bit storage and precision correction uses 5×16 bits (80 bits/param). Low‑memory first‑order methods (AdaLOMO, GaLore, etc.) can achieve better memory usage while also supporting gradient accumulation, which HELENE cannot. Reviewers question the practical setting where HELENE’s trade‑off is beneficial (Reviewers 2, 4).
-   **Missing comprehensive ablations and comparisons** – The ablation study does not isolate the three components (e.g., variants removing annealing, A‑GNB, or clipping); such an analysis is needed to understand each contribution. No comparisons are made with other zeroth‑order optimizers like HiZZO that also leverage second‑order information, nor with first‑order optimizers like Adam/AdamW. Experiments are limited to RoBERTa‑large and OPT‑1.3B; larger models (e.g., LLaMA2‑7B/13B) are not evaluated. The claim that HELENE outperforms Adam is called into question because A‑GNB effectively becomes a clipped second moment of the SPSA gradient, making HELENE similar to Adam with only a few changes (clipping, less frequent second‑moment update, annealing momentum); Figure 5a suggests the main performance gain comes from momentum annealing (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
-   **Annealing mechanism unclear** – The improvement over a standard EMA is not obvious (the correct figure may not be referenced). The description appears contradictory: α decreases with iterations, yet the weight of earlier momentum increases, contradicting the claim of “reducing the impact of past gradients.” The phrase “reducing the learning rate as training progresses” (Line 258) also needs clarification. A wider range of annealing schedules should be ablated (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
-   **Layer‑wise clipping hyperparameters** – The approach introduces additional hyperparameters (clipping thresholds per layer) without sensitivity analysis or guidance on choosing them in practice (Reviewer 1).
-   **Presentation and clarity issues** – The methodology section is disconnected from zeroth‑order principles and the MeZO framework; the three components are presented without clear synergistic motivation, making the method appear ad‑hoc. Typographical errors: Line 134 calls MeZO a “first‑order method,” repeated ε in Algorithm 1, and improper hyperparameter notation. The derivation of Algorithm 2 statement 4 is missing. Algorithm 1 should use \(\hat{g}\) for the SPSA‑estimated gradient. Figure 5a’s reference is ambiguous (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
-   **A‑GNB estimator validity** – A‑GNB is constructed similarly to the empirical Fisher, whose expectation is known not to equal the Hessian. The paper does not prove that the estimator is unbiased (Reviewer 3).
-   **Dubious baseline results** – In Table 2, ICL baselines for RTE, WSC, COPA, and ReCoRD show no or minimal improvement over zero‑shot, and for COPA the ICL result is worse than zero‑shot, raising questions about experimental setup (Reviewer 3).
-   **Missing runtime and iteration‑level comparisons** – The 20× speedup claim is largely theoretical; actual wall‑clock time per iteration for HELENE vs MeZO and iteration counts required to reach a target accuracy vs first‑order methods are not reported (Reviewers 1, 2).
-   **Applicability to first‑order methods** – HELENE appears decoupled from MeZO; one reviewer asks whether it can be applied to standard first‑order optimization and whether any preliminary results confirm this (Reviewer 2).
