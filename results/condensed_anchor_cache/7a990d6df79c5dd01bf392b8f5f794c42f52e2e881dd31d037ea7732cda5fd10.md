## Merged Review

### Summary
The authors propose R3-ProtoPNet, a three-stage procedure to debug prototypical part networks using human feedback. First, they collect human ratings (1–5 scale) of prototype quality on CUB-200-2011 via Amazon Mechanical Turk and train a reward model (Bradley-Terry) that predicts human preferences. Then they: (1) reweight prototypes by biasing their inverse-distance similarity toward high-reward image patches, (2) reselect low-reward prototypes by replacing them with high-reward random patch representations, and (3) retrain the full network to realign features with the updated prototypes. Experiments use VGG, ResNet, and DenseNet backbones. The reward model achieves >90% accuracy in predicting human preferences. R3-ProtoPNet improves average prototype reward by 31.65% and activation precision by 46.85% over ProtoPNet, and maintains or improves classification accuracy; an R3 ensemble outperforms the ProtoPNet ensemble.

### Strengths
- Clear relationship with and differentiation from the original ProtoPNet; good integration of notes, explanation, and notation/math particularly in Section 4.2.
- Original idea of combining RLHF mechanics with concept debugging; the overall technique is sensible and makes good use of existing methods.
- Coverage of related work is good; authors are upfront about limitations.
- The experimental setup is mostly satisfactory (multiple backbones, good evaluation metrics).
- The reward model provides a quantifiable metric for prototype quality and enables flexible alignment of prototypes to human preferences.
- Reward‑guided reweighting and reselection improve prototype interpretability; retraining maintains or improves predictive performance.
- Approach can be applied to different base architectures (VGG, ResNet, DenseNet).
- Results are compelling: high reward‑model accuracy, increased average reward, improved activation precision, no loss of accuracy, improved ensemble performance, and qualitative examples showing reduced dependence on background/spurious features.
- Paper is easy to read.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited evaluation domain**: Experiments use only one dataset (CUB-200-2011). Bontempelli et al. evaluated on three datasets (CUB, synthetic, X‑ray). The choice of single dataset is not justified, and performance on more complex/ diverse datasets (e.g., ImageNet) is unclear. (R2, R4)
- **Missing comparison**: The work of Bontempelli et al. is cited but not compared against; converting ratings to binary labels (e.g., ratings ≤3 → "bad", ≥4 → "good") would be trivial. (R2)
- **No evaluation of the learned reward function**: Its accuracy on held‑out human judgments is reported, but its quality as a reward signal is not directly assessed (e.g., does it correlate with downstream prototype quality?). (R3)
- **Lack of theoretical insight**: Little explanation for why R3 empirically improves prediction accuracy; the paper feels like a straightforward application of RLHF to ProtoPNet without deeper analysis. (R1, R3)
- **Ablation needed**: It is unclear whether both reward reweighting and prototype reselection are necessary; reselection seems more critical, reweighting possibly optional. An ablation study is requested. (R3)
- **Limited visualizations**: Only a few examples are shown; readers need to see how prototypes are used in R3-ProtoPNet reasoning, the closest prototypes to a given image, and the closest image patches to a learned prototype to verify semantic meaningfulness. (R3)
- **Qualitative analysis is superficial**: More detailed examination of both successes and failures (e.g., where spurious correlations still persist) would strengthen the paper. (R1)
- **Prototypes are soft‑edged and blobby**: Crisper highlights of specific parts (tails, feet, beaks) are desirable but not achieved; pixel‑level segmentation maps (as in AP) could provide sharper supervision. (R1)
- **Nuanced definition of “spurious” needed**: Background features (e.g., habitat, sky vs. grass) can be informative for bird identification; the paper should acknowledge context‑dependence and potential usefulness of such features. (R1)
- **No expert evaluation**: Without domain experts (e.g., ornithologists) assessing prototype utility, real‑world applicability remains unclear. (R1)
- **Cost‑benefit of the reward model is not discussed**: Collecting a large number of ratings is expensive; for ProtoPNets (unlike LLMs) the benefit may not justify the annotation cost. (R2)
- **Po‑‑‑like passive learning limitation**: The reward function depends on the initial model and becomes obsolete after fine‑tuning; if the debugged model is still buggy, the same reward function cannot be reused. (R2)
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: The choice of alpha, beta, and gamma thresholds is not justified, and their impact on model quality is unexplored. (R2)
- **Approach feels overly hand‑crafted**: ProtoPNet’s appeal was automatic learning of parts; this method relies heavily on manually provided ratings, introducing human bias and potentially suppressing novel or non‑obvious features. (R4)
- **Duplicate prototypes persist** after R3 training, suggesting reweighting/reselection may not fully eliminate redundancy. (R4)
- **Reward model may not capture cross‑image consistency**: A prototype may be highly rated in some contexts but less meaningful in others. (R4)
- **Potential penalization of useful non‑obvious features**: The reward model prioritizes human‑interpretable attributes, possibly discarding subtle textures or contrast variations that are discriminative. (R4)
- **Minor presentation issues**: Table captions need to be more descriptive; rename “Limitations” section to “Limitations of ProtoPNet”; explicitly cite ProtoPNet in abstract; explain what the Bradley‑Terry model is; clarify that R3 requires one dataset with additional annotations, not two datasets. (R1, R2)
