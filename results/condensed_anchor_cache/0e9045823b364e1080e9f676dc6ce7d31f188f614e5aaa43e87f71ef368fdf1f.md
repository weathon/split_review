## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper identifies three issues in current GNN expressivity analysis—underestimated preprocessing costs, a mismatch between anonymous WL tests and non-anonymous features, and unrealistic assumptions in the CONGEST model (e.g., allowing NP-Complete problems in O(m) depth). To address these, it proposes the Resource-Limited CONGEST (RL‑CONGEST) model, which adds local computational constraints and optional pre/post‑processing. Using this framework, the authors analyze the WL test (showing GNNs need substantial width/depth to simulate it), examine virtual nodes (they reduce computational cost but do not increase theoretical expressiveness), and relate high‑order GNNs to first‑order model‑checking problems. Reviewers generally find the problem motivation strong and the framework interesting, but raise significant concerns about empirical validation, practical applicability, lack of guidance for practitioners, and the strength of some critical claims. One reviewer (score 3) is much more critical of the evidence for the claimed issues, while the others (scores 6, 5, 6) are more positive but still note major gaps.

### Strengths
- **Clear identification of three key issues** in current GNN expressivity analysis (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4). This is seen as a relatively novel perspective (R1) with intuitive examples (R4). One reviewer (R2) found some of these claims unconvincing (see Weaknesses).
- **Introduction of the RL‑CONGEST model**, which addresses previously overlooked computational constraints at the node level (R1, R2, R3, R4). The model has potential standalone value beyond GNNs (R2). The idea to use model-checking problems instead of WL is novel and promises higher granularity for expressivity analysis (R3).
- **In-depth analysis of the WL test’s computational complexity** (R1) and **interesting insights into virtual nodes**—one of the first theoretical looks at why they help in practice (R3). The new results derived by the model are deemed interesting (R4).
- **Well‑structured and nicely presented** (R3, R4); the paper is generally easy to follow (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of empirical validation:** No experiments support the theoretical results. Reviewer 1 explicitly states this absence significantly weakens practical relevance; it remains unclear whether insights translate to real‑world GNN applications. Reviewers 2, 3, and 4 also implicitly call for demonstrations, ranking of architectures, or comparisons on real tasks. This is a major weakness across all reviewers.
- **Lack of guidance on practical use and model design:** Reviewers consistently ask how to use RL‑CONGEST to enhance expressive power, compare GNN variants, or predict performance. R1 asks for concrete design principles. R2 asks for specific complexity classes for GNNs and a clear articulation of expected impact. R3 wants analysis/ranking of popular GNN architectures (e.g., on tasks where node features provide unique identifiability) to show translation to real tasks. R4 asks how to estimate representational power of different GNN variants or evaluate differences between added features (e.g., shortest‑path distance vs. resistance distance). The paper falls short of offering actionable guidance (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- **Issues with specific critical claims:**
  - **Preprocessing time complexity (Section 3.1):** R2 notes the claim that preprocessing is often underestimated is based on an isolated case (Wollschläger et al., 2024) and that Zhang et al. (2023) actually discuss preprocessing time explicitly, weakening the generality. A more systematic analysis is needed. R2 also notes that many papers focus on expressiveness, not computational complexity.
  - **Mismatch between anonymous WL and non‑anonymous features (Section 3.2):** R2 finds the claim lacks clear evidence and needs specific examples where this mismatch caused issues. Furthermore, R3 disagrees with the authors’ perspective, arguing that adding pseudo‑identifiers is precisely the point of many expressive GNN studies; breaking anonymity is a design choice, not a mismatch.
  - **Inappropriateness of CONGEST (Section 3.3):** R2 argues that CONGEST can still serve as an upper bound and the assertion that it is “inappropriate” is unconvincing. Additionally, **Theorem 4 is technically wrong unless the graph is assumed connected** (R2). R2 also notes that adding computational constraints shifts the purpose if the study’s focus is primarily expressiveness.
  - **Allowing node unique IDs in RL‑CONGEST:** R3 argues this is unrealistic from an ML perspective because stable ID assignment is not possible on general graphs, leading to poor generalization. The authors’ justification that real‑world graphs are rich in node features is insufficient; R3 requests data analysis demonstrating unique identifiability of nodes in real datasets.
- **Detachment from GNN practice:** R2 and R3 note that RL‑CONGEST, while interesting, is detached from actual GNN implementation. R2 points out that GNNs are usually implemented with fixed‑size networks running in constant time, so the computational envelope should be constant for realistic bounds—the paper does not discuss appropriate complexity classes. R3 contrasts with the universal approximation theorem for MLPs, arguing that the update function’s power is more defined by approximation quality than by a fixed complexity class.
- **Missing references:** R3 notes that the paper skips the two earliest subgraph GNN papers (https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.00577, https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.06283) when discussing subgraph GNNs.
- **Applicability and scope questions:**
  - R1 asks whether RL‑CONGEST applies to all types of GNNs and tasks, and whether the resource constraints reflect real‑world constraints.
  - R2 asks whether RL‑CONGEST should serve as a new standard benchmark and how it complements existing models like CONGEST.
  - R3 suggests exploring other distributed computing models (e.g., the very restricted model in https://arxiv.org/pdf/1202.1186) that might be more fitting than CONGEST.
  - R4 asks how the framework can be leveraged to evaluate differences between various added features (SPD, resistance distance, etc.) that have different expressive power.
