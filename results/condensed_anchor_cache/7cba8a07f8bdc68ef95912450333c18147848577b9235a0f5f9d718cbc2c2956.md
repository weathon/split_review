## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a low-rank decomposition theory to explain why diffusion models learn high-quality representations in self-supervised manner. It hypothesizes that representation quality is linked to posterior estimation ability across noise levels, provides theoretical analysis under a mixture-of-low-rank-Gaussians assumption, and empirically observes a unimodal trend in classification accuracy as noise level varies.

### Strengths
- Theorem 1 provides a direct connection from the noise level σ_t to empirical classification performance (Reviewer 1).
- The paper relates multi-scale score matching to posterior estimation and representation learning (Reviewer 3).
- The theoretical analysis under the simplified data assumption is thorough (Reviewer 3).
- Section 4.1 offers an insightful analysis of the effect of training with multiple noise levels through the same model on learned representations; it is intriguing that diffusion models have relatively steady representations across denoising timesteps (Reviewer 4).
- The topic is important and interesting, and the paper could potentially be impactful with major improvements (Reviewer 2).
- The paper is well-written and easy to read (Reviewer 1; *contrast: Reviewers 2 and 4 find the writing poor*).

### Weaknesses
1. **Lack of rigor in theoretical derivation.** Theorem 1 is based on several approximations that are not justified:  
   - The replacement of \(x_\theta(x_t, t)\) with \(x_\theta(x_0, t)\) uses informal arguments; the accumulated error from the Markov chain is ignored, and it is unclear how this affects the final result (Reviewers 1, 2).  
   - Equation (9) replaces equation (6) for simplicity, but the connection between them is not clearly established and may not hold in all cases; this should be stated as an assumption (Reviewer 1).  
   - The overall logic is hard to follow, and it is unclear which parts are borrowed from prior work (e.g., assumptions and toy setup from [1]) and which are novel (Reviewer 2).

2. **Key concepts are poorly defined.**  
   - “Representation quality” is used throughout the text and figures without any definition. The paper does not explain how the two measures of quality (bottleneck activation classification accuracy and Class Signal-to-Noise Ratio) are connected, and their maxima occur at different noise levels, undermining the claim that both reflect the same phenomenon (Reviewer 2).  
   - The paper lacks clarity on what “posterior accuracy” means for real CIFAR images; measuring denoising performance for real images is an unsolved problem and the method is not explained (Reviewer 2).  
   - Many details are missing; readers are forced to make assumptions outside the paper (Reviewer 3).  
   - In Section 2.3 the paper states it focuses on sufficient data to avoid memorization, yet Figure 2 appears to be a reproduction of Zhang et al., and the analysis is presented only on the last page (Reviewer 4).

3. **Overly simplistic assumptions limit generalizability.**  
   - The linearity assumption (approximating each class nonlinear manifold by a subspace) is obviously unrealistic for real data; Theorem 1 therefore does not extend beyond the simple low-rank Gaussian model (Reviewer 2).  
   - The arbitrary split of \(U_k\) and \(U_k^\perp\) (what is “relevant” vs. “noise”) requires solving the representation learning problem first; relevance is task-dependent, so “representation quality” as a function of noise level is not well-defined (Reviewer 2).  
   - The assumption that \(K\) class subspaces are independent is strong; real-world classes often have dependencies, and this limitation is not discussed (Reviewer 1).

4. **Insufficient and limited empirical validation.**  
   - Experiments are restricted to small datasets (CIFAR) and simple architectures; claims about general applicability would be much stronger with experiments on ImageNet or with pretrained diffusion models (Reviewer 1).  
   - The evaluation of representation learning is limited to linear probing on the bottleneck features. Other self-supervised learning tasks should be considered (Reviewer 4).  
   - The result in Figure 1b (denoising at higher noise levels loses details) has been known for a long time and lacks proper citations (Reviewer 2).  
   - The “linking posterior estimation to representation learning” is a straightforward implication: later diffusion steps produce noisier samples, leading to lower posterior quality and higher linear probe entropy; this does not constitute a significant contribution (Reviewer 4).

5. **Out-of-distribution setup and architectural choices.**  
   - In the classification setup (line 192), the diffusion model is fed a clean image with a timestep \(t\) as a noise-level indicator; the model is never trained with clean images or \(t=0\), making this out-of-distribution (Reviewer 4).  
   - Limiting evaluation to bottleneck features ignores high-frequency information that could pass through residual connections (Reviewer 4).  
   - The paper does not clarify whether the denoising operates on raw pixels or on a VAE latent space (as in latent diffusion), nor how \(U_k\) is computed for CIFAR-10 (e.g., PCA on all examples of each class) (Reviewer 3).

6. **Minority positive reviewer notes missing insight and unclear key message.**  
   - While Figure 4b shows that the learned multi-scale score matching function behaves differently from the optimal one on a simple dataset, the paper does not explain why (e.g., bias in neural networks as in [1] regarding CNN denoisers). This is a missed opportunity (Reviewer 3).  
   - The overall message is unclear: both posterior estimation and classification show an inverted U-shape, but what is the deeper connection beyond that? (Reviewer 3).
