- Scores: 3, 3, 1

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a semi-simulation benchmarking framework for survival models in causal inference and fairness contexts, introducing a new calibration metric (MALE) and evaluating several survival methods under model mis-specification, confounding, and survivorship bias. Reviewers acknowledge the importance of the problem and the comprehensive literature review on biases in causal survival analysis, but they identify serious weaknesses in technical novelty, clarity of the estimand, practical utility of the proposed metric, benchmarking coverage, and presentation quality. One reviewer scored the paper 1 (poor motivation, no clear conclusions), while the other two scored 3 (interesting but incomplete).

### Strengths
- Addresses an important and underexplored problem: evaluating survival models on their ability to estimate treatment effects and identifying biases in causal inference with time-to-event data. (All reviewers)
- Provides a thorough overview of challenges in causal survival analysis, including key biases (model mis-specification, confounding, survivorship bias) and the shortcomings of current evaluation practices. (Reviewers 1, 2)
- The simulation study covers several varied real-world datasets, and the authors discuss estimands, assumptions, and metrics in a structured way. (Reviewer 1)
- The paper touches on various practical estimators (parametric logistic hazard, CoxPH, time-varying Cox, RSF, DeepSurv, CoxTime, DeepHit) with rich consideration of different sources of bias. (Reviewer 3)

### Weaknesses
**Motivation and Conclusions**
- The paper is not well motivated: theoretically it is already known that estimators are biased under model misspecification or violated assumptions. It remains unclear what additional empirical insight the study provides or why readers should care about these biases instead of using existing sensitivity analyses. One reviewer (score 1) found the paper lacking a clear takeaway, with only scattered observations and no unified conclusion table or dedicated "Conclusions" section synthesizing strengths/weaknesses per estimator. (Reviewer 3)
- The conclusion/discussion does not mention all methods nor provide clear guidance on which method to use. (Reviewer 1)

**Clarity of Estimand and Causal Framework**
- The objective of the estimation is not clearly stated. The paper treats the conditional hazard or survival function as the target, but these are not causal estimands (their difference could be). The authors mention that hazard ratios suffer from selection bias but do not explicitly define the causal estimand. (Reviewer 1)
- The evaluation implicitly assumes a plug-in g-formula estimator but does not state this. Having a better estimation of nuisance functions (hazard/survival) does not guarantee a better estimation of the treatment effect difference—this is well documented (Nie & Wager 2021; Saito & Yasui 2020). The paper should consider multiple estimators (e.g., IPW, multiply robust, and explicit treatment effect estimators) in addition to different nuisance function methods. (Reviewer 1; see also Cheng et al. 2023)
- The question "Is the estimand h(τ|a, x) changing when you change the indices of the covariates that affects the hazard function in your simulation?" was raised; further clarification is needed. (Reviewer 3)

**Deficiencies of the Proposed MALE Metric**
- The MALE metric is introduced briefly (less than half a page) without substantial theoretical justification or demonstration of how it addresses the discussed challenges. (Reviewer 2)
- It relies on knowledge of the ground truth hazard function, which is only available in oversimplified synthetic datasets, severely limiting its practical applicability. (Reviewers 1, 2)
- The paper misclassifies MALE as a calibration metric; since the hazard function can exceed 1, it does not measure calibration (probability prediction). (Reviewer 2)
- The paper does not compare MALE with existing metrics that also measure error in predicted hazard/survival functions (e.g., Survival-l1 in Foomani et al. 2023, RMSE in Curth et al. 2021b, or the metrics in Tang et al. 2022). It does not argue why MALE is superior. (Reviewer 2)
- Even if MALE were valid, good calibration does not ensure recovery of the true distribution; a proper metric for distributional accuracy is needed. (Reviewer 1)

**Insufficient Benchmarking and Outdated Baselines**
- The study includes only seven baseline models, predominantly Cox-based, with the most recent (CoxTime) from 2019. Numerous state-of-the-art survival models developed in recent years are absent. (Reviewer 2)
- Despite aiming to compare causal survival models, the benchmark does not include any explicit causal survival method such as CSA (Chapfuwa et al. 2021), SurvITE (Curth et al. 2021b), CMHE (Nagpal et al. 2022), DNMC (Engelhard & Henao 2022), or compCATE (Curth & van der Schaar 2023). (Reviewer 2)
- The synthetic data generation is oversimplified: the most complex modeling involves a cubic transformation of a single feature or a linear combination of at most two features. This limits the generalizability of findings. (Reviewer 2)

**Factual Mistakes and Presentation Errors**
- Incorrect definition of the hazard function: "the probability that the individual will experience the event outcome in a given interval of time" – missing the condition on survival to the start of the interval. (Reviewer 2)
- Incorrect description of non-informative censoring: Scenario 10 (Table 3) lets event and censoring times both depend on x₀, which is *informative* censoring (T ⊥ C | X violates non-informative censoring, which requires T ⊥ C unconditionally). (Reviewer 2)
- Numerous figure issues:
  - Figure 1a, 1b, 1c are identical (all three reviewers noted this). The meaning of solid/dashed/red arrows is not explained; random variable Z is not introduced until later in text. (Reviewers 1, 2)
  - Figure 2 subtitles are incorrect: based on lines 194-205 they should be (a) Direct Effect, (b) Indirect Effect via Covariate Shift, (c) Indirect Effect via Unmeasured Variables. (Reviewer 2)
  - The text says Figure 2 is in Appendix A.3, but it appears on the same page (Reviewer 1). Appendix A.3 is empty (Reviewer 2). Figures 6 and 7 are duplicates with identical captions. (Reviewer 2)
  - Figure 5 (and similar appendix figures) have no y-axis label; no dataset is indicated. (Reviewer 1)
- Broken figure reference: "Fig ??" (line 402). (Reviewers 1, 3)
- Missing closing parenthesis (line 405). (Reviewer 1)
- Misspelling: "confonding" instead of "confounding" (Reviewer 3)
- Table 2 in main text refers to App B.3 for more details, but App B.3 contains nothing beyond Table 2. (Reviewer 3)
- No mention of code availability, which limits reproducibility and reuse for benchmarking. (Reviewer 1, 2)

**Disagreement Among Reviewers**
- One reviewer (score 1) found the paper lacked motivation, had no clear conclusions, and gave a low score (1). The other two reviewers (score 3) appreciated the problem and literature review but still identified major weaknesses. The contrast underscores the paper's failure to convey its practical takeaway and empirical novelty.

**Questions Overlapping with Criticisms (incorporated above)**
- (Reviewer 1) Be more explicit about the estimand and consider multiple estimators; provide additional metrics applicable without ground truth.
- (Reviewer 3) Be specific to motivate readers on what they can learn and why they should care; give clearer conclusions.
