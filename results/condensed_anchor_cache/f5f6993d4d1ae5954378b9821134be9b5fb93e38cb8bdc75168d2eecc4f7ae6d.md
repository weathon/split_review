## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies the effect of a systematic homoscedastic bias on the efficiency of conformal prediction (CP) intervals for regression, comparing symmetric vs. asymmetric quantile adjustments. Under a stylized model, the authors prove: (1) symmetric intervals increase in length by at most 2|b|; (2) asymmetric intervals are unaffected by bias; (3) conditions under which asymmetric intervals are tighter. They propose a simple algorithm to estimate a scalar bias and evaluate on synthetic data, sparse-view CT reconstruction, and weather forecasting. The work aims to show that asymmetric adjustments can make CP intervals more robust to bias.

### Strengths
- The paper studies an important practical question: how bias affects CP interval length, and makes an interesting observation that asymmetric adjustments can mitigate the inflation [Reviewer 1].
- As CP applies to any given model, the results may help establish a good trade-off between model flexibility and efficiency in applications where data are scarce [Reviewer 2].
- The algorithm for estimating bias from the obtained interval is interesting [Reviewer 2].
- The CP mechanisms, derivation of bias effects, and comparison between symmetric and asymmetric intervals are clearly explained [Reviewer 3].
- Formalizing and theorizing the observation that asymmetric adjustments sometimes yield tighter intervals is beneficial for the field and practitioners [Reviewer 3].
- The paper demonstrates multiple real-world experiments, such as sparse-view CT reconstruction and weather forecasting [Reviewer 4].

### Weaknesses
- **Elementary theoretical findings**: The core results (bias inflates symmetric intervals by 2|b|; asymmetric intervals unaffected) are straightforward from basic CP principles and do not introduce advanced or novel theoretical complexity [Reviewer 3]. Theorems 1 and 2 are direct consequences of the linear assumptions, and Corollary 3.1 lacks clarity: the RHS is independent of b while the LHS depends on b, and the proof of L_asymb ≤ L_sym is not clearly justified [Reviewer 4].
- **Lack of rigor in main theorems**: Theorem 2 is stated for generic intervals (Eq. 2) but the proof appears to focus only on residual errors and CQR; it uses the fact that f_hi(Ŷ_i^{b--}) = f_hi(Ŷ_i^0)+b^{--}, which is not stated as an assumption, and also seems to assume bias is sufficiently large in magnitude [Reviewer 1]. In the proof of Theorem 2 (line 712), there is an inconsistency: first Y_i - f_hi(Ŷ_i^{b--}) < 0 is stated, then Y_i > f_hi(Ŷ_i^{b--}) [Reviewer 1].
- **Confusion about CQR usage**: The paper’s description of CQR (lines 194-196) is unclear – what is n_s, how are the samples Ŷ_i^b generated? If the prediction is deterministic, does this reduce to the residual case? [Reviewer 1]. The score definition from Romano et al. (2019) is presented inaccurately, raising concerns about the foundation of the theoretical analyses [Reviewer 4].
- **Inconsistent notation and presentation**: In equation (1), it is not explicit which bias is addressed and how randomness in the ground truth is handled. Ŷ_i^b is ambiguously defined (scalar vs. set of values); in Section 3 first paragraph Y_i are scalars, second paragraph Y_i = {Y_ij} as a vector [Reviewer 4]. Table and figure references are inconsistent (e.g., “Tab. 4.2.1” vs. caption “Table 1”; “Fig. X” vs. “Figure X”) [Reviewer 3].
- **Simplified bias assumptions**: The assumption of a constant, global bias across all predictions does not capture more complex real-world biases (feature-specific, covariate-dependent) [Reviewer 3]. The paper mentions various bias sources (lines 089-094) but only models a trivial homoscedastic case [Reviewer 3]. It fails to discuss whether improving the underlying model or using more flexible conformity scores (e.g., adding a free shifting term) would be more efficient [Reviewer 2].
- **Limited synthetic experiment settings**: Experiments only use large n and symmetric noise distributions; but the strong point of symmetric adjustment is leveraging asymmetries (skewness) of aleatoric uncertainty – this regime is not evaluated [Reviewer 3].
- **Wrong claim (line 266)**: The statement that “when no bias is present, symmetrical and asymmetrical interval lengths are approximately equal” is incorrect; symmetric adjustment can better leverage asymmetries (skewness) of the noise [Reviewer 3].
- **Bloated section 3.1**: Given their bias model, one could simply take the mean of the calibration set error to retrieve the bias; the section is overly complex [Reviewer 3].
- **Missing justification for algorithm validity**: No theorem guarantees that the proposed algorithm for bias estimation retains the coverage guarantee of CP [Reviewer 4].
- **Simulations missing coverage probability**: The simulations do not report coverage, a critical metric for CP reliability [Reviewer 4].
- **Lack of comparison with related work**: The paper does not compare with recent works that also aim to reduce CP interval length (e.g., Xie et al. 2024, Liang et al. 2024) [Reviewer 4].
- **Non-symmetric CP not new**: The idea of asymmetric adjustments is not new; the paper should provide an intuitive explanation of why asymmetric adjustments have been theorized to yield longer intervals (Romano et al., 2019) but also empirically observed to be tighter [Reviewer 2]. A theoretical comparison of the proposed method with a naive approach that splits calibration samples by residual signs and evaluates two sample quantiles would be helpful [Reviewer 2].
- **Focus on comparable bias in calibration and test**: The paper does not address whether it would be more efficient to handle bias via the point-prediction model or via flexible conformity scores [Reviewer 2]. It also does not discuss the non-exchangeable situation where bias is only present at test time [Reviewer 2].
- **Missing experiments on bias estimation**: The paper proposes an algorithm but no experiments on bias estimation itself [Reviewer 2].
