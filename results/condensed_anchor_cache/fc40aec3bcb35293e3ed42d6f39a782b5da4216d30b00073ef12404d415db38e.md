## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a framework for integrating multiple, possibly conflicting fairness measures via graph-structured feedback, addressing both time-invariant and time-varying importance weights. It studies online learning algorithms that handle limited feedback and provides weak regret bounds. The authors illustrate the setting with an advertising example and evaluate on an advertisement dataset.

### Strengths
- The problem of balancing multiple fairness measures with time-varying weights is significant and practical (R1, R2, R3).
- The partial graph feedback framework is interesting and a reasonable way to capture relationships among fairness regularisers (R3).
- Table 1 gives a comprehensive overview of prior algorithms, helpful for readers (R2).
- Chapter 4 (motivating example) is highly relevant and stimulates reader interest (R2).
- The combination of theoretical analysis and numerical experiments is good; the results demonstrate the difficulty of bounding dynamic regret (R3).
- The setting where weights change over time is particularly intriguing (R1).
- One reviewer (R3) rated presentation 3/3 and found the paper clear with a helpful illustrative example; however, two other reviewers (R1, R2) found presentation poor (see Weaknesses for the disagreement).

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity and organization**: Two reviewers (R1, R2) found the paper poorly structured and unclear. R2 rated presentation 1/3 (poor) and requested more comprehensive preliminaries on graph-structured feedback, action vertices, and the state evolution function P (e.g., eq (3)). R1 noted that the problem formulation (Section 3) and motivating example (Section 4) contain unclear definitions and inconsistencies. In contrast, R3 rated presentation 3/3 and found it clear.
- **Technical novelty**: It is unclear what specific technical novelty exists beyond the proposal of the framework. The description of how Algorithm 1 differs from the ELP algorithm (Mannor & Shamir, 2011) is insufficient, and the challenges in the regret analysis and how they were overcome are not explained (R1).
- **Inconsistencies in motivating example and definitions**: 
  - Equation (4) uses “concave combination” – should it be a convex combination? The example on page 5 with a single scalar weight 0.1 does not appear to be a convex combination of fairness regularisers (R1).
  - Figure 1‑(a): vertex \(a^1_1\) has 6 edges, suggesting it affects \(\{f^1,f^2,f^3,f^1_1,f^2_1,f^3_1\}\); the main text states \(a^1\) affects \(\{f^1,f^1_1,f^2_1,f^3_1\}\) – mismatch (R1).
  - The fairness reward function in eq (4) should depend not only on its own state but also on other regularisers’ states at round \(t\) (R3).
- **Graph structure justification**: The general setting (time-varying compatibility graph and unknown relative weights) and the reasons for applying graph-structured feedback need better justification. Why is this particular graph structure a good fit for fairness tasks? In many scenarios the fairness criteria are already known; learning their weights may add noise (R2).
- **Regret analysis limitations**: Only weak regret (w.r.t. the single best action in hindsight) is bounded. There is no analysis of the optimality of this bound, nor upper/lower bounds for dynamic regret (R1, R3). R1 asked why only weak regret results are provided.
- **Numerical evaluations weak**: The fairness measures used are simple – each regulariser penalizes deviation from a fixed target share, essentially the same type of measure. Results could be strengthened by demonstrating with multiple different fairness measures (R3).
- **Typos**: Eq (4): \(c^t \left( s^{(i,t)}, \ldots, s^{(J,t)} \right)\) should be \(c^t \left( s^{(1,t)}, \ldots, s^{(J,t)} \right)\) (R1). References in eq (1) and eq (2) do not align with the main text (R1). Eq (8): summation index should be \(j\) instead of \(k\) (R1). Eq (5) and eq (6): extra “|” under “max”; paragraph above eq (6): extra “∈” (R3).
