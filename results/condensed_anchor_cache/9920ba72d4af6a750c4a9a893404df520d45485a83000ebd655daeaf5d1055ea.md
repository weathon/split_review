## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes TSSA, a framework to attribute performance drops in time-series classification to temporal data properties using a set of metrics and a double-robustness estimator. Reviewers unanimously recognize the problem’s importance and the value of combining theoretical analysis with empirical healthcare case studies. However, there is disagreement on novelty: two reviewers (scores 8) are positive about the approach, while two others (scores 5, 6) raise concerns about incremental contribution, clarity of definitions, insufficient baselines, and methodological assumptions. Overall, the reviewers converge on the need for stronger experimental validation and clearer exposition.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles a critical and underexplored problem—distribution shifts in time-series data—with high practical relevance for real-world deployment (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- It provides both theoretical analysis (unbiasedness, consistency of the estimator) and empirical evaluation, lending trustworthiness to the method (Reviewers 1, 2).
- The empirical studies use real-world healthcare data with large patient populations and four distinct case studies, offering concrete evidence of interpretability (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- The attribution strategy is intuitive and well-suited to the problem (Reviewer 3). The paper is clearly written with adequate contextualization and easy-to-understand preliminaries (Reviewer 2; though one reviewer dissents on clarity—see Weaknesses).
- The framework systematically gathers metrics that characterize temporal properties (global, local, structural, inter-series), and discusses how to identify specific temporal factors driving prediction error, providing guidance for targeted adaptation (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Novelty and contribution**
   - One reviewer (score 5) finds the contribution incremental, as the framework is mainly built upon similar ideas from static settings [1,2]. Another reviewer (score 6) argues that the metrics and models are not new, and the primary innovation is only the combined use of these metrics to predict performance drops. This reviewer further questions the formulation of the attribution problem as an average treatment effect (ATE) estimation, because treating aspects of the vitals themselves as “treatments” conflates causality and may violate key causal inference assumptions (Reviewer 4). In contrast, two reviewers (scores 8) consider the approach novel and sound, leading to disagreement.
   - The paper claims to be the first to analyze detailed shift patterns in time-series data, but does not clearly differentiate from prior work on non-stationary time series, domain adaptation, or other shift detection methods (Reviewer 4). The related work should explicitly address methods for handling non-stationary data, not just anomaly detection or static shifts.

2. **Clarity and definitions**
   - One reviewer (score 5) finds the paper poorly structured, introducing ill-defined concepts such as “shift within shift” without explanation. Claims about “application-specific” patterns are not justified—the proposed metrics appear to be general time-series properties (Reviewer 1). The criteria for selecting metrics within each desired category are unclear; it is not specified whether other metrics could be used (Reviewer 1). Another reviewer (score 6) notes that case studies lack key methodological details (transformer architecture, hyperparameters, evaluation metrics). A summary paragraph on patient cohort, model training, and evaluation across all case studies would help (Reviewer 4).
   - One reviewer (score 8) found the paper very clear; thus there is disagreement on this point.

3. **Experimental rigor and baselines**
   - The experimental section lacks sufficient baseline comparisons. Specifically, the paper does not compare against existing time-series shift detection or attribution methods, nor against domain adaptation techniques (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 4 adds that there is no comparison to common approaches like fine-tuning on new test data, which would demonstrate the computational benefit or performance trade-off. The Sufficiency Measure is introduced without clarifying what constitutes a “good” value (reported ~0.16 for MSE, ~0.25 for balanced 0-1 loss); an ablation comparing the sufficiency measure to traditional metrics across safe/risk regions is needed (Reviewer 4).
   - Optimization lacks convergence analysis: a proof or discussion of convergence properties (learning rate, batch size, etc.) is missing (Reviewer 3).

4. **Methodological assumptions**
   - The double robustness estimator may be compromised if the shared representation space of the neural network associates the estimation of the propensity score (π) and the outcome model (μ); when one is wrong, the other is likely wrong too, negating the double-robustness benefit. The authors should test the reliability of double robustness under such conditions (Reviewer 2).
   - Equation (39) assumes i.i.d. data, but the paper deals with time series. The i.i.d. assumption should be made explicit in the main body (Reviewer 2). It would also be helpful to clarify whether repeated measures for the same patient are used as independent samples (Reviewer 2).
   - The use of frequency as a discrepancy metric in Section 3.1 needs justification, as the distribution discussed is supported on ℝ (Reviewer 3).
   - The derivation of Equation (21) should be clarified (Reviewer 3).

5. **Specific case study and presentation issues**
   - Case Study 3: The validation set (last 24 hours) occurs temporally after the test set (first 24 hours). This unrealistic partition contradicts the goal of simulating early diagnosis—a model trained on later-stage data is unlikely to generalize to earlier-stage data (Reviewer 4). The rationale is not well-justified.
   - Figure 5: font sizes are too small and difficult to read (Reviewer 3).
   - Figure 7: error bars are missing, inconsistent with Figures 4 and 5(a) which include them (Reviewer 3).
   - R4 asks: In Case Study 2, which dataset is reweighted based solely on the inverse density ratio? If temporal properties are static summary statistics, could Shapley values (for static data) be used instead? (Reviewer 4)
   - No code provided for reproducibility (Reviewer 2).
