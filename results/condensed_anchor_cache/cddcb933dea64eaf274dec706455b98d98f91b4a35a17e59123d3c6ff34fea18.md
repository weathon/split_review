## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces CTBench, a unified library and benchmark for deterministic certified training under the L_infinity norm. It standardizes training schedules, certification methods, and hyperparameter tuning to fairly compare algorithms. Evaluations show that most methods surpass their reported literature performance under the unified settings, and that the advantage of recent methods over IBP shrinks when baselines are properly tuned. The paper also analyzes model properties like loss fragmentation, shared mistakes, model utilization, and OOD generalization.

### Strengths
1. **Important and timely contribution**: The paper tackles a well-recognized problem of inconsistent evaluation across certified training methods. It addresses this by providing a standardized framework, including systematic hyperparameter tuning, correction of implementation mistakes, and fair training/certification settings. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
2. **Comprehensive experimental effort**: The amount of computation and experiments is impressive (R4). The code, pre-trained models, and benchmark are released (R3).
3. **Valuable insights**: The analysis reveals several non-trivial properties:
   - Certified models reduce loss fragmentation compared to adversarial training (R2).
   - There is a high overlap in the examples where different methods succeed/fail, suggesting a sample-specific inherent difficulty (R1).
   - The correlation between propagation tightness and certified accuracy at larger epsilon is absent (R3).
   - Certified training can boost OOD generalization for some corruptions while harming it for others (R1).
4. **Clear writing and presentation**: The paper is well-written, with clear goals and objectives (R2, R4). The evaluation of the IBP baseline as a strong competitor against newer methods is a key finding (R4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty and originality**: The contribution is primarily empirical and benchmarking, with no new algorithmic ideas. Despite some analysis, most components (training schedule, architecture, hyperparameter choices) are reused from prior work (R1, R3). One reviewer (R2) found the analysis partially mitigates this, but the consensus is that the paper would be better suited for a venue emphasizing reproducibility/benchmarking (e.g., TMLR) (R1).
2. **Insufficient experimental support for main claims** (primarily reviewer 3, with partial support from others):
   - **Accuracy-robustness tradeoff not considered**: Improvements in robustness are often accompanied by decreased natural accuracy. Missing baselines like ACERT and ACE, and metrics like ART-score or accuracy-robustness curves, make it unclear if tradeoffs are being improved (R3).
   - **Missing error bars**: The reported improvements (e.g., CIFAR-10 at 8/255) may be within the standard deviation of prior work (Shi et al., 2021 gave ±0.3 certified, ±0.4–0.7 natural) (R3).
   - **Training and certification costs not reported**: Different methods have different computational overhead, and the cost of test-time certification (e.g., IBP-based certified radius) could affect method selection (R3).
3. **Lack of ablation studies**: No systematic decomposition quantifies the contribution of each component (batch normalization, full-batch statistics, stochastic weight averaging, etc.) to the final performance (R4).
4. **Limited scope and generalizability**:
   - **Only L_infinity and deterministic certification**: The benchmark excludes probabilistic methods (e.g., Cohen et al., Matthias et al., Greg Yang) and other L_p norms. This makes it less comprehensive than prior benchmarks like Li et al., which cover L_1, L_2, L_infinity and both deterministic and probabilistic certification (R1, R4). It remains unclear if the proposed “tricks” transfer to other norms (R4).
   - **Single architecture (CNN7)**: All results are on one small, dated architecture; the claims about hyperparameter tuning and fair settings may not hold for other architectures (R4).
5. **Direct comparison to Li et al. is problematic**: Authors claim Li et al.'s leaderboard reports outdated numbers (89% MNIST 0.3, 51% CIFAR-10 2/255), but the current leaderboard (94.02% and 68.2%) exceeds these. Additionally, Li et al.'s benchmark is more comprehensive in number of defenses, models, and perturbation types (R1).
6. **Some findings are expected or already established**: Prior work already established that robust training increases local smoothness and sample complexity/overfitting. The paper’s claims about shared mistakes (intrinsic difficulty score) are already captured by the certified radius metric proposed in works like ACERT (R1, R3). The fragmentation experiments lack a clear explanation of why they matter (R2).
7. **Presentation issues**:
   - Table 1 is hard to parse due to dense numbers; accuracy-robustness plots would help (R3).
   - The definition of certified accuracy (line 133) is incorrect: should be “portion of correctly classified samples that are certifiably robust” (R4).
   - “Adversarial accuracy” (line 135) is not well-defined; it should be clarified as empirical robust accuracy serving as an upper bound (R4).
   - The “NP-complete” claim (line 321) lacks a citation (R4).
8. **Concerns about relevance and sustainability**:
   - Interest in certified training has declined over the past two years; practical deployment for large-scale or generative models is unclear (R4, general comment). Sustainability of the benchmark is also questionable, as other leaderboards (e.g., RobustBench) already exist for adversarial training (R2).
   - The paper’s claims of “state-of-the-art” may be overstated given the narrow scope and lack of full tradeoff evaluation (R3, R4).
