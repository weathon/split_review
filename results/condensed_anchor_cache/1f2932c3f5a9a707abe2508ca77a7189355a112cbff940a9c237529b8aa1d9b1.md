## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes GEAM, a molecular generative framework for drug discovery comprising three modules: goal-aware fragment extraction (via Fragment-wise Graph Information Bottleneck, FGIB), fragment assembly (using Soft Actor-Critic, SAC), and fragment modification (using a genetic algorithm, GA). The framework dynamically updates the fragment vocabulary with new fragments discovered during generation. Experiments show strong performance on molecular optimization benchmarks, with ablation studies confirming the importance of each module.

### Strengths
- The research addresses the important and cutting-edge field of drug discovery.
- The proposed method is well-motivated and simple yet effective, with clear theoretical analysis.
- The paper is well-written, providing good intuition for each module and justifying design choices.
- The related work treatment is comprehensive and situates the research in context.
- The experiments are comprehensive and detailed, providing strong evidence of efficacy.
- Ablation studies highlight the importance of each module, especially the role of goal-driven fragment selection.
- The fragment extraction module using FGIB is a robust strategy for constructing a goal-aware vocabulary, prioritizing fragments relevant to target properties.
- The strategy of using molecules generated by the modification module to expand the fragment vocabulary bolsters exploration.
- The generative algorithms (SAC, GA) not only generate novel molecules but also feed FGIB to extract new fragment instances for vocabulary expansion.

### Weaknesses
- Novelty concern: Reviewer 3 considers the method largely a pipeline combining existing techniques (information bottleneck, SAC, GA) without sufficient adaptation detail. In contrast, Reviewer 2 viewed the framework as innovative and robust.
- Insufficient novelty delineation: The paper does not clearly articulate how the assembly module differs from existing fragment-based assembly methods, or how the modification module's approach to generating novel fragments is distinct from other generative models (Reviewer 2).
- Implementation details missing: The specific adaptations of information bottleneck for fragment extraction (how relevant information is defined and extracted from molecular graphs), SAC for fragment assembly (reward function, action space, state representation), and GA for fragment modification (crossover and mutation operations on fragments) are not sufficiently described (Reviewer 3).
- Lack of discussion of challenges: The paper does not thoroughly discuss the specific challenges of using IB, SAC, and GA in molecule generation tasks, nor how these methods are adapted to work together (Reviewer 3).
- Unclear advantage of FGIB over existing GIB-based substructure identification: The paper does not clearly describe why the fragment-based approach is superior to methods identifying arbitrary relevant substructures (Reviewer 2). Also, the intuition behind the loss in Equation 5 is unclear—specifically, how minimizing the first term (mutual information between fragment representation and target property) leads to selecting relevant and non-redundant fragments rather than simply selecting many fragments (Reviewer 2).
- Potential issue with dynamic vocabulary update: Reviewer 1 questions whether adding fragments generated by the model to the vocabulary could introduce illusion or false information, and suggests a section verifying the reliability and authenticity of generated snippets. This may explain why the method does not show consistent superiority across all metrics (Reviewer 1).
- Inconsistent superiority: The method does not achieve the best results on some datasets or indicators (Reviewer 1).
- Reviewer 2 asks for clarification on how QED and SA are computed for each molecule in the training set (Equation 11), and why some references cite arXiv versions instead of peer-reviewed versions.
