## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates incorporating error-correction data (erroneous steps immediately followed by corrections) into the pretraining phase for math reasoning, using the synthetic i-GSM dataset. The authors show that pretraining with such data improves reasoning accuracy compared to pretraining on equal amounts of error-free data, even under greedy decoding. They explore data preparation, comparison to beam search, masking of errors, required error rate, and whether fine-tuning suffices.

### Strengths
- The problem is interesting and underexplored: teaching self-correction during pretraining rather than relying on multi-round prompting (R1, R3). This offers a fresh perspective.
- The paper is very well written, with clear organization and condensed takeaway messages (R1). Experiments and ablations are well-designed, rigorous, and address clearly stated research questions in a logical sequence (R1, R2, R4). The data-centric controlled setup is a strength (R2).
- Interesting specific findings: even when the model is pretrained on retry data with a high error rate, it does not tend to produce erroneous steps (R4). The retry-upon-regret approach and comparisons of masking vs. not masking errors are useful (R1, R3).
- The study compares several settings: retry upon regret, masking, pretraining vs. fine-tuning with retry data (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited generalizability of the synthetic dataset.** i-GSM uses only one type of error (inserting a wrong parameter that cannot be computed next, e.g., a “skip step” error) and a single reasoning pattern (finding the next node in a DAG). Real-world reasoning involves diverse errors (calculation errors, context misunderstanding, ambiguity) and reasoning types (comparison, logical deduction, commonsense). Three reviewers (R1, R2, R4) doubt whether results extend beyond this synthetic setting. R1 notes that the model’s apparent error detection ability (L259–260) may be due to task-specific encoding, unlikely to generalize to other error types. R2 asks for discussion on how the method could scale to different errors in non-synthetic data. R4 notes the evaluation is limited to a single synthetic dataset, raising concerns about practical impact. R3 adds that the justification for using synthetic data is unclear.
- **Unclear generalization across models.** Only one model is tested; it is unknown if the techniques transfer to other architectures (e.g., LLAMA, Gemma, Mixtral) (R3). The paper also lacks comparisons to existing methods (R3).
- **Previous work overlap.** A prior work [1] has already shown that error-correction data enables higher reasoning accuracy compared to error-free data, potentially reducing the novelty of the contribution (R4).
- **Limited fine-tuning analysis.** The fine-tuning experiment uses only LoRA. Full fine-tuning with a fraction of the pretraining data is not tested. The authors argue cost parity only when full fine-tuning matches pretraining size (L484–485), but using a smaller fine-tuning dataset may change results (R1).
- **Insufficient inference-time analysis.** Most experiments use greedy decoding or occasional beam search. How do methods behave with sampling, different temperatures, consensus voting, or additional inference-time compute? For models trained with retry data, is it better to produce longer chains with higher accuracy or shorter chains with lower accuracy given a fixed computation budget? (R1, R2). Specifically, R2 asks about inference-time scaling properties.
- **Writing clarity and paper structure.** One reviewer (R3) finds the writing and structure unclear and hard to follow: experimental settings and conclusion sections are not clearly delineated, tables could be simplified, and plots would improve readability. (Note: R1 praised the writing, so this is a minority view but must be preserved.)
- **Clarifications needed.** In Section 3.1, the claim that the model “knows can_next(A)=false” is based on a linear classifier on hidden states; it is unclear whether the classifier or the model itself possesses that knowledge (R4). Insights on why “retry miss data” does not improve accuracy much are requested (R4). Additionally, the authors are asked whether code and data will be open-sourced (R4).
