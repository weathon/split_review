## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes using LLMs to incorporate low-frequency factors in medical cohort analysis, focusing on premature rupture of membranes (PROM) risk prediction. It introduces factor-value pair representations, a factor-based interaction map, and both manual and automatic prompt generation. Models (LLaMA3.1, MedAlpaca) are trained on a new dataset with 70 factors, achieving 79% accuracy (78 factors) and 96% accuracy (40 factors). All three reviewers scored the paper 3/5. One reviewer found the contribution stronger (contribution score 3) and praised originality and significance, while the other two were more critical of soundness and presentation (both contribution 2, presentation 1). The majority concerns center on poor writing, missing details, and insufficient validation.

### Strengths

- The overall plan (though unclear) is reasonable and timely (Reviewer 1).
- The study uses LLMs for a novel application: identifying and incorporating low-frequency factors into medical cohort analysis (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3).
- Adopts solid theoretical frameworks, such as an epidemiological perspective (Reviewer 1).
- Includes a human/user study (Reviewer 1).
- Used existing biomedical models in experiments (e.g., LLaMA3.1, MedAlpaca) (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
- Collected a new dataset for the experiments, though details are missing (Reviewer 2).
- Introduces manual and automatic prompt generation techniques, plus a factor-based interaction map (Reviewer 3).
- Focus on low-frequency factors is original and could significantly improve risk prediction in healthcare (Reviewer 3; contribution score 3).
- The paper has partial technical depth and is primarily clear in describing prompt generation and the interaction map (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses

- **Presentation and soundness are poor.** The paper is convoluted, lacks flow, and contains numerous formatting errors and typos (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3). Specific issues include:
  - Paragraph related to Equation 4 is duplicated (Reviewer 1).
  - Left/right vs. top/bottom confusion in Figure 1 description (Reviewer 1).
  - Section 3.2 is very hard to understand; its connection to 3.3 is unclear (Reviewer 1).
  - Line 275 has a separate line fragment (Reviewer 2).
  - Spelling error: “superviesd” at line 402 (Reviewer 2).
  - Figure 2: text overlaps with bars; “predication” should be “prediction” (Reviewer 2).
  - Line 486 appears to be a section heading but is formatted incorrectly (Reviewer 2).
  - Line 522 contains multiple garbled links/text (Reviewer 2).
  - The language feels like it has been written/corrected by an LLM (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing or inadequate contextualization:**
  - No related work or discussion of PROM; it is only fully mentioned in the abstract (Reviewer 2). The PROM use case is not explained well, and its contribution to the ICLR community is unclear – generalization of knowledge is needed (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
  - Line 151: why cite the SHAP paper? Relevance is not motivated (Reviewer 2).
  - The “husband education level” example for a biomedical LLM is confusing; its relevance to the tasks is not justified (Reviewer 2).
- **Insufficient comparison and baselines:**
  - No comparison with traditional ML models (e.g., logistic regression, random forest), which are often preferred in healthcare for interpretability (Reviewer 3 – 3 times, e.g., first and third weakness lists).
  - Baseline comparisons are only with LLMs; a broader set is needed (Reviewer 3).
  - The reported accuracy (79% with 78 factors, 96% with 40 factors) lacks context – no comparison to benchmarks, no clinical significance, no confidence intervals or error bars (Reviewer 3).
- **Evaluation limitations:**
  - Only accuracy is reported; other critical metrics like AUC are missing (Reviewer 3).
  - No error bars or confidence intervals, making reliability hard to assess, especially for medical applications (Reviewer 3).
- **Model and method justification:**
  - Why the factor-based interaction map was chosen and how it improves performance is not explained; no discussion of scalability, overfitting, or managing complexity as more factors are added (Reviewer 3).
  - The models used (LLaMA3.1, MedAlpaca) are outdated without clear justification; modern benchmarks are not referenced (Reviewer 3).
  - The choice of 78 factors is not explained; no rationale for selection or necessity (Reviewer 3).
  - The manual prompt engineering process is impractical for large-scale or dynamic settings; its lack of generalizability across conditions is not discussed (Reviewer 3).
  - The automatic prompt generation pipeline lacks discussion of potential errors, biases, or the risk of generating irrelevant content (Reviewer 3).
- **Overfitting and generalization concerns:**
  - Overfitting is a risk with the factor-based interaction map, but no mitigation strategies (e.g., regularization) are discussed (Reviewer 3).
  - Cohort-specific prompts may overfit to the dataset and fail to generalize to new cohorts; adaptability is not addressed (Reviewer 3).
  - Only a single dataset is used, restricting generalizability; testing on diverse datasets is needed (Reviewer 3 – also repeatedly).
- **Computational and feasibility issues:**
  - Computational cost and feasibility of automatic prompt generation for clinical use are not discussed; no time or hardware requirements given (Reviewer 3).
  - Fine-tuning large models is resource-heavy and not quantified; practicality in clinical environments is unclear (Reviewer 3).
- **Ethics, bias, and fairness:**
  - Ethical concerns around cohort-specific medical data are barely discussed; privacy risks and potential biases in using LLMs on such data are not addressed (Reviewer 3).
  - Data bias and fairness are not considered; the model may propagate existing biases leading to unequal outcomes (Reviewer 3).
- **Overclaimed contributions:**
  - The conclusion overstates the method's applicability, claiming it provides a universal solution despite only incremental improvements; broader relevance is not demonstrated (Reviewer 3).
- **Clarity and terminology:**
  - The term “numerical interpretable evidence” is unclear (Reviewer 3).
  - The introduction states that traditional studies overlook most factors but does not elaborate on why adding so many factors is necessary or how it leads to better outcomes (Reviewer 3).
- **Questions that overlap with criticisms (requested experiments, ablations, clarifications):**
  - How is function v in Eq 4 (or 5) implemented in practice? It seems to play a key role (Reviewer 1).
  - How is the expert-augmented part implemented? Are experts sourced from human participants? What does this mean for general application? (Reviewer 1).
  - Why is the “husband education level” relevant to the medical task? (Reviewer 2 – also a weakness).
  - Why add so many factors? How does this lead to better outcomes? (Reviewer 3 – equivalent to weakness in introduction).
  - Why was the factor-based interaction map chosen? How does it improve performance? (Reviewer 3).
  - Why 78 factors? How were they selected and deemed necessary? (Reviewer 3).
  - Why use outdated models like LLaMA3.1 and MedAlpaca without justification? (Reviewer 3).
  - No error bars or confidence intervals – how robust are the findings? (Reviewer 3).
