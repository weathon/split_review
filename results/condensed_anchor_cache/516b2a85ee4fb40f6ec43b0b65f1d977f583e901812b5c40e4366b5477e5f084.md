## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes FedGuCci(+) to improve federated learning generalization by enforcing linear mode connectivity (LMC) among local models using anchor models. All reviewers acknowledge the novelty of adapting LMC to FL and find the idea intuitive. Two reviewers (R1, R2) rate the paper 3, citing significant theoretical ambiguities, insufficient baseline comparisons, missing convergence guarantees, and evaluation gaps; one reviewer (R3) rates it 6, praising the theoretical foundations and creativity while raising concerns about communication/computation overhead in large-scale settings. There is clear disagreement: R3 considers the theoretical analysis sound, whereas R1 finds Lemma 3.3 and Theorems 3.5/3.8 unclear or vacuous.

### Strengths
- **Novel adaptation of LMC to FL** – The group-connectivity perspective is creative and underexplored in FL (R1, R2, R3).  
- **Well-structured and easy to follow** – The paper clearly presents the intuition and motivation (R1, R3).  
- **Promising experimental results** – FedGuCci and FedGuCci+ outperform FedAvg and several baselines across multiple CV/NLP datasets and architectures (R1).  
- **Ablation studies** – Provided to show robustness and lower computation cost to target accuracy (R1).  
- **Sound theoretical foundations** – R3 considers the proofs and theoretical framing credible; however, R1 raises serious concerns about the clarity and validity of Lemma 3.3 and Theorems 3.5/3.8 (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical clarity issues (R1):**  
  - Lemma 3.3: The randomness is unclear – is the probability over a uniform distribution of \(w\) or deterministic \(w^*_{anc}\)? The bound is vacuous unless \(d_\epsilon < d\) is justified.  
  - Theorems 3.5/3.8: Why is randomness needed when the assumptions already guarantee LMC between each model and the anchor? Dependence on \(d\) is missing from the bounds, and the bound on \(d_\epsilon\) must be shown non-vacuous.  
- **No convergence guarantees (R2):** FedGuCci(+) lacks theoretical convergence analysis, while many compared methods (e.g., FedProx, SCAFFOLD, FedDyn) provide such guarantees.  
- **Inconsistent or insufficient experimental comparisons:**  
  - FedProx appears poorly tuned (performance never matches or exceeds FedAvg, even when \(\mu \to 0\) should recover FedAvg) (R1).  
  - SCAFFOLD’s performance on Tiny ImageNet (Non-IID 100, Table 2) and FedDyn’s on STS-B (Table 3) are inconsistent with prior results (R1).  
  - FedDyn and SCAFFOLD are missing from Tables 4 and 5 (R1).  
  - No comparisons with communication-efficient baselines (e.g., DisPFL, SSFL) that achieve strong performance at high sparsity (R3).  
- **Evaluation gaps:**  
  - Missing test accuracy vs. rounds plots and hyperparameter tuning details (R1).  
  - Figure 4 (group connectivity vs. K) uses too small a client range – the claim that barriers converge lower than vanilla is not well supported; interpretation for larger K needed (R2).  
  - Ablation studies for FedGuCci+ are needed to isolate the contribution of each technique (R2); also requested by R1.  
- **Communication and computation overhead (R3):**  
  - Although no extra communication rounds vs. FedAvg, the method still incurs FedAvg’s full communication cost; no pruning/compression strategies are considered.  
  - Computational overhead from the connectivity loss may limit practical large-scale use.  
- **Practical implementation questions (R1, R3):**  
  - How is the integral over \(\alpha\) in the connectivity loss (Eq. 7) approximated in practice? (R1)  
  - Storage and scalability challenges for very large models (e.g., GPT-3) and very large client counts (>500) are unaddressed (R3).  
  - Behavior in low-bandwidth, high-latency environments is not discussed (R3).  
- **Typos (R1):** Line 95 (“refer to each client’s distribution \(D_i\)”) and Line 113 (should be \(D^t\) not \(D\)).
