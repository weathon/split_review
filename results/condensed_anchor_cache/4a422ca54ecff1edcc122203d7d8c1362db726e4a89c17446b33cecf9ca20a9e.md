## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces FIRM (Focused In-distribution Representation Modeling), a multi-positive contrastive loss for anomaly detection that encourages compact in-distribution (ID) representations while separating synthetic outliers (generated via rotations or Outlier Exposure). Reviewers find the idea interesting and the method straightforward, with promising results on several benchmarks (CIFAR-10/100, Fashion-MNIST, Cats-vs-Dogs) and extensive ablations. However, multiple reviewers raise major concerns: limited novelty (the loss is nearly identical to UniCON, the score function matches CSI), narrow improvement margins (+1.1% AUROC vs CSI), lack of experiments on real-world datasets (e.g., MVTec AD, VisA, medical), reliance on old baselines, and theoretical gaps.

### Strengths

- Addresses an important problem: learning representations for anomaly detection under class collision and limited training signal (R1, R3, R5).
- The idea of using a multi-positive contrastive loss that compacts ID representations while allowing synthetic outliers to be diverse is interesting and novel in this context (R2, R3, R5).
- Method is straightforward and shows promising results on several benchmarks (R1, R5).
- Extensive experiments with multiple scoring functions, ablations against NT-Xent and SupCon, and ablation studies that dissect component contributions (R2, R3). Reviewers appreciate the rigor and hard work (R3).
- Clear intuition and well-supported with figures (R4).

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty concerns:** The objective is essentially identical to L_UniCLR from UniCON, and the anomaly score matches CSI. UniCON already extended this approach to incorporate outlier exposure, so the contribution is unclear (R4). Two other reviewers also question novelty (R1: minor label modification; R5: concerns about novelty). R1 and R5 acknowledge the idea but find the technical advance limited.
- **Narrow improvement margin:** Table 1 shows FIRM improves AUROC by only 1.1% points over CSI (s_ens). Reviewers ask why the gain is so small and whether it is statistically meaningful (R2). Without OE, FIRM fails to outperform CSI on near-distribution OOD detection (CIFAR-100, ImageNet) (R2).
- **Missing large-scale/real-world datasets:** All experiments use small images (32×32 pixels); it is unclear if cats-vs-dogs is also resized. Practical scenarios need larger images (≥128×128). No experiments on industrial anomaly detection (MVTec AD, VisA) or medical datasets, making universality claims unvalidated (R1, R2, R3). R2 and R3 explicitly request MVTec AD, VisA, and ImageNet-30.
- **Unfair Outlier Exposure (OE) setting:** Using OE on CIFAR-10/100 is unfair because OE performs well when its distribution is close to ID (e.g., see RODEO, ICML 2024). The strong results with OE likely reflect the OE benefit rather than FIRM (R2).
- **No evidence on false positive rate:** If normal samples lack representational invariance, false positive detection of normal samples as anomalies should be high, but no analysis is provided. Reviewer 2 requests FPR @ TPR 95% comparisons (R2).
- **Theoretical justification lacking:** No rigorous theoretical analysis why the multi-positive objective is optimal for anomaly detection (R1, R3).
- **Limited augmentation analysis and potential artifacts:** Only rotations are used for synthetic outliers; other semantically meaningful augmentations are not tested (R3). The ensemble scoring method (rotations + random crops) may introduce artifacts—e.g., rotating a car creates an anomaly, random cropping might remove anomaly features. Reviewers ask if the method was tested for generality across augmentations (R3).
- **Old baselines:** The paper compares against older methods (e.g., CSI). Missing 2023-2024 baselines related to OE, such as "Learning to Augment Distributions for Out-of-distribution Detection" (NeurIPS 2022) and DreamOOD ("Dream the impossible: Outlier imagination with diffusion models", NeurIPS 2023) which also generates fake OOD data (R5).
- **Loss landscape visualization unclear:** Figure 1 is not sufficiently clear regarding class collision and representation compactness; a t-SNE comparison against CSI would provide concrete evidence (R3).
