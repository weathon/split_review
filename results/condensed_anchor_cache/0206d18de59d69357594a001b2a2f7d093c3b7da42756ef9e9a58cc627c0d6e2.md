## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper empirically studies weak-to-strong generalization for trustworthiness (fairness, adversarial & OOD robustness, privacy). The authors propose two training strategies – Weak Trustworthiness Finetuning (Weak TFT) and Weak+WTS TFT – where trustworthiness regularization is applied to the weak model only or to both weak and strong models. Experiments on Adult, OOD Style Transfer, AdvGLUE++, and Enron show that some properties (fairness, robustness) transfer well only when both models are regularized, while privacy does not. There is disagreement among reviewers: two find the methods novel and the paper well-organized, while the other two argue the work is a benchmark of existing ideas with limited technical novelty.

### Strengths
• The problem of transferring trustworthiness via weak-to-strong generalization is important and under-explored in prior work. (R1, R2, R3)  
• The paper covers four distinct aspects of trustworthiness (fairness, adversarial robustness, OOD robustness, privacy). (R4)  
• The related works section is comprehensive. (R2)  
• The experiments are relatively thorough for the chosen settings and include a parameter sensitivity study. (R1, R4)  
• The paper is clearly written, well-organized, and its conclusions match the claims. (R1, R2, R3)  
• Some reviewers (R2) consider the methods (Weak TFT, Weak+WTS TFT) novel contributions. However, other reviewers (R3, R4) argue there is no new methodology—only a benchmark of three training strategies.

### Weaknesses
• The motivation for weak-to-strong transfer is questionable. The scenario is only meaningful when labeled data is small and an unlabeled set is large, or when practical/regulatory constraints prevent direct training on original data. The paper does not consider such settings or discuss why a strong model cannot be trained directly. (R3, R4)  
• The assumption that curated trustworthiness datasets are available would make the weak-to-strong problem secondary (e.g., if demographic attributes and a parity definition are already known). (R1)  
• Limited experimental scope: only one dataset per trustworthiness task, only one definition of fairness (Demographic Parity), and small model sizes (need results on 7B models). (R1, R2)  
• Missing generalization across fairness definitions (e.g., Equalized Odds, Predictive Parity). (R1)  
• Lack of technical novelty: the paper benchmarks three training strategies without introducing new methodology. (R3, R4) — note: a minority reviewer (R2) disagrees and sees the methods as novel.  
• Does not explore using multiple weak models (e.g., trained on different data subsets or with different regularizations) to provide a more robust training signal for the strong model. (R3)  
• Mismatch between the motivation in the rebuttal (cross-institutional collaboration, multiple hospitals) and the actual setup (single weak model), undermining practical relevance. (R3)  
• No in-depth analysis of why privacy does not exhibit signs of weak-to-strong trustworthiness. (R1)  
• Missing details and necessary explanations:  
  – Unclear whether the trustworthiness regularizer in Weak+WTS TFT uses the weak model’s outputs. (R1)  
  – Insufficient explanation of how the Enron dataset is used. (R1)  
  – Missing baseline: what is the “vanilla” form of each task without any trustworthiness loss? (R1)  
  – Hyperparameter search details are absent (should at least appear in the appendix). (R3)  
  – No connection drawn between the rate of trustworthiness improvement and prior weak-to-strong generalization results. (R3)  
  – Figure 2(d) shows Weak+WTS TFT exceeding the strong ceiling without an explanation for why this occurs. (R4)  
• Minor formatting issue: subfigures in Figure 3 have varying sizes. (R1)
