## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a novel evaluation protocol to assess the turn-taking capabilities of spoken dialogue systems using a supervised model (trained on human-human conversations from Switchboard) as a judge for five turn-taking events. It presents a user study comparing the full-duplex E2E system Moshi and a VAD-based cascade system, revealing that they sometimes do not understand when to speak up, interrupt too aggressively, and rarely backchannel. It further benchmarks open-source and proprietary audio FMs (SALMONN, Qwen2-audio-instruct, Qwen-audiochat, Whisper+GPT-4o) on curated test sets from Switchboard, showing significant room for improvement. The evaluation platform will be open-sourced.

### Strengths
- The evaluation protocol is novel, well-motivated, and addresses an important gap in assessing turn-taking dynamics of audio FMs (R1, R5).
- The definition of turn-taking events is detailed and clear (R2).
- The protocol provides comprehensive, well-designed metrics and a fine-grained timing assessment, moving beyond corpus-level statistics to granular evaluation of event timing (R4).
- The user study reveals noteworthy and actionable insights: current systems do not know when to speak up, interrupt too aggressively, and rarely backchannel (R1, R2, R4, R5).
- The paper is well-written with sufficient experimental details provided in the appendix (R1).
- The proposed protocol and its evaluation platform will be open-sourced (R1, R3).
- The work provides valuable references and support for future development of conversational AI systems (R2, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Scalability and applicability are limited**: The protocol requires a supervised dataset (e.g., Switchboard) to train the judge model, making it infeasible for languages like Chinese where such datasets are unavailable (R1). The need for trained turn-taking prediction models limits scalability and applicability to other domains (R5).
- **Heuristic backchannel detection**: The filler word set for backchannel detection is heuristic and may miss backchannel cases not included in the set (R1).
- **Limited model coverage**: Only a few open-source and proprietary audio FMs were tested; GPT-4o was notably not evaluated (R2, R4). The paper lacks a comprehensive performance evaluation and summary across models (R2).
- **Arbitrary thresholds**: Thresholds in Sec. 4.4–4.8 are set to arbitrary values without clear justification; units are inconsistent (e.g., threshold_3 = –0.45, threshold_2 = 0.1) (R3).
- **Biased user study participants**: The participants described in Sec. 3 consist of the authors and related individuals, introducing potential bias (R3).
- **Unexplained high confidence in evaluations**: Fig. 3(b) and (e) show high confidence in some evaluations, but no explanation is provided for why confidence is high (R3).
- **Judge model reliability is questionable**: The conclusion that Moshi is “too aggressive” lacks persuasiveness if based solely on comparisons with the judge model (R3). There is low agreement between majority judge labels and human judgments (most below 80%), weakening the claim of high consistency with human decisions (R4). Metric (E) judge labels show low consistency with human relevance judgments, indicating limited reliability for evaluating handling of user interruptions (R4). The paper lacks transparency on the judge model’s training data, architecture, and performance evaluation beyond reported human-judge agreement (R4).
- **Insufficient comparison with prior work**: The paper does not thoroughly compare its protocol to previous turn-taking evaluation approaches like Ekstedt and Skantze (2022) (R5). It is unclear why prior metrics are impractical for audio FMs or how the new protocol differs (R5).
- **Generalizability unverified**: The judge model was evaluated on an out-of-domain task-oriented corpus, but its generalizability to other non-task-oriented spoken dialogue datasets has not been assessed (R5).
