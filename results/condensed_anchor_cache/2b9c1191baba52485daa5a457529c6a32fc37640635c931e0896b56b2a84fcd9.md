## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a simple pretraining method for data-efficient offline reinforcement learning. It pre-trains a shared Q-network to predict next state features (forward dynamics) via supervised regression, then initializes standard offline RL training with that Q-network. The method is evaluated on D4RL and Robomimic benchmarks with multiple offline RL algorithms. The authors also provide mathematical justification and analysis for why predictive pretraining helps.

### Strengths
- **Simplicity and effectiveness**: The approach is straightforward—regression on future states—and is plug-and-play, easily integrating into most offline/online RL methods (R1, R2, R3).
- **Extensive experimental evaluation**: Comprehensive experiments across multiple environments (D4RL, Robomimic) and multiple offline RL baselines, with ablations (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- **Strong quantitative results**: Consistent improvement over baselines across various data qualities and data distributions, with a clear margin (R2).
- **Attempt at theoretical analysis**: The paper tries to explain why predictive pretraining leads to better downstream Q-learning (R4).

### Weaknesses
Four reviewers split: R1 and R2 (scores 6) find the approach simple and effective; R3 and R4 (scores 5) raise stronger concerns about novelty, missing comparisons, and analysis clarity.

- **Novelty concerns**: The method is very similar to prior works that predict future state information for RL (Schwarzer et al. 2020, 2021; Guo et al. 2018; Hafner et al. 2024). Architectures like TD-MPC and JOWA also share a backbone for dynamics and value functions, and JOWA pre-trains using a dynamics loss. The primary claimed difference is the offline RL setting; R2 and R4 ask what other key distinctions exist. R3 states the approach is overly simplistic and lacks novelty.
- **Missing comparisons**: No comparisons with offline model‑based RL algorithms (e.g., MoRel, MOPO, COMBO, RAMBO) that also aim for data efficiency and learn dynamics; R3 recommends including at least two. Extracting AWAC and CQL results from the TD3+BC paper is inappropriate—those numbers are substantially lower than from a reputable offline RL library (R3). R1’s question about the trade‑offs of learning dynamics inside the Q‑function versus a separate model reinforces this gap.
- **Weak theoretical analysis**: The logic chain (predictive pretraining → high‑rank feature space → smaller error between $C(H_\phi)$ and $Q^\pi$ → smaller error between $Q^\pi$ and $Q_{\phi,\theta}$) is unclear. No theoretical proof is given for why higher rank leads to smaller error (R4). The relationship between rank and matrix infinity norm in the paper is unclear (R3). The analysis is largely qualitative and occupies disproportionate space without being informative (R3). Equation 5’s role in the final implication needs explicit derivation (R4). The claim that “higher rank is more likely to reduce error” (line 257) is unsupported (R3).
- **Presentation issues**: Writing contains awkward sentences and inconsistent active/passive voice (R1). Table 2 omits some baseline numbers (e.g., Expert IQL) (R2). The reference to Q‑former as “supervised learning domain” is inaccurate (Q‑former uses TD‑learning) (R3). The definition of “pretraining ratios” (Section 5.1, line 362) is ambiguous—is it the proportion of data used for pretraining or the fraction of total training steps? (R1, R4).
- **Unaddressed reviewer questions (overlap with missing ablations/criticisms)**:
  - Whether the encoder is fixed after pretraining, and which variant performs better (R4).
  - Why increasing data amount sometimes decreases performance (Figure 8 SMM) (R3).
  - The paper is not the first to study data efficiency in offline RL; other works (refs 9,10,11) also experiment with downsampling (R3).
