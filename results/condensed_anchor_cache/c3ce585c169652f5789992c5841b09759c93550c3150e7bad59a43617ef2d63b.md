## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper reexamines complex query answering (CQA) benchmarks, arguing that most queries in existing datasets (up to 98% for some types) can be reduced to simpler problems like link prediction by exploiting training graph triples. It classifies query-answer pairs into partial-inference (reducible) and full-inference (truly multi-hop), shows that state-of-the-art models perform significantly worse on full-inference queries, and proposes a new benchmark of irreducible queries. The paper also introduces a hybrid solver (CQD-hybrid) that explicitly retrieves training edges. Reviewers note that while the bias analysis is interesting, the paper’s query type scope is limited (only BetaE query types, no negation or cycles), key baselines (QTO, FIT) are missing, and the proposed method is nearly identical to QTO.

### Strengths

- The paper conducts an in-depth study of existing CQA benchmarks, revealing systematic biases such as the prevalence of tree-shaped queries and union operators (R1, R2).
- The observation that partial-inference pairs dominate and that full-inference pairs are empirically much harder is interesting, suggesting that current models’ reasoning ability may be overestimated (R2, R3).
- The case study on 2u queries, where filtering out reducible pairs makes performance comparable to 1p, is a valuable analysis (R2).
- Motivation is novel, experiments support conclusions, and the paper is well written and easy to follow (R3, R4).

### Weaknesses

1. **Limited query type scope and datasets.** The paper only covers query types from BetaE [1], excluding negative queries, cyclic queries, and multi-answer variables found in newer benchmarks (e.g., FIT [2], EFO_k). This constrains the generality of conclusions (R1, R2, R3). Temporal CQA datasets are also not discussed (R1).

2. **Missing essential baselines.** The paper does not compare against symbolic search methods like QTO (ICML’23) and FIT, which use neural link predictors with search algorithms and may naturally handle full-inference queries. Given that the paper itself proposes a search-based method, omission weakens the claim that SOTA models fail on full-inference queries (R1, R2, R3).

3. **CQD-hybrid is not novel.** Multiple reviewers note that CQD-hybrid is fundamentally identical to QTO, which also memorizes training edges and normalizes link predictor scores. Differences should be cited and discussed; the paper wrongly presents it as the first hybrid solver (R1, R3, R4).

4. **Dubious 2u filtering.** Filtering 2u queries to require both links missing changes the nature of union queries, since a union requires only one link to hold. The filtered query more resembles a 2i query; reported gains may not reflect true performance on union queries (R2).

5. **New benchmark distribution issues.** One reviewer argues that the benchmark only focuses on full-inference pairs (S_X,III), ignoring partial-inference pairs (S_X,II) arising from the train–test graph split. This fails to evaluate models on logical connectives and variables in S_X,II. Additionally, the benchmark stresses link prediction on missing links, creating a shortcut: improving link prediction alone could close the gap, deviating from measuring multi-hop reasoning (R1).

6. **Imprecise terminology.** The phrase “reduced to easier types” is misleading; e.g., 3i queries do not always outperform 2i queries. More precise language is needed (R3).

7. **Incomplete dataset presentation.** BetaE uses three knowledge graphs but only two are presented in the paper (R3). Also, a typo exists: line 468 says “50.000” instead of “50,000” (R4).

8. **Lack of discussion of related work and inductive settings.** The paper does not differentiate adequately from existing inductive CQA works (NeurIPS’22, IJCAI’22) that also address information leakage by separating training and test graphs (R4).

9. **Unexplained degradation of embedding-based methods.** A reviewer questions why embedding-based methods (which do not use training graph triples except for 1p queries) also degrade on the new benchmark, contradicting the paper’s leakage-based argument (R4).

10. **Overstated contribution.** Scores on previous benchmarks are far from saturated (average <50%). Optimizing on old benchmarks would also improve hard benchmark performance. Recognizing training edge importance is already done in QTO and FIT (R1).
