## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper systematically investigates prompting strategies for code generation with large language models (LLMs), focusing on multi-turn interactions and computational trade-offs. It decomposes chain-of-thought (CoT) into reasoning, instruction, and feedback prompts and conducts an extensive grid search on the competitive programming benchmarks CodeContests and TACO across multiple LLM families and sizes (Llama 3.0/3.1 8B, 70B, 405B; GPT-4o). The study evaluates single-turn vs. multi-turn settings, the role of CoT components, execution-feedback granularities, and the impact of model size and problem difficulty. It also applies Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT) to internalize multi-turn CoT reasoning, showing that finetuned models improve performance and scalability even without prompting at inference.

### Strengths
- **Comprehensive empirical scope**: The paper brings together a wide range of prompting strategies (CoT, reasoning, instruction, execution feedback) in both single-turn and multi-turn settings for multiple models on challenging, algorithm-heavy code datasets. The grid search is extensive and systematically covers model families (Llama 3.0/3.1, GPT-4o) and sizes (8B, 70B, 405B).
- **Decomposition of CoT prompts**: Splitting CoT into reasoning prompts and instruction prompts is an interesting and informative decomposition that reveals which components work best for different model sizes and problem difficulties. The negative result that reasoning prompts are not additive (Table 6) is valuable and non-obvious.
- **Use of pass n@k metric**: Applying the pass n@k metric ensures fairer comparison between single-turn and multi-turn approaches, accounting for different numbers of generated samples.
- **Thorough analysis of error-feedback granularities**: The paper systematically varies execution-feedback granularity and analyzes its impact, including the trade-off between exploration and exploitation (finer feedback induces exploitative behavior, beneficial for simpler problems but detrimental for complex ones).
- **Fine-tuning on multi-turn CoT data (RFT)**: The use of Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning to internalize multi-turn CoT steps within the LLM is a novel and impactful extension. The approach shows that models can learn to reproduce reasoning benefits without prompting at inference time, with potential for scaling.
- **Clarity and structure**: The paper is well-organized, with clear motivation, a systematic framework (visualized in Figure 1), and appropriate examples for each prompt type. The main text is presented clearly.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty**: There is substantial existing work on CoT and self-repair in code generation. The core contribution is largely an empirical combination of known techniques (CoT strategies, error-feedback, Pass n@k) and prompt engineering. The RFT component is the most novel part, but its presentation lacks detail (see below). This limits the theoretical/methodological advancement.
- **Missing baselines and experimental clarity**:
  1. Fine-tuning baseline: No comparison against fine-tuning on problem-solution pairs without CoT data, so it is unclear whether RFT’s benefits come from internalizing CoT or simply from additional supervised training.
  2. Multi-turn baseline for Table 8: It is not compared against simply resampling from the base model, so the role of execution feedback in multi-turn settings is uncertain.
  3. Clarity on CoT and multi-turn CoT strategies (Tables 1, 2): It is unclear whether these strategies are the same across models and across sample sizes.
  4. Figure 6 explanation: Multi-turn CoT differs from the base model at max_turns=1, but if CoT-Retry does not apply CoT in the first turn, they should be identical. This discrepancy needs explanation.
  5. Figure 4 uses different axis scales across charts, making it difficult to compare trends or claim relative improvements (e.g., 1–2% vs. 30–40% improvements cannot be directly compared without consistent scaling).
- **Arbitrary prompt design and lack of reasoning-specific CoT**: The COT-Retry prompt appears to be an arbitrary combination of prompts. The paper does not include chain-of-thought prompting that specifically reasons about *why* a test failed or how to fix the test, which is a natural gap.
- **Additive reasoning experiments**: In Table 6, when combining reasoning prompts, it is unclear what happens if each sample uses a different CoT prompt rather than combining them linearly. This would better test diversity.
- **Limited theoretical analysis**: The paper provides solid empirical results but offers little theoretical explanation for *why* certain prompting strategies work better than others. There is no principled categorization of problem types that might inform which CoT variant to use, nor does the paper explore whether a task-analysis model could choose the optimal CoT strategy.
- **Potential overfitting in RFT**: The fine-tuning on multi-turn CoT data is generated from the same benchmarks used for evaluation (CodeContests/TACO). The training process is not clearly described (e.g., dataset size, number of steps), raising concerns about overfitting to the test distribution. The paper does not evaluate on unseen tasks or domains.
- **Dependence on computational resources**: The extensive grid search across prompt combinations, model sizes, and multi-turn runs is resource-intensive. The paper does not propose adaptive or meta-learning techniques to reduce this burden, limiting accessibility for researchers with constrained compute.
- **Limited discussion of prompt failure modes**: While the paper mentions that CoT can degrade performance in multi-turn settings, it lacks a detailed analysis of specific failure patterns (e.g., why certain combinations lead to dead ends or loops, and how to mitigate them).
- **Presentation issues in the appendix**: Several appendix figures are low-resolution (not vector graphics), missing proper captions, and the typesetting leaves large blank pages. The presentation quality of the main text should be maintained throughout.
- **Generalization concerns**: The findings are limited to competitive programming benchmarks (CodeContests, TACO). It is unclear how well they transfer to other code generation tasks (e.g., repository-level coding, debugging), other LLM architectures (e.g., smaller models or domain-specific models), or unseen problem types.
