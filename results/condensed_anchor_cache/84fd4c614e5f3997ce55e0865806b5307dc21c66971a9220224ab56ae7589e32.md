## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes RoboFlamingo, a framework that adapts the open-source VLM OpenFlamingo to robot manipulation by adding an LSTM policy head after the pooled visual-language embedding, freezing the ViT, tokenizer, and self-attention layers, and fine-tuning only the resampler, cross-attention, and policy head on imitation learning data. It achieves state-of-the-art results on the CALVIN benchmark with less data and compute than large closed VLAs like RT-2, and the authors commit to releasing code. Reviewers (scores: 8, 6, 6, 6) agree this is a promising direction for democratizing VLM-based policies, but raise significant concerns about limited evaluation (single simulated environment), missing baselines (co-finetuning, full finetuning, simpler pre-trained representations), incomplete analysis of key design choices (action token vs. separate head, transfer from internet pre-training, open-loop execution), and presentation clarity. One reviewer (score 8) was notably more positive but still identified limitations in real-world transfer and evaluation scope.

### Strengths
- Outperforms prior methods (RT-1, HULC) on the CALVIN benchmark by a large margin (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Requires much less data and compute than large closed VLAs like RT-2, making VLM policies accessible to academic budgets (R1, R2, R4). The focus on open-source models (OpenFlamingo) and parameter-efficient fine-tuning is valuable (R2, R4).
- Decouples perception and policy, enabling flexible open-loop control and strong zero-shot generalization to unseen tasks and environments (R1, R2).
- Ablation studies are helpful and reveal important insights: history-based policy heads (GPT, LSTM) outperform MLPs, vision-language pretraining is critical, larger VLM backbones are especially beneficial in low-data regimes (Table 3), and pretrained cross-attention weights are crucial (Fig 3b) (R2, R3, R4).
- Clear motivation and easy-to-follow presentation overall (R2, R4). The authors commit to open-sourcing code and models (R2, R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited evaluation**: The method is only tested on the single simulated CALVIN benchmark. This is insufficient to validate generalizability to real-world scenarios with variations in object properties, lighting, sensor noise, or to other simulated environments (e.g., IKEA Furniture assembly) (R1, R3, R4). One reviewer (R3) argues the model likely overfits to CALVIN and loses crucial abilities (spatial reasoning, object relation understanding).
- **Missing baselines**: 
  - No comparison to simpler pre-trained visual representations (R3M, VOLTRON, MVP, VC-1) that also leverage web data but at smaller scale (R2, R4).
  - No study of co-fine-tuning (mixing robot data with original VLM data) or full model fine-tuning, which are core claims of RT-2. This leaves the paper's central claim that partial fine-tuning is key without proper support (R2, R4). 
  - No comparison to directly predicting actions as text tokens in the VLM's output vocabulary (as in RT-2) versus the separate action head (R2, R4).
- **Insufficient analysis of transfer from internet pre-training**: The paper does not measure how much internet-scale VLM knowledge transfers to robotics; the ABC→D CALVIN setting is insufficient for this purpose (R2). The effect of catastrophic forgetting of VLM capabilities during fine-tuning is also not studied (R2).
- **Sample efficiency concern**: Less sample efficient than methods leveraging large offline robot datasets (e.g., MCIL) (R1).
- **Presentation and clarity issues**:
  - Figure 2 only illustrates T=1; the temporal dynamics for T>1 (how language instruction is tiled and fused across timesteps) are unclear (R3).
  - Notation in Sections 4.2.1 and 4.2.2 is unwieldy (e.g., overloaded K) (R2).
  - The source of pretrained cross-attention weights is not stated, and the rationale for not loading pretrained perceiver resampler weights is missing (R3).
  - The instruction fine-tuning experiment (Sec 5.4.3) lacks details on which instructions/datasets were used and how this procedure is integrated into the methodology (R3).
  - Open-loop execution (Sec 5.5) lacks details on how it is performed without retraining (R4). 
  - Writing nits and inaccuracies: “models to encoder” → “encode”, “train the policy” → “training”, missing “both”, subject-verb agreement, “Particularlly”, “look into one” → “looks into one”, “We wonder” → “We study”, “single-frame observation” → “observations” (R2). A sentence on page 3 about the policy head not understanding instructions is inaccurate (R3).
- **Missing details on computational requirements**: Required GPU time and hardware specifics for training and evaluation are not reported, despite the paper’s focus on accessibility (R4).
- **Other missing experiments/ablations requested**:
  - What steps are needed to transfer to real robotic systems? How realistic are the CALVIN simulations? (R1)
  - Can offline robotic data be incorporated to improve sample efficiency? (R1)
  - How can the method incorporate other modalities (force, tactile)? (R1)
  - Can RoboFlamingo be integrated into hierarchical frameworks like PaLM-E? (R1)
  - How does computational overhead compare to other VLM-based methods? (R1)
  - For enriched language evaluations, were all methods evaluated on the same randomly sampled GPT-4 instruction set? (R4)
