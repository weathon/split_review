## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes the Iterative Contrastive Unlearning (ICU) framework for decoder-only LLMs. ICU consists of three components: Knowledge Unlearning Induction (maximizing LM loss on forget data), Contrastive Learning Enhancement (minimizing LM loss and KL divergence on KNN-retrieved similar data), and Iterative Unlearning Refinement (dynamic stopping using BERTScore/BLEU). Experiments on subsets of the Pile corpus (15k samples, forget set 128) show ICU outperforming baselines (KUMPR, DPO, KL, LLMU) while preserving generative capabilities. The method is innovative and well-motivated but has several weaknesses in experimental setup, comparisons, and evaluation.

### Strengths
- The proposed ICU framework is innovative, particularly the use of KL-divergence on retrieved analogous data to maintain output distribution and text coherence (Reviewer 3).
- The KNN-retrieved samples and iterative stopping criterion help balance unlearning and retention of original capabilities (Reviewers 1, 2).
- The paper is well-written, clear, and structured; Figure 2 provides a comprehensive overview of the approach (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- Extensive empirical validation including ablation studies demonstrating contributions of each component (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- Parameter sensitivity studies are provided (Reviewer 1).
- The method avoids generating repeated tokens compared to some prior work, preserving model utility (Reviewer 3).
- The ICU framework is easy to adopt and performs well across different model sizes and datasets (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Requirement for similar data contradicts motivation.** ICU still needs a large relevant set for KNN retrieval, which may be unavailable for privacy-related data (Reviewer 1). The setup assumes access to the original training data to construct the analogous set, contradicting the paper’s critique that prior methods require inaccessible training data (Reviewer 3). Representation unlearning approaches that avoid this issue are not discussed (e.g., [1] of Reviewer 1).

2. **Insufficient comparisons and potentially unfair baseline.** The experiments lack comparisons with many prior methods ([1], [2], [3] as cited by Reviewer 2). Larger, more realistic benchmarks like WMDP [4] should be used (Reviewer 2). The comparison with KUMPR is questionable: Table 1 reports epochs 3.2, 2.0, 6.6 for KUMPR, but the original KUMPR paper reports much higher values (11/17.2, 8/13.8, 5.4/10.8 for UL/UL+ in their Table 2), suggesting insufficient training or mismatched setup (Reviewer 3).

3. **Stopping criterion thresholds and hyperparameters poorly justified.** Selection of thresholds `a` and `b` for the stopping criterion is unclear and may require careful tuning per dataset (Reviewer 1). L369: The rationale for threshold values (taken from prior work) is insufficient for reproducibility; the paper should explain why these specific values are appropriate (Reviewer 4). Hyperparameters α and β are not fully ablated—e.g., α=0.1, β=1.0 is absent from Table 2, and setting α=0 barely worsens results, questioning the gain from the forget loss (Reviewer 3).

4. **Missing robustness analysis.** No evaluation against attacks such as paraphrasing or translation; unclear whether the model forgets knowledge or just prompt patterns (Reviewer 1). Robustness to noise in KNN embeddings is not addressed (Reviewer 1, as question).

5. **Contradictions and inconsistencies in evaluation metrics.** L312-313 criticizes prior work for using BERTScore/BLEU as stopping criteria, but ICU itself uses these metrics for that purpose; need clarification (Reviewer 4). Equation 5 incorrectly specifies BERTScore and BLEU as taking only reference sequences; they require both reference and predicted sequences (Reviewer 4). Information entropy is claimed to measure “information content,” but entropy measures uncertainty; a citation for this claim is needed (Reviewer 4). L377: “our method consistently achieves the best or second-best performance …” is incomplete (Reviewer 4). Inconsistent abbreviations (GLMs vs LMs) and formatting issues (e.g., D_{fgt} written as $fgt$, numbers below 10 not spelled out) (Reviewer 4).

6. **Human and GPT evaluation details missing.** Human annotation scale (1–8) is not justified; number of annotators, their backgrounds, annotation instructions, inter-annotator agreement, and how to score gibberish outputs are not provided (Reviewers 3, 4). GPT-4 was prompted with a 0–10 scale while human scores are 1–8, making direct average comparison questionable; statistical correlation measures are needed (Reviewer 4). The degree of human expertise in the expert study is undefined (Reviewer 3).

7. **Cost and practicality not discussed.** The cost of unlearning equals fine-tuning the pre-trained model on both forget and analogous sets; computational overhead for new user requests is not addressed (Reviewer 3). The paper only considers GPT-2; other LM families are not tested (Reviewer 4, as question).

8. **Missing ablations and analyses.**  
   - The relation between losses (3) and (4) is unclear; no ablation using only one loss, and no justification for minimizing KL on learned data but not maximizing it on forgot data (Reviewer 1).  
   - No random generation baseline to validate that metrics improve over random (Reviewer 4).  
   - No explanation why GPT evaluation does not strongly correlate with other metrics (Reviewer 4).  
   - The meaning of “epoch” in Table 1 is unexplained (Reviewer 3).  
   - Other stopping criteria (e.g., ROUGE, NLI) were not considered (Reviewer 4).  
   - Ratio of unlearned to retrieved data is not specified (Reviewer 1).  
   - The case study in Figure 4 does not clearly state what constitutes D_fgt (e.g., “@ThingsExpo 2016?”) (Reviewer 3).
