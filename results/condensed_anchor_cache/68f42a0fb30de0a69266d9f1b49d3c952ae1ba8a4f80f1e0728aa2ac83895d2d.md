## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies the robustness of policies derived from expert demonstrations (inverse reinforcement learning and imitation learning) compared to vanilla deep RL policies in high-dimensional MDPs. It demonstrates that vanilla RL policies trained with the actual reward signal are substantially more robust to adversarial attacks and random perturbations than policies learned from demonstrations. The authors provide theoretical analysis focusing on inverse soft Q-learning with linear Q-function approximators, showing that learned rewards become random for states outside the manifold visited by expert demonstrations. Experiments on Atari games (e.g., Pong, Seaquest, BeamRider, Breakout) empirically confirm the robustness gap.

### Strengths
- **Relevance**: The problem is timely given the current success of RLHF methods and the alignment problem (Reviewer 1, 3).
- **Novelty**: The paper presents a theoretical analysis of the robustness of inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) algorithms toward state perturbations, which, to the reviewers’ knowledge, has not been done before (Reviewer 2).
- **Empirical demonstration**: The experiments clearly show that IRL-based policies suffer a substantially larger drop in returns under both adversarial attacks and random perturbations compared to vanilla RL policies (Reviewer 1, 2).
- **Theoretical insights**: Section 3 provides interesting insights into the shortcomings of linear Q-function approximators in the Inverse Soft Q-Learning setting, specifically that rewards become uncorrelated with states outside the expert manifold (Reviewer 1).
- **Clarity of structure**: Despite many theorems, the paper is well-organized with each theorem’s role in highlighting IRL’s robustness weaknesses clearly outlined, and the experimental analysis is clearly explained (Reviewer 2).  
  *Note*: Reviewer 3 strongly disagrees on clarity (see Weaknesses).
- **Significance**: The results offer valuable knowledge for the broader RL community, providing a consideration when choosing between vanilla RL and IRL from expert demonstrations (Reviewer 2).
- **Attempt at dual support**: The paper attempts to provide both theoretical motivation and empirical evaluation for its claims (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Unfair comparison**: The vanilla RL policy is trained on hundreds of thousands of transitions, whereas the IRL counterpart sees only a few thousand demonstrations. This makes the robustness comparison unfair (Reviewer 1).
- **Restrictive theoretical analysis**: The theoretical part is limited to linear Q-function approximators, and the assumptions for Proposition 3.5 are very strong. A more general direction (e.g., smooth Q-approximators for Lipschitz MDPs) is not explored (Reviewer 1).
- **No proposed solutions**: The paper does not offer any solutions to the lack of robustness, such as better training methods (regularization) or better strategies for collecting demonstrations (Reviewer 1).
- **Ambiguous and unclear language throughout**: Numerous instances of vague or undefined terms make comprehension difficult (Reviewer 3).  
  Examples:  
  - What is meant by the “reinforcement learning manifold”? The connection to the theoretical and empirical analyses is not evident (Reviewer 3).  
  - “With reward” and “without reward” are used inconsistently; it is unclear whether “without reward” refers to imitation/inverse RL or to unsupervised RL where no reward signal is given at all (Reviewer 3).  
  - “That focus on learning via emulating affect” – does this refer to imitation learning? What is “learning from exploration”? (Reviewer 3).  
  - “Our paper is the first to focus on the adversarial vulnerabilities of deep neural policies that can learn without a reward function” – how exactly does the method learn without a reward? (Reviewer 3).
- **Weak connection between theory and central message**: The purpose and significance of Lemma 3.1, Propositions 3.3 and 3.5, and Definition 4.1 are not clearly tied to the paper’s main claims. Their necessity is ambiguous (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing experimental details**:  
  - The policy architecture (e.g., network structure) is not specified; only “deep neural network” is mentioned (Reviewer 3).  
  - The specific IRL and imitation learning algorithms used are not described; the paper claims “state-of-the-art imitation and inverse RL are trained via inverse Q-learning described in Section 2,” but that description is absent. How can both imitation learning and IRL be trained via the same Q-learning algorithm? (Reviewer 3).  
  - Table 1 and Table 2 mention tasks “Pong” and “Seaquest” but results show three different games; it is unclear how the results were derived and how to interpret the tables (Reviewer 3).  
  - Figure 1 does not state which environment(s) are depicted (is it an average of all three games?). The purpose and message of the figure are unclear (Reviewer 3).  
  - The perturbations used in experiments are not clearly described: what are their magnitude and frequency? Are they Gaussian noise added to state vectors or more structured changes? Do they push the agent into out-of-distribution states? (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing distinctions and comparisons**:  
  - The related work section does not clearly delineate how this work differs from or aligns with prior work (Reviewer 3).  
  - The paper does not distinguish between Imitation Learning and Inverse Reinforcement Learning (e.g., IRL can handle suboptimal data) (Reviewer 1).  
  - The paper does not compare against robustness of policies learned from ranking of trajectories, which could be a potential solution (Reviewer 1).  
  - The analysis is limited to Q-learning–based approaches; policy gradient methods are not considered. The paper should specify the exact type of algorithm analyzed (Reviewer 3).  
  - The paper focuses on high-dimensional cases; how do results vary in low-dimensional state representations? (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing implementation details**: The paper does not provide the online evolution of IRL performance under perturbations when the agent can perform X transitions (Reviewer 1). Also, the meaning of φ in the inverse Q-learning objective is not clarified (Reviewer 1).
- **Writing quality**: The paper contains many lengthy sentences that hinder comprehension; shorter, simpler sentences would be beneficial (Reviewer 3).
- **Contrast between reviewers on clarity**: While Reviewer 2 found the paper well-written and clear, Reviewer 3 rated presentation as 1 (poor) and identified the language as a major barrier to understanding. This indicates a substantial disagreement about the paper’s readability.
