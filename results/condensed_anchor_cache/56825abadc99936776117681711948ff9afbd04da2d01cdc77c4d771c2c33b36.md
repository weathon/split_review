## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes LazyBLO, a lazy Hessian evaluation framework for stochastic bilevel optimization (nonconvex upper, strongly convex lower). It reduces HVP computations by using a single Hessian-vector product for multiple hypergradient estimations. Theoretical convergence guarantees are given for both nonconvex and strongly convex cases, and experiments on data hyper-cleaning and deep hyper-representation tasks show computational savings.

### Strengths
1. Addresses an important practical bottleneck—Hessian computation in bilevel optimization—and the lazy strategy for Hessians has not been theoretically analyzed before. (R1, R3, R4)
2. The presentation is smooth, notations are clear, and the proof details appear rigorous. (R4)
3. Codes are provided. (R4)
4. Numerical experiments show significant improvements in wall-clock time and HVP counts. (R1, R2)
5. This is the first work to theoretically establish that multiple Hessian computations per iteration are not necessary for convergence of stochastic bilevel algorithms. (stated in abstract)

### Weaknesses
1. **Theoretical shortcoming: convergence depends on increasing batch sizes.** The convergence rate of LazyBLO requires growing batch sizes, whereas state-of-the-art single-loop methods (e.g., ALSET, SOBA, FSLA, F²SA) do not. (R2)
2. **HVP complexity is worse than non-lazy counterparts.** The HVP complexity in the nonconvex–strongly convex case is \(T = \mathcal{O}(N\epsilon^{-1})\), which is higher than fully single-loop bilevel algorithms, so the lazy strategy offers no theoretical improvement and is even worse. (R2)
3. **Unclear motivation for reducing only HVP, not JVP.** In single-loop methods (the basis of LazyBLO), both HVP and JVP are computed once per iteration. There is no reason to prioritize HVPs; JVP complexity should also be reported. A JVP complexity comparison in Table 1 is missing. (R2; also R1’s question: “Can the author further explain how to reduce the computation of JVPs as mentioned in line 308?”)
4. **Limited novelty.** The lazy strategy is an incremental adaptation of existing methods; the technical novelty is unclear and the proof seems straightforward. (R3, R4) — One reviewer acknowledges it is the first theoretical guarantee for lazy Hessians in bilevel optimization, but another finds the proof straightforward and the approach not very novel.
5. **Convergence issue with gradient estimation variance.** Theorem 5.3 suggests the algorithm struggles when gradient estimation variance is not arbitrarily small; the only fix is to take a large sample size. Previous methods allow error terms to diminish with iteration, which is a notable disadvantage. (R1) Consequently, Corollary 5.4 shows only bare improvement in partial gradient and HVP evaluations. (R1)
6. **Assumption needed for lower bound of \(l^*\).** The analysis (lines 372, 428) may require assuming \(l^*\) is lower bounded. (R1)
7. **Confusing parameter choices in corollaries.** In Corollary 5.4, with constant \(N\) and batch sizes \(\epsilon^{-1}\), why are Hessian evaluations \(\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})\) when \(T = \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})\)? In Corollary 5.7, if batch sizes \(D^{f_y}\) and \(D^{g_{yy}}\) are constant, \(N\) cannot be constant; the result is unclear. (R4)
8. **Same complexity order for nonconvex and strongly convex settings.** Both settings have the same partial gradient complexity in the corollaries, whereas strong convexity normally yields better rates. (R4)
9. **Experimental explanation is insufficient.** Table 2 reports time to “converge” without defining a convergence criterion (e.g., loss change threshold). Table 3 reports number of Hessian computations when reaching the same “training loss”, but that loss value is unspecified. (R4; also R1: “Can we explain the main reason for such a significant improvement in Table 2 since the theory cannot show such an advantage?”)
10. **Missing complexity comparison in Table 1.** The proposed method’s HVP and JVP complexities should be added to Table 1. (R2)
11. **No discussion of Hessian instability.** The paper does not analyze how LazyBLO performs when the Hessian is unstable. (R3)
12. **Questions about theoretical choices:** (a) Given strong convexity of the inner problem, why is challenge 2 still challenging? (b) With \(N=1\), Theorem 5.3 yields the optimal rate of SOBA; how does LazyBLO improve convergence in theory? (c) Please provide a plot with number of Hessian computations on the x-axis for Figure 3. (R1, R4)
