## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces an actionable machine learning framework for inverse classification that provides minimal, explainable modifications to input data to change classification outcomes. It proposes a novel definition of “action fairness,” which ensures that users from different subgroups incur similar costs when altering their outcomes. The method is evaluated on two real-world credit assessment datasets using logistic regression and SVM. Reviewers generally agree that the problem is important and the underlying techniques are interesting, but they raise significant concerns about positioning against existing recourse and counterfactual literature, clarity of problem formulation, justification and novelty of the fairness definition, and the strength of the empirical evaluation.

### Strengths
- The problem is well motivated and straightforward to understand, addressing a pressing issue of fairness and interpretability in high-stakes decision-making (finance, healthcare, judiciary).
- The underlying techniques (inverse classification) are interesting, and the methodology is considered sound and well explained by one reviewer.
- The framework reduces fairness disparities without significantly sacrificing classification accuracy (balance of fairness and accuracy).
- Evaluation uses simple, interpretable ML models (logistic regression, SVM).

### Weaknesses
- **Literature & novelty**: The problem is indistinguishable from counterfactual explanations and algorithmic recourse (e.g., arXiv:2002.06278). The connection to existing recourse literature is not adequately made; key works are missing, including Ustun et al. (2019) (only briefly mentioned), Gupta et al. (2019) on “Equalizing Recourse,” Kavouras et al. (2023) on fairness-aware counterfactuals, and recent advances by Karimi et al. (2020, 2021, 2022 survey) and Beretta & Cinquini (2023). Also missing standard fairness references (adversarial fairness, fairness constraints, Fairness through awareness, Preventing fairness gerrymandering). The paper claims advantage over Ustun et al. (2019) in execution time and real-life cost values but provides no detailed description to verify this.
- **Fairness definition justification**: “Action fairness” is not adequately justified as a desired property. It is not conditioned on creditworthiness of the group/individual; unlike established metrics such as equality of opportunity or statistical parity, the motivation is unclear (e.g., “if females need to reduce their credit cards and increase their account balance to meet fairness constraints, males should face comparable requirements” is not intuitive). The definition appears to be simply a differencing measure and not a novel fairness notion. The relationship between action fairness and outcome fairness (SP, EOP) is unexplored; experiments lack outcome fairness metrics. (One reviewer suggests that the goal might be fairness of outcome + fairness of actions, but this is not addressed.)
- **Cost function feasibility**: The cost function is very hard to determine in practice. The authors state it would be “established by an expert or learned from historical data,” but no details on feasibility are given. Handling varying costs over large feature sets is unclear; must users specify per-feature cost functions? (e.g., changing a FICO score from 550 to 570 vs. 680 to 700 likely has different costs).
- **Problem formulation & notation**: Several variables are not introduced (e.g., what are y’=0 and S in equation 2?). The definition of cost(a_n) is ambiguous—is it a weight vector or a per-feature cost? Notation should use vector operations. The problem definition does not originally restrict to linear classifiers, but equation (7) is specific to linear classifiers, causing confusion. The description of the weighted Euclidean distance is unclear; is cost(a_n) multiplied by distance already defining cost? The probability P_n(y’=0|S=1) and cumulative cost over n actions are not clearly defined.
- **Missing algorithm**: Algorithm 0 is referenced but never presented; no supplementary material details it. This makes it impossible to evaluate the method’s novelty and significance.
- **Empirical evaluation**: Only two simple tabular datasets and two classifiers (logistic regression, SVM). No comparison with any baselines or existing recourse/fairness methods. No statistical significance tests (e.g., Demšar 2006). The method for ensuring fairness in experiments (post-cost-diff, ranking post-processing) is not clearly explained—how is group assignment determined? Does it remove the most costly actions? How does it affect accuracy? Are new weights found via optimization? Why not directly incorporate fairness constraints? Training time and data splits are not discussed; accuracy calculation is unclear. Overall, the evaluation is insufficient for publication.
