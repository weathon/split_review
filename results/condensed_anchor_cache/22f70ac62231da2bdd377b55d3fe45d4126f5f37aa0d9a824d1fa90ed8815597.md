## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies black-box confidence elicitation from LLMs using a three-part framework: prompting strategies for verbalized confidence, sampling methods for multiple responses, and aggregation techniques for consistency. It evaluates calibration and failure prediction across five datasets and five LLMs (including GPT-4 and LLaMA 2 Chat). Key findings: LLMs are overconfident; calibration and failure prediction improve with model scale but remain far from ideal; human-inspired prompts, response consistency, and better aggregation can partially mitigate overconfidence; the gap to white-box methods is narrow (e.g., 0.522 vs 0.605 AUROC). However, no single method consistently outperforms others, and all struggle on challenging tasks (e.g., professional knowledge).

### Strengths
- The problem is important: reliable confidence estimation from LLMs is critical for trustworthy decision-making.
- The three-part framework (prompting, sampling, aggregation) provides clarity and a systematic breakdown of design choices.
- The evaluation is thorough, spanning many task domains (including commonsense and arithmetic reasoning) and multiple models (GPT-4, LLaMA 2 Chat).
- The two evaluation tasks (confidence calibration, failure prediction) are reasonable and practically motivated.
- The paper is well-written and addresses black-box confidence calibration in a timely manner, targeting LLMs specifically (not just image classifiers).
- Comparisons with white-box methods are included (AUROC gap 0.522 to 0.605), showing a narrow gap.
- These insights could be impactful in practice for users of closed-source LLMs.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns** (minority strong critique from Reviewer 4, rating 5): The prompting/aggregation techniques are standard and well-known; the idea of using LLMs for self-assessment is common. Methodological contribution is marginal; more would be expected for a strong contribution.
- **Findings are not novel** (Reviewer 4): The overconfidence finding is consistent with known LLM behavior (“fluent but non-factual”). The scaling finding (larger LLMs performing better on benchmarks) is general knowledge (e.g., LLaMA 2 paper comparing 7B/13B).
- **No clear best strategy** (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4): Performance varies significantly across tasks, and no prompting/sampling/aggregation combination consistently outperforms others. Given a new task, it is unclear which method to choose or whether it will generalize. The paper itself notes none of the methods are satisfactory.
- **Lack of actionable guidance** (Reviewer 2): The paper does not propose a recommended combination of prompting, sampling, and aggregation for practitioners, leaving the message that “nothing works.” A best-performing method (with limitations) would be useful.
- **Missing white-box comparison on ECE** (Reviewer 3, rating 8, minority more positive): While AUROC is compared, direct comparison of white-box token-probability-based calibration on ECE and failure prediction for open-source models is missing; this gap would clarify how much black-box methods lose.
- **Uncertainty–correctness correlation assumption** (Reviewer 1): No a priori reasoning is given for why model certainty should correlate with correctness, or how to understand when it might or might not.
- **Prompt sensitivity not studied** (Reviewers 1, 4): Minor prompt wording variations can have large effects, but the paper does not explore how prompt text was chosen (optimization vs. experimentation) or how prompt framing (e.g., “Appear confident”) affects overconfidence.
- **Insights are preliminary** (Reviewer 1): The high-level observations enumerated in the introduction are interesting but difficult to operationalize or build upon.
- **Minor issues**:
  - Acronym ECE not defined at first use (Reviewer 1).
  - Typo on page 6: “aevaluation” → “evaluation” (Reviewer 1).
  - Equation 3: should maximize with respect to \(P\) (MLE estimation) rather than as written (Reviewer 3).
