## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces the Linear Partial Gromov-Wasserstein (LPGW) embedding, a linearized technique for the Partial Gromov-Wasserstein (PGW) problem that relaxes the equal mass constraint of GW. By reducing pairwise PGW computation from O(K²) to O(K) for K metric measure spaces, LPGW aims to preserve the partial matching capabilities of PGW while improving computational efficiency. The authors provide theoretical results showing that LPGW defines a valid metric under a PGW-Monge mapping assumption, and validate the method on shape retrieval, synthetic data, and transport-based learning tasks (MNIST). The paper is well-organized, clearly motivated, and contains many mathematical and experimental details. Reviewers gave scores of 8, 6, 6, 6, 8, with two reviewers being more positive (8) and three moderately positive (6). All reviewers noted the need for stronger justification of approximations, theoretical assumptions, and experimental rigor.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written, well-organized, and easy to follow, with many details that aid understanding (Reviewers 1,3,4).
- The proposed LPGW embedding addresses the computational challenge of PGW by reducing complexity from O(K²) to O(K), with a formal proof of equivalence between LPGW and PGW under the PGW-Monge mapping assumption, contributing to the theoretical foundation (Reviewers 1,4,5).
- The method effectively combines advantages of both linearized GW and partial GW, and experiments (shape retrieval, elliptical disks, MNIST) demonstrate that LPGW preserves partial matching properties while improving efficiency, often comparing favorably to baselines (Reviewers 1,2,3).
- The theoretical findings appear correct, and the mathematical formulation is carefully derived, clearly showing how prior work leads to the contribution (Reviewers 3,5).
- Some reviewers found the paper promising and justified by experimental results (Reviewer 1) and well-backed mathematically (Reviewer 5). Note that two reviewers assigned a high score (8), while three gave 6, reflecting moderately positive reception with consistent calls for additional analysis.

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty and incremental contribution** – Several reviewers noted that the work primarily combines existing methods (linearized GW from Beier et al. 2022 and partial GW) without substantial new conceptual advances. The adaptation to the partial setting, while non-trivial, conceptually follows from the partial OT and linearized GW literature. The paper does not explore alternative divergences beyond total variation (TV) for the unbalanced formulation (e.g., Kullback-Leibler divergence), which could yield different properties and better performance (Reviewers 1,2). One reviewer remarked “both the linearized GW problem and the partial GW problem have appeared in previous works. Combining these two approaches … appears to be incremental progress” (Reviewer 2).

2. **Heavy reliance on Monge map assumption** – Many theoretical results (Proposition 3.1(2,3,4), Theorem 3.2(3)) require existence of a Monge map for the PGW problem, but sufficient conditions for this assumption are not discussed in the main text. This is a strong restriction, limiting applicability in practical scenarios where Monge maps may not exist. Reviewers requested: theoretical bounds on approximation error when a Monge map does not exist; empirical experiments on datasets known to violate Monge conditions; and modifications to increase robustness (Reviewers 2,3,4). Specific questions include: “Can the authors comment on sufficient conditions for such a result to hold?” (Reviewer 3) and “Could the authors extend the approximation results … to scenarios where the Monge mapping may not exist?” (Reviewer 4).

3. **Unjustified approximation error from two layers of approximations** – The computational LPGW implementation relies on two sequential approximations: first to aLPGW (Equation 18), then to the final formulation (Equation 20). No theoretical guarantees or empirical evaluation of the error introduced by these approximations are provided. One reviewer called the approximation “very coarse in general; no justifications are provided for when this might be a reasonable approximation” (Reviewer 2). Reviewers request: error bounds for each step, ablation studies quantifying empirical error, and discussion of tradeoffs between computational efficiency and accuracy (Reviewers 2,3,4).

4. **Time complexity claim unsubstantiated** – The claim that the algorithm computes distances between K metric measure spaces in O(K) time is not supported with any time-complexity analysis (or space-complexity tradeoff). Reviewers ask for a dedicated analysis to prove this claim (Reviewers 4,5). Both reviewers stressed that this is a central selling point and requires formal justification.

5. **Insufficient experimental details and reproducibility** – The MNIST experiment lacks specifics: e.g., logistic regression is used for multiclass classification (digits 0-9) but no explanation of how it was extended beyond binary; training details are omitted; and it is unclear if other models (e.g., neural networks) were considered (Reviewers 4,5). Additionally, the experiments are largely replications of those from Beier et al. (2022) and Nieter et al. (2022) with minor modifications (e.g., random rotation of MNIST), rather than introducing new applications or datasets (Reviewer 2). One reviewer noted that the random rotation “appears a bit contrived in the setting of MNIST” (Reviewer 2). The paper also does not include comparisons with other GW approximation methods such as Low-Rank GW (Scetbon et al.) or Sliced GW (Vayer et al.) that could provide meaningful context for LPGW’s advantages (Reviewer 3). Furthermore, while robustness to noise is cited as a key feature of PGW, it is not sufficiently highlighted or addressed in the text (Reviewer 3).

6. **Insufficient discussion of related works** – The paper should more clearly distinguish its contributions from existing mapping-based unbalanced GW formulations (citations [2],[3],[4]), providing a more detailed comparison of computational complexity and performance (Reviewer 1). The current discussion does not fully capture nuances between these methods and LPGW.

7. **Minor issues** – 
   - The barycentric LGW used in the second equality of Equation (10) should be explicitly stated (Reviewer 1).
   - Notation \(\gamma^1\wedge\gamma^2\) on line 260 appears incorrect; should be \(\inf_A \gamma^1(A\cap E)+\gamma^2(A^C\cap E)\) (Reviewer 1).
   - Proposition 3.3 states that the result holds when \(\lambda\) is sufficiently large, but there is tradeoff: if \(\lambda\) is too large, the regularizer dominates. The condition needs clarification (Reviewer 3).
   - In the elliptical disks experiment, the choice of reference space significantly impacts performance; practical guidance or heuristics for selecting reference spaces are missing (Reviewer 4).
   - Proposition 3.1(3) covers the case when the cost function \(g_Y\) is an inner product; could this be extended to squared Euclidean distance with additional assumptions (as explored in [A] for Monge maps)? (Reviewer 4).

8. **Missing baseline and analysis in MNIST experiment** – Reviewer 3 questions whether PGW itself would provide the best baseline (since LOT and LGW do not account for noise) and suggests including such a comparison. This relates to the broader need for stronger empirical validation of LPGW’s advantages.
