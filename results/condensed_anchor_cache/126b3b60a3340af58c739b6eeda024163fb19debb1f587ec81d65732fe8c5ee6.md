## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes a method to align frozen pretrained unimodal vision and language encoders into a multimodal model by training only lightweight MLP projection layers with a contrastive objective, leveraging a curated dataset and a Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) metric to select compatible encoder pairs. The model achieves 76% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet zero-shot and outperforms original CLIP on several tasks. Reviewer scores are 6, 3, 3, 3; one reviewer found the approach effective and impressive, while the other three raised significant concerns about novelty, fairness of efficiency claims, data curation, missing baselines, and the value of the CKA analysis.

### Strengths

- The paper considers an interesting and promising problem: leveraging pretrained unimodal encoders for multimodal tasks.
- The CKA metric offers a systematic way to measure how well a unimodal encoder pair might transfer to multimodal alignment.
- The paper demonstrates that a stronger projector and a high-quality, concept-rich dataset can transfer unimodal encoders into the multimodal domain. The dataset curation approach is interesting and insightful.
- The final model achieves competitive performance with less compute and data compared to traditional multimodal training, outperforming original CLIP on some zero-shot image classification tasks, all presented image-text retrieval tasks, and most multilingual tasks.
- The zero-shot localization, multilingual, and long-text retrieval results are particularly interesting.
- The framework is adaptable to different scenarios (multilingual models, long-context retrieval) and the paper includes extensive experiments and ablation studies, with a detailed appendix.

### Weaknesses

- **Efficiency claims are inaccurate or misleading.** The paper claims 20‑fold data and 65‑fold compute reductions, but this ignores the cost of the pretrained CLIP models used to curate the training dataset, as well as the cost of pretraining the unimodal encoders (e.g., DINOv2, RoBERTa). CLIP baselines are trained from scratch; a fair comparison should account for all training costs. Without this efficiency claim, the contribution becomes very limited.
- **The CKA-based encoder selection is a weak contribution.** 
  - Fig. 1 shows a wide spread of CLIP losses for similar CKA values; the primary determinant for both CKA and CLIP loss is model size, so CKA may not indicate how “alignable” a pair is.
  - Fig. 5 shows ConvNeXt achieving retrieval performance close to DINOv2 with much lower CKA across all text encoders, undermining CKA as a strong predictor.
  - No comparison is made between frozen image encoder performance and downstream retrieval performance.
  - Many model pairs are not shown for all text encoder families; figures are hard to interpret (difficult to identify which data point corresponds to which model pair).
  - Other alignment measures (cosine similarity, mutual nearest neighbor alignment) exist and should have been discussed.
- **Data curation is heavily tailored to the evaluations.** 
  - The dataset is curated to include concepts from evaluation benchmarks (e.g., SBU for Flickr evals, ImageNet concepts for ImageNet), which inflates zero-shot performance. Table 2 confirms that removing these concepts significantly hurts accuracy.
  - When using only comparable datasets (CC12M+CC3M+SBU), the method achieves only 54% on ImageNet, while LiT (Table 13) attains 67% with CC12M alone (training the text encoder fully, but using smaller models). This suggests LiT may be more efficient than training only projectors. No ablations or comparisons against this setup are provided.
  - More analysis is needed on how dataset quality/coverage affects generalization; guidelines for concept selection (representativeness, bias) are missing.
- **Missing important baselines and experiments.** 
  - The paper does not compare to finetuning a pretrained CLIP model on the same curated dataset, or training linear projectors on top of frozen CLIP encoders on the same data.
  - No experiments with scaling up the projector or training data (e.g., from 20M to 50M, 100M, 1B) are provided.
  - The model’s ability to serve as a vision encoder for multimodal LLMs (connecting with LLMs) is not verified.
  - Linear probing evaluations on the frozen image representations (as done in the original CLIP paper) are missing.
  - No comparisons against adding LoRA layers to the frozen encoders are made to explore compute-optimal setups; the choice to freeze encoders and stop at a single token projection layer for text is not sufficiently justified.
- **The idea of aligning via projection layers is not novel and is very common.** The conclusion stating “Our research introduces a paradigm shift in vision-language alignment” is a major overstatement.
- **Section 4.1 claims “Unimodal Performance Does Not Reflect Alignment Quality,”** which appears to contradict findings in the platonic representation hypothesis. Clarification is needed.
- **Several details are missing.** 
  - Table 1 / Section 4.2 do not share the number of parameters in each projector layer (MLP vs. identity vs. token); the results may simply reflect more trainable parameters.
  - The authors do not explain whether the high-quality training dataset will be released.
  - The paper does not discuss whether the approach can avoid using CLIP at all (e.g., for dataset curation), which would strengthen the message.
