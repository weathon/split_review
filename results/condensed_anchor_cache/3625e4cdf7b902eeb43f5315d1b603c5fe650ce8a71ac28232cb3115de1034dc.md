## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces VideoEval, a benchmark suite for evaluating video foundation models (VFMs). It consists of VidTAB (few-shot classification via adapter-based fine-tuning on 8 tasks) and VidEB (embedding retrieval on 4 tasks). The benchmark draws videos from existing datasets and evaluates 20 vision foundation models (image-only, image-to-video, video). The authors report insights on weak generalization, data scaling, pre‑training paradigms, and the benefit of combining paradigms. Reviewers agreed that the work addresses limitations of traditional benchmarks (diversity, saturation, cost) and provides a useful large‑scale comparison. However, they raised serious concerns about dataset justification, unsupported conclusions, missing comparisons to modern multimodal models and state‑of‑the‑art baselines, limited evaluation scope (classification only, few frames), and presentation issues. Reviewers R3 and R4 (scores 5) found the benchmark careful and comprehensive but noted major weaknesses; reviewers R1 and R2 (scores 3) were more critical, questioning the benchmark’s validity and scope.

### Strengths
- **Broadens evaluation scope**: Integrates a wider range of tasks across 6 domains (action recognition, AI for science, content moderation, quality/aesthetic assessment, emotion analysis) and 12 tasks, addressing the lack of diversity, saturated metrics, and high evaluation cost of current benchmarks.
- **Large‑scale evaluation of 20 models**: Provides a valuable reference by evaluating a large set of publicly available image-only, image-to-video, and video foundation models (Vision Transformers, MViT, etc.).
- **Incorporates evaluation cost**: Uses few‑shot learning (VidTAB) and a lightweight adapter (attentive probe) to make evaluation practical for large models; cost is treated as a design criterion.
- **Careful dataset curation**: The authors selected high‑quality, challenging, and diverse video datasets; design choices (few‑shot setting, attentive probe) are motivated and backed by pilot experiments.
- **Two complementary tasks**: VidTAB (adaptation via fine‑tuning) and VidEB (embedding quality for retrieval) give a more complete picture of VFM capabilities.
- **Interesting insights**: Findings such as “more video data can hurt performance on some tasks”, the mixed effect of pre‑training paradigms, and the benefit of combining paradigms are thought‑provoking.
- **Vision‑centric evaluation**: Avoids confounds from language model components, focusing on vision encoder quality.
- **Well‑written and organized**: Paper is clear and easy to follow (according to R3, R4).
- **Useful to the community**: A resource for researchers to benchmark video encoders (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Dataset selection justification is insufficient**: It is unclear why VidTAB focuses on “Action Recognition in Special Scenarios” (e.g., dark scene) without citing prior work, and why VidEB relies on scene retrieval tasks that favor static understanding, making it hard to evaluate motion capabilities. One reviewer (R3) noted that the comparison to prior benchmarks in Table 1 is misleading because VideoGLUE and BEAR also include tasks beyond action recognition (temporal localization, anomaly classification). VidTAB remains purely classification‑based, overlooking harder tasks like temporal action localization.
- **Many conclusions are not supported by the evidence**:
  - “VFMs struggle to adapt to unseen tasks” lacks a comparison to state‑of‑the‑art results on each dataset.
  - “VFMs outperform IFMs” is not generally true – the best IFM beats 9 out of 12 VFMs; this is an interesting negative result that needs discussion.
  - Claim about ViCLIP‑L post‑pretraining diminishing performance in “Science, Safety, Quality, Emotion” is contradicted by Table 5 (performance increases on science tasks, mixed on safety, increases on emotion).
  - “Contrastive learning consistently excels in embedding evaluation” is contradicted by two MVM methods outperforming the best CL method.
  - “Performance scales with data/model size” is not always true: VideoMAEv1‑H (651M) outperforms VideoMAEv2‑g (1037M); ViCLIP‑L‑200M underperforms ViCLIP‑L‑10M on some tasks.
  - Unexplained differences between VidTAB and VidEB: stage‑2 training and Kinetics‑710 fine‑tuning improve VidTAB performance but degrade VidEB performance.
- **Limited evaluation scope**: The benchmark does not evaluate large multimodal models (GPT‑4V, Gemini, video‑llava); it is restricted to video encoders. Task adaptation uses only one adapter (attentive probe), and other efficient methods (e.g., Visual Prompt Tuning) are not considered. Only 8/16 frames per video are used, preventing assessment of long video understanding and motion speed.
- **Missing baselines and comparisons**: No specialist/SoTA results on each dataset are provided to contextualize VFM performance. It is not clearly indicated which models in Table 5 are fine‑tuned vs. zero‑shot. The handling of image models for video (frame aggregation, ranking in VidEB) is not explained. The inclusion of image models in a “video” benchmark is not well‑justified.
- **Presentation issues**: Table 2 and Table 3 fonts are too small to read. Figures 1 and 2 use Comic Sans and occupy too much space. The radar chart is hard to read. Numeric columns are not right‑justified. The name “VideoEval” is generic; the title has a grammatical error (plural missing). The paper devotes too much space to setup (analysis begins on page 8 of 10).
- **Low‑cost contribution is questionable**: Zero‑shot evaluation is already cheap, and fine‑tuning on Kinetics‑710 (0.66M videos) is not few‑shot or low‑cost. The calculation of the TA‑score for zero‑shot visual‑language models (no few‑shot examples) is unclear.
- **Definitions and comparisons with prior work are misleading**: The paper’s definitions of “beyond action recognition” and “task diversity” are unclear. Table 1 misrepresents prior benchmarks by restricting them to action recognition (e.g., VideoGLUE also includes temporal localization; BEAR includes anomaly classification and instructional video tasks). The related work section omits discussion of image foundation models and video adapter methods that are actually evaluated.
- **Missing methodological details**:
  - Frame sampling strategy (8/16 frames, temporal crops?) not specified.
  - Attentive probe is not explained; a reference and description are needed.
  - Which datasets were considered and excluded, and why?
  - Terms DSVs, CSVs, ISVs are not defined.
  - Hyperparameter tuning for fair few‑shot comparison is not detailed (R1).
  - Numbers in the text (e.g., +3.6, 8.0, 37.7, 44.4) do not match the tables.
- **Undiscussed important findings**: The paper does not discuss that VFMs show no gain over IFMs on spatial tasks (Safety, Quality), and that image‑to‑video methods outperform VFMs on most tasks. Future research directions are not explicitly stated.
