## Merged Review

### Summary
Authors propose KAAN, replacing B‑splines in KANs with linear combinations of basis functions to make the network more flexible and MLP‑like. They show MLPs can be seen as a special case of KANs and experiment with seven basis‑function combinations on tabular data and CIFAR‑10, including a convolutional version (CKAAN). The method achieves comparable or better performance than MLPs and ResNets. However, multiple reviewers find the contribution incremental, the computational cost high, and the practical utility limited by the absence of a systematic way to choose basis functions.

### Strengths
- The paper is written clearly and is easy to read.
- The proposed activation makes KANs more flexible and easier to deploy in standard architectures (MLPs, CNNs).
- Experiments on several benchmarks (tabular, CIFAR‑10) show comparable or better performance than MLPs and ResNets (Tabs. 3–5).
- The theoretical framework is elegantly constructed, establishing that MLPs are a specific instance of KANs and that any continuous univariate basis can be used as an activation function.
- Extensive experimental coverage across multiple AI tasks, including a convolutional extension (CKAAN).
- The KAAN parametrization is compatible with standard ANN architectures and may benefit neural architecture search / meta‑learning because the nonlinearity parameters are differentiable.
- The paper provides a novel perspective by viewing MLPs as a special case of KANs.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty.** The KAN arxiv paper (Liu et al. 2024) already provides an MLP‑like interpretation of KANs. The core change – replacing B‑splines with learnable activation functions – is covered by existing literature on learnable activations (e.g., polynomial or sinusoidal bases in MLPs). (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- **High computational cost.** Equation (6) uses several weights per connection (one per activation type) plus parametrized activations, increasing memory and runtime. The performance gains in Tabs. 3–5 are modest relative to the parameter increase. The paper does not report parameter counts, VRAM consumption, or running time, nor does it compare KAANs to standard networks with the same number of parameters. (R2, R3)
- **Lack of interpretability and vague claims.** The paper calls KAANs “intuitive”, but they are arguably more convoluted than standard MLPs and less interpretable than KANs, especially for symbolic regression. The statement “KAN faces the challenges of being unintuitive and inflexible” is asserted without concrete examples, and it is unclear how KAAN improves intuition or flexibility. (R1, R2, R4)
- **No method for choosing basis functions.** Different combinations perform best on different tasks, leaving users to a trial‑and‑error approach. The paper gives no guidelines for selecting an optimal basis function for a new problem, which limits practical use. (R3)
- **Unfair or incomplete comparisons with KANs.** KANs typically require longer training to converge, but the paper does not adjust for this or analyze convergence rates. The computational cost of different basis functions is not examined. (R3)
- **Limited evaluation scope.** Performance on more challenging benchmarks (e.g., ImageNet‑scale, transformer‑like architectures) is not demonstrated. The paper would be strengthened by showing tasks where MLPs or KANs fail but KAAN succeeds. (R1, R4)
- **Writing issues.** The introduction uses uncited terms (LANs, TANs); line 30 makes an overstated claim about breakthroughs (“not many breakthroughs until KANs” ignores transformers, capsule networks, etc.); typos appear (“No many”, extra bracket, non‑plural “Experiment” in section heading). (R2)
- **Incremental improvement.** Even the more favorable reviewers (R3: score 5, R4: score 6) consider the advance incremental and request stronger numerical evidence or a clear practical advantage. (R3, R4)
