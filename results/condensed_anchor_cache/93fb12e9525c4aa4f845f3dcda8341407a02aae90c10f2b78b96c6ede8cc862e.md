## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper studies what automatic differentiation (AD) computes for neural networks with non-differentiable functions (e.g., ReLU, maxpool). It proves that under certain conditions (distinct bias parameters, batch size 1) AD always returns a Clarke subdifferential for fully connected networks with pointwise activations; for larger batch sizes, a convex combination of left/right derivatives is required. Conditions are also provided for CNNs with maxpool and shared biases. Empirical checks on fully connected networks (2 hidden layers 256, 64 trained on MNIST, batchsize 128) and CNNs (VGG11, VGG11-BN, ResNet18 on CIFAR10) confirm the sufficient conditions hold in practice. Reviewers find the problem important and the results valuable, but note limitations: some results are extensions of prior work, conditions are complex, empirical validation is incomplete, and some theorems may be incorrect or contradictory. Reviewer confidence varies: two reviewers gave score 6, three gave 8; one reviewer (score 8) had low confidence due to a medical emergency; another reviewer (score 6) updated after rebuttal but still raised substantive concerns.

### Strengths

- The paper studies one of the most fundamental and pressing theoretical questions for modern deep learning: what AD actually computes at non-differentiable points.
- The theoretical results are rich and strong, applying to a broad class of architectures (fully connected and CNN) and training regimes (various batch sizes).
- Extends prior work from Bouligand subdifferentials to Clarke subdifferentials, covering more practical settings and increasing usability (e.g., defining individual gradient operations so the overall gradient is a Clarke subdifferential).
- Provides checkable sufficient conditions that are empirically validated on realistic architectures (VGG11, ResNet18) with standard datasets (MNIST, CIFAR10).
- Numerical results (e.g., frequency of encountering non-differentiable points: 0 for ReLU6, 9.8 for HardTanh, 13.8 for HardSigmoid) are consistent with the theory.
- Among the only positive results available on the soundness of AD for nonsmooth neural networks.
- Accurate knowledge of AD behavior is beneficial for both training and analysis, as non-differentiable regions can sometimes be problematic despite being ignored in practice.

### Weaknesses

- Theoretical results are not intuitive and are difficult to follow; Conditions 1 and 2 are very complicated without a qualitative explanation of what they mean (e.g., is one condition more general than the other? does “trivial max-pool” collapse one into the other?).
- It is unclear how the numerical/theoretical result from gradient descent differs from that given by subgradient descent.
- Are results in Table 2 from gradient descent, subgradient descent, or Clarke subgradient descent? This is not clarified.
- The paper does not discuss applicability to other nonsmooth activation functions such as the step function.
- The claim that input and hidden dimensions are typically larger than batch size is controversial; large batch sizes are sometimes used (e.g., early self-supervised learning) for stability or speed.
- For batch sizes larger than one, the requirement that AD computes a convex combination of left‑ and right‑side derivatives is unclear: does every element that touches the boundary need to use the same combination weights?
- The empirical validation checks that the sufficient conditions hold, but does not empirically verify that these conditions are indeed sufficient for guaranteeing AD computes a Clarke subgradient.
- The usage of the word “safe” is misleading: AD not computing a Clarke subgradient can still converge to a Clarke‑stationary point (see Bolte‑Pauwels 2020). The sentence “These correctness results … provide no information about what it computes at the remaining inputs” is incorrect; Bolte‑Pauwels 2020 does provide information (an element of the conservative field is computed).
- Is it ever an issue during training that some bias parameters become equal? This potential failure mode is not addressed.
- The contribution is a modest step, nothing particularly groundbreaking.
- One reviewer suspects an error: the Bouligand and Mordukhovich subdifferentials appear to be mixed up on page 3.
- Theorem 1 is a minor extension of Theorem 3.6 in Lee et al. (2023).
- Lemma 2, Lemma 3, and Lemma 7 are complicated formulations of essentially known facts (e.g., the example in Lemma 2 is already mentioned in Kakade and Lee 2018).
- Lemma 3 and Theorem 4 appear contradictory: choosing all λ=0 in Theorem 4 reduces to the setting of Lemma 3, so Reviewer 5 suspects Theorem 4 is false. A similar concern is raised for Theorem 6.
- Theorem 8 lacks discussion on when the algorithm stops with P_l non‑empty (how often does this occur?), its computational complexity, and the overhead compared to standard AD.
- Reviewer disagreement: some reviewers view the results as strong (Reviewers 2, 3), while Reviewer 5 considers Theorem 1 a minor extension and suspects Theorems 4 and 6 may be false. Reviewer 4 acknowledges the importance but expresses very low confidence in the correctness of the proofs.
