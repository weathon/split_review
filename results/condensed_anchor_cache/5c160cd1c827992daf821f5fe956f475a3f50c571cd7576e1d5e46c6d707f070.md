## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Masked AutoEncoder Purifier (MAEP), which integrates Masked AutoEncoder (MAE) into an adversarial purifier for test-time purification. It claims the first study of an adversarial purifier based on MAE, emphasizing defense transferability and attack generalization without additional data. The method is evaluated on multiple datasets and compared with diffusion-based purifiers, showing state-of-the-art performance when trained on CIFAR-10 and tested on ImageNet. Reviewers generally find the idea interesting but raise major concerns about evaluation rigor, novelty, and clarity. Reviewer 3 is the most positive (rating 5), while others rate 3.

### Strengths
- **Innovative use of MAE**: Application of MAE for adversarial purification is considered a novel approach (R1, R2, R3). R3 notes it is the first study of an adversarial purifier based on MAE.
- **Improved accuracy gap**: MAEP achieves a significant reduction in the gap between clean and robust accuracy (R2).
- **Defense transferability and train-test discrepancy**: The paper identifies transferability in purification (R3) and employs a train-test discrepancy strategy that is empirically effective (R3).
- **Comprehensive evaluation across datasets**: Experiments cover multiple datasets and compare against several baselines (R1, R4).
- **Strong cross-resolution performance**: MAEP trained on CIFAR-10 outperforms diffusion-based models pre-trained on ImageNet when tested on ImageNet (R4).
- **Theoretical insights**: The paper provides concise mathematical reasoning (R2).
- **Clear presentation**: The paper is clearly presented (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Outdated and insufficient evaluation (severe concern)**: The evaluation techniques are outdated; recent work (Lee & Kim 2023, Kang et al. 2023, Chen et al. 2024) has shown robustness of diffusion-based purification is overestimated by >20%. The paper uses evaluations from ~2021–2022 without accurate gradient-based attacks, risking obfuscated gradients (Athalye et al. 2018). Robust accuracy >90% in Tables 2 and 5 appears overly optimistic (R2). R4 also notes that the claims far exceed current SOTA (e.g., DiffPure on CIFAR-10 WRN-28-10 vs AutoAttack is 70.64% in the original paper, but the manuscript claims 87.29%; Table 6 shows PGD and AA results very close (avg diff <1.5%) while other papers report ~15% difference). R4 recommends using PGD+EOT and AA(rand) instead of standard PGD and AA. Evaluation should target both the classifier and the purifier (R4). Adversarial example generation details (target model, attack parameters) are missing (R4).
- **Lack of novelty clarification**: Several similar papers already exist (e.g., “Adversarial Purification of Information Masking” 2023, “Denoising Masked AutoEncoders Help Robust Classification” 2023, “MAEDefense” 2023) that are not discussed; the idea of MAE-based purification was not first introduced here (R4). R2 asks specifically to clarify differences from Tsai et al. 2023 and Lyu et al. 2023.
- **Necessity of MAE vs. diffusion models not justified**: Diffusion models align better with adversarial perturbations (trained with Gaussian noise) and have stronger generative capability; MAE masked modeling differs. The paper does not provide insight into why MAE is a good choice (R3). R1 similarly says the specific advantages of MAE for purification are not discussed.
- **Transferability lacks explanation**: While transferability is observed, the paper does not explain it via learned representations, ablation studies isolating contributing components of MAE, or a theoretical hypothesis (R3).
- **Missing evaluations and ablations**: 
  - Ablation studies are lacking, e.g., applying finetuning strategies on diffusion models (R3).
  - DiffPure results are inconsistent across tables; sources vary (some excerpted, some reproduced) – should use uniform source (R1).
  - In Table 6, only DiffPure is reported with standard deviation; rationale needed (R1). Also, DiffPure seems to outperform MAEP in some settings but is not discussed (R1).
  - DiffPure results for training on CIFAR-10 and testing on ImageNet (Table 8 setting) are not reported (R1, R4).
  - The pipeline for applying a CIFAR-10-trained model to ImageNet (different resolution) is unclear – are downsampling/upsampling used? Provide details (R4).
- **Minor issues and presentation flaws**:
  - Table I: symbols in last three columns not defined (R1).
  - Table IV: results are confusing; relationship to training loss design unclear (R1).
  - Recommend showing clean vs. robust accuracy in scatter diagram for clarity (R1).
  - Minimal differences between MAEP and MAEP+LoRA (Table 7) need thorough analysis (R1).
  - Running time: MAEP is better than DiffPure but worse than DISCO; practical implications should be discussed (R1).
  - Methods section contains many experimental results; should discuss why transferability occurs rather than just demonstrating results. Content arrangement can be improved (R3).
  - PDF does not support hyperlinking citations and references (R3).
- **Diffusion model efficiency claims are misleading**: The claim that diffusion models “remarkably increase inference time” ignores recent advances enabling one-step denoising (Carlini et al. 2022) and training-free acceleration (Xia et al. 2023, Li et al. 2023, So et al. 2023) (R2).

*Note on reviewer disagreement:* Reviewer 3 is notably more positive (rating 5) than the others (ratings 3), emphasizing novelty and transferability, but still raises skepticism about MAE necessity and lack of explanation. Reviewer 2 is strongly critical of evaluation methods.
