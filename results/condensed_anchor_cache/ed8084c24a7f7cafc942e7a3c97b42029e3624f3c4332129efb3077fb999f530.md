## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper theoretically analyzes whether Transformer-based LLMs (T-LLMs) can act as universal circuits, concluding they cannot be general learners because constant-depth, poly(n)-size, log-precision T-LLMs lie in TC⁰ and thus cannot execute all polynomial-size circuits. A memorization result is offered to partially explain the gap between this negative result and empirical successes. Reviews are mixed: the research question is widely appreciated, but substantial concerns about missing formal definitions, unclear logical flow, lack of novelty distinction, speculative memorization claims, and insufficient handling of chain-of-thought reasoning weaken the contribution. One reviewer (score 1) is highly critical and questions the core reasoning; others (scores 3,5,5) see value but demand major clarifications and reframing.

### Strengths
1. The research question—whether realistic Transformers can express any polynomial-time-computable function—is interesting and important (R1, R4, R5).
2. The work draws a novel connection between instruction execution (prompts as circuits) and existing circuit-based analyses of Transformers, leveraging the universal circuit framing to conclude that T-LLMs are not universal learners (R4).
3. The discussion of the implications of the TC⁰ limitation for LLM capabilities is valuable, especially the focus on expressive power rather than just function class approximation (R5).
4. The potential to set a precedent in studying LLM limitations through circuit complexity is noted, though one reviewer’s strengths claim appears to refer to a different paper (quantum graph transformers) and is disregarded (R2’s review is a misreview).

### Weaknesses
1. **Missing formal definition of “general learner” and misleading name**: The term is used to mean “can express a universal circuit,” not learning from data. This is confusing and the definition in §2.2 is unclear — it oscillates between a formal definition and a necessary condition derived from some unstated definition. A clear, upfront definition is required. (R1, R4)
2. **Imprecise claim about poly(n) size**: The paper states that the cited literature uses log-precision, constant-depth, poly(n)-size Transformers, but in fact papers like Hahn 2020, Hao et al. 2022, Merrill et al. 2022, Merrill & Sabharwal 2023 consider O(1) neurons, not poly(n). While extension to poly(n) is plausible in the nonuniform setting, the authors should explicitly explain how the results extend. (R1, R4)
3. **Logical flow of main results and relation to prior work is unclear** (R1, R4):
   - Theorem 3 appears to follow directly from the known TC⁰ upper bound for Transformers (a contradiction with P if T-LLMs were general learners), without needing Theorem 2. The role of Theorem 2 is not explained; is it a stronger version for polylog input length?
   - The corollary about “prompts of complexity n” (prompt length n?) seems to be stated as a corollary of Theorem 3, but its dependence on Theorem 2 is ambiguous. The relationships between the results and their proofs should be reordered and clarified with forward references.
4. **Speculative claims about instance memorization**: The paper shows poly(n)-size Transformers can in principle memorize poly(n) instances, but then suggests this explains the empirical success of LLMs. This is highly speculative and unsupported:
   - Empirical evidence (McCoy et al., 2021) suggests LMs memorize n-grams, not full examples.
   - Theoretical arguments (Barak et al., 2022) show it is hard for T-LLMs to learn to memorize instances from single examples due to large-batch training.
   - The authors hedge, but the discussion should be toned down further and should engage with these contradictory works. (R1, R4, R5)
5. **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and auto-regressive inference not properly addressed** (R5):
   - The paper acknowledges CoT allows Transformers to compute beyond TC⁰, but claims this does not apply to the setting. It must clarify whether the results cover decoder-only auto-regressive Transformers used in practice. If they do, why doesn’t CoT overcome the TC⁰ limitation, since the overall function can exceed TC⁰ even if each iteration is in TC⁰? If the argument is that the Transformer cannot output intermediate tokens that are “ignored,” that fails to capture how LLMs actually use CoT.
6. **Lack of novelty distinction** (R5): It is not clear which results are novel technical contributions versus rehashing known facts (e.g., the TC⁰ upper bound for Transformers is known). The authors should explicitly state whether the analysis of limitations of TC⁰ functions in expressing universal circuits is new, or whether the paper is simply applying known circuit complexity results to the LLM setting.
7. **Presentation and readability issues** (R3, also R1, R4):
   - The main technical claim is hard to find; the introduction does not help.
   - The paper argues that lacking the ability to solve *all* problems raises doubts on LLMs, but it is not clear why *most* problems or *most instances* would not be sufficient.
   - A concrete example of a problem (or instance) that T-LLMs cannot solve would greatly help intuition.
8. **Omitted consideration of randomness** (R3): Randomized input or output-as-a-vector could potentially increase expressive power; this is not discussed.
9. **Potential benefit of constrained hypothesis class** (R4): The paper assumes universal circuits are desirable, but a constrained class may provide useful inductive bias; this is not discussed.
10. **Missing related work** (R4): Finlayson et al. (2022) benchmarked Transformers’ ability to execute regular expressions (rather than circuits); this is relevant.
11. **Minor issues** (R5): The beginning of Remark 1 is confusing and should be rewritten; the claim that LLMs solve complex tasks by memorization is problematic without further evidence.
12. **Reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (R1, score 1) is highly confident that the paper has poor contribution and presentation, and questions even the basic separation from P (asking for a reference). Other reviewers (R3, R4, R5) give higher scores but still see major flaws; R4 explicitly says addressing the presentation and Theorem 5 interpretation could raise the score. The paper must address the concerns of the most critical reviewer to achieve consensus.
