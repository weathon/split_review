## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper extends split conformal prediction to control the expected value of any monotone loss function (conformal risk control). The algorithm generalizes the standard coverage guarantee, proving a finite-sample, distribution-free bound on the expected risk that is tight up to an O(1/n) factor under a boundedness assumption. Extensions cover distribution shift (via importance weighting), quantile risk control, multiple and adversarial risk control, and expectations of U‑statistics. Experiments in computer vision (false negative rate, graph distance) and NLP (token-level F1-score) demonstrate the procedure.

### Strengths

- **Important generalization.** Extending conformal prediction from coverage to general monotone risks has clear practical impact for trustworthy ML, e.g., controlling false negative rate or F1‑score. The intuition is strong and the contribution is immediately evident.  
- **Comprehensive treatment.** The paper covers distribution shift, quantile risk, multiple/adversarial risks, and U‑statistics, showing the framework’s breadth. The derivations for these extensions are mostly straightforward consequences of the main theorem.  
- **Theory is sound and accessible.** The full proof of the main result is given in the main text; the paper demonstrates why monotonicity is needed (Proposition 2) and how to monotonize non‑monotone losses (Corollary 1). The theoretical bound is tight up to O(1/n).  
- **Well‑designed experiments.** The empirical illustrations are cleanly formulated and confirm that the desired risk is bounded as predicted. This is especially true for the false‑negative rate and graph‑distance examples.  
- **Well‑written and transparent.** The paper is easy to follow, with a clear motivation and a pleasant writing style.  
- **Minority contrast.** Reviewers 3, 4, and 6 (scores 8) are strongly positive, praising the method’s elegance and potential. Reviewers 1, 2, and 5 (scores 6) acknowledge the cleverness but note areas that need clarification. Reviewer 5 notes the paper reads like a collection of theoretical results rather than a deep dive into one core problem, while others view the breadth as a strength.

### Weaknesses

- **Related‑work differentiation is insufficient.** The paper must explicitly contrast conformal risk control with existing risk‑control methods that handle arbitrary (non‑monotone) losses – in particular the “Learn then Test” (Angelopoulos et al., 2021) and “Risk‑controlling prediction sets” (Bates et al., 2021). The guarantee is in‑expectation; readers need to understand why this is better or different from PAC‑style guarantees, and whether other methods are less tight or do not directly generalize conformal prediction.  
- **Missing experimental comparisons.** No baselines are provided in the Section 3 experiments. If no comparable existing method exists, this should be stated explicitly and discussed as a future direction. A direct comparison to “Learn then Test” (which also controls risk but is not limited to monotone losses) would be particularly informative.  
- **No experiments on the extensions.** The extensions in Section 4 (distribution shift, quantile risk control, adversarial risk control, U‑statistics) are not validated empirically, making it hard to assess their real‑world applicability and limitations.  
- **Lack of analysis of experimental results.** The provided experiments show that the method works, but there is no analysis of how performance compares to standard conformal prediction (e.g., when controlling miscoverage) or when the method might underperform.  
- **NLP experiment is too simplistic.** The open‑domain QA experiment uses a bag‑of‑tokens F1 score, which is not justified as the “risk of choice.” The shift to α=0.3 is given only a brief empirical justification. A deeper discussion of what monotonic risks are appropriate for NLP/LLMs would improve the paper.  
- **Motivation could be stronger.** Concrete examples of when conformal risk control is more appropriate than coverage control are missing (e.g., imbalanced datasets where high coverage still yields poor minority‑class performance). The statement “it is generally impossible to recast risk control as coverage control” (page 2) is not proven or supported.  
- **Definitional and notational clarity.**  
  – Λ (the set of possible λ values) and the operators L_i are not explicitly defined; the text “sup Λ ∈ Λ” is confusing.  
  – The symbol λ̂ is introduced only in an example; it needs a formal definition.  
  – R(λ) notation and indexing (R_n) are unclear – can R(λ) be interpreted as a parameterized conformity score?  
  – In the main text, the hat on λ̂ is missing from the right side of the equality on page 3: “the expected value is α” should be “α̂”.  
  – Theorem 1 uses λ_max and L_i without referring back to the earlier context.  
  – “Examining (??)” in the typos.  
- **Intuitive explanation of the method is weak.** Section 1.1 and the theoretical section (2.1) lack the kind of clear, intuitive walk‑through that makes conformal prediction so popular. The meaning of the constants and the proof steps (including the switch between λ̂’ and λ̂) need more explanation.  
- **Weakness of the guarantee relative to standard split CP.** The guarantee is α – 2B/(n+1) in the simplest case, while split CP gives α – 1/(n+1) for coverage. The paper should clarify whether this gap is inherent to risk control and whether the method recovers the tightest guarantee when the loss is miscoverage.  
- **Computability of λ̂ is a major concern.** The paper does not explain how λ̂ is computed in practice, whether it is non‑trivial, or how the formalization facilitates computation compared to other approaches. The computational complexity should be discussed.  
- **Assumptions need justification.**  
  – The bounded‑loss assumption (≤ B) is used throughout, but the paper gives only a brief mention of clipping. More examples of which losses satisfy/violate this condition are needed, along with a discussion of practical consequences.  
  – The assumption ℙ(J(L_i, λ) > 0) = 0 in Theorem 2 is presented without explanation – which loss functions satisfy it, and why? Is it the CP analogue of a continuous score?  
  – The “monotonizing” procedure (Corollary 1) is said to be powerful only for near‑monotone losses. The paper should provide numerical evidence or at least a basic example to support this claim.  
- **Missing discussions.**  
  – Limitations and future directions are not discussed in the conclusion.  
  – Inverse relation: given a λ, can one compute α to control the risk at that λ?  
  – Under the distribution‑shift setting, the weights must be estimated; how does this affect the risk control?  
  – Is there a link between quantile risk control and training‑conditional guarantees in standard conformal prediction?  
  – Can you give a concrete real‑world scenario where controlling risk under adversarial perturbations is useful?  
- **Structure and scope.** The many extensions in Section 4 dilute the focus on the core result; the paper might have benefited from presenting only the standard setting with a deeper theoretical analysis. (Minority view from Reviewer 5; other reviewers appreciated the breadth.)  
- **Proof placement.** The proof of Theorem 1 should be moved to the appendix, along with all other proofs.
