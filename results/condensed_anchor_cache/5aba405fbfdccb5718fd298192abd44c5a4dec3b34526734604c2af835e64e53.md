## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes ICL-TSVD, a continual learning method that lifts pre-trained features into a higher dimensional space, truncates singular values from the SVD of the lifted features in a continual manner, and solves a truncated minimum-norm least-squares problem. It aims to bridge theory and practice by integrating RanPAC into the Ideal Continual Learner (ICL) framework. The authors claim theoretical guarantees on training and generalization errors and demonstrate empirical stability and performance gains over baselines across multiple datasets.

### Strengths

- Comprehensive theoretical and empirical results (R1). The problem and setup are well motivated, the writing is clear, and the experimental results are extensive and appear significant (R2).
- The method is easy to implement (R4) and the contribution is clearly demonstrated (R3).
- Extensive experiments show ICL-TSVD remains stable with respect to hyperparameter selection (regularization parameter λ and truncation percentage ζ) and consistently outperforms prior CL baselines, especially RanPAC (R3). The method empirically improves upon RanPAC (R4).
- Theoretical guarantees are provided via a recurrence relation, showing small estimation and generalization errors when a suitable fraction of SVD factors are truncated (R3).

### Weaknesses

**Theoretical analysis and connection to ICL**
- The analysis feels contrived (R4). The ICL formulations (Lines 111,116) are purely theoretical sufficient conditions that require storing all previous data and cannot be considered continual learning approaches. The assumption that embedding dimension E is large enough to make the entire task sequence feasible jointly is impractical for most datasets and does not reflect continual learning constraints (R4).
- Theorem 1 implicitly requires extreme overparameterization or data that is already linearly explainable almost perfectly; otherwise \(\|\mathcal{E}_{1:t}\|\) is large and the bound is uninformative. If data is linearly explained by \(W_t^*\), ICL trivially gets zero loss and the analysis reduces to showing that truncating small singular values incurs small loss—a well-known property of low-rank approximations. The connection to ICL provides no additional insight (R4).
- The quantities \(\gamma_t\) and \(a_t\) in Theorems 1 and 2 have an unpacked recursive form; it is unclear how they behave in general, making the guarantees a black box (R2). Based on eigenvalues in Figure 1, with truncation 25% and \(\gamma_t\) around 1, \(a_t\) is \(\sim 10^5-10^6\); the bounds can be very large even with reasonable ground truth and noise, so the theoretical guarantee does not match empirical results (R3). Similar issues exist for Theorem 2 (R3).
- The statements on top of page 3 about the ICL framework (Peng et al., 2023) may not be accurate, as that work maintains a minimum-norm solution using the pseudoinverse (R2).
- The assumption of i.i.d. Gaussian vectors on \(\mathbf{h}\) and the columns of \(\mathbf{H}_{1:t}\) is unrealistic; convergence of \(\|\Lambda - \frac{1}{M_t}\mathbf{H}_{1:t}\mathbf{H}_{1:t}^\top\|\) to 0 as \(M_t\to\infty\) may not hold without it (R3).
- No theoretical guarantee (e.g., bound on \(\|\overline{\mathbf{W}}_t - \widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_t\|_F\)) is provided for the practical implementation of \(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_t\) compared to the original definition \(\overline{\mathbf{W}}_t\) (R3, Q4).

**Missing comparisons and fairness**
- A simple baseline, experience replay, is not compared (R1). Did the authors compare with experience replay? (R1)
- In Section 6.2, ICL-TSVD uses embedding dimension \(E=10^5\) while RanPAC uses the default \(E=10^4\). This is not a fair comparison. Figure 8 and Table 13 show ICL-TSVD does not outperform RanPAC on about half of the datasets (R3).
- No comparison with other CL baselines in terms of training time is provided. SVD-type algorithms typically have high computational cost; is ICL-TSVD still faster than methods without SVD? (R3, Q3). Figure 5 does not plot training time for RanPAC at \(E=50\mathrm{K},100\mathrm{K}\); is this because the training process is too slow? (R3, Q2)
- The time complexity analysis should appear immediately after the algorithm, not as a side note on page 9. Space complexity should also be specified. Moreover, given enough tasks, \(M_t = k_{t-1}+m_t\) can exceed \(E\), making time complexity \(E^3\) just like RanPAC (R4).

**Reproducibility and significance**
- No measure of uncertainty (standard deviation) is reported in the main results (Table 1); improvements upon RanPAC are often small, and it is unclear if they are statistically significant (R2, R4).
- The cause of RanPAC instability attributed to double descent (Lines 202–208) needs more explanation (R2).
- Why does RanPAC achieve only 1.19% final accuracy on StanfordCars (Table 2)? (R3, Q1)
- Why do both RanPAC and ICL-TSVD outperform the \(LC(H_{1:T})\) benchmark trained with cross-entropy? Early stopping might help; could better hyperparameter tuning be needed? Were training and test curves checked? (R4, Q1)
- Figure 4a: x-axis scaling is unclear; what is the maximal truncation percentage examined? One would expect a sharp decrease at high truncation percentages, but it is not apparent. Why can so much be truncated without performance loss despite eigenvalues not decreasing quickly? (R4, Q2)

**Algorithmic and presentation issues**
- The continual implementation of \(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_t\) uses \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_t = [\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{1:t-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{1:t-1}, \widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_t]\). Why not use \(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_t = [\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{1:t-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{1:t-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}_{1:t-1}, \widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_t]\)? What is the difference between keeping only \(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\) and \(\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\) versus keeping all SVD factors? The motivation is unclear (R3).
- Notation used in Lines 242–243 has not been introduced yet (R2).
- In Eq. (5), the denominator should be \(\max_{1\leq i\leq t-1}\mu_{k_{i+1}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_i\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_i^\top)\) instead of \(\mu_{k_i+1}\) (R3, minor).
- Constant values \(\lambda = 10^4\) and \(\lambda = 10^{-5}\) are called “very small” or “very large” without referencing the data scale; the main benefit of truncation is remedying the condition number, not simply removing “small” singular values (R4).
- Figure 1c would be better showing training accuracy (R4).
- Step 3 in Algorithm 1 should use the floor operator \(\lfloor (1-\zeta)M_t \rfloor\) (R4).
- Dimensions of matrices in Eq. (3) (e.g., \(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}},\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}},\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{1:t}\)) should be specified when first introduced (R4).
- The sentence in Line 319 about the values of \(\delta\) is unclear (R4).
- The message of Figure 6 is unclear (R4).
- In Theorem 1, \(\frac{M_t-k_t}{M_t}\) is simply \((1-\zeta)\); specify the subscript for the norm used for \(\mathcal{E}_{1:t}\) (R4).
- The presentation is often overcomplicated and inconsistent: e.g., a random projection matrix \(P\) is introduced in Eq. (1) but most of the analysis ignores it and deals with arbitrary deterministic feature matrices \(\mathbf{H}_t\). Presenting the ICL details after the algorithm itself might be clearer (R4).
