## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces TrendDiff, a two-phase constraint-based algorithm to detect trend-influenced variables in time series and differentiate intrinsic (real) trends from measurement (false) trends. Phase 1 builds on CD-NOD by using a time-index surrogate variable to identify variables whose causal mechanisms change with time. Phase 2 classifies these as intrinsic or measurement trends based on conditional independence structures and applies Savitzky–Golay filtering for detrending. Experiments on synthetic data (with predominantly sinusoidal “trends”) and a real environmental health dataset demonstrate that removing measurement trends improves causal graph recovery.

### Strengths
- The problem of distinguishing intrinsic and measurement trends in time series causal discovery is interesting and practically important, with relevance in finance, environmental science, and healthcare (R1, R2, R4).  
- The two-phase approach (detection followed by classification) is intuitive and effectively employs conditional independence tests and Savitzky–Golay filtering (R1, R2, R4).  
- Causal graph illustrations clearly convey the structural differences between intrinsic and measurement trends (R4).  
- Model assumptions are stated in a comprehensive manner (R1).  
- Extensive experiments on both synthetic and real data incorporate both types of trends, and the real case study (with a clear measurement trend) is well chosen (R1, R2, R3, R4). The demonstration that removing only measurement trends improves causal discovery is a nice touch (R4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Novelty and attribution**: The paper is heavily based on CD‑NOD (Huang et al., 2020). Algorithm 1 and Theorem 1 are taken almost entirely from that work, but the citation gives the impression that only step 4 is new (R1, R2, R3). The main novelty (Phase 2, Algorithm 2) is relegated to the appendix and is described more as a data preprocessing step than a standalone causal discovery method (R2). R2 and R3 consider the contribution low; R1 and R4 also note the reliance on CD‑NOD but see some value in the differentiation aspect.

2. **Theoretical gaps**:  
   - Phase 2 lacks a formal theorem guaranteeing correctness (R1).  
   - Phase 1 (Algorithm 1, Theorem 1) assumes no measurement errors. If errors are present, Phase 1 results may be incorrect, yet Phase 2 depends entirely on them (R1).  

3. **Definitions and assumptions**:  
   - Definition 1 does not capture the directional (increasing/decreasing) property of trends as in White and Granger (2011), thereby confusing trend with nonstationarity (R3).  
   - Definition 3 is too broad: it would classify white noise or Brownian motion as measurement trends even though they have no tendency (R3).  
   - Definitions 2 and 3 should be more rigorous (R4).  
   - The assumption that no variable can have both intrinsic and measurement trends simultaneously is only noted at the very end; it should be stated upfront in Section 3 (R4).  
   - The phrasing “X2 and X3 are not observable” is ambiguous—it could be misinterpreted as meaning they are completely unobserved (R4).  

4. **Missing methodological details**:  
   - Construction of the final causal graph is not fully described; it is unclear whether the initial skeleton from Phase 1 is used directly or if further refinement is applied (R2).  
   - The role, configuration, and choice of the Savitzky–Golay filter are not explained (R2).  
   - How measurement trends are removed in the experiments is not described (R1, Q4).  
   - The exact nature of variable C (the time index) is ambiguous (R4).  

5. **Experimental limitations**:  
   - All synthetic trends are modeled as sinusoidal functions, which represent seasonality rather than true directional trends. This conflates seasonality with trend and may cause Phase 1 to detect seasonality, undermining the paper’s objective (R2, R3, R4). No evaluation is performed with non‑periodic trends (e.g., exponential growth or decay) (R4).  
   - No baseline methods for causal discovery under measurement errors are compared (R1). Comparisons against JPCMCI+ and CDANS (which use time‑index variables and handle time lags) are missing (R2).  
   - Figure 6(b) uses the PC algorithm to compare performance before and after measurement‑trend removal. PC assumes i.i.d. and is not suited for time‑series data, reducing the validity of these results (R1, R2).  
   - There are no experimental results on the accuracy of identifying measurement‑trend variables (similar to Fig 6a for intrinsic trends) (R1, Q1).  
   - Typo: “Guassian” should be “Gaussian” in the Fig 6a legend (R4).  

6. **Potential inconsistencies and conflicts**:  
   - Algorithm 2 defines measurement‑trend variables as the set of time‑trend variables after removing identified intrinsic‑trend variables. Because intrinsic‑trend identification is only partial, the remaining set may contain both intrinsic and measurement trends (R1, Q2). This calls into question the validity of the before‑vs‑after experiments in Fig 6(b) (R1, Q3).  
   - If measurement trends affect Phase 1 correctness, then applying Algorithm 1 after detrending could yield higher accuracy, but this creates a potential cyclic dependency that the paper does not address (R1, Q5).  

7. **Scope and generalizability**:  
   - The model assumes instantaneous causal relations and does not handle lagged effects, which are important in domains like health impact of pollution (R2).  
   - The distinction between intrinsic and measurement trends is not generalized to SDE‑based SEMs (e.g., Dynamic Structural Causal Models), which are relevant for financial modeling (R4, question).
