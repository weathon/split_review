## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Learned Proximal Networks (LPN) that guarantee the parameterization of a proximal operator for a data-driven regularizer, and a proximal matching training strategy that provably recovers the log-prior of the true data distribution. The learned operator is deployed in plug-and-play proximal gradient algorithms (PGD/ADMM) for inverse problems with convergence guarantees. Experiments are conducted on toy data, MNIST, CelebA, and Mayo-CT.

### Strengths
- Novel approach to learn a MAP denoiser for PnP, addressing the prevalent issue of using MMSE denoisers despite convergence results favoring MAP denoisers (Reviewer 1).
- To the best of Reviewer 2’s knowledge, this is the first principled approach to train proximal maps of the log probability and effectively approximate MAP estimation. Reviewer 2 was more positive, stating that numerical results are well-executed and promising.
- The idea to parameterize a denoiser as the gradient of an Input-Convex Neural Network (ICNN) is new in the PnP literature (Reviewer 4).
- The proximal matching loss is the most interesting contribution (Reviewer 4).
- The verification of the nonconvexity of the learned prior is interesting (Reviewer 4).
- Theoretical results are provided, including convergence proofs (Reviewers 1, 3). Experiments show effectiveness (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
**Theoretical issues** (major, noted by Reviewer 4 with high confidence):  
- The end of the proof of Theorem 4.1 is not true; the definition of subdifferential for nonconvex PGD should be the limiting subdifferential. The first equivalence in equation (C.48) does not hold when φ_θ is nonconvex – only the implication holds. Therefore the algorithm cannot converge to a fixed-point as presented, but only to a critical point of h + η⁻¹ φ_θ.  
- The targeted objective function h + η⁻¹ φ_θ contains the stepsize (as a regularization parameter), which is uncommon and undesirable – a change in stepsize changes the objective and the obtained result. Moreover, the stepsize must be bounded by 1/L; the impact of this limitation in practice is unclear.  
- The algorithm analyzed is classical PGD (Forward‑Backward), yet the authors use convergence analysis from (Bot et al., 2016) which is specific to an accelerated scheme. Standard results from (Attouch et al., 2013) for nonconvex PGD could be used directly.  
- “Definable” is an important notion that is not defined, and stability properties (sum, composition, inverse, derivative) are used without proof. Similarly, the exponential’s definability is not justified.  
- Experiments use PnP-ADMM while the theoretical result is for PGD. This is a major issue, as the convergence results of ADMM in the nonconvex setting differ from those of PGD.  
- Lemma C.2: f_θ may only be invertible on Im(f_θ) and φ_θ only differentiable on Im(f_θ); it is unclear if this holds on the whole space.

**Assumptions about the prior** (Reviewer 1):  
- Optimizing the proximal loss (eq. 3.4) assumes the prior is a mixture of Gaussians (or Diracs when γ→0). Why is this a good prior? It seems too simplistic, and in the limit γ→0 it becomes a discontinuous prior.  
- If convexity is enforced on the prior (as suggested in Sections 2 and 3), why is that a proper assumption? Image priors are likely nonconvex. The logic thread from convex to strongly convex is easily lost.

**Experimental weaknesses**:  
- Comparisons are mostly with older methods. Missing comparisons with recent state-of-the-art unsupervised methods, e.g., score‑based diffusion models for inverse problems (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). Reviewer 2 also notes the absence of a comparison with training an energy‑based model and then computing the proximal map of -log E.  
- The datasets (toy, MNIST, CelebA) are small; deep learning often requires substantial data (Reviewer 3).  
- No verification on common real-world tasks such as real denoising or deblurring (Reviewer 3).  
- Training efficiency compared to other methods is not reported (Reviewer 3).  
- Hyperparameter details are missing: how to learn the non‑negative weights in the ICNN? How to set α in φ_θ and η in Theorem 4.1? (Reviewer 3).  
- It is “hidden” in the appendix that LPN is first trained with an l1‑loss and then with the proximal matching loss. This should be stated clearly in the main text. Why is the l1 pre‑training needed? How bad is LPN trained only with the proximal matching loss? (Reviewer 2).  
- Reviewer 1 finds it contradictory that PSNR values are higher for LPN compared to MMSE‑denoiser methods; MAP should give sharper images with lower PSNR (higher MSE), while MMSE minimizes MSE. Also, qualitative results on CelebA look similar across methods and are blurry, which is surprising for a MAP denoiser.  
- In most PnP algorithms, the Gaussian noise parameter σ acts as a regularization parameter; it is not mentioned here and how it is chosen (Reviewer 4).

**Clarity and organization**:  
- The paper is not well organized, making it hard to follow (Reviewers 1, 3).  
- It is unclear whether the final implementation enforces convexity on the prior (Reviewer 1).  
- Algorithm details could be more elaborated in the main text; differences from score‑based diffusion generative algorithms for inverse problems should be pointed out (Reviewer 1).  
- Terminology “proximal operator of target distribution” is used but left undefined and is uncommon (Reviewer 2).  
- Typos: “demillustrates” (should be “illustrates”), abstract “demonstrating” should be “Demonstrating” (Reviewers 2, 3).  
- Equation (3.2) may contain a typo (should be ψ_θ(x,α)?) (Reviewer 2).  
- Section 1: “for almost any inverse problem, a proximal step for the regularization function is always present” is unclear; in (Romano et al., 2017) there is no prox involved (Reviewer 4).  
- Section 2: PnP is not limited to ADMM (Reviewer 4).  
- Section 3: Parameterizing a network as the gradient of an ICNN has already been proposed in the Optimal Transport literature – those works should be cited (Reviewer 4).  
- The claim that the chosen parameterization is more general and universal than (Hurault et al., 2022) is doubtful; the ICNN parameterization is very constraining. This should be supported by a performance comparison (Reviewer 4).  

**Contrast among reviewers**: Reviewer 2 (score 8) considered the work a principled advance with promising results, whereas the other three reviewers (scores 5,5,5) had major concerns about theoretical validity, experimental rigor, and clarity. The theoretical concerns from Reviewer 4 (confidence 5) are particularly severe.
