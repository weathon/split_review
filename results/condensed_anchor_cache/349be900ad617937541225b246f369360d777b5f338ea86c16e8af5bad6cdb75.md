## Merged Review

### Summary

Janus introduces a dual-server secure aggregation (SA) scheme for federated learning that aims to address dynamic user participation, model inconsistency attacks (MIA), and verifiability. It separates aggregation tasks: one server handles masked gradients, the other aggregates masks, so neither alone has access to the final result. A new primitive, Separable Homomorphic Commitment (SHC), enables client-side verification. The scheme achieves constant per-client communication and computation overhead (vs. logarithmic in Flamingo/BBSA). The four reviewers are split: two rate it 6 (positive), two rate it 3 (negative). The positive reviewers praise the dual-server concept and SHC novelty, while the negative reviewers question the threat model assumptions, comparison breadth, experimental rigor, and the cryptographic soundness of SHC.

### Strengths

- **Dual-server architecture for MIA protection**: The idea of preventing either server from accessing the final aggregation result, thereby defending against model inconsistency attacks, is novel and interesting (R1, R2). It helps ensure that a server cannot launch MIA without controlling ≥ n−1 clients (R1).
- **Separable Homomorphic Commitment (SHC)**: This new primitive allows clients to verify the correctness of aggregation without heavy computational cost, and the integration with the dual‑server model is seamless (R1, R2).
- **Reduced overhead**: Per-client communication and computation are reduced from logarithmic to constant scale compared to Flamingo and BBSA, making the scheme more practical for large-scale settings (R1). Comprehensive end-to-end evaluation with model training supports this claim (R3).
- **Clear and well-organized presentation**: The paper explains complex concepts effectively and uses useful visual aids (R4). It addresses scalability challenges of privacy-preserving federated learning (R3).

### Weaknesses

- **Unrealistic non-collusion assumptions** (majority reviewers criticize):
  - **Server collusion** (R1, R3, R4): The scheme relies heavily on the assumption that the two servers do not collude. If they collude, they could reconstruct individual client gradients by combining masked gradients and masks, bypassing all privacy protections. The paper does not sufficiently address this scenario or its implications.
  - **Client collusion** (R3, R4): The assumption that clients do not collude is unrealistic when clients are interested in each other’s updates. The scheme does not specify how many colluding users it can tolerate (R4) or how it prevents a provider from colluding with even one client (or introducing a Sybil client) to gain access to the aggregated model (R3).
  - **Semi-honest server assumption** (R3): Assuming servers are semi-honest makes achieving verifiability trivial. A stronger threat model (malicious servers) would be more appropriate.
- **Missing comparisons with state-of-the-art schemes** (R2, R3, R4):
  - No comparison with multi-server approaches such as 2-party MPC or **ELSA (S&P’23)**, which also provide low client overhead independent of client count (R3).
  - No comparison with single-server verifiable schemes like **VeriFL** that achieve similar privacy and verifiability (R4).
  - The existing comparisons (BBSA, Flamingo) are too limited to fully contextualize Janus’s performance (R2). Additional baseline methods are needed.
- **Insufficient experimental evaluation** (R2, R4):
  - Need tests on more datasets, models (complex architectures, larger data volumes), and varying network conditions (latency, bandwidth) (R2, R4).
  - Scalability with respect to client count and model size is not demonstrated (current experiments use only 100 clients) (R2, R4).
  - Missing empirical data on communication overhead and detailed breakdown of computational costs (e.g., SHC operations, encryption, aggregation) (R2, R4).
  - No statistical significance analysis, number of repetitions, or validation of resistance to MIA and multi-round security (R4).
  - The evaluation of model accuracy and loss is standard; secure aggregation schemes are not expected to differ, so these results do not highlight Janus’s unique advantages (R3).
- **Fault tolerance and dropout handling** (R1, R4):
  - The paper does not explain how the system handles server failures (no fallback or recovery strategy) (R1).
  - User dropout can disrupt the entire aggregation process; no mechanism for partial failures or high dropout rates is provided (R4). Experiments should test varying dropout rates, including >10% (R4).
- **Cryptographic and security proof issues** (R1, R3, R4):
  - The SHC primitive lacks detailed specification and clear notation; the separation of commitments is not explained intuitively enough to assess security (R1).
  - SHC appears to be a variant of existing commitment schemes without substantive innovation; it lacks essential properties like trapdoor mechanisms and equivalence that are present in schemes used by VeriFL (R4). If a simple hash-based commitment is used, length extension attacks may be possible (R4).
  - The security proof contains inaccuracies: Theorem 1 references two ideal functionalities (Figures 6 & 7) but Figure 7 is underdefined; the distinction in scenarios (“whether the servers are corrupted by A”) might incorrectly imply both servers could be corrupted simultaneously, contradicting the stated assumptions (R3).
  - No rigorous formal treatment or sufficient security proofs are provided for SHC (R4).
  - Potential vulnerabilities: If an attacker obtains masked data from two rounds with similar inputs, differential analysis could infer changes in original inputs (R4). Timing attacks or side-channel attacks are not considered (R2).
- **Missing implementation and reproducibility details** (R4):
  - Parameter settings for Janus and comparative schemes (BBSA, Flamingo) are not thoroughly documented.
  - Implementation details for the baselines are insufficient, hampering reproducibility.
- **Unclear properties** (R3, R4):
  - The “versatility” property in Table 1 is not defined (R3).
  - The scheme does not specify the maximum number of colluding users it can tolerate without compromising security (R4).
  - The question of how new users join (and whether a provider can set up Sybil clients) is not adequately addressed (R3).
