## Merged Review

### Summary
DASB is a benchmark for discrete audio tokens across discriminative (ASR, speaker ID/verification, emotion recognition, keyword spotting, intent classification) and generative (speech enhancement, separation, TTS) tasks. Tokens are categorized as semantic, compression, or hybrid. The benchmark provides a unified pipeline with open-source code. Evaluations show semantic tokens generally outperform compression tokens, but a significant gap remains relative to continuous representations.

### Strengths
- **Addresses an important and timely problem**: Evaluating discrete audio token quality is increasingly relevant for audio-language models. (All reviewers)
- **Broad coverage of tasks**: The benchmark includes a wide range of speech and audio tasks, covering both discriminative and generative applications. (R1, R2, R4)
- **Open-source unified pipeline**: A standardized evaluation framework with open-source implementations enables fair comparisons and ease of use for the community. (R1, R3)
- **Clear presentation**: The paper provides a straightforward explanation of tasks, architectures, and datasets, making results easy to follow. (R2, R3, R4)
- **Large number of experiments**: Substantial experimental effort across multiple token types and tasks. (R2)
- **Results appear mostly reasonable**: Overall trends (e.g., semantic vs. compression) are in line with expectations. (R3)
- **Potential for benchmarking across speech, music, and sound**: The project includes not only speech but also broader audio. (R1)

### Weaknesses
- **Unfair comparison conditions**:
  - Training data differs across token types, and the decoder (vocoder) for semantic tokens is only trained on LibriSpeech 960h, making benchmarks on music and sound unfair. (R2)
  - All semantic tokens are tested with a single decoder; differences may reflect decoder quality, not token quality. (R2)
- **Limited novelty and overlap with prior benchmarks**:
  - Experimental design and datasets largely mirror SUPERB, Codec-SUPERB, and SUPERB-SG, offering limited novelty. (R2)
  - Several similar open-source projects have appeared recently (e.g., Codec-SUPERB@SLT 2024, ESPnet-Codec); the paper does not clearly differentiate DASB’s unique advantages. (R1)
- **Inability to isolate token quality**:
  - Each downstream task includes multiple trainable components (e.g., prediction heads, decoders), making it impossible to attribute results solely to the tokenizer. (R4)
  - Results are reported as a single run without standard deviations; no analysis of run-to-run variability. (R4)
- **Lack of deep insight and counterintuitive results**:
  - Most conclusions are already common sense (e.g., semantic tokens capture content, compression tokens capture paralinguistics). (R2)
  - High bitrates (e.g., DAC at higher bps) do not consistently outperform medium bitrates, suggesting possible implementation/hyperparameter issues. (R2)
  - SSL baselines (e.g., WavLM, HuBERT) show best speaker identification/verification performance, which seems unexpected for semantic features. (R2)
- **Limited coverage of audio/music and hybrid tokens**:
  - Audio and music tasks are few and poorly described. (R3)
  - Only one hybrid tokenizer (SpeechTokenizer) is included; many recent hybrid tokenizers are omitted. (R3)
  - For audio events, a pre-trained CNN14 is used instead of transformer-based SSL models pre-trained on general audio (e.g., AudioSet). (R4)
  - No discrete HuBERT/WavLM results for audio and music tasks (Tables 5,6 use CNN14/MERT). (R3)
- **Missing experimental details and inconsistencies**:
  - The continuous baseline (used for comparison) is not explicitly defined. (R2)
  - Which continuous SSL models are used in Tables 2–4? (R3)
  - K-means cluster size (K) for extracting tokens from WavLM/HuBERT is not reported; may significantly affect results. (R4)
  - The selection of layers (1,3,7,12,18,23) for WavLM/HuBERT token extraction is not justified. (R4)
  - Hyperparameters (batch size, learning rate) across models are not stated; consistency is unclear. (R4)
  - Hardware reported inconsistently: paper mentions RTX 3070 (8GB VRAM) for time/memory, but Appendix A1 says tasks run on 32GB+ GPUs. (R4)
  - BERT is incorrectly cited as an autoregressive model. (R4)
  - The citation for speech enhancement with discrete tokens (Goodfellow et al., 2016) is not pertinent. (R4)
- **Metric selection concerns**:
  - For speech enhancement, DNSMOS is used instead of standard PESQ, without justification. (R4)
  - For TTS, automatic MOS prediction is used; its correlation with human ratings is not provided and is known to be limited. (R3)
  - For ASR on two low-resource languages, all models perform poorly; phoneme recognition would be a more discriminative and stable alternative. (R3)
- **Practical and deployment issues**:
  - Inference time can be long (up to 8h for ASR); a smaller development set would aid quick experiments. (R1)
  - No leaderboard or computational cost comparison is provided. (R1)
  - No guidance on selecting a universally effective codec when performance varies across tasks. (R1)
  - Results contradict recent findings (e.g., DAC vs. Encodec on ESC-50); no explanation for discrepancies. (R4)
- **Presentation errors**:
  - The abstract uses “semantic” and “compression” tokens before defining them in Section 3.1. (R3)
  - Incorrect appendix index at the beginning of Section 4. (R3)
