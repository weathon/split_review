## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents IMAC, a co-speech gesture video generation method using a two-branch framework that couples an audio-to-motion branch (predicting SMPL-X pose/expression parameters) with a video diffusion branch conditioned on those motion parameters and a reference image. A two-stage slow-fast training strategy (80-frame long sequences then 16-frame short sequences) is introduced to balance memory and learning long gestures. A new dataset of talk show videos (4 hosts, 33 hours) is collected. Experiments on this dataset claim state-of-the-art performance. Reviewers are split: two rate 3 (weak reject), two rate 5 (accept). The main concerns are limited novelty, repetitive/implausible gestures, insufficient comparisons, dataset questionable, and lack of rigorous validation of key claims.

### Strengths
- The two-branch architecture (audio-to-motion + motion-to-video) is a sound and practical way to handle individualized gesture patterns in video generation. (R2)
- Generated videos show improved background consistency compared to prior methods. (R1)
- Hand and shoulder regions are generated with high image quality, and hand movements appear clearer than in baselines. (R3, R4)
- The paper is well-structured, readable, and presents a clear motivation for avoiding explicit visual controls. (R3)
- Training details (e.g., two-stage scheme) are clearly described and easy to reimplement. (R4)
- The hypothesis that explicit visual control may degrade video diffusion quality is interesting and worth further investigation. (R3)

### Weaknesses
1. **Overstated novelty and limited contribution.** The use of SMPL-X parameters as motion conditions already exists in the MYA framework; IMAC’s pipeline of generating motion then using it as control signal for video diffusion is widely used and not novel. (R1, R3) Moreover, the architecture largely adapts recent audio-driven talking head methods (e.g., Hallo, MuseTalk) to gesture generation without providing new insights. (R4)
2. **Repetitive and implausible gesture patterns.** Generated videos contain repetitive rhythmic motions, suggesting overfitting rather than semantically meaningful gestures. (R1, R4) In several examples, significant body/hand gestures occur even during silent segments, causing visual dissonance (specific files: raw+noah_cropped+cmu0000040960_0000907-0001165_final.mp4, raw+noah_cropped+cmu0000041241_0000401-0000561_final.mp4, videos 1 and 4 of John Oliver in comparisons.html). (R2) The lip synchronization is also poor: the speaker moves his mouth even when only background audio is present. (R4)
3. **Dataset concerns.** The proposed dataset is a subset (33 hours) of an existing 144-hour dataset (Ginosar et al., “Learning Individual Styles of Gestures”) using the same TV show anchors; it is neither novel nor large-scale. (R1) The benefits over existing datasets (e.g., Berkeley Speech-Gesture Dataset with 3D annotations via Habibie et al.) are unclear. (R2) Additionally, the lack of experiments on public benchmarks makes it impossible to fairly compare IMAC against all existing methods. (R2, R3)
4. **Key hypothesis unvalidated.** The central claim that explicit visual control (pose images, TPS) degrades video generation quality is not directly tested in the ablation studies; a proper comparison between explicit and implicit control signals is missing. (R2, R3, R4)
5. **Insufficient experiments and baselines.** Comparisons are limited to too few methods, and separate evaluations of the audio-to-motion and motion-to-video branches against baselines (e.g., feeding identical motion conditions to MYA) are absent. (R1, R3) Ablation results (Table 3) raise unexplained inconsistencies: BAS values are close to the full model while FGD and FVD are an order-of-magnitude worse, and ablating first-stage training degrades FGD more than ablating the motion parameter branch—a result that contradicts intuition if motion parameters provide stronger conditioning. (R2)
6. **Technical description lacks rigor.** Many components are described only at a verbal level without mathematical or visual detail. Examples: how the reference image encoder preserves identity (is the network trained per individual? does it require retraining for new subjects?); what constitutes high- and low-level image features (Line 227); why explicit visual control leads to suboptimal quality and how system complexity compares to prior work (Line 236); how cross-attention diminishes audio information (Line 252) yet is used to pass combined features (Line 258). (R2)
7. **Temporal discontinuities not addressed.** In inference, a sliding window generates short clips sequentially; reviewers question whether this causes unnatural transitions or discontinuity between clips and how this issue is handled. (R3, R4)
8. **Missing efficiency comparisons.** Training and inference times relative to baselines are not reported, a known concern for diffusion models. (R2, R3, R4)
9. **Ethical and bias issues.** Using videos of well-known talk show hosts raises legal/privacy questions. The model can generate novel speech with the likeness of these public figures, posing potential for misuse. Moreover, all subjects are male and share the same profession, introducing unclear bias in generative performance. (R2)
10. **Limited scope.** The method is restricted to single-person videos, only four individuals, fixed background, and front-view only; these limitations should be explicitly discussed. (R2)
