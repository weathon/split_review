## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces Omni-MATH, a new Olympiad-level mathematical reasoning benchmark comprising 4,428 competition-level problems with rigorous human annotation across 33 sub-domains and over 10 difficulty levels. The authors evaluate 15 LLMs, finding that even the strongest models (OpenAI o1-mini, o1-preview) achieve only 60.54% and 52.55% accuracy, revealing significant challenges. They also introduce Omni-Judge, an open-source answer evaluator, and conduct analyses including best-of-N scaling, difficulty consistency, and data leakage. The dataset’s large size and fine-grained categorization make it a potential resource for evaluating advanced mathematical reasoning.

### Strengths
- **Novel high-difficulty benchmark**: Omni-MATH pushes beyond saturated datasets like GSM8K and MATH by focusing on Olympiad-level problems, adding a valuable resource for assessing LLM reasoning limits.
- **Fine-grained categorization**: Problems are labeled into 33 sub-domains and >10 difficulty levels, enabling detailed performance and error analysis (e.g., models struggle with discrete math but do better on algebra/calculus). This detailed structure aids identifying domain-specific strengths/weaknesses.
- **Comprehensive model analysis**: Experiments include 15 LLMs with thorough analysis of best-of-N scaling, difficulty consistency, and domain interactions; findings reveal limitations of test-time scaling methods (e.g., Best-of-N) and guide future research.
- **Reliability study**: Careful investigation of metrics including data leakage and LLM-based judgment reliability; GPT-4o achieves 98% alignment with human annotators (based on a 100-sample subset), Omni-Judge achieves 91% consistency with GPT-4o and 86% with human judgment.
- **Effective Omni-Judge verifier**: The open-source verifier addresses the need for sophisticated answer validation for complex Olympiad problems, improving upon rule-based methods.
- **Clarity and organization**: The paper is well-written and easy to follow; experiments are comprehensive and the analysis is thorough.

### Weaknesses
- **Evaluation reliability concerns**: Key details about the GPT-4o vs. human alignment are missing: whether the 100 samples came from the same LLM, inter-annotator agreement among human judges, number of student judges involved, and specific failure examples (what reasons?). This undermines the 98% claim.
- **Contradictory numbers on Omni-Judge**: The paper reports Omni-Judge achieves 91% consistency with GPT-4o and 86% with human judgments. If GPT-4o aligns with humans on 98/100 examples (98%), then Omni-Judge’s 91% agreement with GPT-4o would imply at most 2 of those 91 could be wrong, giving at least 89/100 (89%) human alignment, not 86%. This discrepancy suggests at most 95% human alignment for GPT-4o, calling into question the reliability of results in Table 2.
- **Insufficient verifier training details**: The training process of Omni-Judge is not clearly explained (e.g., data, architecture, loss). Further clarification is needed.
- **Cost and feasibility of model-based judging**: Using GPT-4o or Omni-Judge is computationally/monetarily expensive. A more practical approach would be standardizing answer formats (like MATH) with rule-based cleaning and matching, using model-based judging only as supplementary.
- **Data leakage risk**: Some evaluated models (NuminaMath, Qwen-MATH series) use AoPS examples in their SFT data, which may overlap with Omni-MATH problems. The paper should explicitly measure overlap and discuss impact. In Table 3, the "Prop." and "Avg.D." columns are unexplained; for Qwen-2.5-MATH-instruct, data leakage appears severe and needs clarification.
- **Missing analysis for RM variants**: The paper does not explain why the RM-version of Qwen2.5-MATH does not outperform the vanilla version, and why Qwen2.5-MATH RM@256 does not outperform RM@8. This phenomenon needs further investigation.
- **Need for advanced test-time search**: The test-time scaling analysis only uses best-of-N. For harder competition-level problems, advanced search algorithms (e.g., tree search or MCTS) should be discussed or benchmarked.
- **Incomplete dataset presentation**: More examples of the dataset should be provided in the main paper or appendix to let readers assess problem format and difficulty.
- **Missing experimental details**: The few-shot evaluation prompt is not provided (Section 4.3). The best-of-1 results are not reported; the exact value of N in Best-of-N is unclear. In Table 3, the notations "Prop." and "Avg.D." are not defined. For discrete math problems, could solutions be written in code and executed to verify correctness?
- **Minor presentation errors**: Line 79 should say "shown in Table 2"; lines 241–242 reference "Figure 4" but it is not present. Also, the annotation principles (line 831–834) and the cross-validation process (line 203) are not sufficiently explained. The education background of students involved in annotation (graduate/doctoral) is not reported.
