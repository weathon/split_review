## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces the Sinkhorn-Newton-Sparse (SNS) algorithm for entropic regularized optimal transport. It combines early-stopped Sinkhorn iterations with a Newton-type subroutine that uses a sparsified Hessian. The authors prove that after some Sinkhorn steps, the Hessian of the Lyapunov potential becomes nearly sparse, enabling a Newton step with O(n²) per-iteration complexity—the same as Sinkhorn. Numerical results show orders of magnitude faster convergence on empirical distributions and Wasserstein distance computations.

### Strengths
- Sparsification yields O(n²) per-iteration complexity, matching Sinkhorn.
- Orders of magnitude faster convergence across a wide range of practical cases (empirical distributions, Wasserstein W₁, W₂).
- A rigorous bound on the approximate sparsity of the Hessian is provided (Theorem 2).
- Clear motivation for second-order methods and a clean link between Sinkhorn iterations and Hessian sparsity.
- The method is clearly explained and the results should be easy to reproduce.
- Notable reduction in both iteration count and time to convergence.
- The definition of sparsity plays a key role in the analysis.
- The algorithm is theoretically motivated by Theorem 1.

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical results are simple corollaries of existing work.** For example, Eq. (7), crucial to Theorem 1, follows from Corollary 9 of (Weed, 2018). Some reviewers question the novelty of the theoretical contributions.
- **Theorem 1 requires large η**, but the paper does not clarify how large η affects convergence or why one would not simply use the unregularized problem. The condition and its practical implications need discussion.
- **No convergence analysis for the SNS algorithm itself.** The claim of “super-exponential convergence” is unsupported—no theoretical guarantees are provided for the combined Sinkhorn-Newton scheme.
- **Missing theoretical guarantees on computational complexity.** Numerical performance lacks theoretical backing regarding runtime or iteration bounds.
- **The approximate sparsity definition is not well motivated.** The connection to standard Frobenius-norm sparsity is unclear. Why approximate the Hessian M with a sparse \(\bar M\) instead of working directly with M? The notion that τ is merely the proportion of nonzero elements needs clarification.
- **Limited evaluation: only a single regularization parameter η=1200.** This is likely a weak regularization term that yields sparse couplings, favoring the method. Experiments should include several strengths (e.g., η=10,100,1000) to assess generalizability. Standard deviations should also be reported in tables.
- **No comparison to other current methods** beyond Sinkhorn and Newton. The paper should benchmark against recent advances such as the Nyström method (Altschuler et al., 2019). The possibility of using quasi-Newton methods should also be discussed.
- **Ablation: what if the Sinkhorn stage is removed (N₁=0)?** It is unclear how much sparsity is due to the initial Sinkhorn steps (Theorem 1) versus the explicit “Sparsify” step in Algorithm 1. This would clarify the source of the speedup.
- **Several minor issues and unanswered questions:**
  - How to choose the number of Sinkhorn steps N₁? Could switching to Newton be triggered when the Hessian is sparse enough?
  - The optimality gap in Fig. 1 requires a ground truth; how is it computed (e.g., the unregularized OT plan)?
  - The target sparsity λ is not explicitly referenced in Algorithm 1.
  - In Section 2, α should presumably be η.
  - η in Eq. (1) needs a proper definition (it should be >0).
  - A preliminary/background section would improve readability.
  - Suggested additional references: Scetbon et al. (2021, 2020) on low-rank Sinkhorn, Huguet et al. (2023) on geodesic Sinkhorn, and Mallasto et al. (2022) on closed-form Gaussian results for accuracy comparisons.
