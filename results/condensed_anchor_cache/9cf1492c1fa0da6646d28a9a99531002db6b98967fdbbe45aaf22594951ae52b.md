## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces the Re-TASK framework, which applies Bloom’s Taxonomy and Knowledge Space Theory to LLM prompting. It decomposes tasks into capability items (further split into knowledge and skills), integrates domain-specific knowledge injection and skill adaptation with Chain-of-Thought, and proposes a structured prompting strategy. Experiments on three datasets—Chinese legal sentencing, FinanceIQ, and MMLU high school math—using open-source LLMs (Yi-1.5-9B, Llama3-Chinese-8b, ~30B models) show improvements, notably 44.42% and 33.08% gains on the legal dataset. Manual curation or LLM-based generation is used to construct capability items.

### Strengths
- [Novelty] Integrating ideas from education and cognitive science (Bloom’s Taxonomy, Knowledge Space Theory) into LLM prompting is original.
- [Theory] The framework’s design is motivated by learning theory, providing a theoretical foundation; the distinction between knowledge and skill acquisition is conceptually interesting.
- [Significance] Prompting strategies have large impacts on LLM performance; the topic is important.
- [Results] Results are promising for domain-specific applications, especially in the law domain.

### Weaknesses
- [Manual Effort & Scalability] The framework requires significant manual effort and domain expertise to identify capability items, decompose tasks, and apply all structured prompting techniques. The process of constructing capabilities seems relatively manual, limiting scalability to broader, less-defined tasks. Even Section 4.3 on automatically constructing capability items lacks sufficient detail; this part is critical for broader applications. (Reviewer 1, 2)
- [Limited Model Scope] Experiments are limited to open-source models. It is not clear why a proprietary model like GPT-4 was not included to validate the framework across a wider range of LLMs. (Reviewer 1)
- [Limited Baselines] Only CoT is used as a baseline. Other applicable methods (e.g., Generated Knowledge Prompting [Liu et al.]) are not compared, and the paper does not clarify how the approach is distinct from existing RAG or knowledge injection methods. (Reviewer 1, 2)
- [Gains and Surprise] It is not surprising that hints (capability items) from ~70B LLMs improve performance of ~7B models; gains on ~30B models are much more modest. The framework may need predefined knowledge assumptions, which could limit flexibility. (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 1)
- [Limited Evaluation & Generalizability] Most evaluation uses custom datasets, making it hard to compare with related methods on general tasks. Two of three domains are limited to Chinese datasets and LLMs, weakening the results. The experiments should include many more benchmarks (e.g., full MMLU, GPQA, MATH, HumanEval-Python, Arena-Hard). (Reviewer 2, 3)
- [Training Adaptation] An important axis for prompting methods is whether they can improve training (e.g., CoT reformulates training data). The proposed Re-TASK framework seems too complicated and difficult to adapt for training. (Reviewer 2)
- [Clarity & Abstract Terminology] The connection to educational theories feels strained; “capability item” seems unnecessarily abstract. A clearer message would be “hints generated by a large LM improve problem solving of smaller LMs.” The paper needs more concrete examples in the main text to illustrate core components, and Figure 2 (which is high-level and abstract) should be moved earlier (e.g., to introduction) and include notation definitions (e.g., C_{ij}) and a concrete example. Section 4.4 appears misplaced. (Reviewer 2, 3)
- [Missing Details on Knowledge Generation] The paper does not specify how placeholders like “[Please Put the Knowledge Recall 1 of Subtasks Here.]” and “[Please Put the Knowledge Application of the overall Task Here.]” are filled—what retrieval or generation process is used? A detailed description of how high-quality hints are generated with large LMs (including the prompting strategy for generating capability items) is needed. (Reviewer 3)
- [Ambiguity of Skill Adaptation] The term “skill adaptation” is somewhat ambiguous; while Section 3.2 provides some clarity, a concrete real-world example would help readers understand it. (Reviewer 1)
- [Uneven Gains Across Domains] The framework shows significant gains on the law domain but smaller gains on finance and math, possibly due to manually obtained procedural knowledge, suggesting reliance on predefined knowledge. (Reviewer 1)

### Questions (from Reviewers, Overlapping with Criticisms)
- How is the approach distinct from prior RAG or knowledge injection methods (e.g., Generated Knowledge Prompting [Liu et al.])?
- Can the framework be applied to more general tasks without extensive manual effort? What is the detailed process for automatic construction of capability items (Section 4.3)?
- If a general strategy to generate high-quality hints exists, why are experiments limited to only a few benchmarks?
- How are the knowledge recall and knowledge application placeholders (Appendix Figures 19, 20) actually filled—what retrieval or generation process is used?
