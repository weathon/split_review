## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies how Transformers solve k-sparse parity problems, showing that without CoT, exponential in k samples are needed (via a lower bound for one-pass algorithms with bounded memory, even though small transformers exist), while with a CoT prompt and minibatch SGD, O(n) samples suffice. The analysis reveals that CoT induces sparse sequential dependencies and interpretable attention, and that sparsity correlates with perfect prediction. The theory is supported by numerical experiments on synthetic parity tasks and real-world finetuning of Qwen models on GSM8K.

### Strengths
- The motivation is significant: understanding how Chain-of-Thought improves sample efficiency for reasoning in LLMs.
- The theoretical setup is natural: parity learning is a well-studied testbed for neural networks and gradient methods, and its sparse structure naturally connects to sparsity in attention.
- The results clearly establish an exponential gap in sample complexity: exponential in k without CoT vs. linear in n with CoT.
- The analysis of training dynamics for a one-layer Transformer with non-linear attention and MLP is technically challenging and original.
- The observation that CoT training induces sparse attention patterns, and that this sparsity correlates with perfect prediction, is interesting and well-validated by experiments.
- The real-world experiments on Qwen models (including a math model finetuned with CoT) showing sparse attention patterns are noteworthy and extend the theory.
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow (Reviewer 3); the ideas are novel and compelling (Reviewer 4).
- The comparison between learning with and without CoT is impressive and significant for future research.

### Weaknesses
- Proof completeness and clarity: Several reviewers flagged substantial gaps. There is no full proof of Theorem 3, only a sketch and supportive lemmas; the derivation lacks rigor and sufficient detail to verify the convergence rate and conditions of the three-phase training (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4). The proof sketch for Phase 1 needs more clarity (Reviewer 1). Many specific steps are unjustified or insufficiently explained: the random matrix theory statement in Lemma conditions is hand-wavy (line 841); the O(1/n⁷) term (line 901) is unexplained; the iterative selection of a_r, b_r (line 913) is not clarified; the claim that parameters can be encoded in O(log n) precision (line 916) lacks justification; the derivation of inequalities from the Assumption (line 960) is not a valid proof (Reviewer 4). Numeric choices (m > 100 at line 831; δ/4 at line 844; 8 instead of 10 at line 896) are given without rationale (Reviewer 4). These issues make it hard to assess the validity of the theoretical results; Reviewer 3 rates soundness 2, Reviewer 4 rates soundness 3.
- The lower bound is described as a sample complexity bound but feels more like a computational lower bound; this distinction should be clarified (Reviewer 1).
- The initialization of W_{r,1:d} in Assumption 1 is non-standard and lacks sufficient justification. It is unclear whether it is needed and how it leads to "stronger correlation with the embedding" (line 273) (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 4). Reviewer 4 suggests that using fixed orthogonal embeddings (instead of random) would simplify proofs.
- Real-world experiments: the difference in entropy of attention layers is not very significant, making it difficult to draw strong conclusions (Reviewer 4).
- The parity learning setup uses fixed locations across sequences (e.g., always positions 1,2,5 for k=3). This limits generalizability; a stronger result would show learning a general parity function over arbitrary location sets, including unseen combinations during testing. The current setup does not fully capture CoT’s potential for flexible reasoning (Reviewer 3).
- Definition 2: The augmented sequence uses a permutation S; it is unclear whether S is random or fixed during training. If random, achieving perfect accuracy in Theorem 3 and the monotone pattern in Figure 3(b) seems impossible; if fixed, this needs clarification (Reviewer 2).
- The paper should include discussion of a recent related work [1] (Reviewer 2).
- Theorem 2’s condition on d, m, n, k is not satisfied for the dimensions used in the proof of Theorem 1, making it hard to relate the two results (Reviewer 4).
- Line 296 states “which only applies to one-pass training,” but Theorem 2 is claimed to apply to multipass training; this is inconsistent (Reviewer 4).
- The CoT prompt design appears to require knowing the secret set of locations, almost providing the answer; alternative prompt choices (e.g., incremental computation) should be discussed (Reviewer 1).
- The relationship to the staircase property (Abbe et al. 2023, 2024), which also yields O(n) learning of parities with fully-connected networks, is not explored; is there a link with the CoT structure? (Reviewer 1)
- The analysis uses no positional encoding; it is unclear whether adding positional encoding would reduce sample complexity in both CoT and non-CoT cases (Reviewer 3).
- Lemma 22 (line 1834) suggests only one step per phase, which implies an unrealistically fast and simple training process (Reviewer 3). Can it be extended to few-shot CoT? (Reviewer 3)
- Minor issues: line 46 (counting letters example not relevant due to tokenization); line 77 (k not introduced); line 191 (bounds sum depends on CoT/non-CoT); line 230 (should be W_{r,1:m} instead of W_{r,1:d}); line 247 typo “for any i” should be “for any i, j and b vector” (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 4).
- Overall, while some reviewers (1,2) were highly positive (scores 8), others (3,4) had substantial concerns about proof completeness and experimental significance, leading to lower scores (6).
