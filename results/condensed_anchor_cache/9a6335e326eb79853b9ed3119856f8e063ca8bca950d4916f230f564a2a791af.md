## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a continuous-time modeling approach for time series imputation and forecasting using conditional implicit neural representations (INR) with a modulation mechanism and meta-learning-based training. The model handles irregular sampling and missing data. It is evaluated on standard benchmarks for imputation and forecasting tasks, claiming state-of-the-art performance.

### Strengths
- The approach is sound and a straightforward application of Dupont et al. (2022) and Tancik et al. (2020) to time series (Reviewer 1).
- The paper is clearly written, with good elaboration of architecture design and training/test algorithms (Reviewers 2, 3).
- Time-series modeling via INR is a relatively unexplored area; the work provides insights that continuous neural functions can be beneficial for this domain (Reviewer 2).
- The paper compares against several important baselines, including recent SOTA methods (Reviewers 2, 3).
- Imputation results are decent: MAE is competitive, and Figure 3 shows good imputation compared to BRITS (Reviewer 1). Forecasting results are good, matching competitors, and the combined forecasting+imputation setting shows potential for real-world applications (Reviewer 1).
- The algorithm’s ability to be applied to previously unseen datasets/time series via auto-decoding is a definite strength (Reviewer 3). Excellent diagrams explain distinctions between training and inference for both tasks (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty.** The overall architecture follows standard FFN designs (Tancik et al., 2020) without handling multivariate time series specifically. The latent modulation and meta-learning training largely follow Dupont et al. (2022). A similar idea of temporally-refined latent variables has been explored by Yin et al. (2023). The discussion of implementation choices does not introduce fundamental time-series-specific innovations (Reviewers 1, 2; three reviewers; majority critical on this point).
- **Inconsistent performance on imputation.** The method is not consistently superior to baselines; it is usually close to BRITS and often not beating other baselines. The datasets used (e.g., Electricity) are simple and regularly sampled, which does not highlight the method’s claimed advantage for irregular sampling. Real-world irregularly sampled datasets like MIMIC should have been included (Reviewer 1).
- **Contradiction in adaptation claims.** Table 17 shows PatchTST often outperforms the proposed method, contradicting the claim that the proposed method is better at adapting to new time series. This raises concerns about generalization (Reviewer 1; also noted by Reviewer 3 about “efficient adaptation” needing further explanation).
- **Computational cost is high.** Table 16 shows the proposed method is more costly than already expensive transformer-based models, limiting practical applicability (Reviewer 1).
- **Fairness of the auto-decoding procedure during inference.** For forecasting, the method assumes a separate look-back window to collect new observations for fine-tuning (3 gradient steps). With hourly sampling, that time gap could also be used to fine-tune baseline models (e.g., last layers of Transformers), which may provide better predictions. The comparison is therefore unfair because baselines are used purely for inference while the proposed method solves an optimization problem on new data (Reviewer 2).
- **Struggles with peak predictions.** The model does not predict peaks accurately (e.g., Figure 5), which is important for applications like electricity supply/cost management. The focus on MSE may obscure this failure (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing comparisons with other time-continuous models.** Neural ODEs and their variants for irregular time series are not included as baselines (Reviewer 2).
- **Hyperparameter analysis is limited.** Sensitivity to latent dimension and number of gradient steps is only shown for one dataset (Electricity, horizon 720, look-back 512). It is unclear if these observations hold for other datasets or window sizes. Are results based on multiple runs? (Reviewer 2)
- **Lack of justification for performance degradation claim.** The paper states that transformer models “often suffer from significant performance degradation” without specifying which metrics are affected (Reviewer 3).
- **Inefficiency claim is unclear.** The phrase “less efficient than the aforementioned discrete models for regular time series” needs clarification: does it refer to sample efficiency, scalability to multiple dimensions, or longer time series? (Reviewer 3).
- **Brief conclusion and limitations discussion.** The paper ends abruptly without discussing limitations or extension to multivariate cases. Section 3.4’s list of conclusions and redirections to the appendix is weak; unnecessary dataset details could be moved to the appendix to leave space for method description (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing justification for model design choices.** The method does not investigate whether structure can be learned from latent spaces across similar datasets, which might explain why other models require full retraining (Reviewer 3).
- **Questions on applicability to arbitrary grids.** Would the method work for imputation/forecasting not on a grid? Could forecasted data be predicted on an arbitrary grid, and is this related to the discrete performance in Table 2? (Reviewer 3) — left unanswered.
- **Missing baseline inclusion.** In Section 4.3, all imputation baselines from 4.1 should also be able to produce forecasts and thus should appear in Table 2 (Reviewer 1). Also, a naive baseline (simply connecting training points with a line) should be computed for Figure 3 to contextualize imputation quality (Reviewer 1).
- **Inconsistency in Tables 13 and 14.** The performance of Autoformer and Informer differs between these tables, requiring explanation (Reviewers 1, 2).
- **Probabilistic version not considered.** A probabilistic variant would be interesting but is absent (Reviewer 1).
