## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proves that any subquadratic alternative to the quadratic attention mechanism in Transformers cannot perfectly solve document similarity tasks (Minimum Inner Product, Maximum Inner Product, Most Similar Documents, Least Similar Documents) under the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH). Reviewers acknowledge the theoretical rigor and novelty of connecting complexity theory to Transformer architectures (R3: "finally more mathematically theoretically founded analysis"; R1: "potential to impact architectural development"; R4: "bringing SETH to transformer architectures"). However, there is strong disagreement on practical relevance: one reviewer (R2, score 3, confidence 4) finds "almost no connection with transformers being used in practice" and notes that subquadratic approximations aim to perform similarly to Transformers but more efficiently – an orthogonal goal. Another (R4, score 6) considers the results incremental because closest-pair hardness is well known. All reviewers raise concerns about missing empirical validation, confusion over exact vs. approximate results, reliance on bag-of-words embeddings, and insufficient discussion of real-world implications. Presentation issues and specific theoretical details (e.g., step 529 in the proof) are also flagged.

### Strengths
- The paper provides a fundamental theoretical limitation: any subquadratic architecture or heuristic cannot perform tasks that require solving Minimum Inner Product, Maximum Inner Product, Most Similar Documents, or Least Similar Documents exactly (assuming SETH). This holds regardless of the specific subquadratic approach.
- A clear constructive proof shows that a single-head, single-layer Transformer can solve these tasks with quadratic attention, highlighting the inherent computational gap.
- The work brings the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH) and fine-grained complexity results to the attention of the deep learning community, offering a rigorous mathematical framing for a widely discussed practical bottleneck.
- The analysis is considered sound and well-executed (R1: "math is accurate and concise"; R2: "steps seem sound"; R3: "rigorous and offering insights that can influence future research").
- The paper is generally well-written, though some structural changes are suggested (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of empirical validation.** No experiments demonstrate that subquadratic models (e.g., Mamba, linear attention) actually fail on the studied tasks (Max-IP, Min-IP, MSD, LSD) while Transformers succeed. The absence of concrete failure cases makes it hard to assess practical relevance. (R1, R2) Also missing is any discussion of how data distribution, noise, hyperparameters, or varying sequence lengths and embedding dimensions might affect the results. (R1, R3)
- **Limited connection to practical Transformers.** The paper targets exact solutions under SETH, but practical subquadratic alternatives aim for approximate or heuristic solutions that perform similarly to Transformers on real tasks. This mismatch reduces the impact of the theoretical result. (R2) Furthermore, some Transformer variants (e.g., decoder-only) are known to fail at counting and copying problems [1], so the claim that “Transformers can solve” these tasks may not hold for all architectures. (R2)
- **Confusion between exact and approximate results.** The paper uses the word “approximately” in its claim about document similarity, yet SETH applies to exact solutions. The distinction is not maintained throughout, leading to ambiguity about whether the hardness holds for approximate matching or only exact. (R4) The paper should explicitly state that the results are for exact solutions.
- **Reliance on bag-of-words (BOW) embeddings.** The hardness results assume BOW representations, which discard word order and syntactic information. This approximation limits the applicability of the findings to more sophisticated embeddings (e.g., contextual or positional embeddings) used in practice. (R4) The paper does not address whether the conclusions would change with richer embeddings.
- **Incremental nature of the core results.** It is already known that finding closest pairs under Euclidean or Manhattan distance requires quadratic time (assuming SETH). The paper’s results for MSD/LSD largely mirror these existing complexity results. While the connection to Transformers is novel, the underlying hardness is not fundamentally new. (R4)
- **Missing discussion of asymptotic bounds in practice.** The paper states that the quadratic lower bound is asymptotic, but does not discuss the concrete sequence lengths and embedding dimensions at which the quadratic behavior becomes dominant. A practical analysis of when the bound matters is needed. (R3)
- **Depth requirements for subquadratic models.** The paper does not investigate whether state-space models (e.g., Mamba) could solve the Orthogonal Vectors problem with moderate depth (e.g., 10 layers). The claim that prohibitively large depth would be required needs theoretical or empirical support. (R3)
- **Narrow task scope.** The focus is solely on document similarity; the paper does not discuss implications for other NLP tasks that rely on attention (e.g., machine translation, question answering). (R3)
- **Presentation and readability issues.** The structure is confusing in places: the abstract has redundant lines; the introduction contains distracting remarks; bichromatic variants (Max-IP, Min-IP) are introduced early but not needed for the main proof, and the definitions of MSD and LSD are tangled (the core proof uses LSD but MSD is also defined). (R3) Specific mathematical errors: in step 529, A_{Q,K,V}(X) is stated as equal to an expression when it should be an upper bound; the factor from XV (the number of 1s in v_i) is missing. (R3) Also, numeric details should be explicit (e.g., line 434 uses “very large” instead of a concrete number). (R3)
- **Lack of comparative analysis.** The paper does not rank different subquadratic approximations or quantify the performance gap between quadratic energy and subquadratic alternatives. (R2) It also does not discuss approximate algorithms for Min-IP or its variants and how they might relate to subquadratic attention. (R2)
- **Missing discussion of practical relevance for recommendation systems.** The paper does not explain how findings for MSD/LSD transfer to practical applications like recommendation systems, where sequence length and model parameters may vary. (R1)

*Note:* Minor typos and formatting issues are also reported (line 259 “finds”, line 310 “multi-layer”, line 534 v_i^* missing star). (R3)
