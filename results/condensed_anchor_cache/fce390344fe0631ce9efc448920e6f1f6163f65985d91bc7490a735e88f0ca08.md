## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces SPMformer, a Transformer-based stochastic partial-multivariate method for multivariate time-series forecasting that randomly samples feature subsets during training and inference. Reviewers had mixed opinions. Some found the work well-motivated and praised the extensive experimental evaluation (long-term, short-term, probabilistic) and the flexibility of the approach (R2, R3, R4). Others criticized the lack of principled rationale for the random sampling, the flawed theoretical analysis (the i.i.d. assumption in Theorem 1 and superficiality of Theorem 2), missing standard errors, unfair FLOPs comparison, unclear notation, and insufficient guidance on hyperparameters (R1, R3). Reviewer scores ranged from 3 to 5, reflecting these disagreements.

### Strengths
- **Clear presentation and detailed methodology**: The paper is generally easy to follow, and Section 3.2 is particularly well described (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- **Well-motivated flexibility**: The work is motivated by the need for a model that can handle varying numbers and types of time series at inference, which is seen as a key practical contribution and a novel stochastic element (R3, R4).
- **Extensive experimental evaluation**: The authors provide comprehensive results across long-term, short-term, and probabilistic forecasting tasks, with analysis of varying subset size \(S\) and number of inference runs \(N_I\). Figure 3 shows a sweet spot for \(S\), and Figure 6 demonstrates robustness to missing features (R2, R3, R4).
- **Attempt at theoretical support**: Some reviewers appreciated the theoretical study (PAC-Bayesian bound) as an effort to justify the method (R2).

### Weaknesses
1. **Insufficient motivation and rationale for random sampling** (R1, R4): The introduction does not adequately justify why stochastic feature subset sampling improves performance over deterministic grouping. It fails to address potential drawbacks (e.g., ignoring important variables, disrupting hierarchical structures) and does not explain why random sampling is necessary. The example of stock sectors is argued to be static, not dynamic. The distribution \(\mathcal{P}\) is implemented as uniform without a principled formulation (R1, R4).
2. **Major flaws in theoretical analysis** (R1, R3):
   - Theorem 1 is derived from PAC-Bayesian bounds that assume i.i.d. data, which does **not** hold for time-series data (R3, *major flaw*).
   - Theorem 2 is superficial, only showing the effect of sample size and instance number without providing meaningful insight into why the method works (R1).
   - The conjecture in Section 3.4 about why increasing \(N_I\) improves results is unsupported: the reasoning that the probability of picking highly correlated subsets increases applies equally to uncorrelated subsets, which should harm performance (R3).
   - The theoretical analysis does not sufficiently explain how random feature subsampling improves generalization (R4).
3. **Missing standard errors and significance** (R3): No standard errors are reported for any experimental result, making it impossible to judge the significance of claimed improvements. The statement that “SPMformer outperforms baselines” is not supported.
4. **Unfair FLOPs comparison** (R3): Figure 7 compares FLOPs for a single forward pass on one subset, but SPMformer requires multiple subsets and multiple trials at inference; a fair comparison would account for total operations.
5. **Lack of hyperparameter selection details** (R3): The paper does not explain how hyperparameters were chosen.
6. **Incorrect citation** (R3): The 0.5-risk metric is not used in the cited papers [Zhou 2021, Makridakis 2022] as claimed in line 315.
7. **Unclear notation and mathematical rigor** (R2): Equation (1)'s notation \(\mathbf{F} \sim \mathcal{P}(\mathbf{F})\) is confusing; dimensions of \(\mathbf{h}_{b,i}^{(0)}\), \(\mathbf{e}_b^{Time}\), and \(\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{F}_i}^{Feature}\) are unspecified, and addition of differently dimensioned vectors appears impossible; validity of Eq. (7) for arbitrary loss functions is not justified.
8. **Training algorithm and sampling questions** (R1, R2):
   - In Algorithm 1, line 7, averaging the loss over different input variable sets is confusing because each element corresponds to a different subset (R1).
   - It is unclear whether the subset size \(S\) (or the sampling distribution) is updated during training; if not, the method reduces to a deterministic case (R2).
   - The variable sample size \(S\) is dataset-specific with no guidance on how to set it (R1).
   - The splitting and extension algorithm may ignore important subsets (e.g., with \(D=3, S=2\), it can miss \(\{F1,F3\}\)) (R2).
   - The model is trained and tested with the same fixed \(S\); generalization to different \(S\) at inference is not shown (R3).
9. **Overclaim about dynamic relationships** (R1): Lines 166–167 claim that stochastic partial-multivariate can handle “dynamic relationships,” but varying input variables cannot necessarily handle instance-level dynamic relationships; this is ambiguous and unsupported.
10. **Suboptimal design choices** (R1): Sharing the linear projection in Eq. (2) across different feature subsets is suboptimal when input patterns differ. Concatenated representations (line 197) may lead to extremely high dimensions.
11. **Potential computational cost** (R2): The model may incur significantly higher running time than baselines due to multiple sampling and inference runs; a computational cost comparison is missing.
