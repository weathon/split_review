## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes augmentation-conditioning, a tuning-free method to generate synthetic training images for downstream classification by conditioning a frozen diffusion model on augmented real images and text prompts. The approach aims to improve diversity without fine-tuning the generator. The method is evaluated on five long-tail and few-shot classification benchmarks, showing improvements over baselines, particularly in few-shot settings.

### Strengths
1. The augmentation-conditioning approach effectively bridges the domain gap between synthetic and real data, producing in-domain images with enhanced visual diversity without requiring fine-tuning of the diffusion model. (R1)
2. The method is validated across multiple challenging benchmarks, including ImageNet-LT (long-tail) and four few-shot classification datasets, and shows strong performance especially in few-shot regimes. (R1, R2, R3)
3. The paper is clearly written with a well-presented method, making it easy to understand. (R3, R4)
4. Extensive ablation experiments are conducted to support the design choices, including testing various augmentations (Mixup, CutMix, Dropout, etc.) and CFG scale. (R3, R2)
5. The approach is straightforward and practical, with qualitative examples supplementing quantitative results. (R3, R4)
6. The CFG scale experiments are interesting, revealing that the optimal guidance scale varies by task (lower for diversity in long-tail, higher for few-shot). (R2)
7. Some results show clear improvement over baselines, particularly in extreme few-shot scenarios (e.g., Table 1 few setting: 55.3 → 63.5). (R1, R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Mixed reviewer opinions on novelty and simplicity**: While two reviewers (R2, R4) find the method overly simplistic and lacking technical novelty (simply adding augmentations to conditioning), Reviewer 3 considers the straightforwardness a strength. The paper does not adequately articulate its novel contribution. (R1, R2, R4)
- **Mixed results and missing analysis**:
  * In Table 1, the random image baseline has the lowest FID, which is not discussed; the claim that “the best-performing augmentation-conditioning method has one of the lowest FID scores” is misleading. (R4)
  * In Table 3, the bolding is unintuitive: “Ours” is bolded in the medium column despite LDM (txt & image) achieving higher accuracy with comparable data counts. (R4)
  * In Figure 6, by 16 shots the novel method already underperforms on 2 out of 4 datasets, yet the writing does not address these failures. (R4)
  * In Figure 5 (bottom-right), performances of the three methods are almost identical without interpretation. (R2)
  * The improvements in Table 1 are more pronounced in few-shot than many-shot settings (e.g., many setting: 72.4 → 72.0; few: 55.3 → 63.5); the paper lacks explanation for this phenomenon. (R1, R4)
- **Insufficient experimental validation**:
  * Only ImageNet-LT is used for long-tail; more datasets (Tiny-ImageNet-200, CIFAR-100, full ImageNet, CUB-LT, Flower-LT) are needed. (R1)
  * Few-shot section missing key baselines: starting accuracy of pretrained ResNet50, and the random-image baseline (included in Table 1 but omitted from Figure 6). (R4)
  * LT algorithmic baselines (e.g., works focused on algorithmic improvements) are not compared. (R2)
  * Comparison with Fill-Up is unclear: “Ours” uses less data and performs worse; the paper should compare Fill-Up at the same data scale to demonstrate superiority. (R3, R4)
- **Cost and efficiency considerations**:
  * The claim that fine-tuning diffusion models is too expensive is not quantified; methods using PEFT (e.g., LoRA) may be low-cost and could achieve better results with minimal extra computation. (R1, R4)
  * The time to generate 1.16M synthetic images (Table 3) is not reported; a comparison of generation cost vs. fine-tuning cost would strengthen the argument. (R2)
  * The choice of timestep T (1000 vs. 50) and its impact on downstream performance is not discussed. (R2)
- **Scope and generalizability**:
  * The application scope appears limited to long-tail and few-shot classification; the paper does not explore other discriminative tasks or image generation. (R1, R2)
  * The paper does not clarify when the method is most beneficial; given mixed results, an in-depth analysis of use cases is missing. (R4)
- **Specific experimental details and claims**:
  * The claim of higher visual diversity (Figure 4) is not well-supported: coloration appears consistent, pose and angle differences are mostly visible only for CutMix and CutMix-Dropout. (R4)
  * FID alone does not measure diversity; the paper should include additional metrics like recall to support diversity claims. (R2)
  * The optimal augmentation choice (Mixup-Dropout, Embed-Mixup-Dropout, Embed-CutMix-Dropout) seems to depend on task/dataset; no clear pattern or recommendation is provided. (R2)
  * The claim that conditioning on augmented images improves diversity is undermined by Fill-Up (which uses random images) achieving higher accuracy in Table 3; the positive correlation between accuracy and dataset scale should be illustrated for the proposed method. (R3)
- **Presentation issues**:
  * Table 3 is misleading: the lower section (Fill-Up) is a separate class with more data, not directly comparable, yet its high numbers are not bolded, confusing readers. (R4)
  * CFG scale experiments take up significant space but add limited value beyond confirming previous findings. (R4)
