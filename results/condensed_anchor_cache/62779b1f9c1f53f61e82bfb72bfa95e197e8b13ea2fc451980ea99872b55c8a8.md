## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses disparate impact from pruning by formulating fine-tuning as a constrained optimization that bounds per-subgroup accuracy change between dense and sparse models. It solves the resulting min-max problem with alternating gradient descent and replay buffers. Experiments on UTKFace and CIFAR-100 show the method (CEAG) reduces worst-group accuracy disparity compared to existing approaches.

### Strengths
- The problem is novel in the sparsification community and practically important for safety-critical applications.
- The approach is interesting, non-trivial, and flexible: it directly optimizes accuracy constraints via a min-max formulation, alternating between a non-differentiable accuracy constraint term and an excess loss term, and uses replay buffers for stability.
- The technique is solid and the experiments are sound (Reviewer 1). Empirical evaluation is very thorough (Reviewer 3), with variations discussed in the appendix and clear documentation of limitations and ethical considerations.
- The method reliably reduces group-wise accuracy disparities after pruning and does not add significant computational overhead compared to standard training.
- CEAG outperforms existing alternatives on the UTKFace and CIFAR-100 benchmarks.
- The paper is well-written, with clear methodology, implementation details, and grounding examples in the appendix.

### Weaknesses
- **Feasibility and formal guarantees** (multiple reviewers): It is not clear from the analysis whether solutions always exist given the constraints, and what relationship exists between the starting point and how tight the constraints can be (R2, R3). No formal results are provided for solving CEAG (Equation 5) – e.g., an analytical solution, convergence guarantees, or conditions under which Algorithm 1 is guaranteed to find a solution when the feasible set is nonempty (R1, R3). R1 also requests formal results for Equation (6) specifically.
- **Limited pruning strategies** (R1, R3): The method is independent of the choice of pruning strategy, but experiments were only run with iterative magnitude pruning (which Reviewer 4 notes is more properly called Gradual Magnitude Pruning, GMP, not IMP). Replication with other pruning methods – including structured pruning, SynFlow, or SNIP – is missing and would strengthen the demonstration.
- **Generalization gap and statistical significance** (Reviewer 4): On the hold-out test set, the improvement in worst-group accuracy (`max_g ψ_g`) over the NFT baseline is small and of questionable statistical significance given reported standard deviations. Concrete examples: UTKFace 99% sparsity: 2.0 vs 2.1 (Table 1); UTKFace 92.5% sparsity: 3.3 vs 3.6 (Table 2); CIFAR-100: 13.8 vs 14.3 (Table 3). The method appears to overfit to the training set’s subgroup distribution, especially when the number of subgroups is large (CIFAR-100), raising concerns about practical significance.
- **Experimental scope** (Reviewer 4): The evaluation is not exhaustive enough. Larger-scale datasets with more complex group structures (e.g., ImageNet, ImageNet-LT, or iNaturalist with hierarchical groups) would better establish real-world applicability. The current small-scale datasets and limited number of groups may not be indicative of behavior on more challenging problems.
- **Missing analysis on several aspects** (Reviewer 3): The paper does not discuss how the method is affected by dataset imbalance (e.g., groups with comparatively fewer samples), whether there is a sparsity level at which the constrained problem becomes infeasible, or how this might play out under structured pruning. The authors also do not explore ways to improve test-case performance, such as through dataset splits.
- **Sensitivity of hyperparameters** (Reviewer 4): The algorithm’s sensitivity to the initialization of the dual parameters λ_g and their update rule is not investigated.
