## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes FedGO, a federated ensemble distillation method that uses GAN-based client discriminators to compute near-optimal weights for aggregating client predictions when generating pseudo-labels. The server distributes a generator (pretrained or trained on a server dataset), clients train discriminators on their local data and that generator, and the resulting discriminator scores are used to weight each client’s predictions during distillation. Extensive experiments on image classification tasks show improvements in final accuracy and convergence speed. The paper also analyzes additional communication, privacy, and computational costs. The three reviewers’ scores (5, 8, 5) indicate disagreement: Reviewer 2 finds the method highly original and well-validated, while Reviewers 1 and 3 are more cautious, emphasizing several missing ablations, resource concerns, and skepticism about the data-dependence of the distillation approach.

### Strengths
- Theoretical grounding: The paper provides a provably near-optimal weighting method rooted in GAN theory, which enhances credibility (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).
- Originality: The integration of client-side discriminators to weight ensemble distillation is a novel approach that has not been extensively explored in federated learning (Reviewer 2).
- Analysis of overhead: The paper analyzes communication, privacy, and computational complexity across different scenarios (e.g., with/without extra server dataset), providing valuable implementation insights (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).
- Experimental thoroughness: Extensive experiments on multiple image classification tasks demonstrate significant performance and convergence improvements over baselines (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
- Presentation: The paper is well-written and easy to follow (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited model architecture evaluation**: Experiments are restricted to a single architecture. The method should be tested on VGG, MobileNet, and other models with different inductive biases and complexities to demonstrate generalizability (Reviewer 1).
2. **Ablation on discriminator and generator quality**: The paper does not study sensitivity to the training quality of the generator and discriminator. Ablation is needed on training epochs, learning rates, architectures, and the risk of mode collapse in GAN training, which could lead to biased weighting and reduced performance (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).
3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis missing**: No ablation on key hyperparameters such as \(E_s\) and \(E_d\). The impact of these parameters on convergence speed and final performance should be systematically explored (Reviewer 1).
4. **Unclear fairness of baseline comparisons**: The experimental settings of baseline methods are not clearly stated; the paper should specify hyperparameter tuning, optimization algorithms, learning rates, and training procedures for baselines to ensure a fair comparison (Reviewer 1).
5. **Computational and communication overhead impractical for resource-constrained FL**: The additional cost of training discriminators at each client and generating synthetic data may be prohibitive for edge devices. The analysis of this overhead is not sufficiently detailed, especially regarding the computational burden of training the discriminator per client (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).
6. **Dependence on auxiliary data and privacy concerns**: Distillation-based FL relies on an auxiliary dataset (either server-owned or generated). The pre-trained generator approach also requires a public dataset, which may not always be available and raises privacy issues if that dataset resembles client data. The paper does not provide a feasible solution to these data-dependency and privacy challenges, leaving skepticism about the practicality of the research direction (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 2 is more positive, arguing that the overhead analysis already addresses some of these concerns, but does not fully resolve them.
7. **Idealized assumptions in theoretical optimality proof**: The claim of near-optimal performance depends on assumptions about data distributions and discriminator performance. Real-world deviations could lead to suboptimal results. The paper does not discuss how to select discriminator architectures or how robust the method is to imperfect discriminator training (Reviewer 2).

**Note**: The higher score (8) from Reviewer 2 reflects strong support for the originality and theoretical contributions, while the lower scores (5, 5) from Reviewers 1 and 3 emphasize the need for broader validation, better ablation, and a clearer handling of data-dependence and resource constraints.
