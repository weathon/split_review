## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper investigates the impact of context length on clinical prediction tasks using EHR data, evaluating four architectures (Mamba, Llama, GPT, Hyena) on the EHRSHOT benchmark and pre-training on a private dataset. It identifies three EHR-specific properties (copy-forwarding, irregular time intervals, disease progression) and quantifies their effect on model performance. The Mamba model with 16k token context achieves state-of-the-art on 9/14 tasks. Reviewers broadly appreciate the thorough analysis, identification of EHR-specific challenges, and the potential of long-context models. However, opinions diverge sharply: one reviewer (10) considers it a major contribution with all weaknesses addressed, while another (5) finds limited novelty and questions the practical relevance of long contexts. Other reviewers (6, 6, 8) raise concerns about missing experimental details, insufficient comparisons, and methodological limitations.

### Strengths

1. **Introduction of long-context architectures to EHR data.** Reviewers note the value of evaluating non-Transformer models (Mamba, Hyena) for medical data, with Mamba demonstrating strong performance and linear complexity (R1, R3). R2 emphasizes that the demonstration that perplexity does not decrease with sequence length (unlike text) is a major deviation from standard assumptions.

2. **Identification and quantification of three EHR-specific challenges.** The paper defines metrics for copy-forwarding, irregular time intervals, and disease progression that can be applied to any EHR dataset (R2, R3, R4). R2 highlights the stratification experiments showing that higher levels of these properties correlate with worse performance, and that longer contexts improve robustness (e.g., improved Brier scores or perplexity).

3. **Rigorous stratification and robustness analysis.** Experiments dividing patients by repetitiveness, irregularity, and context length demonstrate that longer context models consistently mitigate performance degradation across all three properties (R2, R4). R2 finds this evidence convincing and notes it is a key contribution.

4. **Release of code and model checkpoints.** R4 notes the community benefit of publicly available foundation models, given the scarcity of such resources.

5. **Solid technical execution and comprehensive experimental design.** R5 acknowledges the richness of the experimental paper and overall soundness, despite criticism of novelty.

**Minority positive view (R2):** This reviewer strongly supports acceptance, stating that all initial weaknesses were addressed and the paper provides a broad benchmark and key insights for EHR autoregressive modeling.

### Weaknesses

1. **Limited novelty; lack of new methods or models.** R5 states that the paper introduces no new methods, models, or techniques—the core methodology applies existing architectures (Mamba, Llama, GPT, Hyena) to EHR data without algorithmic innovation. In contrast, other reviewers view this application itself as a strength, creating disagreement on novelty.

2. **Missing details on EHRSHOT benchmark tasks.** Multiple reviewers (R1, R4) note that the paper does not specify the 14 tasks (classification vs. regression, prediction targets, time horizons, label distributions, sample sizes), making it difficult to assess the significance of reported metrics like AUROC 0.807. R4 also notes that evaluation metrics per task are not provided.

3. **Unfair context length comparison between architectures.** R1 argues that GPT and LLaMA have a maximum context length of 4096 tokens, so training/evaluating them at 16k is impractical without significant hardware resources (quadratic scaling). This raises fairness concerns when comparing to Mamba (linear complexity), especially at disparate context lengths.

4. **Patient stratification based on overall time interval variability may obscure disease-specific patterns.** R1 points out that grouping patients into quarters by global standard deviation of intervals mixes diseases; testing should be per-disease to avoid confounding.

5. **Token repetition metric does not distinguish clinically meaningful repetitions from non-informative ones.** R3 questions whether the repetition metric (RR) can separate repeated vital signs (e.g., in ICU indicating deterioration) from billing code duplicates, limiting its interpretability. R1 also questions how repetition models would handle chronic vs. acute diseases.

6. **Missing evaluation of EHR-specific foundation models and baselines.** R3 notes that models designed specifically for EHR data (e.g., ExBEHRT, EHRMamba) are not included. Additionally, R4 and R3 point out that the prior SOTA model CLMBR-t-base is not evaluated in the stratified analysis (Table 2) or finetuned (only logistic regression heads used on frozen representations), and its context length is not discussed. R3 also notes that Hyena is missing from Table 2.

7. **Lack of temporal encoding in tokenization.** R3 and R4 note that irregular time intervals are not explicitly encoded; performance drops may be due to this omission rather than architecture limitations. Both suggest exploring time-aware embeddings or relative positional encodings. R4 also recommends testing alternative tokenization strategies (e.g., sub-token level for rare events).

8. **No few-shot or zero-shot evaluation.** R2 criticizes that all evaluations use mean pooling of last L tokens from a frozen model (a detail buried in appendix), which is a limited embedding strategy. R2 argues that few-shot results or autoregressive zero-shot generation (as in prior work [1-3]) would likely show even larger benefits from longer contexts. This is labeled the “most critical improvement” by R2.

9. **Missing compute budgets and performance vs. compute analysis.** R2 requests wall-time, hardware, and a plot of performance vs. compute load to justify whether improvements trade off against added compute.

10. **Inconsistent performance improvements across models and context lengths.** R5 notes that the claim that longer contexts consistently improve performance is not fully supported by the figures; some models (e.g., Hyena) show performance drops beyond 4k tokens. R1 also raises this. This contrasts with R2’s positive view that improvements are clear.

11. **GPT perplexity instability not adequately explained.** R2 finds the wild variation in GPT per-token perplexity (Figure 4) insufficiently explained—calling it “training instability” without deeper analysis makes it look like a bug.

12. **Missing standard deviations and statistical tests between methods.** R3 observes that the paper only reports statistical tests between short- and long-context counterparts, not between different architectures, limiting robustness of cross-model comparisons.

13. **Copy-forwarding hypothesis not well-supported.** R3 points out that the claim that moderate copy-forwarding can be helpful is only observed for CLMBR-t-base and cannot be validated by other models; moreover, CLMBR-t-base shows lower Brier scores overall, confounding interpretation.

14. **Only one EHR dataset evaluated.** R4 notes that generalizability is limited to EHRSHOT; similar experiments on MIMIC would strengthen conclusions. R5 also questions the practical relevance of extremely long contexts given that most patient records may be shorter.

15. **Impact of long context and EHR properties on pre-training not discussed.** R3 notes the missed opportunity to analyze how these factors affect model convergence, stability, and training dynamics.

16. **Typos and formatting issues.** R3 points out a corrupted citation (line 139), misspelling “Diseae” (Section 3.3.3), and duplicate references [Yang et al., 2023a, b]. R1 notes confusion about vocabulary/code/token statistics (R3’s questions: vocabulary 39818 vs. 39811 codes vs. Table 3 counts).
