## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes EEGPT, an EEG foundation model using autoregressive pre-training (next signal prediction) on single-electrode tokens, followed by multi-task fine-tuning with a shared graph network for spatial integration across electrodes. It aims to be a generalist model by combining diverse datasets. The approach is evaluated on 12 datasets across 5 downstream tasks. Two reviewers (scores 5, 5) find the approach innovative and well-validated; two reviewers (scores 3, 3) raise concerns about evaluation methodology, overclaimed novelty, and clarity.

### Strengths

- The paper is well-written and presents a highly engaging approach, with clear figures and tables (Reviewer 1, 2).
- The authors collect and combine diverse EEG datasets (up to 138 electrodes, 37.5M pre-training samples) across multiple tasks (emotion recognition, motor imagery, mental workload, sleep stage, cross-modality), which is a significant effort (Reviewer 2, 3).
- The scalability study with model sizes up to 1.1B parameters confirms that scaling laws apply to EEG data, a relatively rare contribution in BCI (Reviewer 2, 3).
- The empirical results demonstrate consistent improvements over specialist baselines across all downstream tasks, with a margin of 5+ percentage points in many categories (Reviewer 1, 3).
- The ablation studies are extensive, comparing autoregressive pre-training vs. masked autoencoder, and showing the benefits of multi-task fine-tuning (Reviewer 3, 4).
- The authors promise to release code and checkpoints, supporting reproducibility (Reviewer 2).
- The application of autoregressive pre-training (next sentence prediction) from NLP to EEG is noted as innovative, and the comparison with mask-based reconstruction is appreciated (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses

**1. Evaluation methodology does not support "generalist" claim**
- The evaluation uses a cross-subject paradigm within the same datasets for training and testing (validation/test subjects left out from the same datasets). This does not demonstrate cross-task transfer; it is a cross-subject transfer scenario augmented with examples from other tasks. It is impossible to predict behavior on new tasks/datasets (Reviewer 2).
- The claim of being the "first generalist EEG foundation model" is overstated; prior works already explored cross-task transfer (e.g., Yang et al. (2023), Aristimunha et al. (2023), Guetschel et al. (2023)) (Reviewer 2).

**2. Multiple novelty claims are overstated or not first**
- "First electrode-wise modeling strategy": Prior works exist, e.g., Yang et al. (2023), Guetschel et al. (2024), Li et al. (2024) (Reviewer 2).
- "First autoregressive EEG pre-trained model": Banville et al. (2021) used autoregressive pre-training; also, methods like masked autoencoders are similar. See also Foumani et al. (2024), Yang et al. (2023) (Reviewer 2).
- "First generalist EEG model for multi-task compatibility": Yang et al. (2023) already proposed such a model (Reviewer 2).
- The pretraining method does not go beyond BERT's two tasks (mask-based and next sentence prediction); this paper only explores the latter without combining both, limiting innovation (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 1, in contrast, finds the approach highly innovative; Reviewer 4 partially acknowledges innovation but notes the lack of combining methods.

**3. Results are below state-of-the-art on some benchmarks**
- On MIBCI, Zhao et al. (2020) obtained 76.5% accuracy in cross-subject configuration, which is 9.3% above the reported score and 13.2% above the best specialist model (Reviewer 2).
- On SEED-IV, Li et al. (2018) reported a simple SVM achieving 52.8% accuracy in cross-subject transfer, which is 11.5% below the reported score and 18.1% below the best specialist model (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 1 similarly notes that certain datasets appear to have higher SOTA values.
- On emotion recognition (FACED), accuracy is only ~20%, suggesting the method may be impractical for real-world applications (Reviewer 4).

**4. Missing details and clarifications on methodology**
- Tokenization strategy is not clearly explained:
    - No information on EEG pre-processing in main text (only in appendix B). Important details such as sampling rate harmonization, window sizes (1-second tokens, 4-second segments, 25 tokens, 256 Hz) are given without justification or ablation studies (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 1 also requests clearer explanation of tokenization.
    - How dimensional discrepancies across datasets are mitigated in input construction is unclear (Reviewer 4).
- The multi-electrode graph network (TEG) description is insufficient:
    - Graph definition (edges, edge weights) is unclear; equation (8) and (9) have ambiguities (e.g., z_j already a matrix, diag(z_j) confusing) (Reviewer 3).
    - Notation inconsistencies (e.g., ℛ(·), x_i vs x_i^e, mask notation using +) (Reviewer 3).
- Missing details: normalization layers, position encoding type, loss function in fine-tuning (MSE vs L2), mini-batch allocation, data segmentation procedure for pre-training vs fine-tuning (Reviewer 3).
- The model is only validated with sufficient annotated data; the benefits of multi-task fine-tuning require annotation, which is costly and may be impractical for real-world tasks (Reviewer 4).

**5. Experimental reporting issues**
- Figure 1 is difficult to read: axes scaled to data, tickmarks on non-integer values; lack of axis ranges (Reviewer 2).
- No results for BIOT and BCIC4-1 in Table 4 (Reviewer 3).
- Tables 4/5 do not report standard deviations, which are important to assess effect sizes (Reviewer 3).
- Figure 6 is difficult to interpret: unclear whether raw EEG or ETE outputs are plotted; random initializations should be compared with pre-trained representations from BIOT and LaBraM (Reviewer 3).
- Ablation for fine-tuning from scratch (without pretraining) is recommended to highlight pretraining benefits (Reviewer 4).
- Only 9 datasets are used for pretraining while 13 for fine-tuning, with overlap; rationale is not explained (Reviewer 4).

**6. Other concerns**
- The name EEGPT is already used by two prior works (NeurIPS 2024, arXiv 2024) (Reviewer 2).
- The claim that MAE has "inevitable limitations in capturing sequential and temporal dependencies" (line 86) is not motivated; prior work (e.g., Chien et al. (2022)) shows MAEs can capture such dependencies (Reviewer 2).
- The choice of motor imagery datasets (Cho 2017, BCI Competition IV 1) is arbitrary; more common benchmarks exist (2008 BCI competition dataset B, Stieger et al. (2021)) (Reviewer 2).
- Several references should be added: Thomas et al. (2022) on autoregressive vs masked autoencoder for fMRI; Tang et al. (2023) and Guetschel et al. (2024) on electrode-wise modeling (Reviewer 3).
- The paper spends too much text re-stating contributions rather than expanding methods and results (Reviewer 3).
- Grammar/wording issues noted (Reviewer 3).
