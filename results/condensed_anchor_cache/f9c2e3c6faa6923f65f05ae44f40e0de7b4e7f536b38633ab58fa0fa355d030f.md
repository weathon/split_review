## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a standardized bias evaluation framework for unconditional image generative models, defining bias shift as the absolute difference between attribute presence probability in training and generated distributions. A classifier trained on the same dataset labels generated images; attributes are then categorized as subjective (high sensitivity to distribution shift) or non-subjective (low sensitivity) based on the classifier's decision boundary position. The framework is applied to diffusion models and GANs on CelebA and DeepFusion. Reviewers’ opinions diverge: one reviewer (score 6) finds the framework and taxonomy contributions interesting and the paper well-formulated, while the other three reviewers (scores 3, 5, 3) raise significant methodological concerns about classifier reliability, attribute categorization validity, and limited insights.

### Strengths
- The paper focuses on the inductive bias of generative models themselves, isolating it from dataset bias and prompting effects, providing a novel perspective for analyzing bias sources.
- The standardized bias evaluation framework uses the same classifier for all label predictions, ensuring consistency in evaluation.
- The analysis includes a detailed logits-based study that appears to be novel.
- The paper is well-written, well-structured, and explains complex concepts in an understandable way.
- The subjective/non-subjective attribute taxonomy is interesting, and the paper includes a vast amount of empirical results for analysis.
- The authors experiment with different sizes of diffusion models to observe how bias shift occurs.

### Weaknesses
- **Redundant/unused methodological components (raised by two reviewers):** The introduction of $P^{ideal}$ in the method section (Sec. 3) is unnecessary because $|P^{gen} - P^{val}|$ itself suffices to measure bias shift; the extra term overcomplicates and may confuse. Similarly, the definition of “conditional bias” in Sec. 3.1 is introduced but never discussed or studied in the rest of the paper; its purpose is unclear.
- **Argument against pre-trained models (raised by two reviewers):** The paper claims (L142) that pre-trained models introduce their own biases, making predicted labels unreliable. Reviewers disagree: classification accuracy is the key criterion for labeling attributes, and pre-trained models can be finetuned on the training dataset to improve performance. Accuracy, not bias, should determine usability.
- **Insufficient classifier accuracy (raised by two reviewers):** Although most attributes achieve 90%+ accuracy, several have low accuracy (lowest attribute: 68.34% on one setup, 71.65% on another). Lower accuracy implies higher analysis noise and larger ABS measurement errors. Since subjective attributes (with reported ABS ~3–5%) tend to appear in the lower performance list, the classifier (91.7% or 90.5% overall) is not accurate enough to support the conclusions.
- **Distribution shift between training and generated sets (raised by two reviewers):** The classifier is trained on the training set and directly applied to the generation set. Unlike the validation set, the generated images may come from a different distribution, causing the classifier to suffer from distribution shift and domain generalization challenges. This further weakens the reliability of the bias shift analysis.
- **Validity of attribute categorization (raised by three reviewers):** The splitting method based on the classifier’s decision boundary may not match human definitions of “subjective.” For example, “Male” and “Pale skin” in CelebA are called non-subjective, which is surprising. No user study validates the taxonomy, and no complete list of subjective/non-subjective attributes is provided. Additionally, decision boundaries are closely tied to classifier accuracy, so attributes with unclear boundaries (likely subjective) may have higher ABS errors, conflating measurement noise with bias shift.
- **Missing comparison with existing evaluation frameworks (raised by one reviewer):** The paper does not compare its framework to established tools like REVISE [Wang et al., 2022]. It should explain how the proposed framework differs from prior work.
- **Self-contamination issue (raised by one reviewer):** Using the same dataset to train both the generator and the classifier is problematic: bias could originate from the dataset, the generator, or the classifier, and these sources are muddled. A cleaner design would split the dataset, using half for generator training and half for classifier training.
- **Limited takeaways / unanswered “why” question (raised by one reviewer):** The raw bias shifts are strikingly small (e.g., ~1% for non-subjective attributes, 3–5% for subjective). Subjective logits in Fig. 5c look extremely similar between synthetic and real data, yet Fig. 5e shows “Male” landing in non-subjective and “Smiling” in subjective, which is surprising. The paper documents that shifts occur but does not explain *why* some attributes are over/under-represented in synthetic distributions. A more useful contribution would be predicting which attributes will shift based solely on classifier training.
- **Lack of discussion of framework limitations (raised by one reviewer):** The paper does not discuss the limitations of the bias evaluation framework.
- **Unaddressed question about accuracy reporting (raised by one reviewer):** It is unclear whether the accuracy rates in Appendix Figs. 4 and 5 refer to training set or validation set performance.
