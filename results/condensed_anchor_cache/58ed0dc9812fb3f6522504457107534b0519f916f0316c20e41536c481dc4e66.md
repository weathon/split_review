## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper extends holomorphic equilibrium propagation (hEP) to asymmetric networks, analytically isolates bias from finite nudge vs. weight asymmetry, and introduces a Jacobian homeostasis loss to improve functional symmetry. Experiments on vision benchmarks including ImageNet 32×32 show improved performance over previous hEP. Reviewers generally acknowledge the theoretical analysis and new perspective, but express mixed views: Reviewer 2 (score 8) is more positive about the theoretical contributions and empirical advances, while Reviewer 4 (score 5) finds the motivation unclear and biological plausibility stretched. Reviewers 1 and 3 (both score 6) note the analysis is helpful but raise concerns about narrowness, experimental depth, and comparison baselines.

### Strengths

- **Analysis of bias:** The paper provides a helpful separation of the impact of finite nudge and weight asymmetry on gradient estimation (R1).
- **Functional symmetry via Jacobian matching:** The idea of incorporating functional symmetry through matching Jacobians is interesting; it resembles the reconstruction error in Target Propagation but optimizes the homeostatic loss with respect to all weights rather than only feedback weights (R1).
- **Clarity and writing:** The paper is mostly nicely written and clear (R2), though Reviewer 1 notes the introduction may be complex for readers unfamiliar with holomorphic EP.
- **Theoretical contribution:** The theoretical results are not trivial, requiring deep understanding of hEP (R2). The paper extends the theory to asymmetric connectivity, showing exact gradient estimates despite finite perturbations under complex-differentiable assumptions (R2).
- **Broader relevance:** The work targets an area of broad interest spanning machine learning, neuromorphic engineering, and theoretical neuroscience (R2).
- **Advances empirical results in EP:** The paper significantly advances the empirical state of the art for EP-based algorithms (R2). The method outperforms standard hEP and RBP on several vision tasks (R1, R4).
- **New perspective on weight transport:** The paper offers a new perspective on the weight transport problem by analyzing the Jacobian of the network and explaining connections between weight symmetry, functional symmetry, and Jacobian homeostasis (R3).
- **Rigorous analysis and experiments:** The investigation of weight symmetry evolution during training (Section 4) shows careful effort (R3). The section on Jacobian homeostasis improving functional symmetry is very detailed and well analyzed (R3).
- **Informative introduction:** Section 2 provides a clear introduction to the area for readers new to the field (R4).

### Weaknesses

- **Narrow significance:** The contribution is narrow; it improves performance and theoretical understanding of hEP but does not resolve weight asymmetry completely (see Table 2 where performance still lags behind symmetric-weights EP) (R2). hEP itself is a specialized version of EP requiring complex-valued neurons and equilibrium settling, limiting its generality (R2). EP as a whole is limited relative to alternative methods that achieve similar goals (biological plausibility, hardware efficiency) without these constraints (R2). The progress towards biological plausibility or neuromorphic computation seems small (R2). Reviewer 2 emphasizes these limitations despite his/her overall positive rating.
- **Novelty concerns:** The empirical improvement from dealing with asymmetric weights is not a new result; the theoretical insight that weight asymmetry is the dominant issue (compared to finite nudge) is also supported empirically, so the added value of the theory may be limited (R2). The objective function that penalizes asymmetry has some novelty, but older learning rules (e.g., Kolen & Polak, 1994; Payeur et al., 2021) achieve weight symmetry without requiring global iterative equilibria; the present approach could be seen as a step backward in that regard, though it is applicable when connectivity is not reciprocal (R2).
- **Insufficient contextualization:** Alternatives with similar goals (Payeur et al., 2021; Greedy et al., 2022; Ren et al., 2023; Journé et al., 2023) are cited only in passing, in the discussion rather than the motivation, and their better empirical results are attributed solely to lower simulation cost without acknowledging that they also avoid backward passes and are more biologically plausible (R2). The paper should motivate why EP is chosen over such alternatives (R2).
- **Limited biological plausibility:** The hEP assumptions—complex-valued neurons, settling to equilibrium at multiple phases of an ongoing oscillation—are not plausible for biological networks; gamma and theta oscillations are too fast to allow such settling (R4). The requirement for multiple network-wide iterations before a weight update also undermines biological compatibility (R2). The paper's discussion of these limitations is vague and speculative (R2). Even the claim that analog substrates achieve relaxation “for free” is not well supported: specific hardware implementations (Kendall et al., 2020) are ad hoc, and multiple weight updates still consume significant power, making algorithms without relaxation phases potentially more efficient (R2).
- **Unclear advantages for artificial systems:** It is not clear how the proposed modification translates to tangible benefits (learning speed, accuracy, efficiency) compared to standard backpropagation or other state-of-the-art learning algorithms in artificial neural networks where biological plausibility is not a concern (R4).
- **Experimental depth and convincingness:** The performance of the proposed method is relatively not advanced, and the comparison methods are not new, making the work less convincing (R3). The discussion of experimental results, especially the evolution of weight symmetry during training (Fig. 4c,g,k), is superficial: the symmetry metric (ratio of symmetric component norm to total norm) is not sufficiently explained; for example, why the metric approaches one in Fig. 4c (given reciprocal connections) and why it fails to converge to one in Fig. 4g are not discussed, and the significance of the angle between forward and feedback weights in Fig. 4k is only stated without deeper analysis (R3).
- **Presentation flaws:** The abbreviation “hEP” is not defined at its first occurrence (R3). There is a typo: “nothing” should be “noting” in Definition 1 (Section 2.2). The sentence after Eq. (6) is overly long and should be split (R3). Layout issues: the paragraph with Eq. (9) is interspersed by Figure 2, and similar problems occur with Figure 3 and Table 1, hurting readability (R3).
- **Expensive simulations:** Despite using only small networks and simple tasks, the experiments required a multi-GPU cluster, indicating high computational cost (R2).
