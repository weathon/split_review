## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a symmetric reinforcement learning loss, inspired by reverse cross-entropy for noisy labels, to improve robustness in policy gradient algorithms (A2C and PPO). The authors introduce Symmetric A2C (SA2C) and Symmetric PPO (SPPO) by adding a reverse loss term, perform gradient analysis, and evaluate on discrete-action Atari games, continuous-action MuJoCo/Box2D, and RLHF tasks (IMDB sentiment, TL;DR summarization). All reviewers see promise in the idea and the empirical validation of advantage sign reversal. However, most reviewers had major concerns about experimental quality, presentation clarity, and the theoretical justification of the loss adaptation. One reviewer (Reviewer 3) was notably more positive (score 5), praising the empirical evidence across multiple noise scenarios and RLHF tasks, but also noted weaknesses in clarity and missing comparisons. The other three reviewers rated the paper lower (scores 3,3,3) due to unconvincing experiments, organizational flaws, and unclear method derivation.

### Strengths

- The idea of adapting symmetric cross-entropy loss from supervised learning to improve RL training robustness is promising and intriguing.
- Gradient analysis in Section 4.3 provides confidence that the proposed approach does not harm learning.
- Experiments successfully degrade the performance of A2C and PPO with noisy rewards and show that the symmetric versions better handle the noise (Reviewer 1).
- Figure 2 clearly illustrates how advantage normalization can change the sign of an action’s advantage based on mini-batch data, supporting the motivation for symmetric loss.
- The hypothesized reasons for why SPPO improves over PPO (small batch, advantage normalization, off-policy updates) make intuitive sense.
- The experimental section thoroughly examines the probability of advantage sign reversal under different conditions, using empirical data to support the argument for symmetric loss (Reviewer 3).
- Validation across multiple noisy reward scenarios (noise added to rewards) and RLHF tasks (IMDB, TL;DR) demonstrates the algorithm’s ability to mitigate noise from subjective evaluations (Reviewer 3).
- There is interesting empirical analysis of how advantages work in practice (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses

- **Method justification and derivation**: The adaptation of reverse cross-entropy to RL appears shoe-horned; a first-principles derivation would be more convincing. The mapping of the advantage value \(A(x,k)\) to the equivalent of \(q(k|x)\) is not well justified. A potentially better approach would be to map advantages to a simplex and turn them into probabilities before applying RCE (Reviewer 1). The definition of \(Z\) is vague—Equation 5 is said to define it, but it remains unclear (Reviewers 1, 3). The transition from the standard RL loss (PRELIMINARIES) to the modified loss (APPROACH) is difficult to follow (Reviewer 3). Equation 8 lacks the clipping used in PPO, and the presence of \(Z\) in the reverse loss seems incongruous because the PPO loss does not follow the CE structure with a log \(p\) (Reviewer 1). The paper calls it “symmetric RL loss” but it pertains only to policy gradient actor-critic methods; “symmetric policy gradient loss” would be more precise (Reviewer 1).

- **Experimental quality and baselines**:
  - **Atari results underperform known baselines**: The PPO baseline in Figure 3 (apparently at 5M steps) shows significantly lower scores than reported by openai/baselines at 5M steps. For example: Alien 1128 vs. ~1500, Gravitar 371 vs. ~600, Qbert 4352 vs. ~12500, MsPacman 837 vs. ~1750 (Reviewer 4). The paper does not specify the codebase or training recipe used; the authors should indicate which implementation they used (Reviewer 4).
  - **RLHF experiment design is problematic**: The GPT‑4 win rate for the 6B model with SPPO on TL;DR is only 52.50%, whereas comparable work using RLOO achieves 77.9% and PPO 67% (citing https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.14740). Some discrepancies may stem from the training codebase/dataset. The `CarperAI/openai_summarize_comparisons` dataset has double spacing between “TL;DR:” and the actual response, which could cause quirks (Reviewer 4). In RLHF, if the same reward model is used for training and evaluation, it may be prone to overfitting (Reviewer 1).
  - **Section 5.4 contributes minimally**: The observed benefit of SPPO over SA2C likely reflects the inherent advantages of PPO over A2C rather than the symmetric loss itself (Reviewer 2).
  - **Missing comparisons with other robustness-enhancing methods** (e.g., distributional critics, expected policy gradients, or other noise-mitigation techniques) (Reviewers 1, 3).
  - **Performance on clean environments**: SPPO and SA2C still perform better than standard PPO/A2C on clean Atari games without added noise; the authors should explain why (Reviewer 2). The 0.05 standard deviation reward noise used in MuJoCo/Box2D is relatively small and may not cause significant performance drops; some work uses perturbed rewards to boost performance (Reviewer 2).

- **Presentation and organization**:
  - The paper is poorly polished and organized, especially the Experiment section. Figures are placed far from their first citations: Figure 1 on page 5 is first cited on page 2; Figure 2 on page 6 is first cited in the Experiment section on page 10 (Reviewer 2). The caption of Table 2 is inconsistent with its content. “Figure 4.1” on line 466 is unclear (Reviewer 2). The phrase “the sum of rewards as defined in 1” (line 167) is ambiguous (Reviewer 2). Notations in Section 5.2 are confusing: the first and second paragraphs use DA2C and DSPPO, but the third uses SA2C and SPPO (Reviewer 2). The introduction should provide more context on current research directions and challenges in enhancing robustness (Reviewer 3). Various typos and awkward phrasing: line 53, 54, 68–70 (A2C does not do advantage normalization), line 177–178 (advantage misstated), line 189, line 213 (unsubstantiated claim), line 215 (“Has model errors”), line 248 (wrong equation reference), line 403 (Atari rewards incorrectly described as only 0 or 1), line 457 (extra “is”), line 721 (citation style), Appendix A.1 dependence on softmax parameterization should be explicitly stated (Reviewer 1).
  - The table in Appendix 14 does not clarify whether “performance increase” refers to perplexity or reward (Reviewer 1).

- **Questions that overlap with criticisms or missing experiments**:
  - Could a distributional critic (e.g., QR-SAC) serve as an alternative to the symmetric loss? (Reviewer 1)
  - How does the reverse RL loss differ from expected policy gradients, and would that be worth comparing? (Reviewer 1)
  - Is the gradient analysis in Appendix A.1 dependent on softmax parameterization? (Reviewer 1)
  - Why do SPPO and SA2C still perform better on clean Atari games? (Reviewer 2)
  - Could the symmetric RL loss be applied to algorithms that do not use advantages to improve robustness? (Reviewer 3)
  - What codebase was used for the Atari / MuJoCo experiments? (Reviewer 4)

- **Contrast**: One reviewer (Reviewer 3, score 5) was more positive about the paper’s novelty and empirical validation, while the other three reviewers (scores 3) found the experiments unconvincing and the presentation lacking. This contrast is reflected in the difference between the claimed contributions and the experimental rigor needed to support them.
