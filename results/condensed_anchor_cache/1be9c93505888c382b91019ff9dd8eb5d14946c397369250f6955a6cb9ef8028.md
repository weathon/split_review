## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes ET-SEED, an SE(3) equivariant diffusion policy for trajectory-level imitation learning. The key theoretical contribution is a theorem showing that in the Markov denoising process, only the final step requires SE(3) equivariance; all earlier steps can be invariant to the context (observation). This is achieved by classifying denoising steps into p1 (invariant to context), p2 (equivariant to context via last-step transformation), and p3 (fully equivariant) types. ET-SEED uses an invariant transformer for K-1 steps and an equivariant transformer for the final step. Experiments are conducted on six simulated and four real-world robot manipulation tasks (rigid body, articulated, deformable objects), comparing against DP3 and EquiBot. Results show substantial performance gains, especially in unseen poses, with only 20 real-world demonstrations. All reviewers acknowledge the value of combining equivariance with diffusion, but there is disagreement on presentation clarity, novelty depth, and key methodological limitations.

### Strengths

- **Novel decomposition of SE(3)-equivariant Markovian diffusion.** Reviewers 1 and 4 highlight the novelty of the theoretical framework. Reviewer 4 specifically notes that ET-SEED achieves full equivariance by first generating a trajectory independent of the context frame, then transforming it equivariantly at the last step—a new insight for defining equivariance conditions in diffusion models. Results in Appendix E show potential advantages of p1 over p3-type denoising, though limited to a single timestep. (Reviewers 1, 4)
- **Strong empirical results with clear performance margin.** The benchmark against DP3 and EquiBot across 6 simulated and 4 real-world tasks (e.g., rigid body, articulated, deformable objects) shows ET-SEED outperforms by a substantial margin in most settings, especially on unseen configurations. Real-world experiments with only 20 demonstrations demonstrate both data efficiency and proficiency. (Reviewers 1, 3, 4)
- **Real-world validation and baseline comparisons are valuable.** Reviewer 2 specifically praises the inclusion of real-world experiments and comparisons against state-of-the-art 3D representation policy learning works and EquiBot. (Reviewer 2)
- **Clear writing and straightforward implementation.** Reviewers 1 and 3 find the paper well-written and the proposed method easy to implement yet effective. However, Reviewer 2 disagrees on presentation quality (see Weaknesses). (Reviewers 1, 3; minority contrast from Reviewer 2)
- **Overall contribution worth sharing with the community.** Even with limitations, Reviewer 4 considers the paper’s insights valuable. (Reviewer 4)

### Weaknesses

- **Missing critical visualizations and ablation of contributions.** Reviewers 2 and 4 both note that the paper lacks visualizations demonstrating multi-modality in generated trajectories and equivariant representations. Without such visualizations, it is unclear whether performance gains stem from the probabilistic modeling, the geometric equivariance, or other factors. The trajectory generation process is abstracted into a single success rate metric. (Reviewers 2, 4)
- **No interdependence between keyposes; not a true trajectory-level generative model.** Reviewer 4 points out that each keypose is denoised independently without interaction terms. This contradicts the joint modeling principle behind prior successes like diffusion policy and action chunking transformer. Example: if a robot needs to open a bottle cap and a drawer, the model cannot reason about sequential dependency (grasping then opening). This issue might be mitigated in closed-loop receding horizon control but is problematic for open-loop planning. Reviewer 4 states this is the primary limitation affecting soundness. (Reviewer 4)
- **Presentation quality concerns and figure issues.** Reviewer 2 criticizes the teaser figure as cluttered, failing to convey trajectory equivariance and multi-modality; suggests referring to teasers from diffusion policy and vector neurons. Reviewer 3 notes a typo: in Figure 3, the blocks for Inv. SE(3) transformer and Eqv. SE(3) transformer are identical. Additionally, Reviewer 2 finds the definitions of equivariant/invariant functions (L162-172) overly verbose and questions the space taken by Algorithm 1 and Figure 3. (Reviewers 2, 3)
- **Inappropriate inclusion of unrelated math in appendix.** Reviewer 1 states that most content in Appendix Sections A and B is unrelated to the paper and lowered the score because of this. (Reviewer 1)
- **Undefined math notations.** Example: \(E_{equiv}\) and \(E_{inv}\) are not defined. (Reviewer 1, also repeated as a question)
- **Unclear observation format and equivariance verification.** Reviewer 1 asks: what is the observation format (RGB images? colored point clouds?)? How do the authors guarantee that the transformation \(T\) applied to the input is a simple transformation for verifying policy equivariance? (Reviewer 1)
- **Algorithm 1 does not explicitly show where equivariance/invariance is enforced.** Reviewer 1 notes that the algorithm does not indicate how the equivariance/invariance properties are included; asks whether equivariance is only guaranteed at inference. (Reviewer 1)
- **Implicit assumptions: segmentation, coordinate system, and task-space representation.** Reviewer 2 notes that perception requires point cloud and expert-specified coordinate system; policy assumes 6 DOF end-effector representation and an inverse kinematics/dynamics controller, limiting applicability to dexterous tasks (e.g., pen spinning). Reviewer 4 adds that ET-SEED relies on object segmentation (e.g., SAM), restricting it to object-centric tasks and preventing manipulation of non-object entities (e.g., a pile of tiny objects) or global scene understanding. (Reviewers 2, 4)
- **Limited validation of p1/p2 decomposition.** Reviewer 4 notes that the experiment in Appendix E comparing p1 and p3-type denoising is limited to a single timestep, ignoring potential positive interactions across timesteps with p3-type denoising. (Reviewer 4)
- **Why SE(3)-transformer over more advanced models?** Reviewer 4 asks why the authors used the SE(3)-transformer rather than more efficient/scalable models like Equiformer v2. (Reviewer 4)
