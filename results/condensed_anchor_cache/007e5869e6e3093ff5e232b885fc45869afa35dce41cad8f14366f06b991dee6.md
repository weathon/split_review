## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes PDETime, a long-term multivariate time-series forecasting model that treats the series as samples from a continuous dynamical system governed by partial differential equations (PDEs). It uses an encoding-integration-decoding architecture: the encoder maps inputs to latent codes, a learned neural PDE solver integrates the time derivative of the latent state, and a decoder maps back to the data space. Experiments on seven benchmarks report state-of-the-art results. Reviewers found the core idea novel and promising, but raised significant concerns about fairness of comparisons, lack of efficiency analysis, writing quality, missing differentiation from ODE-based methods, and insufficient justification of the PDE assumption.

### Strengths
- **Novel PDE perspective.** Framing time-series forecasting as an initial-value problem governed by PDEs is a unique and theoretically grounded approach (Reviewers 1,2,3,4,5). It is particularly relevant for multivariate data from sensors measuring the same phenomenon across locations (R3).
- **Strong empirical performance.** PDETime consistently outperforms all baselines across seven diverse real-world datasets and multiple forecasting horizons (R2, R3, R4, R5). The improvements are often large (R2).
- **New PDE solver.** The proposed solver may avoid problems associated with standard Neural-ODE solvers (R3). The architecture mitigates error accumulation common in autoregressive models (R4).
- **Comprehensive experiments.** The paper includes extensive evaluation, ablation studies on key components (solver, temporal/spatial features), and hyperparameter analysis (R1, R3, R4). These enhance reproducibility and scientific rigor (R4).
- **Potential for new research direction.** The approach could serve as a starting point for alternative forecasting paradigms (R2). The paper is well-written with solid experiments (R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Unfair comparison setup.** PDETime’s historical input length \(H\) is optimized per dataset, while all baselines use a fixed \(H\). This biases results because (1) the test set size differs across models, and (2) input length strongly affects performance. Either standardize \(H\) or optimize it for all baselines (R1).
- **Missing code and reproducibility.** Code is not released, reducing credibility and independent verification (R1). Without more implementation details, replication is difficult (R1).
- **Unclear loss weighting.** The weight description in Equation (14) is ambiguous, and \(\mathcal{L}_p\) appears on both sides of the equation (R1, R2). How the losses are balanced is not transparent (R1).
- **Lack of efficiency analysis.** No runtime, memory, or computational complexity comparison with baselines is provided. This is essential for evaluating scalability, especially for long forecasting windows (R1, R2, R4, R5). One reviewer specifically questioned performance with smaller Euler steps (R5).
- **PDE vs. ODE distinction unclear.** The model only computes time derivatives for a fixed spatial encoding \(s\), effectively reducing to an ODE approach. The key difference from established Neural ODE methods (GRU-ODE-Bayes, Latent Linear ODEs, Continuous Recurrent Units) is not adequately explained (R2, R3, R5). The motivation “We are coming from PDEs not ODEs” is insufficient without treating spatial derivatives (R2). For data like ETTH, the PDE assumption seems unmotivated (R5).
- **Missing recent baseline comparisons.** The evaluation omits 2024 methods, including LLM-based approaches (LLM4TS, GPT4TS) and auxiliary series construction (CATS, ICML 2024). These should be added (R5).
- **Limited scope to long horizons.** The model is only evaluated for long-term forecasting; performance on short horizons is not reported (R3).
- **Inaccurate claim about prior work.** The paper states that existing models rely solely on either historical observations or time points, ignoring Informer which uses both local and global time embeddings (R3).
- **Poor writing quality and presentation.** The paper contains undefined variables (e.g., \(\mathbf{X}_{his}\), \(\mathbf{c}_t\), \(\tau_t\), and many weight/bias symbols), incorrect subsection title (Section 3.1 is titled “Problem Formulation” but describes model overview), vague statements (lines 502-505), table ordering errors (Table 1 should appear before Table 3), and numerous typos (e.g., “hiders” → “hinders”, \(\mathbf{x}_{x_0} \rightarrow \mathbf{x}_{t_0}\), extra parentheses in Eq. 10, duplicated \(t'\) in Alg 2). Notation is inconsistent (time index subscript in Eq. 2 vs. superscript in Eq. 1) (R3). Some reviewers found the paper well-written (R5), but these issues undermine clarity.
- **Encoder architecture unclear.**
  - The motivation for the specific encoder design is missing (R3).
  - The notation \(\mathbf{X}^{(k)^i}\) vs. \(\mathbf{X}^{(K)^i}\) is not clearly defined (R3).
  - Equation (7) operations are insufficiently explained; there is a discrepancy between the algorithm (Alg 1, line 3) and the formula (Eq. 7) (R3).
  - In Eq. (6), the left-hand side has subscript \(t\) while the right-hand side does not reference \(t\) (R3).
  - The “For loop” in Alg 1 is not mentioned in the main text (R3).
- **Solver explanation insufficient.**
  - The paper does not clearly compare the proposed solver with the Euler solver or Neural ODE solvers, nor does it illustrate its advantages with a toy example (R3).
  - Figure 2b does not effectively visualize the solver (R3).
  - Figure 3 is claimed to show solver advantages over Euler, but it only shows hyperparameter results for \(k, N, S\) (R3).
- **Ambiguities in experimental details.**
  - The forecasting horizon used in Table 1 is not stated in the caption; one must look at Table 7 in the appendix to learn it is averaged over multiple horizons (R2, R3).
  - There is a discrepancy in the third decimal place of PatchTST results between Table 1 and Table 7 (R3).
  - Table 7 is not referenced until after Section 4.2, making the text confusing (R3).
- **Missing definitions.**
  - The reconstruction loss \(\mathcal{L}_p\) is not explicitly written down (R2).
  - The input time features \(\mathbf{c}^{(0)}\) for each dataset are not described (R2).
- **Solver stability and step size.**
  - The modified Euler solver may be sensitive to step size; using \(\Delta\mu=1\) without theoretical or empirical evidence of stability and boundedness is a concern (R4). High-frequency or abrupt changes may not be well modeled (R4).
  - The choice of step size and potential tradeoffs are not discussed (R4).
- **Motivation for PDE assumption.** The paper does not justify why multivariate time series should be assumed to have a latent PDE structure, especially for datasets where variables measure different aspects (e.g., physiological data: pulse vs. blood pressure) (R3, R5). The condition “sensors at different locations measuring the same thing” is not universally true (R3).
- **Purpose of encoder/decoder and activation functions not justified.**
  - The role of the encoder/decoder alongside the PDE solver is not explained (does it change input dimension for integration?) (R5).
  - The use of both \(\sin\) and GeLU activations lacks mathematical rationale (R5).
