## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates whether sparse autoencoders (SAEs) with a simple linear+ReLU encoder can accurately recover ground-truth sparse codes from synthetic signals and from GPT-2 activations. The authors prove that the encoder’s weight matrix has rank at most the signal dimension M, which is less than the code dimension N, so the encoder cannot recover all sparse codes (“amortisation gap”). They compare standard SAEs against MLP encoders, inference-time-optimised (SAE_ITO) encoders, and classical sparse coding on small synthetic tasks, finding that more powerful encoders (especially sparse coding) outperform SAEs when the FLOP budget is high. They also train a standard SAE and an MLP‑SAE on a single layer of GPT‑2, reporting that MLP features achieve a lower loss and are similarly interpretable to SAE features when evaluated by GPT‑4. Reviewer disagreement is substantial: two reviewers (R1, R3) find the theoretical result interesting and the experiments a useful first step, while one reviewer (R2) argues the theory is flawed, the experiments are not novel, and the interpretability evaluation is insufficient.

### Strengths
- The paper is well‑structured and clearly written, with a thorough “Background and Related Work” section.
- The theoretical proof that a standard SAE encoder (rank ≤ M) cannot perfectly recover sparse codes when N > M is novel and of interest to the interpretability community (R1, R3). R3 calls it “extremely interesting and relevant” and views it as evidence that the worry over powerful encoders is misguided.
- The suite of synthetic experiments is comprehensive, broken into three independent pieces, and uses reasonable alternate encoding schemes (MLP, ITO, sparse coding) (R1, R3).
- The result that MLP‑SAE features on GPT‑2 activations are equally interpretable to standard SAE latents is practically relevant and challenges assumptions from the original SAE design (R1, R3). R1 notes this result is suggestive despite the small scale (N=200 latents each).
- The paper applies sparse‑coding ideas to SAEs, a direction that the mechanistic interpretability field should explore more (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Synthetic experiments are at an unrealistically small scale.** The table below shows the gap between realistic SAE training and the experiments (R3):  
  | Quantity           | Reality             | Experiments |
  |--------------------|---------------------|-------------|
  | N                  | 10k – 10M          | 16          |
  | # training samples | 100M – 10B         | 1024        |
  | K (sparsity)       | 20 – 200           | 3 – 9       |
  | M                  | 500 – 5000         | 8           |
  R1 also notes that for the “Unknown sparse codes and dictionary” case only 2048 samples were used, whereas real SAE training often uses hundreds of millions of samples in a single‑epoch regime.
- **Synthetic data distribution is unrealistic.** The latent codes are drawn from a Gaussian, but real LLM activations exhibit rich structure (e.g., categorical/hierarchical concepts) (R3; also acknowledged by the authors). R1 similarly notes the strong assumptions may not generalise to real LLM activations.
- **The theoretical argument is narrow and, according to one reviewer, contradicts prior work.** R2 argues that the proof ignores the nonlinearity in the SAE encoder; sparse‑coding algorithms such as ISTA and matching pursuit use the same low‑rank matrix \(D^\top D\) yet can provably recover sparse codes. R2 cites evidence that ReLU autoencoders can recover the support of sparse codes (Rangamani et al., ISIT 2018) and that gradient‑descent training on autoencoders can recover the ground‑truth dictionary (Nguyen et al., AISTATS 2019). R2 therefore claims the “amortisation gap” may be resolved and is not a new finding. This is a strong minority disagreement (R2 is the most negative, score 3; R1 and R3 score 6 and 5 respectively).
- **Missing engagement with a large body of prior work** on deep networks for compressed sensing/sparse recovery, including algorithm unrolling (Gregor & LeCun 2010, Monga et al. 2021), deep compressive sensing (Zhang et al. 2021), and analyses of autoencoder loss landscapes (Kunin et al. 2019, Refinetti & Goldt 2022) (R2). R2 also notes that deeper MLPs outperforming shallow architectures is already known from unfolding networks and is not a new finding.
- **Experiments on GPT‑2 are very limited.** Only one model (GPT‑2), one layer, one SAE size, one sparsity level, and one architecture (standard vs. MLP) are tested (R1, R3). R3 raises the concern that performance differences might be due to different training compute or different numbers of dead neurons. The paper does not use modern SOTA SAE architectures (topk, jumprelu, gated) that reduce dead features and might erase the gap (R3). Furthermore, only SAE and MLP are compared on real data; SAE_ITO and sparse coding are omitted, presumably for computational reasons – a weakness that should be stated explicitly (R3).
- **Interpretability evaluation with GPT‑4 is insufficiently described and may not be reliable.** R2 questions the circularity of using an LLM to judge interpretability of features from another LLM. The results show only marginal improvement for MLP features, and R2 notes the paper does not explore whether this is due to the evaluation technique or a fundamental limitation of MLPs. R3 asks whether the difference in interpretability scores is statistically significant; if not, the claim “and in some cases exceeding” (line 502) is unsupported. R3 also asks which deciles of activating examples were used – top‑activating examples are typically more interpretable but may not give the full picture.
- **Figure 1 is confusing:** it labels the decoder as producing “activation space” (inference) while the decoder is usually thought of as mapping latents to reconstruction; a more standard label would be “linear down projection” (R3).
- **Questions/requests in the reviews that overlap with criticisms:**
  - Is the red line (SAE) missing from Figure 4c? (R1) – a potential error that should be checked.
  - Why is the mean correlation coefficient used (eqn 7) rather than MSE for measuring alignment between learned and true dictionary vectors? Would average nearest‑neighbour cosine similarity be more appropriate? (R2, R3).
  - What is \(d\) in the definition of MSE (eqn 6)? (R2).
  - What are the “stepwise improvements in training” for SAE_ITO (line 362)? The paper states SAE_ITO does not train; are the checkpoints from a normal SAE training run? (R3).
  - How close can a linear+ReLU encoder get to perfect recovery? The theory shows impossibility but does not bound the approximation error (R3).
