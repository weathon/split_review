## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Behavioral Cloning with Pointwise Mutual Information Weighting (BC-PMI) for recovering diverse policies from expert trajectories. It assumes trajectories come from stylized experts and weights state-action pairs by their relevance to the assigned style using pointwise mutual information (PMI) between (s,a) and style code z. These weights are used in a style-conditioned behavioral cloning objective. Theoretical justifications show BC-PMI interpolates between standard BC and clustering-based BC under extreme cases (zero mutual information, no style overlap). Experiments are conducted on Circle 2D, Atari games (Alien, MsPacman, Space Invaders), and the Professional Basketball dataset. Results show improved style calibration and diversity compared to baselines. However, BC-PMI requires style labels for each trajectory during training, while the baselines are unsupervised; the paper briefly mentions labeling functions but includes no experiments with them. Several reviewers raised concerns about the technical contribution, missing comparisons, ablation, and the significance of PMI over ordinary mutual information.

### Strengths
1. The paper addresses an interesting and important problem – recovering diverse policies from expert trajectories – with potential benefits for imitation learning data efficiency and robot tasks.
2. The motivation is simple but effective; the idea of selectively weighting state-action pairs based on their relevance to a given style (rather than treating entire trajectories uniformly) is intuitive and impactful. Existing methods typically operate at the trajectory level.
3. The paper is well-written and clearly communicates the problem, method, and results. The use of pointwise mutual information (PMI) and Mutual Information Neural Estimation (MINE) is well connected.
4. The paper provides theoretical insights showing that BC-PMI interpolates between traditional behavioral cloning and clustering-based behavioral cloning under extreme cases (zero mutual information, no overlap among styles).
5. Extensive comparative experiments are conducted across three settings: Circle 2D, Atari games (Alien, MsPacman, Space Invaders), and the Professional Basketball dataset, validating effectiveness compared to baselines. (One reviewer noted the baselines run unsupervised while BC-PMI uses style labels.)

### Weaknesses
1. **Technical contribution**: Reviewers 1 found the key proposal is merely a data weighting algorithm, which does not represent adequate technical contribution. Reviewers 2 and 4 also raised concerns about the significance of PMI over simpler or established alternatives.
2. **Missing ablation/comparison with equal-weighting baseline**: It remains unclear how BC-PMI compares to conditional behavioral cloning (CBC) conditioned on style with equal weights (i.e., without PMI weighting). From Table 1, the improvement of BC-PMI over CBC appears marginal (Reviewer 1).
3. **Missing comparison with other reweighting methods**: No comparison to other data-weighting approaches, e.g., weighting based on discriminator output or policy probability of the action (Reviewer 4). Also unclear if PMI can be replaced with ordinary mutual information; more evidence or discussion about generalizable performance boosting of PMI over MI is needed (Reviewer 2).
4. **Requirement of style labels and unfair comparison**: The method requires style markers for each trajectory, which can be expensive. The paper only briefly mentions labeling functions but includes no experiments with them. This makes comparison with unsupervised baselines (e.g., InfoGAIL) unfair. To ensure fairness, reviewers suggested including supervision in other methods (e.g., train the posterior network in InfoGAIL using style labels) (Reviewer 3).
5. **Limited experiments on number of styles and label noise**: The experimental section lacks analysis on performance as the number of styles increases or when labels are noisy. Given that labeling functions may be imperfect, it would be beneficial to include experiments measuring calibration as the number of styles increases and robustness to label noise (Reviewer 3).
6. **Separate state and action diversity metrics**: The current evaluation focuses on state-action diversity. Separate metrics for state diversity (coverage of state space) and action diversity (variety of actions) could be considered; it is unclear if the method excels in both or only one aspect (Reviewer 4).
7. **Disentanglement discussion**: Previous works (e.g., [1], [2]) built benchmarks for disentanglement between style code and state-action pairs; adapting metrics such as mutual information between style code and the pair could be worth referencing (Reviewer 2).
8. **Methodological choices unexamined**: Why was MINE used to estimate PMI? A simpler classifier-based approach (learning p(z|s,a)) could suffice and should be compared for calibration performance (Reviewer 3). Also, which specific BC algorithm is used as baseline, and does PMI generalize well across different BC algorithms? (Reviewer 1). The calibration metrics in Tables 3/4 need clarification: DTW, ED, or KL? (Reviewer 3).
