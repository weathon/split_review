## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper investigates availability attacks that generate unlearnable examples to prevent unauthorized use of data. It finds that existing supervised attacks fail to achieve contrastive unlearnability, posing a risk if attackers use contrastive learning. The key insight is that stronger data augmentations during poison generation create shortcuts for contrastive learning. The authors propose AUE and AAP attacks that boost worst-case unlearnability across supervised and contrastive algorithms. Extensive experiments demonstrate Pareto improvements over existing methods.

### Strengths

- The problem is well-motivated and the threat model considers worst-case unlearnability, which is practically important because an attacker may use any learning method (Reviewers 1, 4).
- The paper provides a thorough and principled experimental evaluation with extensive literature review (Reviewers 1, 4).
- The proposed attacks (AUE, AAP) achieve state-of-the-art results and show Pareto improvements over existing methods for both supervised and contrastive learning (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- The paper introduces a quantitative measurement of the gap between supervised and contrastive attack effectiveness using alignment and uniformity metrics (Reviewer 5).
- Incorporating contrastive learning–style data augmentations into supervised poisoning generation yields a method that works under both training frameworks (Reviewer 5).
- The use of label information in perturbation generation contributes to stable worst-case unlearnability (Reviewer 2).
- The method is straightforward and easy to implement (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses

- **Clarity and organization** – The paper is difficult to read; Sections 3 and 4 are not clearly structured, the transition between them is abrupt, and the roles of Theorem 4.1 and the empirical analyses are poorly explained (Reviewers 4, 5).  Sections 4.2 and 4.3 (AUE vs. AAP) are redundant because they differ only in existing objectives (error-minimizing/maximizing) rather than a core contribution (Reviewer 2).  The definitions of alignment and uniformity losses (Wang & Isola, 2020) should have been in the background section (Reviewer 4).  Table 1’s grouping of poison methods is unexplained, and the rationale for testing EntF and HYPO (ineffective against both SimCLR and SL) is unclear (Reviewer 4).  The paper would benefit from moving related work from the appendix to the main text (Reviewer 3).

- **Theoretical analysis** – Theorem 4.1 assumes a linear network, mean squared error loss, and only one negative example – unrealistic assumptions that are poorly connected to the complex networks used in experiments (Reviewers 4, 5).  The upper bound is not linked to the main messages, and the paper does not discuss the limitations of this theoretical simplification (Reviewer 4).  The relation between CE loss and InfoNCE loss is not convincingly established; the observed decrease in InfoNCE loss when optimizing CE loss with augmentations may be coincidental (Reviewer 5).

- **Novelty of augmentation strategy** – The core finding (stronger augmentations improve unlearnability) was already known in the community; e.g., ISS and UEraser(-Max) demonstrated that stronger augmentations improve defenses (Reviewer 2).  The augmentation strategy resembles Expectation Over Transformation (EOT) from REM, and the paper does not adequately distinguish its novelty from existing adversarial techniques (Reviewer 5).  A single tuning parameter _s_ controls augmentation strength for three functions; it is unclear whether different hyperparameters per augmentation could further improve performance (Reviewer 4).

- **Comparison with existing methods** – The empirical improvements on worst-case unlearnability are not consistently strong: CP‑BYOL achieves 41.8% on CIFAR-10 (competitive with the proposed methods), and TUE‑MoCo gives similar worst-case unlearnability on CIFAR-100 (Reviewer 4).  SEP demonstrates a Pareto improvement over all proposed attacks except UT‑AAP (Reviewer 1), and UT‑AAP is not strictly better than UT‑AP in Figure 1 (Reviewer 2).  The paper does not sufficiently explain why existing methods (CP, TUE) already achieve good performance or how the proposed method improves over them (Reviewer 4).  A discussion of computation overhead and transferability of TUE and CP is missing (Reviewer 2).

- **Missing experiments and baselines** – Additional defense baselines should be tested, including ISS variants, UEraser variants, and AVATAR (Reviewer 2).  Adaptive defenses with extra augmentations during contrastive learning should be evaluated (Reviewer 3).  Surrogate-free methods like OPS and robust methods like REM should be included (Reviewer 3).  Experiments on ImageNet‑100 (or another ImageNet subset with 100 classes) would better align with prior work (Reviewers 2, 3).  The choice of Tiny-ImageNet (not down-sampled) and Mini-ImageNet (limited samples per class) is not justified (Reviewer 5).  Mean performance values should be reported alongside worst-case values (Reviewer 3).  A correlation analysis (e.g., Pearson coefficients) between alignment/uniformity gaps and contrastive unlearnability is missing to support the claimed correlation (Reviewer 4).  It would be useful to apply the proposed augmentation to other poisoning attacks (TUE, CP) to see if worst-case learnability can be further improved (Reviewer 4).

- **Interplay of attack and defense augmentations** – The paper does not sufficiently explore the trade-off between augmentation strength during attack and defense phases; Table 3’s results are inconclusive about whether stronger defense-phase augmentations can negate stronger attack-phase augmentations within a tight perturbation budget (Reviewer 2).  The relationship between perturbation budget and augmentation strength is underexplored (Reviewer 2).  Section 5.3 frames early stopping as a mitigation from the defender’s perspective, but unlearnable examples are intended for data protection – this framing is confusing (Reviewer 4).

- **Presentation and minor issues** – Tables 3–6 are far too small and should be enlarged or moved to the appendix (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).  Figures lack clear legends/labels (Reviewer 1).  Equation (1) should use `argmin` instead of `argmax` (Reviewers 1, 3).  There are typos (e.g., “obey the same distribution. .” should have a single period) (Reviewer 2).  Code Listing 1 breaks across two pages (Reviewer 2).  The Alignment loss in Figure 3(c) first decreases then increases – this requires explanation (Reviewer 5).  The paper reports that GAP reflects the difference between clean and poison distributions, but Table 1 does not show the expected correlation between GAP and accuracy (Reviewer 5).
