## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a data curation method for DPO-based preference optimization in text-to-image diffusion models. The key idea is to rank image preference pairs from the Pick-a-Pic dataset using a quality metric defined as the product of the winning image’s reward score (e.g., from PickScore/HPSv2) and the inverse (1-X) for the losing image. The authors then fine-tune SD-1.5 and SDXL with Diffusion-DPO (LoRA) using only the top-ranked pairs (5.33% of the dataset). Results show improvements in both human preference reward model evaluations and user studies over models fine-tuned on the full dataset, while dramatically reducing training data and compute.

### Strengths
- The approach is straightforward and does not require complex architectural changes, making it accessible for integration with existing systems.
- Significant improvement in both alignment with human preferences and training efficiency is demonstrated: using only 5.33% of the full dataset outperforms models fine-tuned on all ~1M pairs.
- The paper starts an important discussion on data quality for T2I preference datasets. Filtering existing datasets to get much better data is essential for improving results.
- The proposed quality metric is simple yet provides consistent gains across different DPO variants (including ORPO).
- The paper addresses a relatively novel topic in the field of image generation.
- A series of experiments (quantitative and qualitative) are conducted to validate the approach.

### Weaknesses
- The contribution is basic and limited: essentially introducing a quality metric to rank pairs and select the top portion for Diffusion-DPO fine-tuning. More insights for curating future preference datasets would strengthen the work.
- The method relies heavily on clear preference pairs (“easy samples”). This likely leads to overfitting on easily distinguishable image pairs (as seen in Figures 6 and 7, where selected pairs have clear content differences but may lack nuanced human preferences), limiting generalization to real-world ambiguous inputs. This concern is well-documented in other works.
- Generalizability is not validated: all experiments are conducted solely on the Pick-a-Pic dataset. No cross-dataset testing is performed, making it hard to assert the model’s ability to generalize.
- No ablation or analysis on whether the issue is with the images themselves or with noisy labels (e.g., wrongly assigned winners/losers by annotators). For instance, relabeling Pick-a-Pic using HPSv2 and analyzing the resulting DPO process (as in Diffusion-DPO experiments) would provide stronger quantitative support.
- No comparative experiment using a random subset of the full dataset of the same size as the QSD subset (5.33%) to benchmark the effect of quality-based selection vs. random selection.
- The 5.33% threshold is not well justified. How was this specific value chosen? Different scoring methods use different percentages (HPSv2 and PickScore → 5.33%, Aesthetic predictor → 16%, ImageReward → all samples). Using the same number of samples across methods would enable fairer comparisons. The ImageReward case (no filtering) adds no apparent value.
- Potential bias from using a single reward model (HPSv2) as the scoring metric. The paper notes this but results show more pronounced improvement on HPSv2 metrics compared to others, so more extensive validation is needed to comprehensively confirm QSD superiority.
- The method depends entirely on AI reward models, which may be biased or inaccurate, negatively affecting alignment with genuine human preferences.
- Concerns about data quality in Pick-a-Pic: e.g., Figure 6 (row 1, column 3) shows a winning image that does not align well with the prompt. An ablation study filtering out low-quality/noisy pairs (rather than just focusing on high-quality pairs) would help assess DPO training improvements.
- The approach relies on costly existing preference-pair datasets. Exploring automated pipelines to create high-quality preference data at scale is suggested.
- Lack of detailed explanation of how the quality metric ranks pairs and why specific thresholds (5.33%) were chosen. Improved readability is needed.
- The paper is more of an experimental report than a theoretical or methodological innovation.
- No discussion of limitations or future work directions.
- Presentation issues: Figures 5(a) and 5(b) appear identical; the caption for 5(a) reads “SD1.5-DPO vs SD1.5-DPO” which seems incorrect. Figure 3 and Figure 4 placement is inappropriate, and table design is incomplete.
- (Minority more critical views: Reviewers 1 and 3 rate 3, emphasizing overfitting, lack of generalizability, and limited novelty. Reviewers 2 and 4 rate 5 but still note the contribution is basic and want more analysis and validation.)
