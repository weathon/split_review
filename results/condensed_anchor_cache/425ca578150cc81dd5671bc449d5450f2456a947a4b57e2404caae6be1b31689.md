## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces refusal tokens – meta-tokens (e.g., [REFUSE], [RESPOND]) prepended to responses during fine-tuning – to control a language model’s refusal behavior at inference by adjusting the softmax probability threshold for the refusal token. Two reviewers (ratings 6 and 6) praise the simplicity, thorough analysis, and improvement in task accuracy (Tables 2, 3). Three reviewers (ratings 3, 3, 3) raise major concerns about novelty, missing baselines, clarity, and robustness, and note that the method still requires retraining on a modified dataset. The reviews are sharply divided, with the negative reviewers questioning the contribution and experimental completeness.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important and timely problem: calibrating refusal behavior in LLMs for safety and user trust.
- Technically simple and intuitive solution; multiple reviewers note elegance.
- Extensive analysis across multiple settings, datasets (UltraChat, Coconot, Alpaca), thresholding strategies, and refusal categories.
- Surprising results: training with refusal tokens improves task accuracy on standard LM benchmarks (Tables 2, 3), though significance not shown.
- Threshold sweep allows finding a good true positive rate without many false positives.
- Related work section is comprehensive and informative; Table 1 is useful.
- Multi-category refusal tokens improve normativity and interpretability by clarifying why a refusal is issued.
- Experiments show mostly effective results on both a multi-category refusal dataset and temporal refusal (post-cutoff queries).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns**: The approach is similar to prior controlled generation methods (Quark, Prefix-Tuning, CoCon) and to Sennrich et al. (2016) on side constraints. It also overlooks the closely related work *Refusal in Language Models Is Mediated by a Single Direction*. Reviewers disagree on contribution: some see it as a specific application of existing techniques; others find the application sufficiently novel.
- **Missing comparative baselines**: No comparison to other refusal calibration methods, e.g., alignment-stage or input-processing approaches from Wen et al. (2024) survey, or to directly training a classifier for refusal detection.
- **Omitted evaluation on XSTest**: The XSTest dataset (Röttger et al., NAACL 2024) specifically benchmarks over-refusal; its omission limits analysis of controlling exaggerated refusal.
- **Overclaim**: The method is described as requiring “no further fine-tuning,” but it does require training the model on a modified dataset with refusal/respond tokens – i.e., retraining is needed.
- **Statistical significance not shown**: Results in Tables 2 and 3 lack error bars, making it unclear if the task accuracy improvements are significant.
- **Clarity and organization issues**: The paper is difficult to follow; primary contributions are not well delineated. Some experiments (e.g., Figure 5) are considered distractive; the best method should be presented upfront with variants in appendix. Vague expressions in the introduction (e.g., “refine the token’s effectiveness”) need quantification.
- **Out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization insufficiently explored**: The effect of refusal training on other datasets (e.g., TriviaQA) is small and its desirability unclear. Generalization to refusal on unseen data categories is not studied.
- **Robustness to exploitation not tested**: No experiments against adaptive attacks (jailbreak, backdoor). The risk of users injecting response tokens in inputs to bypass refusals is not mitigated or discussed.
- **Multi-category results are mixed and unclear**: Figure 3 lacks a baseline refusal rate; the “safety” category shows cross-category suppression similar to “humanizing.” In Figure 4 (right), the false positive rate plateaus around 0.35 without explanation.
- **Temporal setting validity questioned**: Whether a query about a pre-cutoff date implies the model knows the answer is questionable – memorization vs. knowledge is not justified. The Coconot experimental setting (training on UltraChat with contrast from Coconot) is unclear.
- **Calibration of softmax probability untested**: Using the softmax probability as a confidence measure assumes the model is calibrated; the GPT-4 paper shows instruction-tuning hurts calibration, and this is not evaluated.
- **SFT vs. DPO choice not justified**: In Figure 2, SFT and DPO have similar performance; the reason for using DPO and the gap between them is unexplained.
- **Missing details on LLM-as-a-judge evaluation**: Manual verification of the Llama-3.1-70B judge’s effectiveness lacks detail (e.g., sample size, agreement metrics).
- **Figure 5 thresholding variant not justified**: Sum thresholding’s advantage over single refusal token is not clarified.
- **Refusal tokens only at start of response, not within**: Longer outputs mixing safe and harmful content are not addressed; no mechanism to refuse partial content.
- **Effect of response tokens on harmlessness not studied**: Whether adding a response token affects output when refusal threshold is not met is unknown.
- **Minor**: Table 4 could be presented as a heatmap (confusion matrix) for clarity.
