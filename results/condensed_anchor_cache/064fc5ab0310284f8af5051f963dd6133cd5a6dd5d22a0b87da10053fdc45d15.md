## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes C-MORL, a two-stage algorithm for multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL) that combines constrained policy optimization with Pareto front discovery. The first stage (Pareto initialization) trains a set of policies in parallel, each optimized towards a sampled preference vector. The second stage (Pareto extension) selects policies based on crowding distance and performs constrained optimization (CPO or IPO) to maximize one objective while constraining others, aiming to fill vacancies on the Pareto front. The method is evaluated on 10 benchmarks (discrete and continuous control, up to 9 objectives) using hypervolume, expected utility, and sparsity, and compared against GPI-LS, CAPQL, PGMORL, and others.

### Strengths
- **Originality**: The combination of constrained policy optimization (CMDP formalism) with MORL is novel. The crossover from MOO epsilon-constraint methods to sequential decision-making is creative (Reviewer 2). It provides a fresh perspective on Pareto front discovery (Reviewer 1).
- **Theoretical rigor**: The paper provides sound propositions and proofs (e.g., Proposition 4.3 on sufficient conditions for Pareto-optimal solutions via constrained optimization; Proposition 5.1 on complexity). The theoretical analysis is built on established CMDP foundations but adapted to MORL (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).
- **Extensive evaluations**: Experiments cover 10 diverse benchmarks (discrete and continuous, up to 9 objectives), including challenging environments like Building-9D. Results show improvements over recent MORL methods (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4). The problem of deciding which directions to learn when approximating a Pareto front is highly relevant (Reviewer 3).
- **Good presentation** (Reviewer 2): The paper is well set up, with clear motivation and coherent flow. This contrasts with other reviewers who found clarity lacking (see Weaknesses).
- **Solid foundation**: The optimization approach builds on extensive constraint theories, and conclusions align with research questions (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Scalability concerns**: The method requires a separate neural network per policy, which does not scale in memory, especially for image-based observations (e.g., CNN-based policies). This is a practical limitation for real-world deployment (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3). Storing all intermediate policies in a buffer is resource-intensive; constructing the CCS through a vast number of policies is inefficient (Reviewer 4). The number of policies required to cover Pareto front may increase exponentially with the number of objectives, making the algorithm’s linear-time claim (Proposition 5.1) potentially misleading because users may need to increase N with n (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing comparisons and baselines**: No comparison against **MORL/D** (with PSA weight adaptation), which is a closely related evolutionary approach with state-of-the-art results (Reviewer 2). The paper does not discuss **Willem et al., 2024** (“Divide and Conquer: Provably Unveiling the Pareto Front with Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning”) which also uses constrained MDPs to identify Pareto policies (Reviewer 3).
- **Evaluation fairness and statistical issues**:
    - Linear scalarization methods (GPI-LS, CAPQL) are evaluated with a fixed number of preference vectors (e.g., 100), while evolutionary methods (PGMORL, C-MORL) use an unbounded archive of policies, which biases metrics like sparsity in favor of the latter (Reviewer 2).
    - Underlying RL algorithms differ: GPI-LS and CAPQL use SAC/TD3, while C-MORL uses PPO. Performance gains may come from the base RL algorithm rather than the expansion technique. No reimplementation with PPO was reported (Reviewer 3).
    - In MO-Ant-2d, the reported HV (3.10±0.25 vs 3.13±0.20) and EU (4.28±0.19 vs 4.29±0.19) overlap with standard deviations; this cannot be considered a solid improvement over GPI-LS (Reviewer 4). Bold metrics in Table 2 overlap with competitors (Reviewer 3).
    - Only three random seeds are used, which is insufficient for statistical significance (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4).
- **Clarity and missing details**: Many aspects are poorly explained (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4):
    - Number of initial policies, number of extension policies, and distribution of weight preferences are not specified (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 4). The paper does not explain how many policies are stored and used to construct the CCS versus PGMORL (Reviewer 4).
    - Hyperparameters: constraint relaxation coefficient (β in Eq. 3) and parameter t of function φ in Eq. 5 are not discussed; their impact on optimality is unclear (Reviewer 3). Do these require problem-specific reward range knowledge? (Reviewer 1).
    - The algorithm has many stages (Pareto initialization, policy selection, Pareto extension) with design choices (crowd distance vs random selection; C-MORL-CPO vs C-MORL-IPO) that are studied neither via ablation nor explained well (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 4). It is unclear which version (CPO or IPO) is actually used in the experiments (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4).
    - Terms are undefined: projection operators Γ_λ and Γ_π (Reviewer 3), “solution buffer” and how it enhances diversity (Reviewer 3), “fixed point of MORL policy” (Reviewer 3), “Slater’s condition” in Theorem 4.5 (Reviewer 3). The notation G for expected return is nonstandard (J or V is typical) (Reviewer 3).
    - Reference to PG-MORL solving a knapsack problem is not found in Xu et al. 2020 (Reviewer 3).
- **Limited innovation and flawed assumptions**: The core contribution is limited to using crowding distance to select policies for constrained optimization on top of PGMORL (Reviewer 4). For unseen preferences, the method simply matches to the nearest existing policy without discovering new ones, yielding locally optimal rather than globally optimal solutions (Reviewer 4). The method **defaults to selecting extreme solutions** for extension, but those have already reached performance bottlenecks on particular objectives and do not facilitate exploration of the potential CCS—especially problematic for non-convex Pareto fronts (Reviewer 4). Preferences remain unchanged during extension, causing policies to converge around certain preferences, hindering CCS exploration (Reviewer 4). PGMORL addressed this with a utility prediction method, but C-MORL does not mention the problem (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing ablations**: No ablation of the Pareto Extension procedure (e.g., comparing CPO/IPO against solving for random linear weights) is provided, leaving unclear whether the constrained optimization overhead yields actual gains (Reviewer 3). Similarly, CPO vs IPO are not ablated in the main results (Reviewer 4).
- **Overuse of formulas and theorems**: The paper includes many formulas that contribute little to the core algorithm (policy selection and assignment), making it harder to follow (Reviewer 4).
- **Concave Pareto fronts**: The paper does not analyze how C-MORL would react to concave pockets on the Pareto front; constrained optimization may cause policies to prefer easier paths in convex regions (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4). (The paper notes that constrained optimization sometimes fails to extend policies toward certain objective directions, but does not discuss concave cases explicitly.)

*Contrasting notes*: Reviewer 2 strongly praised the presentation and novelty, while other reviewers found the paper unclear and lacking innovation. Reviewer 2 emphasized theoretical rigor with high impact, whereas Reviewer 4 saw the contribution as a straightforward encapsulation of existing methods. These disagreements indicate that the paper’s strengths in originality and clarity are not uniformly recognized.
