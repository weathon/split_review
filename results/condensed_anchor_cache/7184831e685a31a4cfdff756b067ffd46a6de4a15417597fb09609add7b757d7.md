## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Matryoshka Diffusion Models (MDMs) for high-resolution image and video synthesis. MDM uses a diffusion process that denoises inputs at multiple resolutions jointly via a NestedUNet architecture, with a progressive training schedule from lower to higher resolutions. The method is evaluated on class-conditioned image generation (ImageNet), text-to-image (MS-COCO, CC12M), and text-to-video (WebVid-10M). While the qualitative results are visually impressive given limited compute and data, reviewers identify significant performance gaps (especially on FID), unclear novelty relative to prior work (f-DM, Stable Diffusion XL), missing quantitative evaluation for video, and concerns about the fairness and rigor of comparisons.

### Strengths
- **Visually impressive high-resolution results** (R2, R3, R4), especially given the relatively small training data (CC12M, 12M images) and limited compute (R2, R3). R3 notes the output “looks very good.” R2 and R4 describe the high-resolution outputs as “impressive.”
- **Training flexibility over cascaded diffusion models (CDMs):** MDM does not require earlier stages to be fully trained before later stages become meaningful. Unlike CDMs, joint generation from scratch is possible even when the base stage is immature (R1, R3). R3 specifically contrasts with CDMs where “one cannot generate images from scratch in the middle since the base stage has not been fully trained yet.”
- **Solid ablation studies** that quantify the effects of progressive training and the number of nested levels on output quality and alignment (R2, R3).
- **Clear exposition:** The framework is “straightforward and easy to understand” (R2), drawing inspiration from existing GAN literature. The paper is generally well-written and well-motivated (R2, R3, R4).
- **Faster convergence** compared to traditional cascaded diffusion models in terms of training iterations (R1, R4).
- **Minority positive view:** One reviewer (R2, score 8) finds the paper’s evaluation on multiple tasks yields “comparable results” and that the qualitative results are particularly notable given the small-scale training data.

### Weaknesses
- **Large performance gaps to strong baselines:**  
  - On ImageNet 256×256, MDM achieves FID 6.62 vs. LDM 3.60 (R1, R4).  
  - On ImageNet 512×512, FID is “~3x higher than SotA (e.g., MDT)” (R3).  
  - R2 notes the “noticeable gap … FID score for MDM is significantly worse” and all reviewers request an in-depth analysis of why this gap exists—whether due to insufficient training or an inherent limitation (R2, R3, R4). R1 specifically notes MDM does not surpass LDM with classifier-free guidance.  
- **Missing quantitative evaluation and comparisons for text-to-video:** Video generation is highlighted as a contribution but no quantitative metrics or comparisons to existing video models are provided (R2, R3, R4). No video files (mp4/gif) are included, even for moving scenes (R3). Reviewers ask about training duration, joint image/video training, and performance on dynamic scenes (R3).
- **Novelty concerns:**  
  - Multi-resolution training is already present in Stable Diffusion XL (R1).  
  - The NestedUNet architecture appears similar to the prior f-DM framework (Gu et al.); the only apparent differences are progressive training and larger-scale experiments. R3 asks: “Are there any other differences … apart from the progressive training idea and larger-scale experiments? … f-DM uses the same NestedUNet idea, but the f-DM authors just do not call it a ‘NestedUNet’.”  
  - The paper claims “end-to-end” training, but the method still requires progressive stage-by-stage training and performs considerably worse without it (R3).  
- **Unfair comparison to CDMs:** The reported CDMs are likely undertrained given the limited budget—the base stage should receive more focus since later stages converge faster (R3). It is “unclear if the reported results for CDMs are the best achievable within the given budget” (R3).
- **Computational trade-offs unclear:**  
  - Increased computational load is anticipated (R1).  
  - Training costs are not reported rigorously; only GPU count is given without duration (R3).  
  - The “faster convergence” claim uses training iterations, not wall-clock time; R4 requests time/iteration for each baseline.  
  - R2 asks how multi-resolution prediction affects sampling speed.  
- **Missing implementation details:** Learning rate, batch size, model configurations are omitted (R4). Variables in the NestedUNet pseudocode are unclear and need a brief description (R2). The CFG weight used for Figure 5 (ImageNet samples) is not reported (R3).
- **Missing comparisons for CC12M text-to-image:** The paper claims good zero-shot results on CC12M but does not compare to existing large-scale generators (e.g., Imagen, Parti). R3 notes that judging by the maximum CLIP score on the CLIP/FID trade-off chart is “misleading since … even bad models can attain very high CLIP scores under strong guidance.”
- **Missing data points in Figure 4:** Points for some baselines (e.g., Latent DM after 200K in Figure 4(a)) are absent without explanation (R4).
- **Lack of clarity in Figure 3:** The novel pathways introduced by NestedUNet should be explicitly labeled to distinguish from standard UNet skip connections (R2).
- **Unaddressed request for explanation about train/test gap:** R3 asks why the model does not suffer from the CDM train/test gap—i.e., how later stages can generate meaningful images even when the low-resolution stage is not fully trained.
- **Multiple typos and formatting issues:** “crtical” → “critical” (page 2), “under performs” → “underperforms” (page 6), “eg” → “e.g.” (page 7), missing spaces before citation parentheses on page 8 (R2, R3).
