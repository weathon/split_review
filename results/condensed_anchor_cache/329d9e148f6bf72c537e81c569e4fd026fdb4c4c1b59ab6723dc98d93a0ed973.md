## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper investigates uncertainty-based abstention in LLMs (Llama 2) to improve correctness on QA datasets (TriviaQA, SciQA, CoQA, StrategyQA, GSM8K), reduce hallucination on unanswerable questions (SelfAware, SciQA), and increase safety on adversarial prompts (AttaQ, AutoDAN). Two categories of uncertainty metrics are tested: statistical (negative log-likelihood, predictive entropy, semantic entropy) and in-dialogue (InDU, based on hedge word counts). Experiments show that thresholding uncertainty scores can improve correctness by 2–8%, avoid 50% hallucinations, and increase safety by 70–99% at the cost of abstention. Different metrics work best for different tasks. RLHF effects are also examined. Reviewer scores range from 3 to 5, with two reviewers positive and three negative, primarily due to limited novelty and missing comparisons.

### Strengths

- The core idea of using uncertainty for abstention to improve factuality, reduce hallucinations, and enhance safety is timely, important, and well-motivated for real-world LLM deployment.
- The paper is well-written, clearly organized, and easy to follow.
- The experiment design is sound; multiple relevant datasets are tested across three task categories (knowledge QA, unanswerable questions, adversarial safety).
- In-Dialogue Uncertainty (InDU), counting hedge words as a confidence signal, is a new metric not proposed in prior work (as far as the reviewers know).
- Experiments test diverse uncertainty measures (NLL, predictive entropy, semantic entropy, InDU) across different model sizes and variants (with/without RLHF).
- The paper outlines multiple applications (correctness, hallucination, safety) and underscores the importance of uncertainty for abstention.

### Weaknesses

- **Limited novelty**: The high-level conclusion that uncertainty is useful for abstention has already been thoroughly explored (Kadavath et al., 2022; many earlier papers cited in related work). The core contributions—InDU and the observation that different uncertainty metrics are suited to different tasks—are seen as small, more fitting for an appendix rather than a full paper. The safety result is interesting but not explored in sufficient depth (only one paragraph).
- **Missing related work**: The paper omits several recent works showing similar results, including:
  - https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.21028 (abstention for factuality)
  - https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18418
  - https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.13387
  - https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09677
  - https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00474
  - https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.06730
  - https://aclanthology.org/2024.naacl-long.301/
  - https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13734 (classifiers based on hidden representations)
  - https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.00367 (ensemble prompting)
  - https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13213 (similar findings)
- **Outdated models**: Experiments are limited to Llama 2; newer, more performant models (e.g., Llama 3) are not tested, weakening claims about generalizability.
- **No unified method**: The paper does not provide a single recipe or method that works well across all settings; it only recommends task-specific choices without deeper analysis.
- **In-Dialogue Uncertainty limitations**: InDU is a heuristic that may not be generalizable (e.g., some prompts may require hedge words). Its use requires accounting for all possible hedge words, which is impractical. It does not consistently outperform statistical methods.
- **Missing comparisons with more sophisticated approaches**: The paper does not empirically compare against classifier-based uncertainty (e.g., using hidden representations) or ensemble methods (e.g., LLM ensemble prompting), despite acknowledging their weaknesses but not demonstrating them. Since distribution shift is not a focus, there is no reason to omit these baselines.
- **Insufficient evaluation breadth**:
  - Hallucination experiments only use the SelfAware dataset; additional datasets are needed, especially given the prevalence of RAG-based approaches (e.g., Self-RAG).
  - Safety experiments rely on AttaQ and AutoDAN; broader evaluation with PKU-SafeRLHF (safe decoupled preferences, red-teaming prompts) and case-by-case GPT-4 evaluation is missing.
  - Current evaluation uses keyword-based, static methods (e.g., Llama Guard) that may miss nuanced harmful responses (emotional abuse). Granular GPT-4 evaluation is recommended.
- **RLHF claims not fully supported**: The claim that "RLHF enhances uncertainty awareness regarding safety" is not convincingly demonstrated. RLHF alone does not guarantee safety (e.g., GPT-4 technical report shows brittle behaviors). Comparisons with safety-specific alignment methods (RLCD, Safe RLHF) are needed.
- **Figures and clarity**: Figure 3 uses vague terms like "statistical uncertainty" instead of specific measures and model names. Baseline curves are missing, making it hard to see improvements. The plots should clearly indicate the specific metric and model.
- **Lack of explanation**: The experiments mostly report observations without attempting to explain why certain uncertainty measures work better for certain tasks.
- **Unclear practical impact**: The paper does not adequately address the tradeoff between abstention and usability in practical deployment.
- **Minor issues**: Various typos (e.g., "In-Dialogoe" in Introduction, "unanswerable vs. unanswerable" at L.377, "abstaining to answer" at L.471, InDU misspelled and acronym not used). The abstract/intro does not clearly signal that this is an analysis paper rather than a proposed training/inference method.
- **Specific reviewer questions (as weaknesses)**:
  - How can practitioners account for all possible hedge words in practice?
  - The findings on hallucinations are limited to unanswerable questions; how extendable are they to other hallucination types?
  - Statistical uncertainty metrics perform at similar levels; what is the key takeaway for practitioners?
  - The claim that practitioners should distinguish question types before choosing uncertainty metric is not directly supported by the experiments.
