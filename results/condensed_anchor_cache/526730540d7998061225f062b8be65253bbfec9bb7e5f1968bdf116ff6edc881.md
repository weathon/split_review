## Merged Review

### Summary
All four reviewers (scores 8/8/8/8, confidence 4–5) emphasize the paper’s strong contribution to SSL robustness evaluation in open environments: a comprehensive benchmark covering tabular, image, and text data with 20 SSL methods (statistical, classical deep, robust deep), novel metrics (RAC, AUC, EA, WA), and a solid theoretical framework. The work is seen as meaningful, well-motivated, and reliable due to the combination of empirical and theoretical analysis. Weaknesses center on presentation density (too much text, overcrowded Figure 2, proof sketches in section 7 rather than full theorems), unclear definition of the inconsistency variable `t`, the choice of “robust” over the more precise “safe,” insufficient explanation of relationships among the many metrics, incomplete text-dataset evaluation for some algorithms, and several open questions about the theory’s scope and practical applicability.

### Strengths
1. **Novel and scientifically sound evaluation tools** – The use of Robustness Analysis Curves (RAC) and derived metrics (AUC, EA, WA) provides a fresh perspective on SSL robustness and will guide future algorithm design. [Reviewers 1, 3, 4]
2. **Comprehensive benchmark** – The work covers three open-environment inconsistency scenarios (data distribution, label space, feature space), three data modalities (tabular, image, text), and a wide range of algorithms (20 methods including statistical SSL, classical deep SSL, and robust deep SSL). This breadth makes the benchmark highly valuable for the SSL community. [Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4]
3. **Solid theoretical foundation backed by extensive experiments** – The paper unifies three challenges in a single theoretical framework and supports it with thorough experimental results, ensuring reliability and providing deeper insights into robustness mechanisms. [Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4]
4. **Clear and well-articulated motivation** – The challenges of label/unlabeled inconsistency in open environments are clearly presented, setting a strong foundation for the study. [Reviewer 4]

### Weaknesses
1. **Presentation density and lack of visual clarity** – The main paper is text-heavy and lacks sufficient tables and figures to present results. Section 7 includes only proof sketches; full theorem statements should appear in the main paper. Figure 2 plots too many methods, making comparison difficult; a smaller set of representative baselines would improve readability. [Reviewers 2, 4]
2. **Unclear definition of the inconsistency variable `t`** – `t` is introduced as a measure of inconsistency but is not clearly quantified (e.g., via KL divergence or Wasserstein distance) nor linked to the specific inconsistency types (distribution, label space, feature space). A concrete example with a toy dataset is needed. [Reviewer 4]
3. **Terminology mismatch: “robust” vs. “safe” SSL** – The robustness studied is essentially safe semi-supervised learning, where the goal is to avoid degrading below a supervised baseline. Using “safe” would be more precise and align better with existing literature. [Reviewer 4]
4. **Relationships among the multiple metrics are unclear** – The paper proposes RAC, AUC, EA, and WA but does not explain how they relate to each other, what specific robustness aspect each captures, or why each is necessary. More discussion of motivations and interdependencies is required. [Reviewer 4]
5. **Incomplete evaluation on text datasets** – Some algorithms were evaluated only on tabular and image data, and not on text, without explanation of the reason. [Reviewer 3]
6. **Limited scope of the evaluation framework** – As the authors note, the framework cannot assess highly complex real-world scenarios with low computational complexity. [Reviewer 1]
7. **No unified ranking method** – Despite many experimental results, the paper does not provide a single ranking metric or method to compare algorithms, making it hard to determine which algorithm is best overall. [Reviewer 2]
8. **Open question on theory’s applicability** – The theoretical results appear to hold only when the degrees of inconsistency are the same across the three types; it is unclear whether the theory applies when degrees differ. [Reviewer 4]
9. **Lack of guidance for correlating metrics to real-world inconsistency** – The paper does not explain how the inconsistency metrics used in the evaluation framework can be correlated with real-world inconsistency in practical applications. [Reviewer 1]

*Note: Minor disagreement exists on presentation (Reviewer 4 gave 4, others 3) and contribution (Reviewer 3 gave 3, others 4); all other ratings are consistent.*
