## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes KTD, a method for training data detection (membership inference) that treats the problem as variable selection and applies Knockoff Inference (KI) with multiple knockoff draws to achieve false discovery rate (FDR) control. The method requires white-box access to the model (gradients) and does not need a validation set containing both training and non-training samples (non-training samples are still required). Theoretical analysis claims asymptotic optimality for FDR and power. Empirical evaluations are performed on several LMs using datasets WikiMIA, XSum, and Real Time BBC News.

### Strengths
- The problem of detecting whether a sample is in the training set is important and timely (R1, R4).
- The idea of applying Knockoff Inference to training data detection is novel and well-motivated; it is the first adaptation of KI to LLMs (R1, R3, R4).
- KTD offers a new perspective by not requiring a validation set containing both training and non-training samples (only non-training samples are needed) (R3).
- The method is a clean extension of KI, preserving its good properties and providing asymptotic optimality at the limit (R1, R2).
- The theoretical foundation is rigorous, with careful mathematical analysis and proofs (R2, R1).
- The method scales well with model size and the number of knockoff draws m, obtaining strong results in many settings (R3).
- The paper is well written, with assumptions and prerequisites mostly outlined; the method is easy to understand (R3). *One reviewer (R1) rated presentation as 2, indicating disagreement on clarity.*

### Weaknesses
- **FDR control violation at low FDR:** The FDR control bounds from Proposition 2 do not appear to hold in general (Figure 1), particularly for the important low‑FDR regime. This is a critical weakness because low FDR is the motivating setting for legal applications (R1, R3). Reviewer 2 notes this limitation but considers it acceptable; the other reviewers treat it as a serious concern. Experiments should be extended to cover the low‑FDR regime (e.g., 1% and 0.1%) (R3). The authors should explain why the bound fails and whether the method can control FDR at very low targets (R3).
- **Unclear assumptions and ad‑hoc choices:** The complete assumptions for asymptotic optimality (Theorem 1 and 2) are not clearly stated. Lemma 1 is more of an assumption than a proven lemma; the required symmetry of the knockoff statistic \(W_j\) for non‑training samples is not guaranteed by the knockoff construction, especially in high‑dimensional NLP settings (R1, R3). Moreover, the choice of using the L2 norm of the model’s gradient as the KTD score is not adequately justified; it seems ad‑hoc, and no statistical analysis is provided for determining the number of knockoffs m (R4). Without stronger justification, the theoretical guarantees are less meaningful (R4).
- **Missing experimental details:** The exact fine‑tuning setup (learning rate, optimizer, number of epochs) is not reported (R1, R3). The method used to generate knockoff samples is insufficiently detailed, hindering reproducibility (R1). The paper also lacks information on how gradients are computed and whether any optimization techniques are used for efficiency (R1). Reviewer 4 questions why fine‑tuning is performed on the training samples at all, as it may cause overfitting and make detection easier than it should be (R4).
- **Missing ablations:** Important ablations are absent, such as varying the concentration of training samples in the training set and the ratio of training to non‑training samples in the detection set. The performance and FDR control when training samples constitute only a small fraction (e.g., 10%, 5%, 1%) of the detection set should be investigated (R1, R3). The paper should also examine how the performance changes as the proportion of training samples in the detection set decreases (R3). Additionally, no experiments test different KTD scores (e.g., influence‑based scores) or larger models like Llama3 (R4).
- **Missing related work and limited novelty:** Several relevant methods are not cited or compared: membership inference with FDR control by Carlini et al. and Mireshghallah et al., contamination detection by Dekoninck et al. (ConStat), and evasion of contamination detection by Dekoninck et al. (B) (R1, R3). Reviewer 2 notes that the core method is similar to the foundation established by Mattern et al. (2023) and Fu et al. (2023), making the contribution incremental; this should be acknowledged and discussed.
- **Limitations not discussed:** The method requires computing several gradients with respect to model weights, limiting applicability to open‑weight models only (R1, R3). It cannot detect training data that have been significantly rephrased (Dekoninck et al. B) (R1, R3). The computational cost of generating multiple knockoffs and computing gradients for each is not addressed (R1, R2). The paper should explicitly discuss these limitations and the trade‑off between computational expense and improved performance (R1, R2).
- **Presentation and clarity issues:** Some portions of the paper are confusing or lack sufficient background. For instance, the problem statement on page 1 is ambiguous, the notation \(a \vee b = \min(a,b)\) is non‑standard and confusing (R4), equation (1) may contain a typo (\(\vee\) should likely mean maximum), equation (7) should probably be \(\arg\min\), Definition 2 could be clearer if done element‑wise, and the meaning of “achieve FDR” in Table 2 is unclear when \(q=1\) (R3). The paper should also clarify the difference between training data detection and membership inference (R3). Reviewer 4 notes that an outsider would benefit from a brief explanation of how knockoffs work earlier in the paper (R4).
- **Questions raised by reviewers (overlapping with weaknesses):**
    1. Why does the FDR bound (Proposition 2) fail for low q? Can all experiments be repeated for the low‑FDR regime (1%, 0.1%)? (R3)
    2. What are the exact fine‑tuning parameters? Is any background data used to dilute the training data to more realistic proportions (e.g., 10%, 5%, 1%)? (R3)
    3. How does FDR control change if the portion of training samples in the detection set is reduced to 10%/5%/1%? (R3)
    4. In Figure 2, the tails of the training samples appear thick. Can a comparison of just the positive and negative tails of \(W_j\) be plotted? Symmetry even there is required for FDR control at low FDR. (R3)
    5. Can Figure 4 be extended to focus on the low‑FDR regime (smaller q) with a logarithmic x‑axis? (R3)
    6. Why was the L2 norm of the gradient chosen as the KTD score? Can you provide a statistical justification or analyze alternative scores? (R4)
    7. Can you provide a statistical guarantee for how to determine the number of knockoff samples (m), for example in the spirit of hypothesis testing? (R4)
