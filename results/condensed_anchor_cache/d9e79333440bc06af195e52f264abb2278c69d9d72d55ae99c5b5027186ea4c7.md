## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Multidimensional Trajectory Optimization (MTO), which extends flow and diffusion models by using multidimensional coefficients (rather than unidimensional ones) to parameterize the interpolant/trajectory. The coefficients are optimized via adversarial training (generator-discriminator setup) to improve final sample quality. The approach is validated on 2D synthetic datasets, CIFAR-10, FFHQ, and AFHQv2, using EDM and Stochastic Interpolant backbones. The paper reports performance improvements at low NFE (e.g., 5) and claims state-of-the-art results on CIFAR-10 conditional generation.

### Strengths
- **Novelty of multidimensional coefficients**: The core idea of generalizing unidimensional diffusion/flow coefficients to multiple dimensions is seen as interesting and potentially impactful (R1, R3, R4). The parametric family ensures boundary conditions and permits efficient learning (R3).
- **Empirical breadth**: Extensive experiments across multiple datasets (2D synthetic, CIFAR-10, FFHQ, AFHQv2) and model backbones (EDM, Stochastic Interpolant) (R1, R2, R3). Results show consistent lower Wasserstein distances on synthetic data and performance on par or slightly better on real image generation, particularly at low NFE (R1, R3).
- **Writing clarity (mixed)**: One reviewer found the method easy to understand and the writing clear (R2), though others raised significant clarity concerns (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and incremental contribution**: Several reviewers question the depth of novelty. The adversarial training component is already explored in Adversarial Diffusion Distillation, Consistency Trajectory Models, UFOGen, and others (R1). The multidimensional coefficient is seen as a direct generalization of single-step generation methods (CTM, UFOGen, GDD, GDD-I) (R1). One reviewer notes that the ablation study shows EDM‑γ + Adv. θ achieves comparable performance, suggesting the critical component is the adversarial objective, not the trajectory coefficients (R2). Another notes that the method requires simulation and a complex adversarial setup, moving away from the simulation-free training advantage of standard flow/diffusion models (R4). The paper does not clearly differentiate from or discuss existing methods for learning diffusion trajectories such as "Where to Diffuse..." and Neural Flow Diffusion Models (R4).
- **Missing comparisons and experiments**:
  - No results on ImageNet-64, a standard benchmark for few-step generation (R1).
  - Comparisons on FFHQ and AFHQ are limited; many established one- and few-step distillation methods (DMD, UFOGen, Diffusion‑GAN, Diff‑Instruct, iCT, GET‑onestep, TRACT) are not cited or compared (R1).
  - The paper does not provide flow-matching results on image datasets (R2).
  - In Tables 2–4, MTO (with up to 5 NFE) is often outperformed by one-step distillation methods (e.g., CD, CTM) despite having higher NFE; the authors do not explain this (R1, R4). It would be fairer to run CD/CTM with the same NFE (5 or 6) (R3).
- **Clarity and exposition**:
  - The mathematical formulations, especially for the multidimensional coefficients and adversarial loss, are hard to follow and lack intuitive explanations (R1, R4). The adversarial loss in Section 4.4 is particularly unclear: the discriminator Dψ is introduced in equations but not described in text (R3, R4). The notation Lψ, Lφ, Lθ does not clarify whether gradient flow is stopped; Algorithm 1, Line 12 is vague (R3). The rationale of minimising Lθ (encouraging large Dψ output for xt) is inconsistent with the adversarial principle (R3).
  - Definition 1’s boundary conditions for γ are not motivated; later the paper allows γ to depend on x1 after initially forbidding it (R4).
  - Notation is inconsistent: ρ1 and ρT are both used for the noise distribution (R4). The motivation for using hinge loss in adversarial training is not given (R3).
  - Figure 2 needs a clearer caption to explain the differences (R3). The comment “NFE = 6 (+1 for γφ)” is not explained (R3). The reason for choosing N=5 in image experiments and whether different samplers/time schedulers can be used after training is unclear (R2).
  - While one reviewer found the writing clear (R2), others consider it a weakness due to the above issues.
- **Training cost and scalability**:
  - No evaluation of training cost is provided. The method requires backpropagation through an ODE solver, which is computationally expensive (R3, R4). Discussion of scalability to higher-dimensional datasets (e.g., ImageNet) is missing (R1, R4, R3).
  - The optimization of γ is performed only at the specific time schedules used during training (Equation 13); does this force the inference schedule to match? (R2)
- **Pretraining stage details**: How are “randomly sampled trajectories” obtained? Does the pretraining also use the proposed parametric family? Which parameters are random and what distributions do they follow? How is it ensured that pretraining does not degrade performance? (R3)
- **Theoretical foundation**: The paper lacks theoretical insight into why MTO works; it is noted as missing by one reviewer (R1).
- **State-dependent coefficients**: Can the multidimensional coefficients depend on the state x(t)? (R2) The paper initially forbids dependence on x1 but later allows it, causing confusion (R4).
- **Equation verification**: Presumably γ₀(T) on the right-hand side of Equation (8) should be verified (R2).
