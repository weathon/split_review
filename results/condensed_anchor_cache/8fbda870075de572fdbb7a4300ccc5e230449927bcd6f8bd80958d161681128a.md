## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes GradSimCore, a coreset selection method that selects representative training instances by computing the cosine similarity of loss gradients (from the last layer) among samples within the same class. After running the model for a few epochs, a cross-similarity matrix is built, averaged column-wise, and sorted to pick the top-\(k\) samples. Experiments on MNIST, CIFAR10, CIFAR100, and ImageNet are conducted at very low coreset percentages (<20%) and compared against methods included in the DeepCore library. The paper claims that GradSimCore outperforms those DeepCore baselines in most cases. An anonymous code link is provided.

### Strengths

- The problem of efficient coreset selection is important and needs further research (Reviewer 4).
- The proposed metric based on gradient similarity is novel and intuitive (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- The method is simple and straightforward: after a few training epochs, measure gradients of the last layer, build a cross-similarity matrix, average column-wise, and sort (Reviewer 1). The method is testable/implementable from the paper (Reviewer 4).
- GradSimCore outperforms baselines implemented in DeepCore on CIFAR10, CIFAR100, and ImageNet for most of the reported low-coreset sizes (Reviewer 2).
- Code is provided (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses

1. **Lack of theoretical justification** – The assumption that samples whose gradients are closer to the average gradient of the class are the most representative is not rigorously justified. The importance of noisy gradients for effective learning (e.g., hard-negative mining, label softening, dropout) is well documented, and the proposed assumption may only hold under stringent constraints on the loss manifold (Reviewer 1).

2. **Computational expense and scalability** – The algorithm is extremely expensive. For full ImageNet, modern server-class instances likely cannot even hold the gradients in memory, and identifying the coreset could cost more than training on the full dataset. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of memory requirements, time complexity, or the overhead of building the similarity matrix (Reviewer 1).

3. **Incomplete comparisons with SOTA baselines** – The method is compared only against baselines inside the DeepCore library, missing recent state-of-the-art coreset selection methods such as EL2N, CCS, and Moderate. Without these comparisons, it is not convincing that GradSimCore is competitive (Reviewers 2, 4).

4. **Unclear which DeepCore method is used as the baseline** – The paper states it compares against “DeepCore” but does not specify which algorithm from the library is used, nor the settings. A direct and transparent comparison is needed (Reviewers 1, 4).

5. **Selective and narrow evaluation** – Results are reported only for very small coreset sizes (<20% of the dataset) in Tables 2, 5, and 6. Even within these tables, performance drops noticeably as the percentage increases. The effectiveness at larger coreset percentages is not shown, which would be necessary to demonstrate practicality (Reviewer 2). Experiments are limited to small datasets and small models, and the method only works at low accuracy levels; it does not achieve parity with full-dataset accuracy, limiting practical usability (Reviewer 1).

6. **Ambiguity in framing** – The paper is unclear whether GradSimCore is presented as a new baseline or as a new state-of-the-art method. The terminology switches between “baseline” and “outperforms state-of-the-art” (Reviewer 4).

7. **Presentation and clarity issues** – The manuscript appears stretched to fill page limits: tables are stretched, Algorithm 1 could be half the width, the notation table is unnecessary and could be described in text, and Table 1 could be shortened (Reviewer 4). Equation (3) should be explicitly called cosine similarity; Equation (4) uses expectation notation where a simple average would be clearer; Equation (5) describes a threshold but experiments actually sort by \(f(x)\) and take the top \(x\%\) (Reviewer 4). Overall typography, experiments, theoretical proofs, and methodology are poor (Reviewer 3).

8. **Applicability to gradually collected data is unclear** – The importance scores are computed on the entire dataset first, then the subset is identified. This contradicts the motivation of requiring smaller datasets earlier (Table 1) and does not address settings where data arrives incrementally (Reviewer 4).

9. **Lack of originality** – The use of gradients as an importance indicator is not original to this paper (Reviewer 3). The only positive aspect noted is the provision of code (Reviewer 3).

**Reviewer disagreement**: Three reviewers rated the paper a 3, while one reviewer (Reviewer 1) gave a 1 and considers the method impractical, lacking theory, and poorly evaluated. Reviewer 3 is also strongly negative, stating the paper should not be resubmitted.
