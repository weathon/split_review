## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Physiological Dynamics-Driven Hierarchical Diffusion Model (PDH-Diffusion), a framework for synthesizing fMRI signals that incorporates brain hierarchical regional interactions (via hypergraph-based signal generation) and multifractal dynamics (via a dynamics properties guiding module) into a cross-brain region progressive diffusion process. All reviewers acknowledge the novelty and the solid theoretical foundation of the work, with two reviewers rating 8 and two rating 5 and 6. The majority positive reviewers emphasize the conceptual advances and rigorous quantitative validation; the minority more critical reviewers question the practical significance, task validity, and generalizability of the findings.

### Strengths
- Novelty: The integration of brain hierarchical regional interactions and multifractal dynamics into a diffusion model for fMRI synthesis is a novel and well-motivated approach. (Reviewers 1,2,3,4)
- The three core components (hypergraph-based signal generator, dynamics properties guiding module, cross-brain region progressive diffusion) are clearly described and have strong theoretical support. (Reviewer 2,3)
- Extensive quantitative validation: The model outperforms multiple time-series forecasting and diffusion baselines on MAE, MAPE, and RMSE metrics, and ablation studies confirm the contribution of each module. (Reviewer 1,2,4)
- The paper is well-organized and the models in the main text are presented with reasonable quality and clarity. (Reviewer 2,4)

### Weaknesses
- **Reproducibility and missing details**: The paper lacks critical information about the dataset (resting-state vs. task-based, subject health conditions, rationale for reducing ROIs from 268 to 82), data split (train/test division, sample counts), and network architecture specifications (layer details, kernel sizes, padding, activation functions) – these must be provided in an appendix if code is not released. (Reviewer 1)
- **Limited dataset and generalizability**: Experiments are conducted only on the HCP dataset with the AAL atlas. Testing on multiple fMRI datasets (including clinical populations) and finer parcellation atlases is missing, which limits the robustness and generalizability of the findings. The framework also has not been extended to other modalities with multifractal properties (e.g., EEG, MEG). (Reviewer 3)
- **Lack of qualitative and physiological validation**: No qualitative comparisons (e.g., Functional Connectivity Maps, visual representations of generated signals) are provided. Metrics like MAE/RMSE alone cannot fully assess signal realism. The model’s ability to preserve multifractal properties is not explicitly validated, and expected network features (e.g., default mode network for resting-state fMRI) have not been checked. (Reviewer 1 Q2, Reviewer 2 Weakness 3, Reviewer 2 Q3)
- **Unclear practical significance and downstream task validation**: The claimed clinical motivation (reducing acquisition time, aiding diagnosis) is not supported by experiments. No demonstration that synthetic fMRI retains subject-specific information or can be used in downstream tasks such as disease diagnosis, brain network analysis, or cognitive prediction. The forecasting task alone does not convincingly show practical utility. (Reviewer 1 Weakness 2, Reviewer 2 Weakness 2, Reviewer 3 Weakness 2, Reviewer 4 Weakness 1)
- **Concerns about the forecasting task validity**: For a 32-timepoint prediction, MAE is ~30, implying high error; baseline models achieve comparable performance. Increasing prediction length to 64 or 96 timepoints yields only marginal error increases, which is counter-intuitive. No comparison against a naive baseline (e.g., straight-line prediction) is performed, raising doubts about whether the model captures meaningful temporal patterns beyond simple trends. (Reviewer 4 Weakness 2, Reviewer 4 Q2)
- **Technical issues and risk of overfitting**: The variance schedule parameters (α_n, β_n) may not be optimized for different regions or scales, potentially causing over-smoothing or overfitting. Conditioning on historical data could also lead to overfitting if training data does not cover the full spectrum of brain dynamics. Mitigation measures (e.g., adaptive variance schedules, regularization) are not discussed. (Reviewer 2 Weakness 4, Reviewer 2 Q4)
- **Manual determination of “optimal” brain region range**: The results suggest an optimal range of brain regions, but how this range is determined (manual or automated) is unclear. (Reviewer 2 Q2)
- **Terminological confusion and typographical errors**: The term “physiological fMRI” is not clarified (resting-state vs. task-based). Multiple typos: “share radio” instead of “share ratio” in Section 4.3 and Figure 3(b) caption/text, line 465 typo “69→96”, and inconsistent numbering elsewhere. (Reviewer 2 Weakness 1 & 5, Reviewer 1 Q3)
- **Sampling frequency and resolution limitations**: The sampling frequency for the fMRI data is not reported. The synthesized data is bounded to the ROI level; extension to vertex-level synthesis is not shown. (Reviewer 1 Q1, Reviewer 4 Q3)
- **MAE definition inconsistency**: Line 451 should double-check the definition of MAE used. (Reviewer 4 Q4)
