## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Angle-DFQ, a data-free weight rounding method for DNNs that minimizes the angle between pre-trained and quantized weight vectors instead of rounding to nearest. It derives optimal rounding boundaries for ternary quantization under a Gaussian assumption, then presents a greedy cosine-similarity-based algorithm that extends to mixed-precision quantization. Experiments on ImageNet with ResNet-18, VGG-16, and AlexNet at 3–6 bits claim consistent improvements over existing data-free quantization methods. Reviewer opinions range from positive (score 5, finding the approach novel and effective) to critical (score 1, raising major concerns about theory, methodology, and presentation), with one reviewer providing moderate support (score 3).

### Strengths
- **Novel orientation-preserving perspective**: Focuses on preserving the direction (angle) of weight vectors during quantization, a unique approach not used in other baselines. (R1, R2, R3)
- **Data-free and privacy-friendly**: Requires no training data, synthetic data generation, fine-tuning, or batch-norm statistics, making it suitable for privacy-sensitive applications. (R1)
- **Theoretical derivation for ternary quantization**: Provides a clear, closed-form solution for the optimal decision boundary under a Gaussian weight assumption. (R1, R3)
- **Method is straightforward and well-motivated**: The greedy cosine-similarity algorithm is easy to understand, and the motivation for minimizing angle error is made clear. (R3; note that R2 questions the significance of angle)
- **Mixed-precision compatibility**: The method adapts to mixed-precision settings and can be combined with other quantization techniques. (R1)
- **Empirical performance**: Outperforms existing data-free quantization methods on ImageNet classification, especially in low-bit regimes. (R1, R2; though R2 notes it does not surpass several other PTQ methods, and R3 questions the experimental setup)
- **Adaptive threshold for ternary weights**: Introduces a specific threshold scheme for ternary quantization. (R1)

### Weaknesses
- **Presentation and formatting**: Numerous spelling and grammatical errors (e.g., “due a number”, “course weight quantization” for “coarse”, “asymtotic”, “out preforms”, “Relu”, “Alexnet”, “Imagenet”, “ect.”). Severe formatting issues: Table 1 is a low-resolution screenshot instead of a proper LaTeX table; an equation extends outside the text bounding box; equation numbering is non-uniform and randomly referenced; references are inconsistent (e.g., Nagal et al. 2020 does not exist; “Qin et all 2023”; missing citation for Zhang et al. (2019) in the reference list). Derivations in Appendix A have errors (e.g., pdf definition should be for \(|v_i|\geq 0\), not \(v_i\geq 0\); the symbol \(\varepsilon\) is switched mid-derivation). (R1, R3)
- **Limited relevance of Theorem 1**: Theorem 1 only holds for continuous probability distributions whose support is restricted to \(|x|<\frac{1}{2}\). Practical distributions like Gaussian and Laplace do not satisfy this; for a standard normal, \(c\approx 0.62\) falls into the regime where the theorem does not hold. This makes the theorem’s practical relevance questionable. (R3) One reviewer notes that the proof’s last step (limit equals 0) is unclear. (R2)
- **Disconnect between theory and algorithm**: The theory section solves ternary quantization in closed form, but the actual method performs greedy optimization over much larger quantization grids. The theoretical analysis does not directly translate to the general method, weakening the theoretical underpinning of the empirical results. (R3)
- **Computational overhead**: The method requires iterative quantization per layer and per neuron, with no analysis of convergence properties (number of iterations, variation across layers/architectures) and no discussion of training or inference time impact. It also may need to store an optimal scaling constant per neuron. (R1)
- **Incomplete motivation and analysis for angle minimization**: It is unclear why minimizing the angle between pre-trained and quantized weights helps improve quantization performance; the paper does not sufficiently justify this connection. (R2)
- **Selective application to a subset of weights and layers**: The method is applied only to a limited subset of weights (not clearly specified), and for mixed-precision quantization, the layer selection process is ad-hoc (e.g., “high dimensionality”) without a systematic criterion or ablation study. The impact of quantizing all layers versus a subset, and how scale factors depend on architecture, is not explored. (R1)
- **Narrow experimental scope**: Evaluation is limited to image classification (ResNet-18, VGG-16, AlexNet). No experiments on object detection, segmentation, or NLP tasks; notably, large language models (LLMs) are not tested despite their practical relevance for quantization. (R1, R3)
- **Insufficient comparison with other PTQ methods**: The accuracy on ImageNet does not surpass several recent post-training quantization algorithms (e.g., OBQ, COMQ), and the comparison against such methods is inadequate. (R2)
- **Methodology concerns with mixed precision**: The paper combines Angle-DFQ with a hand-tuned mixed-precision bit allocation, making it hard to isolate the contribution of the angle-minimization component. Different networks use different configurations (different methods, bit widths), preventing a consistent experimental setup across networks. The main competitors (TNT, Krishnamoorthi) should ideally be reported for each network. (R3)
- **Algorithmic clarity**: The algorithm for angle minimization is not well presented, making reproducibility difficult. (R2) Questions remain about whether Equation (2) holds as stated in Algorithm 1 when weights are scaled before quantization. (R1)
- **One reviewer (score 5) is strongly positive about the approach, while another (score 1) finds the theoretical contribution weak and the experimental methodology flawed.** (R1 vs. R3)
