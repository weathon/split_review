## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SPADE, a structured pruning algorithm for model-based deep learning (MBDL) networks applied to inverse problems. SPADE uses Dependency Graph (DepGraph) and group L1 norm to prune non-essential CNN filters within MBDL networks, followed by three fine-tuning strategies (supervised, school, self-supervised) to recover performance. The method is evaluated on compressed sensing MRI and image super-resolution, demonstrating test-time speedup with some performance loss.

### Strengths
- The application of structured pruning to MBDL networks for inverse problems is novel and promising, addressing high test-time computational costs relevant for large-scale imaging. [R1]
- SPADE achieves faster inference for multiple MBDL frameworks, specifically deep equilibrium models (DEQ) and deep unrolling (DU). [R1]
- The method shows good speedup for CS-MRI (Fig. 2) and super-resolution (Table 4). [R2]
- The use of DepGraph (Fang et al., 2023) for pruning in the MBDL context is a principled design choice. [R3]
- Three fine-tuning strategies (supervised, school, self-supervised) are proposed to comprehensively cover different scenarios: with ground truth, with pre-trained network only, or without labeled data. [R3]
- Extensive numerical experiments on two inverse problems support the effectiveness of the approach, with competitive performance while improving computational speed. [R3, R4]
- Motivation for reducing computational complexity of deep learning solutions for inverse problems is well presented. [R4]

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and reliance on existing techniques:** The core components (DepGraph-based pruning, group sparsity regularization, and fine-tuning losses) are not new; the paper largely combines existing methods without demonstrating non-trivial adaptation to MBDL. [R1, R2, R3] Several prior group sparsity pruning works are not discussed or compared (e.g., Wen et al. NeurIPS 2016, Yoon & Hwang ICML 2017, Li et al. CVPR 2020, Kumar et al. 2021, Mitsuno & Kurita ICPR 2021, Huang et al. NeurIPS 2021), making it hard to assess novelty over general pruning. [R2] The claim of being the first network pruning algorithm specifically designed for MBDL models is questionable. [R2, R3, R4]
- **Lack of MBDL specificity:** It is unclear why SPADE is particular to MBDL rather than a general CNN pruning method; the paper fails to explain how MBDL-specific characteristics (e.g., iterative structure, measurement model) interact with pruning or require special treatment. [R1, R2, R3, R4] The only potential MBDL-specific component is the self-supervised fine-tuning loss (Eq. 10), but it shows the worst performance in results. [R4]
- **Insufficient comparisons to alternative acceleration methods:** The paper does not compare SPADE with other methods for improving inference speed (e.g., [1], [2] mentioned in the introduction) in terms of performance drop vs. speedup. [R1] This makes it difficult to quantify the significance of results; e.g., a 0.77 dB PSNR drop with 51% speedup for CS-MRI (Table 1) may be large, and it is unclear how other methods compare. [R1]
- **Limited evaluation scope and missing experiments:** Only two inverse problems (CS-MRI, super-resolution) are tested; more tasks (e.g., deblurring, inpainting) and network architectures would strengthen generality. [R3] For CS-MRI, it is unclear if diverse sampling patterns were used; if different patterns require re-pruning, the approach may be less convenient. [R2] For super-resolution, evaluation on more diverse test datasets (common in SR literature) is missing. [R2] The paper does not investigate the impact of different pruning ratios on the performance-efficiency trade-off. [R3]
- **Fine-tuning methods not novel:** The three fine-tuning strategies are simply three popular losses used in inverse problems (supervised, distillation/KL, self-supervised) without special treatment for pruned networks. [R2, R3] It is unclear if combining these losses could improve performance. [R1] Their novelty over existing fine-tuning approaches is not justified. [R2]
- **Speedup vs. pruning ratio discrepancy:** The speedup achieved is relatively low compared to the pruning ratio. For example, 10% pruning yields only 0–3% speedup, and E2EVar (Table 1) has only 24% speedup with 65% pruning ratio. This raises questions about whether pruning removes lower-complexity layers or if timings include compilation overhead (XLA, jit, etc.). [R4]
- **Practical relevance and trade-offs:** It is unclear whether the performance drops (0.77 dB for CS-MRI, 0.68 dB for super-resolution) are acceptable in clinical or practical settings; missing clinical information could be critical. [R2] The necessity of pruning for inverse problems requires more convincing arguments and practical experiments. [R2]
- **Presentation and clarity issues:** The explanation of how pruning works (e.g., dependency definition “f_{\theta,i} has dependency on f_{\theta,j}”, how layers are actually removed) is insufficient, especially for readers unfamiliar with DepGraph or MBDL. [R4] The background section is repetitive and could be shortened to include visual results (Fig. 6–8 in supplemental) for CS-MRI. [R1] Typographical errors need proofreading. [R1] The contribution relative to DepGraph is not clearly stated. [R4]
- **Missing analyses and details:** Training time increase for SPADE compared to baseline is not reported. [R1] Memory complexity at test-time is not discussed. [R1] It is not explored whether different fine-tuning losses can be combined to improve performance. [R1] The impact of different pruning ratios on the trade-off is not studied. [R3]
