## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a unified algorithmic framework for stochastic proximal point methods (SPPM) that encompasses multiple existing SPPM variants and introduces new ones (SPPM with Nonuniform Sampling, SPPM with Arbitrary Sampling, SPPM‑LC). Under μ-strong convexity and a parametric assumption on iterates, correction and control vectors (Assumptions 5–6), the authors establish a single convergence theorem (Theorem 1) that recovers best‑known rates for several methods without requiring individual smoothness. Numerical experiments on least‑squares problems illustrate some theoretical properties.

### Strengths
- The universal SPPM algorithmic framework is interesting and, according to one reviewer, novel in its generality (Reviewer 4).  
- The presentation is clear, well‑written, and easy to follow, with interesting remarks and a well‑organized appendix (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).  
- The theoretical analysis is solid: assumptions and statements are clear, proofs are technically sound and contain sufficient detail (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).  
- The framework unifies multiple SPPM variants under a single theorem, making relationships and convergence behavior easier to understand (Reviewer 3).  
- The development of new variants (SPPM with Nonuniform Sampling, SPPM with Arbitrary Sampling) expands the applicability of SPPM (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and relation to prior work (minority view: Reviewer 1 is more critical)**:  
  - The main idea is a generalization of the analysis from Lipschitz‑continuous gradients to differentiable functions, but the approach is similar to prior works (Gorbunov et al. “A unified theory of SGD”; Traore et al. “Variance reduction techniques for stochastic proximal point algorithms”). Hence the contribution is limited (Reviewer 1).  
  - The claim that “a unifying framework is missing” is false; both prior papers already provide such a framework under different assumptions (Reviewer 1).  
  - The paper appears more like a review article than a novel contribution, and may not align with ICLR’s scope (Reviewer 3).  
- **Overstatement of contributions**:  
  - Title and text claim “without smoothness,” yet analysis assumes differentiability (i.e., smoothness of the objective), not truly non‑smooth functions (Reviewer 1).  
  - Assumption 5 is more general than that of Khaled & Jin but involves unknown quantities; it is unclear how practically more general it is without concrete problem classes (Reviewer 1).  
- **Practical relevance of assumptions**:  
  - Assumptions 5–6 appear to be designed only for convergence analysis; it is unclear whether they are satisfied by real applications that are not smooth (Reviewers 2, 3).  
  - Assumption 6 combined with strong convexity: is it actually weaker than ν‑smoothness? Concrete examples satisfying Assumption 5 or 6 but violating standard smoothness are requested but not provided (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).  
- **Weaknesses of Algorithm 1 and convergence bounds**:  
  - Theorem 1’s bound is non‑vanishing unless \(C_1=C_2=0\); only variance‑reduction variants achieve this, while standard SPPM (with \(h_k\equiv0\)) does not. It would be more interesting if the bound could also capture a sublinear rate for standard SPPM (Reviewer 4).  
  - Algorithm 1 uses a constant, sufficiently small step size, which is rarely practical; the paper should discuss comparison with diminishing step sizes (e.g., Davis & Drusvyatskiy, SIOPT 2019) (Reviewer 3).  
  - The two algorithms SPPM* and SPPM GC require gradients at the solution, making them impractical (Reviewer 1).  
- **Insufficient empirical evaluation**:  
  - Experiments are limited to toy strongly convex least‑squares problems (Eq. 17), which satisfy smoothness assumptions, so they do not demonstrate the claimed advantage of the non‑smooth framework (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).  
  - The paper should test on non‑smooth learning tasks (e.g., SVM with hinge loss) and on problems where the new assumptions hold but standard smoothness fails (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).  
- **Scope and missing analysis**:  
  - The strong convexity assumption excludes non‑convex and weakly convex problems; no discussion of extension to such settings is provided (Reviewer 3).  
  - Comparison with the diminishing step‑size proximal point method of Davis & Drusvyatskiy (SIOPT 2019) is missing (Reviewer 3).  
  - It is unclear whether Theorem 1 can be strengthened to interpolate between sublinear rates (standard SPPM) and linear rates (corrected/variance‑reduced SPPM) (Reviewer 4).  
- **Additional missing element**: The paper introduces seven algorithms but does not guide which one is best in practice (Reviewer 3).
