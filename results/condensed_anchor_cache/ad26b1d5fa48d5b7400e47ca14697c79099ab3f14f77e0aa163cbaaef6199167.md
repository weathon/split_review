## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a dual generative replay framework (CuGRO) for continual offline RL, using two diffusion models—one for state generation and one for behavior generation—to mitigate catastrophic forgetting without storing all previous data. Reviewers generally find the problem timely and the idea novel, but concerns center on insufficient experimental validation, lack of external baselines and more complex benchmarks, computational efficiency, missing convergence guarantees, and clarity issues. One reviewer (R4, rating 3, confidence 5) is substantially more critical, arguing that the experimental evaluation is too limited and the presentation is unclear in key technical sections. The other three reviewers (ratings 6, 5, 5; confidence 2–4) are more positive but still note important weaknesses regarding computational cost, motivation for diffusion models over alternatives, and the need for broader comparisons.

### Strengths

- **Novel framework:** CuGRO is among the first to leverage diffusion models for continual offline RL, decoupling the policy into a generative behavior model and a multi-head critic, and using a separate state diffusion model for generative replay (R1, R2, R4). The problem of CORL is understudied, making contributions in this area welcome (R4).

- **Practical memory savings:** By storing knowledge in two compact diffusion models instead of large replay buffers, the approach addresses memory constraints and potential privacy issues, generating high-fidelity pseudo-samples (R1, R2). The method avoids storing all previous task data (R1).

- **Strong empirical results:** CuGRO closely matches the Oracle (which stores all previous data) and outperforms baselines (Noise, None) across four MuJoCo task sequences (Table 1, Figure 2) (R1, R2, R3). Ablation studies on the hyperparameter λ (controlling replay weight) are provided (R1). The paper is well-organized and easy to follow (R3).

- **Technically sound:** The incorporation of diffusion models for generative buffer replay is technically sound (R3), and the overall algorithm carefully combines the three components (R4).

### Weaknesses

- **Insufficient experimental evaluation** (R4, also R1, R2, R3):
  - The evaluation uses only simple MuJoCo tasks with short sequences of 4 tasks. More complex benchmarks (e.g., Meta-World, CausalWorld, RLBench, CompoSuite) are needed, especially given the motivation that diffusion models are necessary for complex behaviors (R4). The curves in Figure 2 show progressive degradation—scalability to longer task streams must be studied (R4).
  - No external continual learning or offline RL baselines are compared. The evaluation only includes variations of the proposed method (Oracle, Noise, None). Many existing methods cited in the paper are not compared (R1, R3, R4). In particular, there is no comparison to typical continual RL baselines, such as those evaluated on the “continual world” benchmark (R3). Missing baselines include the method cited as [1] by R3.
  - The analysis does not isolate the contribution of the diffusion models. An ablation showing the performance of the diffusion models themselves (e.g., sample quality) is missing, as is a sensitivity analysis to noisy or imperfect generated samples (R2, R3). The training data for the diffusion models and the effect of noise degradation on overall performance are unclear (R2).

- **Computational efficiency concerns** (R1, R2, R3):
  - Training two separate diffusion models (one for states, one for actions) is computationally expensive, both for training and for sampling at test time (parallel sampling is not possible) (R1, R3). The paper lacks a detailed analysis of training time, resource usage, memory footprint, and sampling time during replay, compared to alternative generative replay methods (R2, R3). The feasibility of this approach in resource-constrained environments is not addressed (R2).
  - The trade-off between the memory savings from condensing tasks into models versus the cost of training and sampling from those models for each new task is not quantified (R1, R2). The size of the diffusion models may exceed the size of saved replay buffers, especially in visual domains—this is not discussed for the considered setting (R4). Exploring concatenating {s, a} into one diffusion model could improve efficiency (R1).

- **Missing justification for diffusion models over alternatives** (R2, R4):
  - There is insufficient reasoning for why diffusion models are uniquely suited for this problem compared to other generative techniques (e.g., behavior cloning, GANs, VAEs), especially given the computational overhead (R2). The argument in footnote 2 that diffusion models capture opposite actions given the same state is weak, because task conditioning (e.g., via a task indicator) could resolve ambiguity even with Gaussian policies (R4). A comparison to non-diffusion generative approaches is needed (R2).
  - The motivation conflates two distinct ideas: multimodal behaviors within a single task and capturing a breadth of behaviors across tasks. The latter is hinted at but never explained or exemplified (R4).

- **Clarity and precision issues** (R4, with agreement from others on presentation):
  - Section 2 (preliminaries) is not stand-alone and contains unclear points: the advantage-weighted regression description misses that the Q function is with respect to μ (Eq. 4) and not the standard Q^π; Eq. 5 is unclear regarding whether it corresponds to Eq. 3 and whether Q_θ is w.r.t. π or μ (R4). The diffusion model description omits the forward diffusion process, making the noise prediction before Eq. 6 underdefined; the sentence before Eq. 7 leaves ambiguous whether Q evaluates actions w.r.t. μ or π (R4). The term “re-sampling” is not defined (R4).
  - Sections 3.2–3.3 contain confusing passages: the motivation in Sec 3.2 conflates multimodal behaviors with task variety (R4). Classifier-free guidance is mentioned but not actually used, creating confusion (R4). The loss functions (Eq. 10, 11, 13, 14, 15) are easy to write but lack convergence guarantees—especially training a diffusion model on data generated from the previous diffusion model may accumulate errors over tasks (R3, R4). The timing of generated state sampling (before or after state generator updates) is only clarified in Algorithm 1, not in the main text (R4). The term “behavior cloning” for regularizing the critic is misapplied; “functional regularization” is more precise (R4).
  - The description of the CORL setting in Sec 3.1 is vague: it is unclear which MDP components change across tasks and whether the task distribution P(M) is stationary (R4). The formalization of CORL could be moved to preliminaries (R4). The overview in Sec 3.1 would benefit from being placed in Sec 2 (R4).

- **Convergence and stability** (R3, R4):
  - There is no theoretical or empirical analysis of the convergence of the iterative training procedure, particularly training the state diffusion model on data from a previous, potentially imperfect, diffusion model. Error accumulation is a concern (R3, R4). The sensitivity of the method to the quality of generated samples and to noisy data is not examined (R2, R3). It is unclear whether applying other types of generative models would yield similar performance (R2).

- **Other minor issues** (R4):
  - Typos/grammar: footnotes misplaced before periods (e.g., “...keep emerging\footnote{text}.”), “scored-based” instead of “score-based,” “datasets emerge” could be “constructed,” misuse of “replayed” for generated samples (suggest “generated” instead) (R4). The abstract is clear (R4). The paper’s notation clarification could be moved to the beginning of Sec 2 (R4). 
  - R4 also notes that the claim “the generator is the only constraint on task performance” is not an improvement over other continual learning methods, and the statement about equivalence to joint training is only true for an optimal generator—optimality over long task sequences is questionable (R4).
