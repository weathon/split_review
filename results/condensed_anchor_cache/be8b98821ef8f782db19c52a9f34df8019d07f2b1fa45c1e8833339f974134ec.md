## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes CVX-DPO, a lightweight variant of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) that uses a convex optimization reformulation of neural networks together with the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM), implemented in JAX for training on a single RTX-4090 GPU. The method aims to reduce resource requirements for preference learning by eliminating the need for a frozen reference model and enabling training to global optimality in polynomial time. Experiments are conducted on three datasets (one synthetic educational) using DistilGPT2, GPT-2, and GPT-2-medium models; human evaluation (with 17 or 25 volunteers, inconsistently reported) shows CVX-DPO outperforms traditional DPO in preference generation. Reviewers raise major concerns about clarity of the method, lack of standard evaluations and benchmarks, insufficient evidence for claimed efficiency gains, limited model scale, and novelty.

### Strengths
- Addressing scalability in preference learning is desirable; reducing hardware requirements makes DPO accessible to researchers without multiple GPU systems (R1, R2, R3).
- The use of a convex optimization reformulation of neural networks as a surrogate for the DPO loss is considered novel (R2).
- The paper introduces a reference‑free method for direct preference learning and uses a convex‑NN for more efficient training of a two‑layer NN block (R5).
- However, R1 and R4 strongly question whether any real efficiency gain is achieved beyond tuning a smaller model: R1 notes that DPO does not cost extra memory when the reference model’s logits are pre‑computed, and R4 is not convinced why a different optimization algorithm would lead to better performance. R1 also states that the paper does *not* make DPO use fewer resources as claimed.

### Weaknesses
1. **Unclear method and notation.**  
   - The objective for DPO‑convex is not clearly stated; it is unclear how the convex‑NN is constructed and integrated with the DPO objective (R3, R4, R5).  
   - Symbols in equation (4) are left undefined: F, G, D_i, the matrix‑shaped X (claimed to be a two‑layer ReLU MLP) and the proximal term “prox” (R3, R5). “FST” is also unexplained (R3).  
   - No explanation of which part of DPO requires an MLP that is convexified, nor which loss is solved via ADMM instead of SGD; concepts are introduced as disconnected pieces (R4).  
   - The extraction of hidden features as the policy model optimizes is not described (R5).  
   - The paper would benefit from a preliminary introduction of convex‑NNs, and the text on JAX (which contains unsubstantiated claims such as “our past work has found it to be extremely performant” with no citation) should be reduced to make space (R4, R1).

2. **Insufficient and non‑standard evaluation.**  
   - The experiments report only human evaluations (with 17 or 25 volunteers, inconsistent between lines 053 and 069), which is not standard; LLMs as evaluators are common in the field, and human evaluations with such a small number have strong subjectivity (R4, R1).  
   - The win rates in Table 1 (e.g., values of 1, 3, 4) are unclear and “4” does not make sense as a win rate (R1, R3).  
   - The evaluation in Section 4.4 does not match standard notions of evaluation; if the method is claimed to be better, standardised evaluations should be used (R1).  
   - Only three small models (DistilGPT2, GPT‑2, GPT‑2‑medium) are fine‑tuned; scalability to SOTA models (Mistral, Llama 3) and complex tasks (MT‑Bench, Alpaca‑Eval) is uncertain (R3).  
   - The method is not compared against existing reference‑free methods such as ORPO or SimPO; performance relative to SOTA DPO on datasets like those in arxiv 2407.13709 and arxiv 2403.19159 is missing (R5, R2).  
   - The dataset generation strategy is questionable: using most of a natural conversation as rejected responses contrasts a direct response to the prompt with responses that would not appear in the same context; the novelty of this approach is unclear as datasets like HH already draw multiple samples from the same conversation (R5).

3. **Lack of evidence for claimed efficiency.**  
   - Speed and memory gains are claimed without any quantitative evidence; there are no reported metrics for memory usage or training time (R1, R3).  
   - The emphasis on a single RTX‑4090 GPU is artificial – most of the memory gain comes from tuning a smaller network, not from the method itself; the claim that the method “circumvents the need for FSDP” (Section 3) is only true because the model is smaller (R1).  
   - No code is provided to reproduce the results or verify the resource claims (R3).  
   - R1 notes that when DPO is implemented properly (pre‑computing reference logits), it should not cost additional memory; the claimed efficiency gain beyond using a smaller model is unclear.

4. **Limited novelty and theoretical contribution.**  
   - The method is largely a combination of existing well‑established approaches (convex reformulation of NNs, ADMM, JAX) and thus incremental (R2).  
   - Little theoretical analysis is offered on preference optimization (R2).  
   - It is unclear why the reward should be defined using a convex‑NN instead of the original log‑probability definition, given the same reference‑free loss; the motivation is not explained (R5).

5. **Poor writing and presentation.**  
   - The introduction contains sweeping generalizations that are incorrect (e.g., “it is possible to perform RLHF on off‑policy data” is mischaracterised, and “This model is able to infer what a human user wants and output a realistic answer that a human might like” is not an accurate description of preference learning) (R1).  
   - The overall tone is too informal; claims should be made more precisely and odd, extra text omitted (R1).  
   - The paper is very difficult to understand; R4 could not comprehend the method even to comment on strengths and recommends major revision (R4).
