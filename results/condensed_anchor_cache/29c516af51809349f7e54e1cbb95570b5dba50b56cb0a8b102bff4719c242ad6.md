## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Self-Steering Optimization (SSO), an automated alignment method that autonomously generates on-policy preference signals during iterative training using predefined contrastive principles, eliminating the need for human annotation. SSO combines a self-steering loss term with an on-policy weighting function (inspired by WPO) and is built on base losses such as IPO and DPO. It is validated on Qwen2 and Llama3.1 across multiple subjective and objective benchmarks, with additional experiments on reward model training. One reviewer (R3, score 5) finds the method promising and the benchmarking extensive, while the other three reviewers (R1, R2, R4, scores 3) raise concerns about clarity, experimental methodology, and incremental gains.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses an important problem: reducing human annotation costs and improving scalability in LLM alignment (R2, R3, R4).  
- It introduces a novel combination of principle-based alignment with iterative preference optimization (DPO, IPO), which has substantial drawbacks that SSO aims to fix (R1).  
- SSO is flexible and can be extended to different base loss functions beyond IPO (R2, R3).  
- The method demonstrates improvements on both subjective and objective benchmarks with effective length control (R2).  
- The paper provides extensive benchmarking across multiple scenarios; R3 considers the experiments robust, while R4 notes the evaluation methodology is questionable (contrast preserved).  
- Automated alignment reduces dependence on costly human annotations, making training more scalable and cost-effective (R2, R3).  

### Weaknesses
**Clarity and Presentation**  
- The paper is hard to follow without proper background; notation is not properly defined and grammar mistakes exist (R1, R2, R4).  
- Several terms are undefined or ambiguous:  
  - Principles $p^+$ and $p^-$: what they are and why only two (R4).  
  - What is $y^0$ and where does it come from? (R4).  
  - How does $x^+$ relate to $p^+$? (R4).  
  - "Accurate signal" and "We also designed a W for learnable signals" are unclear (R4).  
  - $p^+, p^-$ from $\mathcal{G}$ in Equation (1) do not appear in later content (R2).  
- The self-steering loss term in Section 2.3 is poorly explained and motivated; the authors introduce multiple QA pairs without explaining expectations (R1, R2, R3).  
- R1 advises moving the related work (2.1) to a separate section before Section 2 and explicitly defining the problem setting.  
- The "backdoor" problem on line 199/200 is not clearly explained (R1, R2).  
- Notation: using $\theta$ for loss weight is confusing because $\theta$ commonly denotes policy parameters (R1).  
- The method is offered as a solution without detailing how it solves the fundamental problem; more reasoning is needed on why the proposed losses work (R3).  

**Experimental Methodology and Reporting**  
- The improvements over baselines are often marginal or within noise; many results in Table 1 (MATH, GSM8K) appear within noise, and AE2 gains are minor (15.0 vs 14.9) (R1).  
- No error bars are provided; this is especially problematic when differences are small (R1).  
- There is a misbolded entry: GSM8K iter1 for SSO is lower than modified PBAA iteration 1 (R1).  
- The reason for PBAA’s drop in AE2 is not analyzed, which could be an interesting insight (R1).  
- Several results (Table 3 and Table 4) present only SSO without comparison to baselines, making it difficult to evaluate the method’s contribution (R4).  
- The "Modified PBAA" baseline is never defined (R4).  
- It is unclear why alignment methods are evaluated on MMLU-Pro and Math; these benchmarks may not test alignment, and the contrastive principles in the appendix often have nothing to do with them (R4).  
- The authors compare PBAA-based methods with Ultrafeedback, which are fundamentally different datasets; this needs justification (R4).  
- The SFT baseline uses basic data, not synthetic data; DPO/IPO trained on SSO-generated data are not compared (R2).  

**Missing Ablations and Comparisons**  
- The improvement stems from two components (synthetic data and new objective), but the experiments do not separate their contributions (R2).  
- A critical ablation is missing: simply removing the self-steering term (keeping only the WPO-inspired term) to understand its impact (R1).  
- The effect of the on-policy weighting function W (taken from WPO) versus the self-steering term is not isolated (R4).  
- The authors use different base models (pretrained, instruct, SFT) across experiments without clear justification (R2).  
- It is not explained why in Figure 4(a) IPO causes a gradual decrease in accuracy, given that both optimization methods and data differ (R2).  

**Method and Motivation**  
- The delta over prior work is unclear; the authors do not articulate what specifically is new in SSO versus principle-based alignment methods. The main difference appears to be the on-policy weighting function from WPO, but its importance is not justified (R4).  
- The design of the self-steering and weight functions is simplistic, as the authors themselves note in limitations (R3).  
- Assumptions behind key choices: why should $x^-$ prefer $y^O$ over $y^+$? Further explanation is needed (R2).  
- The method’s scalability to smaller models is not addressed (R3).  
- SSO’s handling of scenarios where human-like feedback is ambiguous or lacks clear contrastive principles is not discussed (R3).  

**Reviewer Disagreement**  
- One reviewer (R3) finds the benchmarking extensive and the method robust, while the other three (R1, R2, R4) judge the experimental results as incremental, the writing as unclear, and the methodology as insufficiently justified. R3 initially scored higher but lowered after no response, indicating some uncertainty.
