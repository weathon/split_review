## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces the Needle-in-a-Time-Series-Haystack (NiTH) benchmark, comprising synthetic data (Poisson-based spike generation) and real-world spiky time series from traffic, energy, and biomedical domains. It proposes Scaled Dynamic Time Warping (SDTW) as a metric tailored to spikes and evaluates six time series foundation models (TSFMs) zero-shot over 64 million observations. The authors also demonstrate that continual pre-training on the proposed synthetic data can improve forecasting of spikes. Reviewers agree the problem is important and the volume of experiments is substantial. However, they raise fundamental concerns about clarity, metric suitability, missing baselines, arbitrary benchmark constraints, overlap with pre-training data, and insufficient analysis of loss functions and normalization. One reviewer (R4, score 6) is notably more positive, praising the theoretical generation scheme and dataset diversity; the other three reviewers (scores 3) find the contribution shallow and the presentation hard to follow. All reviewers acknowledge the potential impact of the benchmark while demanding stronger justification, more rigorous evaluation, and better connection to real-world applications.

### Strengths
- **Importance of the problem**: Forecasting spikes in time series is understudied and practically relevant (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- **Extensive experimentation**: The paper evaluates 6 TSFMs across 64 million observations, covering multiple design choices (R1, R2). The volume of experiments is a notable strength (R1, R2).
- **Useful new benchmarks**: NITH-Synth and NITH-Real capture an important subset of forecasting problems and provide a way to probe foundation models; benchmarks are likely to be cited and guide future research (R2, R3, R4).
- **Scalable spike generation framework**: The Poisson-based framework and the use of Markov chains to generate spiky time series with outlier/rarity constraints is theoretically grounded (R1, R4). R4 specifically highlights the clear generation scheme (separate Gaussian processes for haystack and needles).
- **SDTW metric**: The introduction of Scaled Dynamic Time Warping addresses issues with existing metrics for evaluating spike forecasts; it is a helpful contribution (R3). R1 also notes the metric as a contribution, though with reservations.
- **Dataset diversity**: The benchmark includes data from traffic, energy, and biomedical systems, making it rich and useful for TSFM evaluation (R4).
- **Minority positive view**: One reviewer (R4, score 6) finds the paper well-organized and the synthetic generation scheme convincing, stating that the dataset is useful for evaluation.

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity and presentation**: The paper introduces excessive notation (α, β, θ, ω) that is hard to follow; symbols are used before definition (e.g., lines 219–225) (R1). Several equations lack clarity (τ, τ̂, U(3,5)) (R2). “D_T” is not defined on page 4 (R2). Figure 2 includes “pred1” not discussed in text (R2). Figures are often hard to read – Figure 3 is visually crowded, and Figures 4, 6–8, 14–35 have font sizes too small (R2, R4). The authors use phrases like “We believe” which signals uncertainty and undermines conclusions (R2).
- **Over-claimed novelty while missing basics**: The paper proposes many new things but overlooks simpler, well-known approaches. For spike injection, [1] provides a straightforward method. For the haystack, Gaussian processes are used when simpler polynomial/linear trends + sine + noise would suffice. Better spike-focused metrics exist (e.g., [2]) and a simpler weighted metric like αᵀ(Y−Ŷ) could be used (R1). The focus on modifying only the stochastic component is limiting; it is unclear whether the deterministic part can be varied independently (R2, R4 question).
- **Incomplete baselines**: Only time series foundation models are considered. No statistical models (e.g., ARIMA) or models specifically designed for spiky time series are included (R1, R2). Prior work on modeling/evaluating spiky time series (e.g., [3–6]) is not discussed (R1). Classical forecasting approaches are ignored entirely (R2). Smaller models that could be fine-tuned more effectively on spiky data are not compared (R1).
- **Metric issues**: MAE, MSE, and standard DTW are not appropriate for evaluating spike predictions because they are dominated by non-spike timesteps. Metrics that assess timing of spikes (e.g., concept drift or change point detection measures) would be more suitable (R2). DTW does not satisfy the triangle inequality, so calling it a “metric” is inaccurate; this affects the analysis and the proposed SDTW (R2). SDTW also requires ground truth needle locations, which limits its applicability; a standard filter that avoids this requirement would be better (R3 question).
- **Normalization oversight**: All TSFMs use normalization (e.g., Chronos uses mean scaling, TimesFM uses RevIN), which likely affects forecasting performance on spikes. This is a critical design component not adequately analyzed (R1).
- **Loss function**: TSFMs are typically trained with MSE or MAE (conditional mean/median). These losses are inappropriate for spiky data, so the poor performance of TSFMs is not surprising. The paper should have investigated alternative loss functions more thoroughly (R1). It is also unclear what loss function was used for the continual pre-training of Chronos and TimesFM (R1 question).
- **Memorization claim**: The paper claims the benchmark tests memorization of spikes but provides insufficient evidence for this claim (R1). Additionally, there is a risk that the NITH-Real dataset (which includes UCR Anomaly and NAB) overlaps with TSFM pre-training data, so performance gains could be due to memorization rather than generalization (R3). Results should be separated by benchmark and overlap analyzed (R3).
- **Arbitrary benchmark constraints**: The synthetic benchmark imposes strong constraints (e.g., minimum 8 spikes, 512 segments) that are not justified. The choice of 8 spikes seems arbitrary and may not reflect real scenarios with fewer but significant spikes. The 512 segment length limits applicability to longer series. Performance variation as these constraints are relaxed is not analyzed (R3).
- **Loss of generalization from continual pre-training**: Continually pre-training on NITH-Synth may lead to catastrophic forgetting of patterns learned on standard benchmarks. The authors should evaluate models trained on NITH-Synth on other forecasting benchmarks (e.g., M4, M5) to check for this (R3).
- **Missing connection to real-world applications**: The role of random lags in spike forecasting is unclear, and the paper lacks a concrete use case (e.g., energy load prediction). Different subproblems (predicting spike existence, count, amplitude) require different metrics and are not disentangled (R1). The paper’s goals are hard to discern from the title and abstract; if real-world spikes are common, the contribution appears to be just a data compilation (R2).
- **Incomplete evaluation scope**: The benchmark only evaluates forecasting; other tasks like classification could be relevant for spike detection (R4). Other stochastic processes for generation (beyond Gaussian processes) and an ablation study are missing (R4). The authors do not justify why the haystack distribution is set to a scaled Poisson distribution, nor why Gaussian processes are chosen over alternatives (R4 questions). Advantages over other generation methods (e.g., [1], [2]) are not discussed (R4 question).
- **Methodological gaps**: The “filtering algorithm” mentioned is not described or evaluated (R2). The claim “No TSFMs could accurately forecast needles across all settings” appears to be a result but is stated without sufficient support – is it derived from this work or cited? (R2). The impact of adding noise to synthetic data on signal-to-noise ratio is not thoroughly discussed (R2). The appendix on time series statistics includes many plots but little substantial statistical information (R2). The base “haystack” is not clearly defined as stationary or any behavior (R2 question). SDTW requires differentiable DTW if it is to be used as a loss function for training; did the authors handle this? (R1 question).
