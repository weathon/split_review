## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes VISAGNN, a staleness-aware training method for large-scale GNNs that uses historical embeddings. It addresses the staleness of these embeddings by incorporating staleness criteria into the message-passing mechanism (dynamic staleness attention), the loss function (staleness-aware regularization), and the node embeddings (staleness-augmented embeddings). Experiments on large-scale benchmarks show improved accuracy and efficiency compared to existing historical-embedding-based methods.

### Strengths
- Well-written and easy to follow; clear motivation.
- VISAGNN scales to ogbn-papers100M (over 100 million nodes).
- The proposed method can be integrated with various historical baselines (e.g., GAS, LMC, FM, FreshGNN).
- It innovatively and dynamically incorporates staleness measurements into the training procedure (attention, loss, embeddings), enabling adaptive mitigation of stale embedding effects.
- Accuracy improvements over baseline methods are demonstrated.
- VISAGNN shows improvements in both accuracy and efficiency.

### Weaknesses
- Performance gains are marginal; for example, Table 1 shows only small accuracy improvements over baselines across datasets.
- Efficiency details are unclear: Table 3 shows VISAGNN uses more memory but less time than GAS—why? The experiment setup is vague (how is the historical embedding cache constructed? Fixed size? Eviction rule? FreshGNN uses all available memory; how is that measured?).
- Missing comparisons with related work on staleness: “Staleness-Alleviated Distributed GNN Training via Online Dynamic-Embedding Prediction”, “Staleness-based subgraph sampling for large-scale GNNs training”, and “SANCUS: Staleness-Aware Communication-Avoiding Full-Graph Decentralized Training in Large-Scale Graph Neural Networks”.
- Two reviewers raised scalability concerns: one reviewer noted the method scales to ogbn-papers100M; another argued that the datasets used do not fully substantiate scalability claims and that truly large-scale benchmarks such as MAG240M and IGB260M are absent.
- The bound in Theorem 1 appears overly relaxed (summation over layers with products of per-layer values), limiting practical utility.
- The authors claim that “the model parameters tend to converge, resulting in smaller gradient values and fewer updates to the embeddings in later epochs” and that “the influence of staleness diminishes as training progresses,” but no experiments or figures support these claims.
- In Section 3.2 (2) Centrality, the specific centrality measure used is not stated, and no justification or theoretical reasoning is given for its choice.
- Gradient norms are used in the attention score calculation (Eq. 5) and as part of the “staleness-augmented embeddings”. These gradient norms may come from different generations of the weight parameter. Since gradient magnitudes decrease with training, comparing them across different weight generations may be invalid. No justification is provided.
- The method should be demonstrated on various GNN backbones (e.g., GCN, SAGE, PNA), not only on one backbone.
- Standard deviations across replicates are missing from Tables 1–4 and Figures 2–3.
- Time comparison in Table 3 is unfair: system-level optimizations from ReFresh are excluded, which are important components. If the goal is to compare convergence in terms of iterations, epochs should be reported instead.
- Table 5 is confusing: FreshGNN is designed for neighbor sampling but is evaluated with METIS clusters, which appears out of scope. It is unclear whether any adaptation was made. Also, the paper does not clearly state which sampling method VISAGNN targets (neighbor sampling like FreshGNN, or subgraph sampling like GAS, FM, LMC?).
- The ogbn-products accuracy for FreshGNN is reported as 78.7 in Table 1, whereas the original FreshGNN paper reports 78.87.
- The historical embedding memory cost remains a challenge for large-scale graphs, limiting real-world applicability.
- Does the proposed method apply to homophily graphs? (Reviewer question)
- The paper’s theoretical analysis is weak; techniques are largely empirical. More sound theoretical backing is expected.
- In figures 2–3, “Accuracy (%)” should be “Accuracy”.
