## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper assesses off-the-shelf VLMs on 3D reasoning using indoor scene layout tasks (coordinate communication, free‑space reasoning, complex joint reasoning). Five proprietary/open VLMs are evaluated on 3400 questions from a 3D‑FRONT‑based dataset. Key findings: VLMs prefer normalized coordinates, perform better with simplified sketch or text‑only input than with detailed renderings, struggle with free‑space and complex spatial reasoning, and rely heavily on language over vision. Several reviewers note that the tasks are essentially 2D rather than true 3D, and that many findings overlap with known limitations in visual grounding.

### Strengths
- **Systematic task decomposition** – the study breaks scene reasoning into clear sub‑tasks (coordinate communication, free‑space, alignment/orientation/functionality), making the evaluation easy to follow (R1).
- **Extensive experiments** – 3400 questions, five input modalities, and five state‑of‑the‑art VLMs (proprietary and open) (R2).
- **Novel perspective** – approaching VLM spatial reasoning from the angle of indoor scene layout synthesis, which is recent and underexplored (R2).
- **Useful insights** – the work highlights VLM over‑reliance on symbolic/language reasoning and identifies specific failure modes (e.g., absolute coordinates, collision checking), providing direction for future model improvements (R1).
- **Well‑written and easy to understand** (R4; though R3 strongly disagrees on writing quality – see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Overclaiming “3D” reasoning** – the experiments are limited to 2D floorplan layouts. They do not evaluate depth perception, occlusion, varying viewpoints, or any true volumetric understanding, making the title misleading and the findings less relevant to genuine 3D understanding (R1, R3, R4). The omission of 3D LLMs further limits the benchmark’s scope (R2).
- **Limited dataset scope and quality** – the dataset uses a single room type from 3D‑FRONT with uniform appearance (square shape, wooden floor, light brown walls) and only 25 questions per task, which is insufficient for robust conclusions. Real‑world complexities such as overlapping/interleaved objects are absent. Visual markers and axes in prompts are often occluded, raising doubts about whether models can perceive them (R1, R2, R4).
- **Key findings are not novel** – the observation that VLMs perform better with text/sketches than with detailed visuals, and their struggles with absolute coordinates, are well‑documented in prior work on visual grounding and multimodal hallucination. This reduces the paper’s contribution and questions the necessity of using VLMs for layout reasoning when text‑only approaches suffice (R3, R4).
- **Lack of in‑depth analysis and proposed solutions** – the paper documents failures but does not analyze root causes (e.g., why text‑only helps) or propose new methods to mitigate them. The conclusion merely calls for “improved training” without elaboration. The community would benefit more from concrete analyses or fixes (R2). Specific requests from reviewers include: explaining why simplified visuals improve accuracy (R1), exploring hybrid evaluation strategies that force visual reasoning (R1), testing chain‑of‑thought prompting or visual prompts (R2), and suggesting how the findings should inform model training (R2).
- **Missing baselines and comparisons** – no human performance baseline is provided (R4). The evaluation also omits broader comparisons with other mainstream VLMs or an online leaderboard (R1; note that R2 considered the evaluation extensive, so this point is contested).
- **Poor writing quality and formatting** – multiple typos (e.g., line 151 “combaining”, line 297 “an With”), missing table elements (Tab. 2 lacks final “Avg.”, insufficient caption), and inconsistent formatting (Tab. 5 no bold/red, Tab. 3 no red) hinder readability (R3, R2). While one reviewer found the paper well‑written (R4), another found it poorly written and hard to follow (R3).
- **Limited generalizability** – the evaluation relies solely on the 3D‑FRONT dataset; a wider range of spatial configurations and room types is needed to assess generalization (R1). Additionally, the embodied view is skipped for task type 3, restricting applicability to top‑down/perspective views rather than the first‑person views common in embodied AI (R2).
