## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces an LLM-powered framework for multi-step retrosynthesis. It uses molecular-similarity-based retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to propose an initial full synthesis route, then iteratively refines it using expert feedback (reaction validity, commercial availability). The framework is tested with GPT-4 Turbo, Claude-Haiku, and DeepSeek, and compared against Retro*. RAG improves reaction round‑trip validity from 24.42% to 51.64%; after refinement, validity rises to 89.81%, yielding an overall route validity of 79.5% with a perfect query success rate. The approach aims to offer a flexible, customizable alternative to traditional tree‑search planners.

### Strengths
- The idea of generating an initial complete route followed by route refinement is promising and contrasts with iterative tree‑search methods.
- The framework is clearly presented (especially Figure 2) and the integration of specialized models and human feedback (Table A2) is flexible and powerful.
- The paper explores multiple LLMs (GPT‑4 Turbo, Claude‑Haiku, DeepSeek) and provides a straightforward baseline for using pretrained models in retrosynthesis.
- Retrieval‑augmented generation demonstrably improves the quality of initial route proposals (RAG vs. no RAG, Table 3).
- Experiments are well structured and support the claims; the authors clearly identify drawbacks (lines 333–343, 371–377), showing scientific integrity.
- The paper is generally well‑written and easy to follow; the Introduction and Preliminary sections effectively guide the reader.

### Weaknesses
- **No finetuning was performed.** The pretrained models used are already outdated, and finetuning (e.g., LORA or on SMILES data) could better adapt them to small‑molecule chemistry. Reviewer 1 questions the stated resource burden, noting that finetuning can be low‑cost (e.g., GPT‑4o mini at $0.15/1M input tokens, free daily limits until Oct 2024). This limits exploration of the approach’s true potential.
- **Benchmark limitations:**
  - Test problems are relatively simple (average route lengths <5 steps) and extracted from patent literature, which has well‑known data quality issues. Rapid inspection of ground‑truth routes in Figure A.3 reveals synthesis‑relevant chemical errors in 2 of 3 examples.
  - The results do not demonstrate a performance advantage over the baseline (Retro*); real‑world applicability remains distant. Broader evaluation (e.g., MCTS‑based methods) could yield more comprehensive insights.
- **Metrics concerns:**
  - “Query success rate” only indicates whether the model copies the target SMILES and produces plausible‑looking text; it does not evaluate reaction relevance or correctness. The practical value of this metric is unclear.
  - “Round‑trip validity” is used inconsistently: originally defined for single reaction steps, here applied to full synthetic routes without a clear definition. The method’s limitations for determining round‑trip validity (p.5) should be acknowledged.
  - It is possible that round‑trip validity is artificially inflated by iterating until a route that matches valid SMILES and purchasable molecules in the database is found, rather than genuine chemical feasibility.
- **Literature omissions:** The discussion omits foundational contributions to retrosynthesis – Corey’s seminal work, the first ML/AI demonstrations (Segler, Coley), and the state‑of‑the‑art non‑AI CASP by Grzybowski.
- **Unclear motivation:** The paper does not clearly articulate what specific drawbacks of traditional synthesis planners the LLM‑based approach aims to address, aside from intuition about accessibility and ease‑of‑use.
- **Insufficient detail on refinement loop:** The validity‑availability iterative loop (steps 3–7 in Figure 2) and the expert models are not explained concretely until later pages; the methods section would benefit from earlier, more explicit examples.
- **The RAG similarity assumption** (fingerprint similarity) may not hold for molecules where stereochemistry or other structural features make synthetic routes diverge. This limitation is not acknowledged in the main text.
- **Expert models are subject to their own limitations** (training data biases, limited reaction coverage), yet the text implies they provide ground‑truth feedback. This should be discussed in the main text, not only the appendix.
- **Overlapping content** between Chapters 3 and 4.1; a single consolidated experimental setup section would improve readability.
- **Code availability is not discussed**; reproduction of experiments is not possible.
- **Figure quality issues:** Some figures have thinly rendered molecular bonds, unreadable text in “LLM” boxes (Figures 1, 5), drafty layout (Table 3), missing caption (Figure 4), and pseudocode in the appendix is hard to read due to mixed text styles.
- **Outdated pricing:** The paper’s price tables are outdated; e.g., GPT‑4o mini costs $0.075/1M input tokens and $0.3/1M output tokens, and GPT‑4o is cheaper than GPT‑4 Turbo. Updated pricing and evaluation with latest models would improve the evaluation.
- **Unanswered questions (from reviewers):**
  - Why are ML models used for reaction assessment instead of traditional tools like RDKit?
  - How reliant is the framework on the success of the similarity‑based search? Can it propose routes for molecules far outside the database (analogous to the “no starting point” case)?
  - Where do LLM‑based planners fit on the scalability spectrum for HTS applications? The paper discusses operational costs but not use‑case limitations.
  - Are there experiments with actual user feedback in the refinement loop? Only mentioned as possible, not tested.
- **Minority reviewer contrast:** One reviewer (score 5) was more positive, praising the paper’s quality and addressing an important challenge, but still raised several of the above weaknesses (motivation clarity, code availability, figure quality, and the need for earlier explanation of the refinement loop). This positive view does not override the overall assessment of the other two reviewers (scores 3 and 3) that the paper’s contributions and current evidence are insufficient.
