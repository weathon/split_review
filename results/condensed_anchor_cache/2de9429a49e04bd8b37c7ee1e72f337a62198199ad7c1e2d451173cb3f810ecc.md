## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper identifies training instability in predictive coding networks (PCNs) – exponential growth of latent state norms, prediction error concentration, exploding/vanishing gradients – and proposes stabilizing techniques: length regularization and sequential training with skip connection modules (SPCN). Evaluated on MNIST and CIFAR-10, the approach attempts to enable deeper networks. Reviewers agree the problem is important and the theoretical analysis is interesting, but raise serious concerns about clarity, biological plausibility, missing baselines, low accuracy, limited novelty, mixed results, and lack of validation.

### Strengths
- Scaling PC and understanding its learning dynamics is a long-standing problem in neural networks (R1, R2, R3, R4).  
- The paper introduces an interesting new way of training PCNs via sequential addition of nodes and skip connections (R2 – originality).  
- Contains extensive analytical derivations and theoretical analysis using dynamical mean-field theory and state-interaction matrices; theoretical predictions match empirical experiments (R2, R4).  
- The problem is important for scaling local message-passing models to deep architectures, with potential implications for neuromorphic hardware (R4).  
- Skip connections reduce the required number of inference iterations \(T\) (R4).  
- Proposed stabilizing techniques show empirical promise in some cases, notably MNIST (R3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Clarity and presentation**: The paper is too dense and fails to explain the bigger picture clearly (R1, R2). The introduction to PC is clear, but the rest is hard to follow (R2). It is unclear how sequential training works and how the skip connections factor into the inference dynamics – do they act as standard PC connections except with fixed weights? (R1, R2). Sentences around lines 121–122 (“The latent state updates diminish the information…”) are confusing and need expansion or removal (R1, R2). Mathematical quantities \(p^{l,t}, q^{l,t}, r^{l,t}\) are introduced without sufficient intuitive explanation (R3). A pseudocode and clearer experimental sections are needed (R4). Fig. 3 uses too many values of \(T\) (9 values) and \(l\) (6 values), making the legend unreadable; fewer values should be used (R1, R2).

2. **Hyperparameter reporting and stability**: Hyperparameters are not reported, and it is unclear whether any hyperparameter optimization was attempted, making comparisons difficult (R1, R2). Moreover, changing the optimizer or learning rate causes the model to collapse; adding more fully-connected layers also leads to collapse, undermining the claim of stability (R1).

3. **Biological plausibility concerns**: The sequential training requires external control that decides when specific layers are updated, violating the “minimal external control” requirement of predictive coding (Whittington & Bogacz, 2017). If such external control is allowed, PC can be turned into backpropagation (Salvatori et al., 2022; Song et al., 2020), which would solve the stability problems – but also undermines the biological motivation (R4). This approach also makes neuromorphic implementation harder (R4). On CIFAR‑10 the experiments use CNNs with repeated weights, which are biologically implausible; if biological plausibility is not maintained, why use PC at all? (R1, R2). The paper lacks a discussion of how skip modules relate to biology (e.g., skip connections in cortex, non‑hierarchical brain organization; Hegdé & Felleman, 2007; Suzuki et al., 2023). Improving stability alone is not a sufficient justification (R3).

4. **Low accuracy and poor baselines**: SPCN performance is far from stellar and still degrades for deeper networks, raising questions about significance (R1, R2). On MNIST, both PCN and SPCN achieve only ~90% accuracy, whereas a 4‑layer KNN reaches ~97% and a random network (with only the last layer trained) ~98% – the model is worse than these trivial baselines (R1). On CIFAR‑10, results are low (below 60% for deeper networks), while a simple 8‑layer MLP can obtain 48% and LeNet ~68%; other bio‑inspired methods (DFA, Target Propagation variants, PEPITA, Sign Symmetry) clearly outperform this approach. Thus SPCN does not enable practical PC (R1). Reviewers note that on these small tasks PCNs can match backprop (98%+ on MNIST, 85%+ on CIFAR‑10; Pinchetti et al., 2024); the low accuracy here is unexplained (R4). Accuracies on MNIST are comparable to linear regression (R4). The CIFAR‑10 results fluctuate with depth (PCN even outperforming SPCN at some depths, peaking at L=6), which is inconsistent with feedforward models with skip connections trained by backprop and raises reliability concerns (R3).

5. **Missing baselines and ablations**: No validation set is used, indicating sequential overfitting; therefore results cannot be trusted (R1). A crucial baseline is missing: a PCN with simple L2 normalization on weights or activations, to determine whether improvements are specific to SPCN or just due to regularization (R3). Ablation studies applying the regularization and skip‑connection techniques separately are also needed (R4). The connection between weight initialization and error propagation suggests that BP‑optimized initializations may not be optimal for PC – the paper could explore this (R4).

6. **Limited novelty and omitted related work**: While R2 finds the sequential training approach original, R1 strongly disagrees, noting that skip connections for better gradient flow are not new (ResNet, WideResNet, DenseNet, ResNeXt, skip‑RNN, physical neural networks). Skip connections in the backward pass were introduced in Sparse Attentive Backtracking (SAB; NeurIPS 2018), and skip connections within predictive coding already appear in a recent survey by Friston and Rao (2023, eq. 9). Many prior works that address or resolve the same PC limitations are neglected (R1). The theoretical connection of PCNs to dynamical systems is trivial: it was shown by Frieder & Lukas (2022) for PCNs specifically, and by Li et al. (2020) for skip‑connected networks in general. Lemma 1 is a standard result in matrix theory and should cite standard texts (Horn & Johnson; Bellman) (R1).

7. **All experiments are on small datasets** (MNIST, CIFAR‑10); no results on larger datasets such as ImageNet, whereas other bio‑inspired alternatives have recently scaled to such tasks (R1).

8. **External control versus autonomy**: The authors should explain what practical advantage SPCN offers if it requires external control that breaks PC’s autonomous parallel updates. If control is relaxed, the method could be used solely with the skip‑connection and regularization ideas, leaving the sequential training aside (R4).
