## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces MedJourney, a method for counterfactual medical image generation using instruction-learning from multimodal patient journeys. It processes two chest X-rays from different time points and their reports, uses GPT-4 to generate a natural language disease progression description, and trains a latent diffusion model with a two-stage curriculum (pretraining on single image-report pairs, then fine-tuning on counterfactual triples). Experiments on MIMIC-CXR show improvements over InstructPix2Pix and RoentGen across pathology, race, age, and spatial alignment metrics. All three reviewers acknowledge novelty but raise concerns about GPT-4 description validation, image registration’s role, evaluation limitations, and missing comparisons; one reviewer is notably more critical about the core methodological contribution.

### Strengths
- First paper to develop models for unconstrained counterfactual medical image generation following arbitrary natural-language disease progression descriptions, going beyond simple class edits (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- Innovative use of GPT-4 to automatically extract progression descriptions from sequential imaging reports, providing a scalable alternative to manual synthesis (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- Introduction of a two-stage curriculum learning strategy that leverages abundant non-sequential image-report pairs to enhance the base model when time-series data is scarce (Reviewers 1, 3).
- Benchmarking against both general-domain (InstructPix2Pix) and medical-domain (RoentGen) methods, with substantial outperformance reported (Reviewer 2).
- Comprehensive evaluation suite including pathology, race, age, and spatial alignment metrics, plus the CMIG score for accuracy and feature retention—a reasonable first attempt at quantitative evaluation (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- Well-structured and easy-to-follow presentation (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses
- **GPT-4 progression description validity**: No systematic evaluation of GPT-4’s generated texts against radiologists’ opinions or clinical ground truth. The paper lacks sanity checks to prevent GPT-4 from generating illogical or irrelevant progressions (e.g., when images show no significant changes or come from different patients). This directly affects the correctness of generated counterfactual images (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Minimal advantage over impression sections**: The benefit of using GPT-4 over the existing impression section in reports is marginal (Table 2). Clarification on how the impression section was used and why GPT-4 is essential is needed, with examples showing clear advantages (Reviewer 1).
- **Image registration contribution unclear**: Substantial improvements appear to stem from image registration using SimpleITK, raising questions about MedJourney’s originality. The paper should quantify image registration’s impact and explain how it was adapted beyond standard preprocessing (Reviewer 1). One reviewer (score 5) is particularly critical that this reliance undermines the claimed novelty.
- **Missing comparison with recent instruction-following methods**: No comparison to more recent works like Imagic (text-based real image editing with diffusion models) that perform per-example optimization for high-fidelity edits and closely follow instructions and reference images (Reviewer 2).
- **Lack of reader study**: Despite quantitative metrics, no reader study with radiologists to assess interpretability, authenticity, and clinical utility of the counterfactual images (Reviewer 2).
- **Ethical considerations and limitations not discussed**: No discussion of ethical implications, risks, or potential misuse when counterfactual images could influence clinical decisions (Reviewer 2).
- **Demographic bias not addressed in main paper**: Dataset overrepresents white elderly population (acknowledged in appendix); this bias should be highlighted in the main paper (e.g., Limitations section) as it affects generalizability and fairness (Reviewer 2).
- **Generalizability to other modalities unclear**: No demonstration on MRI, CT, or ultrasound datasets; uncertainty about adaptation across diseases, anatomical regions, and imaging techniques (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing FID metric**: FID should be added as an additional qualitative metric, as used in previous works (e.g., UnixGen, RoentGen) (Reviewer 2).
- **Evaluation details unclear**: (i) Which report’s pathology labels are used for comparison—the reference or those from Cohen et al. 2021? (ii) Why use the prompt “a photo of chest x-ray”? No justification or ablation provided. (iii) In Table 4, MedJourney segmentation results are more accurate than the reference image itself; does this imply that segmentation metrics are unreliable for evaluating generated image quality? (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Terminology question**: Why use “counterfactual” instead of the more intuitive “image editing,” when reference images exist and the task does not appear strictly counterfactual? (Reviewer 3).
- **Minor issues**: Missing GPT-4 citation (Related Work, page 3); Figure 3 placed too early (before experiments); Figure 1 never referenced in text; inconsistent use of “Figure” vs. “Fig.”; many citations missing parentheses (Reviewers 1, 2). Figure 4 should include the corresponding reference image (Reviewer 3).
