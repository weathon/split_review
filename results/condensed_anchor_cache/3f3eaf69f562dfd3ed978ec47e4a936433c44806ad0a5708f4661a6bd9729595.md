## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Neural Spacetimes (NSTs), a trainable geometry for embedding weighted directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) into a pseudo-Riemannian manifold that jointly represents edge weights (via a learned quasi-metric) and directionality (via a learned partial order). The architecture consists of three neural networks: an embedding network that maps nodes to events in a product manifold, a neural quasi-metric (built on a modified neural snowflake) to encode spatial distances, and a neural partial order to encode causal (temporal) structure. The authors provide theoretical guarantees: a universal embedding theorem stating that any k-point DAG can be embedded with 1+𝒪(log k) distortion while preserving causality, with sub-cubic parameters and improvement to 𝒪(log k)+2 spatial and 2 temporal dimensions for planar Hasse diagrams. Experiments on synthetic and real-world DAGs (e.g., webpage hyperlink graphs) show that NSTs achieve lower embedding distortions than fixed-geometry baselines (Minkowski and de Sitter spaces). Reviewers note the method is well-motivated, the theoretical framework is strong, and the empirical results are promising, but raise concerns about the lack of downstream task evaluation, limited comparisons with standard graph neural networks (GNNs), scalability, and insufficient guidance on hyperparameter selection.

### Strengths

1. **Novel and well-motivated architecture** – NSTs elegantly combine a quasi-metric (for spatial weights) and a partial order (for causal direction) into a single trainable geometry, enabling representation of both aspects simultaneously. The ability to embed DAGs into multiple temporal dimensions is a novel contribution (Reviewer 2, 3, 5).
2. **Strong theoretical guarantees** – The universal embedding theorem proves that any k-point DAG can be embedded with low distortion while exactly preserving its causal structure. Proofs are provided for all propositions and theorems, and parameter counts are given explicitly (sub-cubic in k, linear in DAG width; planar improvements) (Reviewer 1, 2, 3, 5).
3. **Clear experimental improvement over fixed-geometry baselines** – On both synthetic and real-world DAGs, NSTs consistently achieve lower embedding distortions than Minkowski and de Sitter space embeddings, especially in low-dimensional settings where average distortion remains near 1.0 while baselines degrade sharply (Reviewer 3, 5). Near-perfect distortion is reported on several datasets (Reviewer 4).
4. **Well-organized presentation** – The paper is clearly structured with a defined problem statement, proposed solution, theoretical development, and experimental validation. Real-world network analysis (Table 2) demonstrates practical applicability (Reviewer 3).
5. **Practical potential** – The framework offers a powerful tool for representing discrete DAG structures in continuous manifolds with low approximation error, opening applications in various domains (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses

1. **No evaluation on downstream learning tasks** – The paper reports only distortion and directionality metrics. There are no experiments on standard graph learning tasks such as node classification, link prediction, graph classification, or semi-supervised/unsupervised benchmarks. Comparisons with conventional GNNs (GCN, GAT, GraphSAGE, Graph Transformer) and heterophily-specialized GNNs (e.g., CPGNN) are missing. This makes it difficult to assess the practical utility of NSTs beyond embedding quality (Reviewer 1, 4; also Reviewer 5’s request for more baselines).
2. **Limited dataset scale and no scalability analysis** – All tested graphs are relatively small; the largest experiment uses ~200k nodes (cited as a limitation by Reviewer 3). Scalability to massive graphs (e.g., OGBN datasets) is not addressed, and a time complexity analysis is missing (Reviewer 1, 3, 5).
3. **Minkowski baseline achieves near-equal distortion on some real-world datasets** – Reviewer 4 notes that, on real-world graphs, the Minkowski embedding attains distortion very close to the NST result. This suggests the added benefit of the learned geometry may be small for those datasets, raising concerns about simplicity or task difficulty.
4. **Lack of guidance on temporal dimension selection** – The paper provides no practical advice on choosing the number of temporal dimensions. It does not discuss how DAG structural properties (e.g., length of longest chain, width of widest antichain) could inform this choice, leaving practitioners without guidance (Reviewer 3).
5. **Local vs. global geometry preservation not validated** – The optimization focuses on local structure, and while theoretical guarantees claim global preservation, empirical evaluation only measures local metrics. No analysis is provided on how well NSTs preserve global properties such as path lengths between distant nodes, reachability, or higher-order DAG features like branching structures and hierarchical organization (Reviewer 3). Multi-scale or hierarchical approaches are suggested to bridge this gap.
6. **Missing analysis of spatial/temporal component separation** – It is unclear how the embedding network guarantees that spatial information ends up only in the spatial (first D) dimensions and temporal information in the temporal (last T) dimensions, given that the same network produces the full coordinates (Reviewer 2).
7. **Insufficient explanation of performance gain over closed-form spacetimes** – The paper does not provide a clear explanation of *why* the learned geometry outperforms fixed spacetimes (e.g., Minkowski). More baselines and deeper analysis of the mechanism are requested (Reviewer 5).
8. **Presentation issues** – There is imprecise language in lines 420–422 regarding variable conventions (switching from T to W without clarification). A typo on line 215 uses ρ(x₁,x₂) instead of ρ(f(x₁),f(x₂)), which could cause confusion (Reviewer 2, 5).
9. **Robustness and breakdown analysis missing** – The paper does not test NSTs on diverse DAG distributions or examine when/why the architecture might fail. Its robustness to perturbations in underlying assumptions is not explored (Reviewer 2).
