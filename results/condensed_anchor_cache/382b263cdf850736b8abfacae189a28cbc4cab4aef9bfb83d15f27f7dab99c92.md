## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes iMotion-LLM, a multimodal large language model (LLM) fine-tuned with LoRA to perform text-guided trajectory prediction for autonomous driving. It introduces InstructWaymo, a data augmentation of the Waymo Open Motion Dataset (WOMD) with templated textual driving commands, and defines new metrics (Instruction Following Recall – IFR, Direction Variety Score – DVS). The model can generate trajectories aligned with a feasible instruction or reject an infeasible one, aiming to enhance safety and interpretability. Most reviewers scored the paper 3 (weak reject), with one reviewer (R3) scoring 6 (weak accept).

### Strengths

- **Novel task**: Text-Guided Intention Trajectory Prediction is a new way to incorporate human intention into motion prediction; it could impact safety-critical discussions in autonomous driving (R1, R3).
- **InstructWaymo dataset**: The data augmentation technique enriches WOMD with textual driving commands, making trajectory prediction more adaptable and providing a reusable resource (R1, R2, R5).
- **Integration of LLMs** with trajectory prediction expands the scope of traditional motion prediction and is an interesting technical approach (R1, R2, R4).
- **New metrics**: IFR and DVS measure instruction adherence and diversity of predicted modalities, offering additional angles overlooked in prior work (R4, R5, R6).
- **Insightful experimental result**: Table 2 shows that feasibility detection can lower instruction adherence for infeasible instructions, an interesting finding (R4).
- **Clear illustrations** (R4) and the method depicted in Fig. 3 is technically sound (R6).
- **Overall well-written and easy to follow** according to one reviewer (R3; note this is a minority opinion – most other reviewers found the writing poor).

### Weaknesses

- **Unclear motivation and task definition**: The paper fails to convincingly demonstrate why instruction-conditioned prediction is beneficial for autonomous driving. Benefits such as safety improvement or diversity generation are not substantiated, and existing multi-modal models already capture diverse behaviors (R2, R4, R5). The format of “instructions” is never formally defined; it is unclear whether they are single categories (Table 1) or complete sentences (R4, R6). The application scenario is ambiguous – how instructions would be obtained at test time in real driving is not discussed, and the task may better be framed as simulation/generation rather than prediction (R5).
- **Poor writing and structure**: The paper lacks logical flow; evaluation metrics are introduced before the model (R4). Sections 3–5 are hard to follow with excessive detail (e.g., half a page describing GameFormer) and unexplained terms (e.g., “caption” defined only at line 306) (R4). Numerous typos exist (e.g., “basline” line 460) (R5). Figures are confusing: Fig. 1 examples (U‑turn, Stop Sign, Agent‑2) are incorrect or misleading; Fig. 5 (Stationary) gives poor reasoning (R5).
- **Data augmentation limitations**: The rule-based approach uses absolute directions and speed thresholds that do not generalize to curvy roads or lane changes; categories like “go straight” are ill-defined on curved roads (R2). Feasibility conditions (e.g., maximum range 60 m, speed change parameters) are arbitrary and lane association is not clearly explained (R2). The templated instructions are far from human-generated language; no comparison is made with using an LLM/VLM to generate descriptions from scene renderings (R2). Quality of augmented descriptions is not measured (R3).
- **Insufficient and problematic experiments**:
    - Only GameFormer is used as the motion predictor; reviewers request comparisons with MTR and MotionLM (R1, R2). In Table 2(a), GameFormer without instructions often beats iMotion-LLM, even on “actual scenario instruction” (R1). OF/INF drops significantly with feasibility detection, indicating rejection of feasible instructions (R1).
    - Weak ablations: only two ablations (conditional GameFormer and iMotion‑LLM without feasibility) are compared, yielding mixed results (R2). Freezing the motion prediction model is not ablated (R2).
    - Custom data split of only 2,311 samples raises statistical confidence concerns; selection criteria not provided (R4). Tables 3 and 4 use different test/val splits, making evaluation incomparable (R4). For Table 2(a), it is unclear how GameFormer (which does not take instructions) is evaluated on instruction‑following metrics (R4). The conditional GameFormer baseline is not properly described (R4). Method names are confusing (e.g., multiple rows labelled “ours”) (R4).
    - Improvements from instructions may come from information leak (instructions contain future modal) rather than genuine language understanding (R5). DVS measures diversity but does not assess prediction accuracy (R6).
    - Performance with different training data ratios (20%, 50%, 80%) is not shown (R3). Failure cases (e.g., instruction not obeyed, crowded scenes) are not analyzed (R3).
- **Feasibility detection concerns**: The feasibility detector can reject feasible instructions, harming recall (R1). Parameters for feasibility are hard to interpret and not empirically justified (R2). IFR for infeasible instructions (INF) is reported as “lower is better”, but it is unclear why the model is tested with infeasible instructions that have no ground-truth in training (R5).
- **Misleading or unsubstantiated claims**: The statement that previous models lack comprehension of diverse driving behaviors is inaccurate (R2). Claim that instructions enhance safety is not demonstrated (R2, R3). No discussion of related language-conditioned trajectory works (TrafficGen, Language Conditioned Traffic Generation) (R3).
- **Architecture design issues**: The complex architecture with multiple components lacks justification; using an LLM for simple categorical/directional prediction may be unnecessary – a tuple of primitives (e.g., [dir:0‑7, speed:0‑4]) could be simpler and more intuitive (R6).
