- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 6, 6, 5, 6

## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes an “intention model” as a latent variable framework for understanding in-context learning (ICL) in large language models. It formulates a no-free-lunch theorem stating that ICL emergence depends on next-token prediction error, prediction smoothness, and demonstration quality. The model is used to explain phenomena such as learning with flipped labels and demonstration selection. The approach shares similarities with Xie et al. (2022) but relaxes assumptions on perfect model alignment. Experimental validation includes randomized label adaptation, linear probing to classify intents, and induction head isolation/knockout. The paper received scores of 6, 6, 5, 6.

### Strengths
- Introduces user “intent” as a first-class citizen in ICL theory, helping accommodate phenomenology where altered context still yields the same output (Reviewer 1).
- The latent-variable intent model is a reasonable and plausible modeling choice; weak assumptions are explicitly stated (Reviewers 2, 4).
- Theoretical results include conditions for ICL emergence, theoretical understanding of demonstration selection, and adaptation to random label changes (Reviewer 2).
- Provides an interpretable way to connect LLM next-token prediction quality and demonstration quality to ICL performance (Reviewer 3).
- Experiments with task shifts, linear probes for intent classification, and isolation of induction heads provide some justification for the model; the induction head knockout experiments appear to confirm the importance of identified heads (Reviewer 2).
- The analysis breaks away from typical assumptions about perfect model alignment, offering a more grounded explanation with thorough mathematical notation (Reviewer 4).
- The writing is generally clear and the notation well-defined (Reviewer 4; other reviewers disagree – see weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing reference and comparison.** A crucial missing reference is Lin and Lee (“Dual operating model of ICL”, ICML 2024). The authors should delineate how their contribution differs from that work (Reviewer 1).
- **Incremental contribution relative to Xie et al. (2022).** The theoretical framework largely follows the Bayesian inference derivation of Xie et al., adding only an error term. This extension is not groundbreaking; the contribution feels incremental (Reviewers 3, 4). The paper does not clearly distinguish its results from Xie et al.’s (Reviewer 3).
- **Weak experimental validation and lack of direct confirmation.** The theory makes few easily verifiable causal predictions. Section 5.2 admits it is challenging to estimate the values predicted by Theorem 1, so only indirect experiments are performed. This is a significant weakness – the theory essentially cannot be experimentally confirmed or falsified (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 3 echoes that the experiments are circumstantial; intervening on activations can support many theories, and there is no clear causal link that the model is computing intentions or an external matrix T.
- **Experiment section too small and deferred to appendix.** The main paper’s experiment section is severely cut, with most details relegated to the appendix. Which model and which ICL task were used? Without this information, the validity of the claims is hard to assess (Reviewers 2, 3). The experiments that attempt to confirm the plausibility of the intent model within real LLMs are in Appendix F and should be discussed in the main body (Reviewer 2).
- **Intent recognition experiment not convincing.** The task uses features from LLM layers plus a 2-layer network to predict intent. A baseline that does not implement an intent model is needed. Missing details: what are the 50 intents (instructions or tasks?), how are train/test splits done? (Reviewer 2). Also, do the induction heads identified in F.2 affect intent recognition in F.1? (i.e., if you knock out those heads then extract features, does intent prediction degrade?) (Reviewer 2).
- **Induction head knockout experiments are speculative.** Knocking out induction heads may remove general ICL ability, and it is unclear if the failure is specifically in inferring the correct intent rather than some other capacity (Reviewer 1).
- **No explanation for completely novel tasks.** Like Xie et al., the no-free-lunch theorem does not explain ICL on tasks where the intention θ is not in the family of intentions learned during pretraining (Reviewer 3).
- **Issues with the external mapping / flipped labels.** The concept of an “external mapping” is unclear. Users do not provide such a mapping; they present demonstrations with a transformation. If the mapping is randomly generated (e.g., flipped labels), it is surely not in the pretraining corpus, making the theory inconsistent with Min et al.’s results showing that models can infer the correct intention with random labels. The paper defers this to future work, but it is an important contradiction (Reviewer 3).
- **Assumption 4 is strong and conflates inferred and ground-truth intention.** The next-token error is conditioned on θ, but the LLM’s inferred intention depends only on the context. When the LLM infers the wrong intention, the error can be large, and the assumption does not bound this case. There is also no way to get predictions for a different intention given the same context, so the assumption is not verifiable (Reviewer 3).
- **“No Free Lunch” terminology is misleading.** The standard connotation of “No Free Lunch” is about universal impossibility. Here the theorem merely states that ICL depends on certain factors; using this term may confuse readers (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 3 also questions why it is called that, since no one expects ICL to emerge with high next-token prediction errors.
- **Unexplained aspects of the theoretical decomposition.** In Equation 13, the effect of demonstration quality is multiplicative, while next-token error and prediction smoothness are additive. The intuitive reasoning behind this difference is not explained (Reviewer 1). Why a neighborhood of intentions is needed (vs. Xie et al.’s exact intention with modeling error) is not clarified (Reviewer 3). The difference between Assumption 3 and Assumption 5 is unclear (Reviewer 3).
- **Poor presentation, clarity, and writing quality.** The paper has many typos, grammatical errors, and confusing phrasing. Examples: “Introducing an external to modify” (missing word), “error of next-token predicting”, “GTP-4” instead of GPT-4, “matrixes”, “can be wrapped in the way” (unclear), “It shows that ICL” – what is “it”? (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). Citations are used improperly: only \citet, no \citep, and citations are placed inconsistently (Reviewers 1, 3). Section 3.3 should define δ₁,₁, δ₁,₂, δ₄,₁ and explain “forbidden tokens/transitions” (Reviewer 3). The symbol o is used for both delimiter and document tokens, causing confusion (Reviewer 3). Line 297: θ_g is now called “inferred intention” but was previously “ground truth.” Line 292: where does m come from? (Reviewer 3). Table 1 is referred to as Table 2 in the text (Reviewer 3). The paper seriously suffers from these issues, hampering readability (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). A clear rewrite is recommended (Reviewer 3).
- **Unclear or missing experimental details.** What is “n” in Table 1? How does Table 1 “show that larger LLMs can capture the intention”? Isn’t it just scaling? (Reviewer 2). In Appendix L1182: “group them into 2 to 5 categories” – which ones? Provide details and samples for dataset preparation (Reviewer 2).
- **Difficulty in interpreting empirical support.** The experimental evidence does not clearly reflect the theoretical components (e.g., prediction smoothness, distribution smoothness in Equation 13). This disconnect weakens the alignment between theory and results (Reviewer 4). Results showing that ICL declines with more complex tasks do not specifically imply that the model is computing intentions; they are expected from any reasonable theory (Reviewer 3).
