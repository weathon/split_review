## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Cannistraci-Hebb training (CHT), a brain-inspired sparse training methodology built on Epitopological Sparse Meta-deep Learning (ESML) using the CH3-L3 link-prediction rule. The goal is to achieve ultra-sparse connectivity (~1% links retained) and demonstrate an advantage over fully connected networks. Experiments are conducted on 7 vision datasets, comparing CHT against SOTA dynamic sparse training methods and fully connected baselines on VGG16, GoogLeNet, ResNet50, and ResNet152. CHT acts as a gradient-free oracle for link placement and also performs automatic neuron removal (percolation). Reviewers find the paper dense, detailed, and original, with thorough experiments and clear methodology. However, concerns are raised about limited graph-structure comparisons, non‑competitive SOTA performance, missing standard deviations, legibility issues, and unanswered questions about architectural motivation and applicability to non‑overparameterized networks.

### Strengths
- Originality: the paper cites existing literature well and distinguishes its approach from prior work. (R1)
- Well-written and clear in explaining methodological details. (R1, R2, R3)
- Thorough empirical results with meaningful baselines and a nice variety of datasets. (R1)
- CHT is a new 4‑step training methodology that uses ESML to learn ultra‑sparse hyperbolic topologies. (R2)
- With only 1% of links retained during training, CHT surpasses fully connected networks on VGG16, GoogLeNet, ResNet50, and ResNet152. (R2, R3)
- Potential for wide‑ranging real‑world applications (image classification, NLP, speech recognition, recommender systems, medical diagnosis). (R2)
- Methods bring unique perspectives to sparse training, and various experiments show promising results. (R3)
- Detailed experiment descriptions provide transparency and allow readers to understand the methodology. (R3)
- Visual illustrations aid comprehension of the dense presentation. (R3)

### Weaknesses
- The paper only compares CHT to fully connected graphs; other graph structures such as Erdős–Rényi (ER), Barabási–Albert (BA), and Watts–Strogatz (WS) should also be considered to better contextualize the architecture. (R2, question about extra experience)
- Compared with SOTA dynamic sparse training models, the performance of CHT is not competitive. (R2, minority more critical view)
- Some results lack standard deviation reporting, raising questions about the reliability and robustness of those specific findings. (R3)
- Text within figures is too small; legibility should be improved by enlarging text in the figures. (R3)
- The paper’s language could be simplified to enhance accessibility to a wider audience. (R3)
- Including Table 1 or Table 2 (perhaps in reduced form) in the main text would improve readability. (R1)
- In Table 2 (page 15), some accuracy results are highlighted despite not being the best; clarification is needed on the highlighting criteria. (R2, question)
- The inspiration for the current architecture is not clearly explained. (R2, question)
- The method’s efficacy on non‑overparameterized neural networks with limited depth and width – particularly on large datasets – is not addressed. (R3, question)
