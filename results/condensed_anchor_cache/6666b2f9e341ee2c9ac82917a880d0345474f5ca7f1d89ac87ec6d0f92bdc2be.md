## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper tackles hierarchical retrieval with dual encoders, where if a document is relevant to a query then all its ancestors are also relevant (e.g., “kid’s running shoes” should retrieve “kid’s shoes” and “running shoes”). The authors first prove (Theorem 1) that embeddings satisfying a separation margin can always be constructed (Algorithm 1), but learning them empirically requires far fewer dimensions. They identify a “lost in long distance” phenomenon – accuracy degrades for ancestors farther up the hierarchy – and propose a pretrain-finetune recipe with oversampling of distant relevant documents. Experiments on a synthetic W-tree and WordNet (82,115 documents) show the method improves long-distance retrieval without sacrificing near performance.

### Strengths
- Novel problem formulation: a ranking problem with hierarchical relevance (DAG structure) motivated by keyword-ad matching.
- Theoretical contribution: a constructive algorithm (Algorithm 1) and proof that dual-encoder embeddings can separate relevant from non-relevant ancestors with a margin (Theorem 1).
- Empirical identification and characterization of the “lost in long distance” phenomenon.
- Simple yet effective solution: oversampling distant ancestors combined with a pretrain-finetune strategy that outperforms standard upsampling (R2,R3).
- Experiments on carefully constructed synthetic data and a real-world hierarchy (WordNet) demonstrate the approach.
- The paper is well structured, with a clear logical flow from theory to practical recipe (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation and real-world applicability**: It is unclear why the hierarchy cannot be used directly (e.g., retrieve most specific nodes and propagate). The paper assumes the hierarchy is unknown, yet queries and documents are nodes in the tree – this setup ignores textual semantics and may not generalize to free-form text queries (R1,R2,R4). The perfect-balanced W-tree assumption is unrealistic; real hierarchies are usually unbalanced (R4). Experiments are limited to a small vocabulary (82K WordNet); larger, more realistic retrieval datasets (product search, PubMed, ConceptNet, Microsoft Academic Graph) are needed (R1,R3,R4).
- **Limitations of the constructive algorithm (Algorithm 1)**: The algorithm only achieves separation with probability, not deterministically as claimed; no proof is given that it satisfies Cases 1 and 2 (R1,R4). The handcrafted embeddings require orders of magnitude more dimensions than learned ones, making the theoretical upper bound conservative and the constructive contribution of limited practical value (R2). The proof in Appendix A.1 is hard to follow, contains notation errors (e.g., Eq. 572 uses chi-squared incorrectly, “~” for distribution bounds is imprecise), and makes a strong “well-calibrated model” assumption (R1).
- **Experimental design issues**: Training/evaluation set construction may cause leakage (e.g., parent-child pairs could span train/validation splits) (R2). The batch size of 4096 is high relative to 82K documents, risking false negatives in the InfoNCE loss (Eq. 1) (R1). The loss is not modified to account for multiple relevant documents or distance along the hierarchy – a natural fix that could avoid the long-distance problem without complex training (R1). The comparison in Figure 2 between constructive and learned embeddings favors learned ones even though the learned model uses no text encoder (R4). Queries are assumed to be nodes in the tree; semantically similar but grammatically different queries are not considered (R2).
- **Missing comparisons and related work**: There is no related work section (R1,R4). No comparison to existing hierarchical retrieval methods, hyperbolic embeddings, or non-Euclidean approaches (R1,R2,R3). The pretrain-finetune method is presented as novel but is a direct application of continual pretraining without citation (R2). No baselines beyond standard InfoNCE and naive upsampling (R2). The paper would benefit from a discussion of hyperbolic embeddings (designed for hierarchies) and why they cannot be used with standard nearest-neighbor search (R1,R4).
- **Writing clarity and quality**: The paper has several typos, undefined terms (e.g., “long(er) distances”, variable s in line 183), and unclear experimental setup (R1,R4). The proof of Theorem 1 is difficult to read and contains imprecise notation (e.g., “d ± …” not denoting an interval) (R1). The paper does not discuss the potential of varying embedding sizes or provide an ablation (R3).
- **Reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (R1) rated the paper 1, finding fundamental flaws in motivation, theory, and experiments, while two reviewers (R2,R3) rated it 5, appreciating the problem framing and the pretrain-finetune solution. The fourth reviewer (R4) gave a 3, citing weak writing and unrealistic assumptions. This contrast highlights that the paper’s contributions are considered significant by some but insufficiently justified and validated by others.
