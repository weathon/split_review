## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a self-supervised pre-training strategy for image segmentation inspired by diffusion models. Instead of additive Gaussian noise, it uses a masking mechanism with variable masking ratios (t) to occlude parts of an image; a U-Net is trained to reconstruct the original image using an SSIM loss. This Masked Diffusion Model (MDM) is pretrained on unlabeled data; then a small segmentation network is trained on the labeled data using the decoder’s representations. Experiments on medical (GlaS, MoNuSeg) and natural (FFHQ-34, CelebA-19) images show that MDM outperforms self-supervised approaches such as MAE, DDPM, and SwAV, especially in few-shot settings. Ablation studies examine the effect of SSIM vs. MSE, dynamic masking, and different masking schedules.

### Strengths
- **Novel concept**: The probabilistic/dynamic masking offers an alternative to static masking and standard denoising, contributing a new angle to self-supervised representation learning (Reviewers 2,3,4).
- **Extensive experimental evaluation**: Results are reported on 2 medical and 2 natural image datasets, with comparisons to many relevant baselines (MAE, DDPM, SwAV, and supervised models) and ablation studies (Reviewer 2,4). Table 1 shows MDM consistently outperforms MAE and DDPM on all datasets in both full (100%) and low-data (10%) settings (Reviewers 1,2,4). For example, on GlaS 10%: MAE 76.19 vs. MDM MSE 82.70 (Reviewer 2). Table 4 shows SSIM improves both DDPM and MDM (Reviewer 2).
- **Well-written and easy to read** (Reviewer 4; also noted by Reviewer 2: presentation 3). One reviewer found the paper well-organized, while another (Reviewer 1) disagreed (see Weaknesses).
- **Reproducibility**: Authors plan to release code on acceptance (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and contribution concerns**: When the masking ratio is fixed, MDM reduces to a vanilla masked autoencoder with SSIM loss, making dynamic masking the main contribution (Reviewer 2). One reviewer considered the contribution solid (Reviewer 4, contribution 3), but another rated it fair (Reviewer 3, contribution 2). The method’s improvement is not consistent across datasets: on FFHQ-34, MAE obtains 57.06 IoU while MDM with MSE obtains 55.06 (Reviewer 2).
- **Lack of a proper validation set**: Hyperparameters (number of timesteps, blocks, patch sizes, training schedules, loss function choice) appear to be selected without a clear validation split, raising concerns about whether test-set performance might be optimistic or overfitted (Reviewer 4). This concern is shared by Reviewer 1 (Figure 2 and 3 lack Dice scores, making comparison difficult).
- **Insufficient benchmarking for segmentation**: The evaluation focuses only on segmentation; several specialized self-supervised segmentation methods (e.g., Leopart, IIC, MaskContrast, DenseCL, MoCoV2, DINO) are not compared on standard datasets like COCO or PVOC (Reviewer 3). A broader assessment across classification, detection, and linear probing is missing (Reviewer 3). One reviewer also questioned whether the representations are truly better if only tested on segmentation (Reviewer 1).
- **Unsupported claims**:
  - “This paper decomposes the interrelation between the generative capability and representation learning ability inherent in diffusion models” – unclear and not investigated (Reviewer 4). Similarly, “the representation ability of diffusion models does not originate from their generative power” is not shown; generative capability of the proposed model is not demonstrated (Reviewer 4).
  - “a scalable self‑supervised representation learner” – the term “scalable” is not defined or evidenced (Reviewer 4).
  - “the prediction task demands a focus on high‑level, low‑frequency structural aspects” – this claim is not well‑supported (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 1 also noted that many claims in the introduction (e.g., “such efforts risk deviating from the theoretical underpinnings of diffusions”) are poorly supported.
- **Choice of SSIM over MSE**: The switch from MSE to SSIM is empirical and not well justified. Other losses (e.g., normalized cross‑correlation, MAE) are not considered, and the trade‑off between learning a general representation vs. tuning toward segmentation is not discussed (Reviewer 1). Reviewer 2 notes that a fairer comparison would be MAE with SSIM loss to isolate the effect of dynamic t, since Table 4 shows SSIM improves both DDPM and MDM.
- **Decoder feature selection**: It is unclear which decoder level representation is fed to the segmentation network (single last layer or combination). The same level should be used for DDPM and MAE to ensure fair comparison (Reviewer 2). Performance variation with different decoder features is not analyzed (Reviewer 2).
- **Limited domain adaptability**: Results are only on image segmentation; demonstrating performance on audio, text, or time‑series would emphasize broader applicability (Reviewer 3).
- **Robustness not evaluated**: No experiments on noisy or corrupted data to assess real‑world resilience (Reviewer 3).
- **Interpretability and visualization**: The paper lacks visualizations of the diffusion or dynamic masking process to help readers understand how the representations are learned (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing discussion of limitations**: Computational cost of the diffusion process, training time, resource requirements, and scenarios where the method might fail are not discussed (Reviewer 3). Also, whether trained model weights will be released is not stated (Reviewer 4).
- **Disagreement on writing quality**: Reviewer 1 (rating 3) found the writing weak and claims unsupported, while Reviewer 4 (rating 6) found it well‑written and easy to read. Reviewer 2 gave presentation 3 (good), Reviewer 3 gave 2 (fair). Overall, the paper could be better organized (Reviewer 1).
- **Minor strength note**: Reviewer 1 noted “statistical results shown in Table 1 look promising if all the methods are compared fairly,” but this is contingent on fair comparison.
