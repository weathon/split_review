## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies whether, under a fixed memory budget, it is better to reduce model complexity by decreasing the number of parameters or by lowering numerical precision. Using compound scaling (EfficientNet-style) and quantization-aware training, experiments are conducted on image classification (CIFAR-10, ImageNet), keyword spotting, and language modelling (Wikitext). The main finding is that 2-bit weight quantization with more parameters is optimal across tasks and architectures. However, reviewers raise substantial concerns about the clarity of the memory constraint definition, the validity of heavily compressed baselines, missing practical metrics (runtime, training costs), insufficient ablation (activation changes, mixed quantization methods), small model scales, incomplete experimental rigour for non-CIFAR tasks, and presentation quality. There is notable disagreement: two reviewers are very critical (scores 1 and 3), while two are more positive (scores 5 and 6), collectively questioning the generality and fairness of the setup.

### Strengths
- The paper poses a practically relevant and timely question about the trade-off between model complexity and numerical precision, offering valuable insight for practitioners and further research (Reviewer 2; echoed by Reviewer 3).
- A good amount of QAT experiments are conducted across vision, NLP, and audio tasks (Reviewer 1).
- The research question is clear, well motivated, and supported by experiments covering multiple use cases (Reviewer 2).
- The problem is important and challenging; the paper provides evolving evidence on low-precision vs compact models, referencing recent developments (Dettmers et al. ICML 2023, Zhang et al. NeurIPS 2023) (Reviewer 3).
- Numerical precision is a valuable attribute when scaling larger networks, and the setup is verified on different types of datasets (Reviewer 4).
- The study explores a factor (precision) not always considered in scaling-law analyses (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- *Unclear memory constraint definition*: The memory budget is not clearly defined and varies significantly across tasks (CIFAR-10: 270k params, ImageNet: ~650k FP32 params, NLP: 8.4M params, audio: ~57.5k FP32 params). Even similar vision tasks use different constraints; the rationale for choosing these budgets is not justified (Reviewer 1).
- *Invalid baseline data points*: In Figures 4 and 5, the full-precision (W32A32) baselines are obtained after heavy compression (10x–32x), yielding unusable models (e.g., top-1 error ~90%). Since compression techniques require maintaining acceptable quality, these data points are invalid, and the observed trend largely relies on failed models, undermining generalizability (Reviewer 1). (This concern is most strongly raised by Reviewer 1; Reviewer 4 also questions practicality.)
- *Missing runtime reporting*: The paper does not report inference or training run-times, which are critical for resource-constrained edge devices as implied by the title. Practical applicability is therefore limited (Reviewer 2).
- *LLM experiments of limited applicability*: The WikiText-2 language model is extremely small, and compressed models perform very poorly (>40 perplexity vs 18 baseline, as noted in Appendix A.1.1). This weakens the conclusions for language tasks (Reviewer 2). (Reviewer 3 also notes the model size is 10× smaller than EfficientNet-B0.)
- *Incomplete rigour beyond CIFAR-10*: The CIFAR-10 experiments are rigorous (varying compression ratios, width-only scaling), but subsequent experiments lack these variations. For ImageNet and other tasks, only one compression ratio is tested, and width-only comparisons are missing (Reviewer 2).
- *Unverified base model modifications*: The EfficientNet activation function is replaced from SiLU to ReLU to improve quantization bucket usage. This change may already favour quantization without verification that full-precision quality is preserved. An ablation to check for regression is necessary (Reviewer 3).
- *Unpractical training setup*: The base model used is 10× smaller than EfficientNet-B0, making it unclear if a small model provides a convincing baseline. Training on ImageNet for only 30 epochs likely does not ensure convergence (Reviewer 3).
- *Mixed quantization algorithms*: Part of the experiments uses LSQ, while others use an undefined “plain linear quantization”. This mixture introduces an uncontrolled variable that requires ablation (Reviewer 3).
- *Incomplete precision range*: The study does not extend to 1-bit weights due to large model training limitations, weakening the conclusion that 2-bit is optimal (Reviewer 3).
- *Impractical precision choices*: Some precision configurations tested (W3A3, W5A5, W6A6) are not deployable on modern GPUs, limiting the applicability of the recommendations. Network width, depth, and input size are not problems on GPUs, so these precision choices restrict model size (Reviewer 4).
- *Training costs of QAT not considered*: Quantization-aware training (e.g., LSQ) requires storing FP32 weights, gradients, and optimizer states, which increases training memory costs when lowering precision to accommodate more parameters. Maintaining FP32 weights in training significantly helps convergence, biasing results toward low-precision outcomes (Reviewer 4). (Reviewer 2 also mentions missing runtime but not training memory.)
- *Small experiment scale*: The scale is too small for a scaling-law study compared to works like EfficientNet or LLM scaling laws (Reviewer 4).
- *Presentation issues*: In Figure 3, model parameters/width are not visible; a table would be more informative (Reviewer 3). Overall presentation quality is below average for a conference paper (Reviewer 4). Additionally, formatting suggestions: correct the typo “attemp[t]” (line 20), improve notation (eq. 2 uses same letter for α and α*), and the title overpromises connection to edge AI (Reviewer 2).
- *Minor clarity question*: The use of the scaling factor \(k\) in Equation setup (bottom of p.4) should be clarified (Reviewer 2).
