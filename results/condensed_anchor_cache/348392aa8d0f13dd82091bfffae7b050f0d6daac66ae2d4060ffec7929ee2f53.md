## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes FreeCG, which implements Clebsch–Gordan (CG) transforms on permutation‑invariant abstract edges derived from real edge information. This approach frees the design space of the CG transform layer without breaking permutation equivariance. Building on this, FreeCG introduces group CG transform with sparse paths, abstract‑edge shuffling, and an attention enhancer to boost expressiveness and efficiency. The model achieves state‑of‑the‑art force prediction on MD17, rMD17, MD22 and property prediction on QM9 (improvements >15% and up to >20%). The authors claim the method represents a new paradigm that can improve other models (e.g., QuinNet), but experimental support for that claim is limited. The paper uses only small molecule datasets and raises several concerns about missing baselines, SO(3)-equivariance, and efficiency.

### Strengths
- **Flexible CG design via abstract edges**: Invariance transitivity with permutation‑invariant abstract edges solves the narrow design space of CG transforms, allowing complete freedom in layer design without compromising the crucial symmetry. (R1, R2, R3)
- **Powerful architectural modules**: The FreeCG model incorporates Group CG transform with sparse path, abstract edges shuffling, and Attention enhancer, improving representation power and computational efficiency. (R1, R2)
- **Competitive empirical results**: FreeCG achieves SOTA force prediction on MD17, rMD17, MD22 and strong property prediction on QM9 (several improvements >15%, max >20%). (R1, R4)
- **Extensive MLFF experiments**: The paper provides solid empirical support across multiple benchmarks in the force‑field domain. (R3)
- **Robust and flexible representations**: Aggregating high‑irreducible edge features with attention‑weighted permutation‑invariant abstract edges yields expressive embeddings. (R3)
- **Compatibility with EGNN architectures**: FreeCG reduces CG‑transform overhead and can be integrated with various equivariant message‑passing networks. (R2)

### Weaknesses
- **Energy prediction on rMD17 (Table 2)**: FreeCG is not effective on energy prediction for rMD17; the authors should explain this. (R1)
- **Missing baselines**: Table 4 should include equiformerV2; Table 2 should include recent methods such as Graph ACE (Bochkarev et al., 2024) and PONITA (Bekkers et al., 2024) to substantiate the SOTA claim. (R1, R3)
- **Small datasets and robustness**: Results are only on small datasets (MD17, rMD17, MD22, QM9). Performance on a large dataset like OC20 would better demonstrate effectiveness and efficiency. (R1)
- **SO(3)-equivariance not properly addressed**: The paper does not make clear how FreeCG ensures SO(3)-equivariance. Because abstract edges are aggregated via a weighted sum over irrep components, the model may lack full SO(3)-equivariance. The authors need to either show how equivariance is preserved or justify using abstract edges within the CG transform. (R3)
- **Unclear advantages over Allegro**: FreeCG’s approach resembles the custom tensor product in Allegro (Eq.13 in [1]). The paper should clarify specifically how permutation‑invariant abstract edges offer a distinct advantage over Allegro’s pair‑wise features and environment embeddings. (R2)
- **Expressivity trade‑off of sparse paths**: A more detailed ablation study is needed on the impact of sparse paths – breakdown at different sparsity levels and effect on long‑range interaction capture. (R2)
- **Inconsistent ablation and module contributions**: Table 5 reports final FreeCG with group number 8, but if the best modules are used the group number should be 32 (Table 7). Moreover, ablations show that performance gains come primarily from increasing group number, casting doubt on the independent contributions of shuffling and attention. A granular analysis of each module is needed. (R2)
- **Unsubstantiated “new paradigm” claim (QuinNet integration)**: The claim that FreeCG forms a new paradigm and can enhance models like QuinNet is not supported. Only training curves are shown; QuinNet was trained for 1000 epochs versus 3000 for other models. No clear advantage of QuinNet+FreeCG is demonstrated, and it is unknown whether longer training would improve QuinNet alone. (R4; note: this criticism comes from the most positive reviewer, score 8.)
- **Lack of efficiency comparisons**: For MLFF, speed and memory are critical. The paper does not report number of parameters or training/inference speed; the proposed method appears to increase complexity significantly. (R4)
- **Scalability concern (Eq. 26)**: The summation over all permutations in Equation (26) may impose O(N × N!) complexity. Scalability with number of atoms should be discussed. (R3)
- **Loss function not specified**: What loss(es) are used is omitted. (R3)
- **Notational and presentation issues**:
  - Some notations are undefined: \(N\) in line 227 (possibly should be \(Z\)), \(\bar{E}_i^L\) and \(d\bar{E}_i^{L+1}\) in line 277. The relationship between \(d\bar{E}_i^{L+1}\) and \(\bar{E}_i^{L+1}\) is unclear. (R2, R3)
  - No explanation of how \(\bar{E}_i^L\) is constructed in the first layer – initial abstract edge features are not described. (R2)
  - Page layout (margins between paragraphs, figures, titles) reduces readability. (R3)
  - Figure 3 shaded regions impair readability; a transparent background would help. (R3)
  - Unnecessary line breaks in Appendix equations (7) and (15). (R3)
  - Table 8 is unnecessarily expanded to full line width. (R3)
- **Reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms/ablation requests**:
  - For abstract edge shuffling, did the authors try a linear layer to mix channels of \(\hat{E}_i^L\)? (R2)
  - Did the authors consider applying FreeCG to more representative tensor‑product EGNNs to further validate the method? (R2)
  - Why was the AIMD‑Chig dataset chosen for memory and inference‑speed benchmarks? (R3)
  - Why does abstract edge shuffling improve the model? A brief discussion of the role of shuffling is needed. (R3)
