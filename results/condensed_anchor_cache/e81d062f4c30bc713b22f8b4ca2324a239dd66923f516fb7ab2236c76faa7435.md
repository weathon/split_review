## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SMOACS, a method that uses gradients from differentiable property prediction models (ALIGNN, Crystalformer) to directly optimize crystal structures for multiple target properties (band gap, formation energy) under constraints like electrical neutrality and perovskite structure maintenance. It optimizes lattice parameters, atomic positions, element probabilities, and oxidation patterns via backpropagation, using oxidation templates to enforce charge neutrality. Experiments compare against Bayesian optimization (TPE) and generative (FTCP) baselines, reporting higher success rates; DFT validates two candidate materials. Reviewers found the problem important and the direct optimization approach interesting, but raised significant concerns about novelty (prior work like CDVAE), fairness and scope of evaluation, low success rates, method limitations (reliance on good models, non-differentiability of GNNs, restricted oxidation templates), and clarity.

### Strengths
- The problem of simultaneously optimizing multiple properties for materials discovery is important and significant (R2, R4).
- The method effectively utilizes gradients from property prediction models to optimize materials designs, enabling conditioning on multiple properties—a feature many generative models lack (R1, R3).
- SMOACS can enforce structural constraints (e.g., perovskite) by simply restricting optimization variables, making it adaptable to various design cases (R1, R3).
- The approach is straightforward and can use any differentiable predictive model without retraining (R3).
- Extensive experiments were conducted, including DFT verification of predicted band gaps (R4).
- Some reviewers found the paper well-presented and clear (R1, R4).

### Weaknesses
**Novelty and Relation to Prior Work**  
- The core idea of optimizing material properties by backpropagating through property prediction models is not new. Multiple reviewers note that this approach has been used in prior works, specifically CDVAE (Section 5.3 of [1]) and others [2][3] (R2, R4). The paper does not sufficiently differentiate from these methods (R2, R3).  
- Methods such as reinforcement learning, GFlowNets, diffusion models, flow matching, and language models for crystal structure design are not discussed in related work (R1). Comparison with generative models using state-of-the-art prediction networks is missing; FTCP deliberately uses worse prediction models (R3). Training-free conditional generation methods should be acknowledged (R3).

**Evaluation and Fairness**  
- Experiments are limited to one dataset; further experiments on other crystal structure datasets (e.g., Perov-5) are needed (R1). Only two properties (band gap, formation energy) are optimized; how to handle more properties (e.g., mechanical) and conflicts between models is not addressed (R4).  
- The comparison with baselines may be unfair. It is unclear whether alternative methods (TPE, FTCP) explicitly target the same properties; the weighting of objective terms is not specified. TPE outperforms SMOACS on band gap and FTCP on formation energy in Table 2 (R3). The success rate metric may favor SMOACS's own constraints (R3).  
- SMOACS tends to find less stable materials (higher formation energy, smaller inter-atomic distances) compared to the generative approach FTCP. Stability also requires competing phase analysis, not just formation energy (R3). This is a limitation of the optimization-only approach versus generative methods that keep structures in-distribution (R3).  
- The success rate is low in many cases; the paper should discuss why and how future work can improve, and clarify why this is not a significant issue (R1, R4).

**Method Limitations**  
- The method heavily relies on good property prediction models; with limited data, results may be poor (R2). Non-differentiability of GNN-based models (graph changes when atoms move) is not addressed (R3).  
- Electrical neutrality is enforced by restricting to D oxidation templates; the choice of D is not discussed, and this restriction limits the search space (R3). Parameter λ in Eq.14 (balancing target property and formation energy) is critical but its selection is not explained; handling multiple properties would require more such parameters (R4).  
- Computational feasibility of verifying electrical neutrality for a given structure is questioned: the paper claims it is infeasible to compute, yet enumerating oxidation combinations is combinatorial, but computing neutrality for a given structure is straightforward (R3). Gradient signals for properties not covered by prediction models (e.g., oxidation state) are unclear (R1). Compute cost comparison with baselines is missing (R1).

**Clarity and Presentation**  
- The paper is hard to read, especially lacking a clear problem formulation and concise method description early on (R3). Section 3.1 is particularly unclear (R2). The method needs to be better differentiated from prior approaches (R2). (In contrast, some reviewers found the paper well-presented (R1, R4).)
