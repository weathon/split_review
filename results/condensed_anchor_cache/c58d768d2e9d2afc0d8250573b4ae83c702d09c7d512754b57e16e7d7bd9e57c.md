## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers overall find the paper well-organized and the connection between higher-order posterior moments and derivatives of the posterior mean theoretically sound, with a clever subspace iteration method for high-dimensional imaging. Scores are 8, 6, 3, 6. Reviewer 1 (score 8, confidence 5) is very positive, praising the clarity and relevance. Reviewer 2 (score 6, confidence 3) and Reviewer 4 (score 6, confidence 3) are moderately positive but raise significant concerns about limited scope, lack of baselines, and underwhelming visualizations. Reviewer 3 (score 3, confidence 3) is critical, questioning originality, motivation, and practical value. Confidence ranges from high (Reviewer 1) to fair (others); Reviewer 2 and 3 explicitly state math/details not carefully checked.

### Strengths
- The paper provides a theoretical connection (Theorems 1, 2) between higher-order central moments of the posterior and higher-order derivatives of the posterior mean, extending Tweedie’s formula. The derivation is recursive and well-structured. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4)
- The method is applicable to any pre-trained black-box denoiser, requiring only forward passes (no back‑propagation, no additional training or fine‑tuning). (Reviewers 1, 4)
- Subspace iteration avoids explicit computation of high-order moment tensors, making the approach fast and memory‑efficient, suitable for high‑resolution images. (Reviewers 2, 4)
- The paper is well written, with precise notation and clear graphical illustrations of results. (Reviewer 1)
- The MNIST example in Fig. 4 clearly highlights different modes (e.g., 9 vs. 4). (Reviewer 4)
- The linear approximation in Eq. (15) effectively handles the differentiation complexity for higher‑order tensors. (Reviewer 2)
- The paper demonstrates potential for uncertainty visualization in Gaussian denoising. (Reviewer 2)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited scope and practical relevance.** The method is only validated for additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN). Real‑world noise (Poisson, Laplacian, Speckle, etc.) is not considered, and no extension to other inverse problems (inpainting, super‑resolution) is shown. This severely restricts the impact. (Reviewer 2)
- **Questionable theoretical novelty.** The core analysis leverages well‑known properties of the Gaussian distribution and recurrence relations for its central moments. It is not shown that the posterior is actually Gaussian, so the guarantees are unclear. (Reviewers 2, 3)  Reviewer 3 notes that the second‑order moment has already been used in denoising uncertainty quantification.
- **Lack of baselines and quantitative evaluation.** No existing uncertainty quantification methods are compared against. No quantitative calibration metrics (e.g., expected calibration error) are reported. The paper does not demonstrate that the proposed method provides better uncertainty estimates. (Reviewer 2)
- **Visualizations are underwhelming and hard to interpret.** For natural images (Fig. 5), the variations introduced by the principal components are very subtle; one must zoom to distinguish them. This may limit practical application. (Reviewers 1, 4)  Reviewer 2 adds that it is unclear how the presented curves connect to changes in ground‑truth geometric features. Reviewer 1 notes that the experiments are well presented but the variations are not impactful.
- **PC implementation and interpretation are unclear.** It is not explained how the principal components relate to specific image features or how users can control which directions are extracted. (Reviewer 2)
- **Motivation not justified.** Why compute higher‑order moments analytically instead of sampling from the posterior (e.g., using the pretrained score as a log‑prior with MCMC or amortized Bayesian inference)? (Reviewer 3)  A related question: how should the method be used when the noise level is unknown? (Reviewer 3)
- **Missing details.**  $\sigma^2$ in Eq. (4) is not defined. (Reviewer 3)  Computational complexity is not reported. (Reviewer 2)
- **Minor issues.**  (Reviewer 1)  – Historical denoising references should include classical works (pre‑2017).  – Clarify that “score” refers to gradient w.r.t. observations, not parameters.  – Eq. (1) is an observation model, not a denoising problem.  – Tweedie’s formula originated with Robbins (1956), not Miyasawa (1961); the first equality in Eq. (4) resembles Efron (2011) Eq. (2.8).  – Typos: “memory efficient” → “memory‑efficient”; “connects between … and” → “connects … with”; “most well known” → “best known” or “most well‑known”.
- **Absolute error comparison is missing.** Figures 4 and 5 should overlay absolute reconstruction error to verify that the uncertainty estimates correctly reflect restoration error. (Reviewer 2)
- **Possible improvement to visualizations.** Consider scaling eigenvalues more aggressively or combining multiple eigenvectors to show joint modes of variation. (Reviewer 4)
