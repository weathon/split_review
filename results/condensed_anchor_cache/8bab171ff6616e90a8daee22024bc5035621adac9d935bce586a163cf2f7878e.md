## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes MacNet, a multi-agent collaboration framework that organizes LLM agents into directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) for autonomous task solving. Agents are partitioned into "executors" (nodes) and "instructors" (edges) to enable iterative refinement while a memory control mechanism prevents context explosion when scaling to over 1000 agents. Extensive experiments on MMLU, HumanEval, SRDD, and CommonGen-Hard compare multiple DAG topologies against CoT, AutoGPT, GPTSwarm, and AgentVerse. Key findings include: (1) random topologies often outperform regular ones; (2) a collaborative scaling law where normalized solution quality follows logistic growth with agent count; (3) the small-world collaboration phenomenon. The authors claim collaborative emergence occurs earlier than traditional neural emergence.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses a novel, unexplored problem: scaling the number of agents during inference to achieve performance gains, analogous to neural scaling laws (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- The DAG-based organization is applied flexibly across diverse topologies (e.g., chain, star, tree, random) and the functional bipartite structure (instructors on edges, executors on nodes) is a clear extension of prior interaction DAG settings (Reviewer 1, 4).
- The experiments are comprehensive and cover multiple perspectives: comparative baselines, topology analyses (densities, directions, scales), and scaling experiments up to 1000 agents—a rare finding in LLM agent literature (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- The paper addresses the key limitation of content explosion when many agents interact, enabling scalable collaboration (Reviewer 3, 4).
- The discussion on why random topologies perform better and how increasing agents leads to longer “tail” aspects (diversifying solutions) is particularly enlightening (Reviewer 2).
- The paper is clearly articulated, well-structured, uses precise formal language, and provides interesting visualizations (Reviewers 2, 4).
- The proposed method is highly generalizable, requiring little human supervision or prior knowledge (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Collaborative emergence claim is not rigorous.** The improvement from ~55% to ~70% on certain datasets is too small to qualify as emergence, compared to neural emergence where models leap from image recognition to PhD-level reasoning. A more convincing demonstration would be a very difficult problem that only becomes solvable once a sufficient number of agents collaborate (Reviewers 1, 2; echoed by Reviewer 4 noting “collaborative emergence is undefined”).
- **Benchmark selection and performance gain are insufficient.** Even with up to thousands of agents, the maximum improvement is only ~20%, and on HumanEval MacNet often underperforms baselines. Moreover, single-agent methods (e.g., LDB, hierarchical debugging) already achieve >0.8 on MMLU and HumanEval, outperforming MacNet. The paper does not compare against these state-of-the-art single-agent methods, raising questions about whether multi-agent collaboration is necessary for these tasks (Reviewer 1, 2). Missing justification for why the observed gains are not a “breakthrough” akin to moving from grade school to PhD.
- **Reliance on closed-source models (GPT-3.5/4) limits generalization and analysis.** Using only the GPT series prevents isolation of model architecture, training strategy, or parameter count. It is impossible to compare e.g., a single 7B model against a MacNet of 7x1B models. The scaling law results and insights may not transfer to other LLMs; experiments with open-source models are needed (Reviewer 1, 2, 4).
- **Entangled sources of performance improvement.** Since actual FLOPs or compute time are not tracked, it is unclear whether MacNet’s gains come from multi-agent collaboration or simply from increased inference-time compute (more rounds, more tokens). Without normalizing for compute, the method could be reducing to a technique for scaling inference compute, and should then be compared against other non-multi-agent inference-time scaling methods (Reviewer 4).
- **Implementation details are under-specified.** (a) The rationale for distinguishing “instructor” and “executor” is unclear—both could be nodes with different profiles (Reviewer 3). (b) Agent profiles and tools are generated by GPT-4 and randomly assigned; it is not clear how this process is fully automated without human intervention at scale (1,000+ agents), nor whether random assignment guarantees the network can solve a given problem (Reviewer 3). (c) The memory control mechanism’s potential omission of context information is not evaluated (Reviewer 3). (d) Number of generated agent profiles/tools and repeated measurements with variance are not reported (Reviewer 3).
- **Scaling law analysis is incomplete.** Although the sigmoid functional form is described, the law is not actually fit to the data, nor are parameter estimates provided. There is no discussion of *why* the scaling law should be sigmoidal rather than power-law or other forms (Reviewer 4).
- **Minor issues:** (a) The iterative feedback between instructor and executor could create loops, violating the directedness of the DAG (Reviewer 4). (b) The paper does not explore whether agents themselves can determine good topologies (e.g., specifying where to send outputs) (Reviewer 4). (c) The claim that collaborative emergence occurs “earlier than traditional neural emergence” requires clarification and detailed discussion (Reviewer 2). (d) No mathematical theory is provided to differentiate increasing model size vs. increasing the number of collaborating models (Reviewer 2). (e) The averaging method across benchmarks (e.g., same number of tasks per dataset?) is not explained (Reviewer 4).
