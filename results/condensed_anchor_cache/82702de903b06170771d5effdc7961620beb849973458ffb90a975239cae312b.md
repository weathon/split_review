## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a new formulation for selecting the best Large Language Model (LLM) from a collection for new tasks by repurposing benchmark datasets to learn a "router" model. The problem is reduced to a collection of binary classification tasks that predict correctness per model, and three scoring methods (S1, S2, S3) are proposed for model selection, with special attention to out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization. Experiments on 29 HELM datasets show that the approach outperforms the single best-model average (BMA) and a per-instance perplexity baseline, achieving near 90% of Oracle accuracy (Table 1). Reviewers note strong empirical results and thorough OOD discussion, but raise concerns about limited baseline comparisons, lack of comparison with Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) and fine-tuning methods, notation issues, and practical limitations such as the cost of obtaining benchmark evaluations for large models. Reviewer scores range from 3 to 6, indicating mixed assessment.

### Strengths

- Formulates LLM routing as a collection of binary classification tasks; achieves better performance than a strong single model. (R1)
- Showcases the potential of using benchmarks for routing LLMs and explores three scores for model selection, especially the third score (S3) that accounts for OOD because a new task is likely different from benchmark datasets. (R2)
- Routers only depend on the input \(x\) (no need to obtain LLM generations), making them more efficient than prior works. (R2)
- Conducts robust experiments and provides compelling evidence that an imperfect correctness predictor can enhance LLM performance. (R2)
- Writing is well-structured and comprehensible, even for non-trivial technical details (e.g., Lemma 4.1). (R3, R4)
- Addresses a significant problem: selecting the best model from many for a new task. (R3)
- Provides comprehensive experimental evidence on the HELM benchmark, outperforming BMA and a per-instance perplexity baseline; achieves near 90% of Oracle accuracy (Table 1). (R3, R4)
- Thoroughly investigates and discusses OOD issues: Table 1 shows the Oracle accuracy gap; paragraphs on “reducing the OOD gap” discuss the phenomenon and how much data might be needed to mitigate it. (R4)

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of comparison with Mixture-of-Experts (MoE):** No detailed analysis of how the approach differs from MoE (gating mechanisms, computational overhead, performance trade-offs). The paper does not clarify whether the non-parametric method would remain efficient for traditional MoE, where multiple experts might be selected. (R1, R2)
- **Notation needs clarification:** The definition of \(g_m(x)\) and its relationship to the gold label \(y\) is unclear; the input and output spaces of \(g_m\) should be explicitly defined. Equation (1) is confusing because \(g_m\) appears as both an independent and dependent variable. (R1)
- **Results of “S3 true \(p\)” require practical analysis:** These results rely on access to the true accuracy of correctness predictors. The paper should discuss practical limitations of this assumption and sensitivity of the method to inaccuracies in the predictors. (R1)
- **OOD discussion in Eq.3 is incomplete:** There is no notation of \(P(y|x)\) in Eq.3, and the relation between the solution of Eq.4 and OOD is not clearly explained. (R1)
- **Only one optimization problem (Eq.1) to find the best predictor:** More discussion is needed on how to design a better predictor function. (R1)
- **Some tricks (thresholding in Eq.4) are popular in traditional classification** and not novel. (R1)
- **Unclear why an “imperfect” (non-parametric) predictor is chosen over a “perfect” one:** The paper should justify the design choice of non-parametric classifiers over parametric ones, discussing trade-offs and generalization capabilities. (R2)
- **No clarification of the difference from fine-tuning methods:** Does the proposed method outperform fine-tuning? If the selected LLM is fine-tuned, would it perform better than an unselected LLM? Trade-offs between routing and fine-tuning are not addressed. (R2)
- **Table 5 shows larger models outperform smaller ones,** suggesting choosing the largest model might be optimal. The paper should provide granular task-level analysis to demonstrate where smaller models might outperform larger ones, thereby justifying the routing approach. (R2)
- **Application scope may be limited:** The method relies on extensive prior benchmark evaluations, which can be costly for large LLMs and impractical for specialized tasks not covered by standard benchmarks. An alternative baseline (evaluating all relevant LLMs on a very small dataset) is not compared. (R3)
- **Baseline comparisons are inadequate:** A simple baseline of evaluating all relevant LLMs (<100) on a small dataset and selecting the best-performing model is not considered. (R3)
- **Only compares with a straightforward instance-based approach (perplexity),** while the related work section mentions more routing methods that are not empirically compared. (R4)
- **No comparison with other accuracy estimation methods** (e.g., G-eval) that might overcome OOD issues. (R4)
- **No clear practical conclusion on which score to use:** NN experiments show S1 performs best, while Table 1 shows S3 performs best. With only 28 datapoints, it is impossible to draw reliable conclusions. (R4)
- **Question: For S3, what is \(u(d)\)?** It is confusing because Appendix A uses \(u(d)\) as dataset distance. (R4)
- **Question: By using S3, how many tasks end up using BMA?** The difference between Table 1 and Table 3 requires clarification. (R4)
