## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a noise-aware training (NAT) strategy for quantum neural networks (QNNs) that adapts to dynamic noise and “fatal errors” – noise configurations that degrade performance below random guessing. It uses a low-complexity genetic algorithm to search for fatal error sets and optimizes a combined loss over noiseless, average noisy, and fatal accuracy. Evaluations on classically simulated noise include image classification, POS tagging, and regression tasks. Reviewers found the idea novel and simple (ratings 5, 6, 3, 3), but raised serious concerns: experiments use only 4 qubits, real-device tests (Appendix H.1.1) showed no improvement and were worse than the error‑free baseline almost half the time, and advantages over simpler noise‑injection baselines are not clearly demonstrated. One reviewer (rating 6) was more positive about performance improvements, while the others (ratings 3, 3, 5) considered the flaws substantial.

### Strengths
- Identifies the limitation of existing static NAT methods that assume fixed error rates and proposes a novel NAT strategy that adapts to dynamic noise and fatal errors. (Reviewer 1)
- The equitable training approach (adding a fatal error term to the loss) is simple, concise, and novel. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4)
- Low‑complexity genetic algorithm search efficiently identifies fatal errors, optimizing robustness without excessive computation. (Reviewers 1, 4)
- Experimental evaluation is comprehensive across multiple domains: image classification, NLP (POS tagging), and regression. (Reviewer 2)
- Improves performance over various benchmarks (Reviewer 3), maintaining competitive accuracy under average error rates while improving fatal error scenarios. (Reviewers 1, 2)
- Overall writing is clear and well‑structured. (Reviewer 2)

### Weaknesses
- Scalability concerns: experiments only on 4‑qubit QNNs; current simulators can handle 20+ qubits, and larger‑scale experiments are needed. (Reviewer 1)
- Effectiveness deteriorates as the number of layers increases (Table 2); complex tasks requiring 10+ layers may make the method less effective. (Reviewer 2)
- Missing results in Table 2 (e.g., M4 in 5, 7, 10Layer; F4 in 7Layer) raise suspicion; justification or a full table in the appendix is required. (Reviewer 2)
- Real‑device tests (Appendix H.1.1) showed no significant improvement and were worse than the error‑free baseline almost half the time. This crucial negative result is relegated to the appendix, giving the impression of hiding it. (Reviewer 2)
- The method may become irrelevant as hardware improves and fatal errors become less frequent; the NISQ era will eventually end. (Reviewer 2)
- Only three noise types considered (Pauli‑X, Y, Z) – generalization to broader noise models (e.g., incoherent errors) is not shown. (Reviewer 1)
- Loss function design questioned: why not use a weighted sum over randomly sampled noise scenarios or average noisy loss instead of worst‑case fatal loss? (Reviewer 4)
- Figure 4 does not clearly support superior performance: in some settings (e.g., N.Acc(H)) NI‑H outperforms equitable training, suggesting the fatal loss may be ill‑suited for random noise. (Reviewer 4)
- Baseline fatal and noisy accuracy exhibit a large gap (Tables 5/6); equitable training shows no obvious advantage over noise‑injection in the N.Acc scenario. (Reviewer 4)
- The fatal error model is itself a specific error model with fixed rates; it is unclear how fatal errors are determined in Table 2 (e.g., derived from a Gaussian distribution?). (Reviewer 4)
- Lack of comparison with existing error mitigation techniques (e.g., Acharya et al. 2023) and combination with error correction during execution (only briefly in appendix). (Reviewers 1, 3)
- Efficiency of combined strategies and identifying the highest contributors of fatal errors would be of interest. (Reviewer 3)
- Only limited qubit variation (4 and 10 qubits) shown in image classification; effect of number of qubits not systematically explored. (Reviewer 2)
- Training via classical simulation then deploying on different QPU architectures (superconducting, trapped ions, neutral atoms) is not addressed. (Reviewer 3)
- Minor issues: typos (“ithrough” line 352, “signle” line 354, “minimium” in Figure 2 caption); inconsistent x‑axis range in Figure 4; incorrect bolding in Figure 5 (0.94 ± 0.01 for NI‑M in N.Acc(L) also bold); “error‑free” baseline terminology is misleading (suggest “error‑neglect”); Tables 5/6 mislabeled as Figures 5/6; bolding choices may violate the style guide. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4)
- Contrast in reviewer opinions: one reviewer (rating 6) was more positive, noting that the method improves performance, but still criticized the limited error subset and insufficient discussion of error mitigation combination. The lower‑rated reviewers (3, 3, 5) considered the real‑device failure, scalability limits, and lack of clear advantage to be fatal weaknesses. (All)
