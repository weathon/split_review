## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Primitive-level Skill Prompt Learning (PSPL) for rehearsal-free lifelong robot manipulation. The method uses a two-stage scheme: first learning motion-aware skill prompts (via optical flow from RAFT and CLIP embeddings) to capture shared primitives across skills, then adding new skill prompts via cross-attention with existing prompts to transfer knowledge. Evaluations on a large-scale simulation dataset (LIBERO-Goal, MimicGen) and real-world tasks show superior success rates against several baselines. The problem is important and the method has novel components. However, reviewers raised significant concerns about design choices, baseline comparisons, assumptions, and presentation clarity, with one reviewer (rating 3) being substantially more critical.

### Strengths
- The problem of continuous skill learning for robot manipulation is fundamental and important (R1, R2, R3).
- The paper is well-written and easy to understand (R1, R2) – though one reviewer (rating 3) found it hard to read and containing sweeping statements (see Weaknesses).
- Experiments are conducted on both simulation (LIBERO-Goal) and real-world settings, and the results outperform the baselines (R1, R2, R3).
- The method prevents catastrophic forgetting without requiring a replay buffer or memory mechanism (R4).
- Using optical flow and CLIP embeddings together to discover fine-grained motion primitives is a novel and sensible idea (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Model design choices are insufficiently justified**: Why are both CLIP (a vision-language model) and a separate ResNet needed to encode visual features? Could optical flow be removed without degrading performance? (R1). Why are lifelong prompts and primitive prompts summed before the product with motion-aware prompts to compute P, rather than concatenated before the transformer decoder? (R1). Why are task prompts applied as prefixes to each layer – this is uncommon in state-of-the-art methods and requires ablation (R3). How exactly do prompt tokens split into \(p_k\) and \(p_v\)? How are prompt tokens \(P_m\) decided and how do they relate to the prompts in Section 4.2? (R3). The MSA layer is not defined (R1). In Algorithm 1, line 11, what is the result of \(MAP_k\) and where is it used? (R1).
- **Definition and visualization of “primitives”**: How are “primitives” defined? Can they be learned? Can they be visualized? (R1).
- **Assumption that language labels lack shareable information is questionable** (R3). The paper argues that language task descriptions may not provide useful sharable information between tasks, motivating the use of optical flow. Denser and richer language labels (e.g., “keep pot grasped, reach close to target, then place”) could provide motion information, making the optical-flow advantage unclear without a direct comparison against denser language. One reviewer (rating 3) considers this claim not well-supported.
- **Reliance on optical flow**: Using optical flow from a pre-trained model (RAFT) may introduce errors that propagate and compound in downstream action prediction (R2). How well does optical flow work for more complex tasks? The paper should compare against alternative motion representations such as keypoint tracking or object tracking (R3).
- **Insufficient baseline comparisons**: Missing several important continual learning and lifelong learning baselines, including EWC (Overcoming catastrophic forgetting in neural networks, NeurIPS’17), PACKNET (Adding multiple tasks to a single network by iterative pruning, CVPR’18), and others from the language/vision continual learning literature (e.g., Ke et al., Wu et al.) (R2, R3). The ER (experience replay) baseline is reportedly barely better than sequential fine-tuning – which is extremely surprising. The memory size selection for ER is not explained, and other rehearsal-free methods like EWC were not tried (R3). The paper should also compare against relevant prior robotics works: Search-Based Task Planning with Learned Skill Effect Models for Lifelong Robotic Manipulation (ICRA’22) and LEAGUE: Guided Skill Learning and Abstraction for Long-Horizon Manipulation (RA-L’23) (R2). No explanations of the baseline methods are provided, making it hard to understand specific improvements (R4).
- **Missing experiment details and evaluations**: What is the performance on other LIBERO benchmarks (LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Object, LIBERO-100)? (R2). What are the failure modes of the proposed method? (R2). In line 341, MimicGen is a machine-generated dataset, not human demonstrations; the description of MimicGen tasks should be more detailed (R2).
- **Assumption that new skills are related to existing skills** (R3): The method assumes during lifelong learning that new skills share knowledge with previously learned skills. Would the approach fail if a completely unrelated skill is added? This is a concern in robotics settings with limited skill coverage.
- **Dataset and generalization limitations**: The method requires a priori knowledge of skills – specifically, primitive-level skill demonstrations with natural language descriptions. Constructing such a dataset is expensive, possibly more so than using a memory mechanism, and this limits extension to other settings (R4). The learned skills in the dataset (e.g., “open the drawer”, “grasp the mug”, “place the mug”) are simple and already decomposed into sharable primitive behaviors. There is no evidence the method generalizes to more complex, composed skills represented by longer prompts (e.g., “put the mug in the drawer” as a single prompt) (R4).
- **Presentation issues** (multiple reviewers): Figure 2 has confusing colors (R1). Figure 3 contains wrong images for the lifelong new skill 2 “grasp the croissant” (the robot is grasping a banana) (R1, R4). There are typos: line 142 “we leverages” → “we leverage”; line 179 “to obtained” → “to obtain”; line 180 “preprend” → “prepended”; line 292 missing brackets on the RHS of the equation; sentences [54-56] and [109-111] are identical (R4). The paper makes a sweeping statement in line 256 (“Parameter-efficient methods have shown remarkable success in mitigating catastrophic forgetting …”) without citations; adapters do forget less but they still forget and also learn less (Biderman et al.) – this claim needs proper support (R3). One reviewer (rating 3) found the overall paper hard to read and understand.
