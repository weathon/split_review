## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper investigates whether transformers can learn to perform search by training small decoder-only models on synthetic graph connectivity data (directed acyclic graphs). The authors find that transformers can learn to search under restrictive training distributions and, through a novel mechanistic interpretability technique, discover that the model learns a progressive message passing algorithm that expands reachable sets per vertex across layers. However, the models struggle as graph size increases, and this difficulty persists even with larger models or in-context (chain-of-thought) exploration. The paper introduces data augmentation strategies to improve generalization. Reviewers note that the topic is timely, the approach is novel, and the mechanistic analysis is valuable, but concerns are raised about logical clarity, data leakage, the simplified model setup, and the broader implications relative to prior work on LLM graph reasoning.

### Strengths

- The study tackles an intriguing and practical research question: understanding the mechanisms behind search capabilities in LLMs, with meaningful implications for real-world applications (Reviewer 1).
- Training small transformers on synthetic graph data is a reasonable and well-justified approach (Reviewer 1).
- The topic is interesting and may have influence in the community (Reviewer 2).
- The major strength lies in the motivation to understand transformer mechanisms in search-based tasks. The authors take carefully designed experimental exploration with careful discussion on data distribution and propose a new mechanistic approach to analyze the learned algorithm. The discovery of a message passing algorithm (progressive neighborhood sharing leading to exponential path merging) is a key contribution (Reviewer 3).
- The paper touches upon the difficulty of training on larger graphs and proposes in-context tasks to help exploration, providing in-depth analysis (Reviewer 3).
- Combining mechanistic interpretability with the search problem is novel, as most prior interpretability works focus on classification-style tasks with restricted output spaces. The new setting prompts a new algorithm for mechanistic analysis, of interest to the interpretability community (Reviewer 4).
- The exposition style is well-liked, with each section introducing growing complexity and experimental findings that sufficiently support the findings (Reviewer 4).
- The authors study nuanced challenges for transformers to generalize on algorithmic tasks under distribution shift (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses

- **Logical flow and claim support (Reviewer 1, negative; others more positive):** One reviewer (score 5) finds the logical flow weak in several areas. Specifically, the claim in line 51 (“We demonstrate experimentally that transformers can indeed be taught to search, but only under fairly restrictive conditions on the training distribution”) is not fully supported by Figure 3, which only shows limited generalization to larger lookaheads and does not substantiate conclusions about the training distribution itself. The claim lacks a clear definition of what constitutes a “restrictive” training distribution and how this restriction manifests in the data. Experiments should include more controlled variation of the training distribution to isolate the impact of specific properties. In contrast, other reviewers (scores 8 and 6) found the experimental design careful and the findings sufficiently supported.

- **Pattern and hypothesis formation (Reviewer 1):** In line 359, the authors state “We noticed a pattern and formed a hypothesis about the algorithm the model has acquired,” but the pattern observed and its connection to the hypothesis remain unclear. The description lacks specific details about the observed features/behaviors and how they led to the concrete hypothesis. More elaboration is needed.

- **Method clarification (Reviewer 1):** In line 337, “path of explainable attention operations” – was this path inspected manually? In line 358, “a number of input examples” is unspecified; providing the exact number would improve robustness.

- **Data leakage issues (Reviewer 2):** The authors generate training data online and save the first few results as test data, claiming overlapping samples are removed. However, they do not explain how they compare whether two graphs are identical. String matching cannot detect isomorphic graphs (e.g., node 1->2 and node 3->4 are equivalent but undetectable via string matching). Thus, the test set likely overlaps with the training set. Additionally, given a fixed number of vertices and max in/out edges, DAG generation is finite, contradicting the claim of infinite graph generation.

- **Simplified model not corresponding to current LLMs (Reviewer 2):** 
   1. The authors use full attention rather than causal attention, effectively training an encoder-only model, not a decoder-only model.
   2. The model uses only one-hot token embeddings and position embeddings.
   3. For the graph connectivity problem, the model outputs only one token, whereas current LLMs typically have multiple reasoning steps.

- **Mechanistic interpretation methodology issues (Reviewer 2):** The proposed method requires performing perturbation and a forward pass for each element in every attention map, then another forward pass to see effects on output logits, which is time consuming. In line 318, determining the influence of modified tokens on attention through frozen previous layers is not reasonable, because modified tokens also influence previous attention calculations and thus the activations for each layer.

- **Sequence length and in-context exploration (Reviewer 3):** How are training samples decided for higher sequence length? How many steps in DFS traces are necessary for the model to learn? If the same ‘K’ padding tokens were provided to the experiments in Section 4, would the models generalize better?

- **Distribution of path-merge operations (Reviewer 3):** Are there patterns in the distribution of path-merge and copy operations across layers? Is there a trend in the number of path-merge operations per layer relating to graph depth?

- **Evaluation with sparse and disconnected graphs (Reviewer 3):** Do trained models generalize to extremely sparse graphs? For graphs with two disconnected components, what is the model output for start and goal vertices not in the same component? This is a critical edge case that could reveal limitations in understanding of graph connectivity.

- **Hyperparameter sensitivity in Section 4 (Reviewer 3):** How are values of α, κ1, and κ2 decided? What is the sensitivity of results to these hyperparameter choices? More discussion on selection process and impact on mechanistic analysis is needed.

- **Clarification questions (Reviewer 3):**
   - Line 303: What does “log attention weight” mean? How is “important operation” defined?
   - Line 354: The method requires “many forward passes (linear in the number of attention operations and in the number of input examples).” Can the authors provide the exact number of passes? Does the number of passes depend (logarithmically) on the length of the search process for a given input example?

- **Broader implications relative to prior work (Reviewer 4):** Several prior works show that LLMs can implement graph algorithms including connectivity, and that reasoning improves with appropriate chain-of-thought adaptations. This paper does not clearly delineate whether its findings contradict, confirm, or offer more nuanced insights to prior works. It should clarify whether observed limitations stem from the specific synthetic task design, graph scale, or inherent architecture limitations.

- **Missing theoretical and literature context (Reviewer 4):** The paper lacks situating in the LLM planning/search and theoretical expressivity literature. It cites works but does not discuss theoretical underpinnings for why transformers struggle (e.g., attention limitations in capturing long-range dependencies, challenges in learning recursive algorithms). A discussion of implications for broader understanding of transformer capabilities in complex reasoning is needed.

- **Limited practical guidance from data augmentation (Reviewer 4):** The data augmentation strategy that strengthens search ability may not translate to practical guidance due to the synthetic nature of the task. It lacks a clear connection to real-world search problems (e.g., planning in robotics, NLU). The paper should discuss how these techniques could be adapted to more realistic scenarios, limiting the practical impact of the findings.
