## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes replacing the single class-name vector used in zero-shot VLM classification with multiple attribute vectors per class, generated by prompting an LLM for subclasses, attributes, and variants (e.g., “pear” → “whole pear”, “pear slices”). Predictions are averaged over the top‑k generated subclasses and then mapped back to the base class. The method is evaluated on datasets with hierarchical structure, object states, and geographic diversity (MIT States, Breeds, DollarStreet, GeoDE) and compared against CLIP‑based baselines (DCLIP, WaffleCLIP, CHiLS). Reviewers’ scores range from 3 to 6, reflecting largely positive views on the idea but disagreement on significance, practicality, and evidence.

### Strengths
- **Diverse and relevant experimental suite** – The paper evaluates on multiple datasets that capture hierarchies, object states, and geographic diversity, providing a broad assessment of the method’s behavior (S1).  
- **Sound motivation** – The problem of representing within‑class diversity using only a single class vector is well‑motivated and aligns with the flexibility of VLMs (S2).  
- **Simple and modular approach** – The method is technically straightforward and requires only an LLM query plus top‑k selection; it does not involve retraining (S3).  (One reviewer notes that compute is modest, but this is disputed – see W9.)  
- **Leverages VLMs’ open vocabulary** – Using diverse word attributes rather than a single label better exploits VLMs’ ability to recognize many textual variants (S4).  
- **Clear intuition** – The reasoning behind using sub‑classes to capture diversity is clearly stated (S5).  
- **Improves interpretability** – Attributes provide human‑understandable explanations of model predictions (S6).  
- **Outperforms baselines** – The method is reported to outperform DCLIP, WaffleCLIP, and CHiLS on the evaluated datasets (S7).  (One reviewer questions the magnitude of improvement – see W1.)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited practical improvement** – The performance gain over baselines is often marginal. In many cases the method performs nearly the same as WaffleCLIP, which uses completely random text sequences (W1; Reviewer 1 is critical, while Reviewers 2 & 3 find the improvements valid but do not quantify the gap).  
- **Practical relevance of the motivating problem** – The within‑class diversity problem may be artificial. For many real‑world tasks, class granularity is defined by the problem, not by arbitrary broad labels.  Classifying an “arctic fox” as “fox” might increase accuracy on a benchmark but does not necessarily yield a more useful output.  The fundamental issue may be dataset construction rather than a limitation of single‑vector classifiers (W2; Reviewer 1).  
- **Dependence on LLM quality and prompt engineering** – The method’s success hinges on the accuracy and relevance of the LLM‑generated subclasses. Although a fixed set of prompts was used, crafting prompts that work across datasets required tuning.  Additionally, preparing subclasses per class may require as much effort as building hierarchical or attribute‑learning datasets (W3; Reviewers 1 & 2).  
- **Selective qualitative evidence** – The qualitative analysis (Figure 5, Appendix A) is based on a few hand‑picked examples and may not reflect overall model behavior (W4; Reviewer 1).  
- **Narrow evaluation scope** – The method is only tested on datasets that explicitly contain sub‑populations or attribute diversity.  It is unclear whether it would help on datasets like ImageNet or StanfordCars, where classes are fine‑grained and within‑class variance is low (W5; Reviewer 1).  
- **Misleading illustration in Figure 4** – The figure is intended to show that atypical classes have more close subclasses from the correct major class, but this is not clearly demonstrated by the presented data (W6; Reviewer 2).  
- **Unclear performance on fine‑grained classes** – As class variance shrinks (e.g., StanfordCars), the method may fail because the decision space becomes too tight (W7; Reviewer 2).  
- **Redundant inference step** – VLMs already possess zero‑shot recognition ability.  The paper does not justify why one should first infer attributes and then classify, rather than directly applying CLIP/OpenCLIP with a single vector.  Missing empirical comparison with standard single‑vector zero‑shot inference (W8; Reviewer 3).  
- **Computational cost vs. benefit** – The method scales linearly with the number of attributes used.  Reviewers disagree on whether the compute is “modest” (Reviewer 1) or “more expensive” (Reviewer 3).  It is not shown that performance improves proportionally with more attributes; if not, the added cost is questionable (W9; Reviewer 3).  
- **Missing methodological details** – It is unclear how the “worst % x classes” are selected (same across models or per model?) and whether they are re‑selected at each point when varying \(k\) or \(\lambda\) (Q1).  For the Breeds dataset, which level of the hierarchy is used as ground‑truth labels is not specified (Q2).  
- **Questionable example image** – The “red wolf” image in Figure 4 may not actually be a red wolf (it could be a misclassified dog), raising doubt about the correctness of the qualitative example (Q3).
