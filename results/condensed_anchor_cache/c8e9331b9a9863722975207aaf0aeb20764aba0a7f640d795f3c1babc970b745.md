## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper formulates Stateless Mean-Field Games (SMFG) as a generalization of congestion games where cost functions need only be monotone (rather than monotone increasing). It considers finite-agent settings with two feedback models (full-feedback and bandit). The authors propose independent learning algorithms (effectively L2-regularized gradient descent) with convergence guarantees to approximate Nash equilibria, and validate them numerically, including a real-world Tor network experiment. Reviewers disagree on novelty and technical depth: one finds the paper novel and well-executed (score 8), another finds significant overlap with known work (score 3), and a third raises concerns about scaling and bias terms (score 5).

### Strengths
- The Tor network access experiment (live real-world experiment using real Tor latencies) is very creative and interesting (Reviewer 1).  
- Empirical results comparing different game dynamics in real-world settings are valuable (Reviewer 1).  
- The paper proposes a seemingly novel way to deal with massive state space in multiagent RL (Reviewer 2).  
- Focus on a new finite-agent setting for mean-field games (Reviewer 3).  
- Independent learning algorithms are provided with performance guarantees to learn Nash equilibria (Reviewer 3).  
- Numerical results verify theoretical findings (Reviewer 3).  
- The paper is generally well-written (Reviewers 1, 2).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and prior overlap**: Approaching congestion games with variational inequalities is standard; relaxing cost functions from monotone increasing to just monotone is a direct implication—SMFG essentially equals congestion games with both increasing/decreasing costs (Reviewer 1). The use of L2-regularized gradient descent (instead of standard no-regret dynamics) is not new: making the problem strongly monotone, handling stochasticity, and implementing decentralized learning are known in monotone setups (Reviewer 1).  
- **Scaling and mean-field approximation**: Theorems 1 and 2 require \(T\) to grow polynomially in the number of agents \(N\) (e.g., \(T = \Omega(N^2)\) or larger) to obtain meaningful error bounds, so the results cannot be extended to the \(N=\infty\) limit considered in prior mean-field game literature. This suggests the algorithms may not be suitable for large populations (Reviewer 3). The justification by comparison to [2] (a simpler multi-armed bandit collision setting) is not reasonable because [2] does not involve mean-field approximation (Reviewer 3).  
- **Bias term \(O(1/\sqrt{N})\)**: This term appears in the bounds but is not explained. Such a bias does not arise in other independent learning settings (e.g., potential games, two-player zero-sum games). It is unclear whether this bias is inherent to the problem formulation or an artifact of the analysis. More insight and lower bounds are needed (Reviewer 3).  
- **Approximate vs exact equilibrium**: The algorithms find only approximate Nash equilibria; it remains open whether exact Nash equilibrium is possible with this methodology (Reviewer 2, also raised as a question).  
- **Assumptions**: Lipschitz and monotonicity of payoffs are imposed; though the authors make a reasonable case they are not too limiting (Reviewer 2).  
- **Dependence on \(K\) in Theorem 1**: The bound should explicitly state the dependence on \(K\) (number of actions) instead of hiding it as a constant; likely it should be \(\log(K)\) in the full-information setting (e.g., using exponentiated gradient descent) (Reviewer 1).  
- **Missing lower bounds**: The paper would benefit from lower bounds to clarify whether the \(O(1/\sqrt{N})\) bias and the polynomial dependence on \(N\) are necessary or due to loose analysis (Reviewer 3).
