## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes ChunkKV, a KV cache compression method with two components: (1) chunk-level token eviction to preserve semantic information, and (2) layer-wise index reuse to reduce computational overhead by sharing eviction indices across recent layers. Experiments on LongBench, Needle-In-A-HayStack, and GSM8K with LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct, and Qwen2-7B-Instruct show state-of-the-art performance at a 10% compression ratio, sometimes even surpassing the full KV cache.

### Strengths
- Explores chunk-level granularity for token eviction, which has some enlightening significance (R1).
- Clear writing and easy to follow (R3, R1).
- Extensive experiments across tasks and ablation studies (R1, R3).
- Interesting combination of chunking and cross-layer similarity-based reuse (R2, R4).
- One reviewer (R4) is more positive, highlighting that fragmentation preserves semantic information and yields strong benchmark results, and that layer-wise reuse improves deployment efficiency.

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation and related work need clarification.** The discrete vs. chunk comparison in Figure 1 is intuitive but lacks experimental verification (e.g., attention score or L1 loss between full cache and compressed cache for both methods) (R1). The paper claims prior work lacks chunk semantic preservation, which is inaccurate: SnapKV already uses a pooling layer (pool1D) for clustered-token granularity selection (R2, R4). A discussion and comparison with SnapKV and MiniCache (which also leverages cross-layer similarity) is needed (R2).
- **Limited novelty.** The contributions are incremental (R1, R2, R3). Cross-layer similarity reuse has been studied in MiniCache (R2). Chunking resembles clustering in SnapKV’s pooling approach (R2, R4).
- **Insufficient experimental evaluation.** Missing actual memory reduction, latency, and throughput statistics (R1, R4). Need accuracy vs. memory trade-off analysis (R2). Comparison with SnapKV and PyramidKV is not conclusive; key hyperparameters (chunk size, reuse ratio) and experimental settings (compress interval, whether compressing prompts or whole sequence) should be reported (R1). Higher and more diverse compression ratios need to be tested (R1, R3). Results with n=1 should be added to Figure 6 to observe chunk effectiveness (R1). The difference in reuse ratio between Figure 5 (llama3) and Figure 16 (appendix) requires explanation (R1). In Table 4, check the bold error for Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 at 10% compression (70.03 vs. 70.41) (R1).
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity and overfitting risk.** Many parameters (w, c, N_reuse, number of reuse layers) are introduced without guidance on robust selection, making the method hard to use in practice and subject to overfitting on tested datasets (R2, R3). Chunk size variation across tasks introduces an inductive bias; optimal chunk size may not generalize to open-ended evaluations (R3). The chunk size ablation is not combined with compression ratio, yet these are highly correlated (R1).
- **Layer reuse performance trade-off.** Performance drops linearly with the number of reused layers; a minimal-loss layer reuse scheme would be preferable (R4). The simplicity of the layer-reuse solution leads to a performance compromise (R3). The reason for the reuse ratio discrepancy between Figure 5 and Figure 16 needs clarification (R1).
