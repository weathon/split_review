## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates the influence of shape, texture, and color cues on learning semantic segmentation. Unlike prior work that analyzes biases of pre-trained classifiers, the authors train “cue expert” models on datasets transformed to contain only a single cue or a chosen mixture. The proposed framework decomposes natural images into cue-specific versions (e.g., HSV for color, edge detection for shape, Voronoi-based texture). Experiments are conducted on Cityscapes, PASCAL Context, and a synthetic CARLA dataset, using both CNN and Transformer backbones. The main finding is that no single cue dominates; however, a combination of shape and color (without texture) yields surprisingly strong results. The study is presented as the first to probe cue importance for dense prediction tasks.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles a relevant and under-explored problem: understanding which visual cues are important for training semantic segmentation models. This can be beneficial for domains like explainability, domain adaptation, and domain generalization (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- The study is comprehensive in scope, covering many cue combinations, multiple real and synthetic datasets, and both convolutional and Transformer architectures (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- The methodology for extracting individual cues from natural images enables a principled decomposition, and the paper provides a clear summary of all experimental settings (Table 1) (Reviewers 3, 4).
- The paper is well-written and easy to read, with a significant level of experimental detail (Reviewers 1, 2).
- Prior shape/texture bias work rarely considers dense prediction tasks, so this contribution is valuable for the community (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and depth of insights:** The findings are often unsurprising—e.g., shape cues align with boundaries, texture helps in broader regions, and using all cues is best. The paper does not explore the strength or quality of cues, interactions under noise or perturbations, or why certain cue combinations work. The analysis remains at a high level without dissecting underlying causes within the network, such as which filters or attention mechanisms capture shape vs. texture (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). There is a lack of practical takeaways or concrete ideas for improving model robustness based on the results (Reviewer 3).
- **Inconsistent results across datasets:** The rank change column in Table 3 shows different cue orders for different datasets (especially CARLA vs. Cityscapes), but the paper does not adequately explain why these differences occur or what dataset properties drive them. This undermines the clarity of the conclusions (Reviewers 2, 4).
- **Concerns about texture cue extraction:** The Voronoi-diagram-based texture creation is confusingly described; it is unclear how diagrams are generated, whether they depend on image content, and whether original class frequencies and distributions are preserved. Assigning Voronoi cells to classes randomly may not reflect the original distribution (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Methodological concerns about cue experts and evaluation:**
    - The shape cue relies on external edge detectors (EED/HED), which may introduce semantic bias if they were trained on semantic boundaries. Low-level filter-based detectors should be considered (Reviewer 1).
    - For the color-only cue expert, it is unclear whether 1×1 convolutions replace all layers or only the first; if all, the network may lack capacity, making poor performance an artifact rather than evidence that color is a weak cue (Reviewer 4).
    - Evaluating a cue expert (trained on transformed data) on the original images introduces a distribution shift that can severely degrade performance. The authors should also test each expert on the specific cue dataset it was trained on, as done in Table 2 (Reviewer 4).
    - The evaluation protocol needs a clearer separate description, including what input each expert uses and how edge cases are handled (Reviewer 3).
- **Metric and presentation issues:**
    - mIoU may not be sufficient; frequency-weighted IoU and pixel accuracy should also be reported (Reviewer 1).
    - Figures 10 and 11 are important and should be moved to the main paper (Reviewer 1).
    - Figure 6 is not clear enough to discern trends between segment size and cue influence (Reviewer 2).
    - Tables and figures should be placed right after they are referenced in the text (Reviewer 3).
- **Unconvincing discussion on segment size:** The claim that texture experts perform better on large segments may be confounded by class frequency or texture uniqueness. This requires more rigorous analysis (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing analysis of why Transformer outperforms CNN on cue subsets:** Table 2 shows a large gap between architectures on partial cues, but this is not explained (Reviewer 3).
- **Outstanding questions not addressed in the paper:** Whether similar conclusions hold for panoptic segmentation (esp. thing vs. stuff classes); whether specific deep learning operations can be used to mitigate cue weaknesses; and the unclear sentence at L193 (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
