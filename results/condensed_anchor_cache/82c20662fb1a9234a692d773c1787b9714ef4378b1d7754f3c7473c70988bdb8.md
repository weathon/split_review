## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a new problem, imitator learning (ItorL), where an agent learns to on-the-fly reconstruct imitation policies from only a small number (here one) of expert demonstrations for unseen tasks, without fine-tuning. The authors propose Demo-Attention Actor-Critic (DAAC), which integrates imitation learning into a meta-RL paradigm with a heuristic imitator reward. DAAC uses a demonstration-based attention architecture to output actions by adaptively tracing states in the single demonstration. Experiments are conducted on a custom navigation benchmark and a state-based robot manipulation environment. Reviewers generally find the problem interesting and the paper well-motivated, but raise significant concerns about the novelty relative to existing few-shot IL/meta-learning/IRL approaches, the restrictive assumptions (tracebackable MDP, coverage of rollout states by demonstration states), the heuristic reward design, limited evaluation benchmarks, and lack of comparisons with relevant baselines (e.g., GAIL, SQIL, ROT, Mandi et al.). Scores are 3, 5, 5, 6, with one reviewer (score 3) strongly questioning novelty and experimental rigor, while the most positive reviewer (score 6) finds the experiments sufficient and the direction practically meaningful.

### Strengths

*   **Interesting and well-motivated problem:** The paper addresses an appealing and challenging goal — one-shot, out-of-the-box imitation learning without fine-tuning, which meets practical needs for deploying RL agents. (All reviewers)
*   **Well-designed attention architecture:** The demonstration-based attention mechanism is a sensible way to retrieve relevant states from the single demonstration. (R2, R3)
*   **Ablation studies and scaling trends:** The authors provide ablations showing the usefulness of both the proposed reward and architecture, and include scaling results. (R3)
*   **Thorough appendix:** Implementation and environment details are helpful for reproducibility. (R3)
*   **Good experimental results:** The performance on the proposed navigation and manipulation tasks is convincing. (R2, R4)
*   **Clear writing:** The paper is generally well written and easy to follow. (R4)

### Weaknesses

*   **Novelty and problem framing:**
    *   The problem setup seems to fall within existing few-shot imitation learning, meta-learning, or context-based meta-RL, and the definitions in Section 2.2 do not add insight (e.g., could be replaced with "train a goal-conditioned policy"). (R1)
    *   The setting is very similar to inverse RL works (GAIL, ROT), which also use expert demos + online interaction and can work with a single demo. (R2) *Disagreement*: One reviewer (R3) states the precise problem statement seems original. (R3)
*   **Restrictive assumptions:**
    *   The method fundamentally retrieves the nearest neighbor state in the expert demo, assuming that rollout states are covered by the demonstration states (or training trajectories). This assumption is not clearly stated in the task formulation (only mentioned in Appendix F) and limits applicability to complex scenarios, undermining the claim of being a real out-of-the-box method. (R2)
    *   The “tracebackable MDP” assumption (a policy exists to return to states in the demonstration) is a strong constraint; it may not hold in many robotic manipulation tasks with irreversible subtasks or stochastic transitions, severely limiting the method’s scope. Examples and explicit limitations should be provided. (R3)
*   **Heuristic reward design and generalizability:**
    *   The imitator reward (Eq. 1) relies on a distance function between states, which is hard to scale meaningfully to high-dimensional observations (images, complex configurations). (R3, R4)
    *   The reward has several heuristic components and hyperparameters, making it theoretically hard to analyze and potentially brittle across tasks, raising concerns about overfitting to training tasks. (R3)
    *   The method may not generalize to established meta-RL benchmarks (e.g., Meta-World) or high-dimensional inputs (Atari Games). (R3, R4) One reviewer suggests it would be better if theoretical guarantees for imitation ability were provided. (R4)
*   **Limited evaluation and baselines:**
    *   Only tested on a simple maze-like environment and a single robot manipulation task; missing comparisons on mainstream control benchmarks (DMC, robosuite, Meta-World). (R1, R3)
    *   Missing comparisons with standard IL methods (GAIL, SQIL) and the very similar approach by Mandi et al. (R1, R2)
*   **Architecture fragility:**
    *   The demonstration-based attention mechanism is susceptible to issues when the current state is far from any demonstration state (e.g., due to compounding error or exploration), leading to potentially unstable behavior. (R3)
*   **Paper organization and detail placement:**
    *   Important details (assumptions, related work comparisons) are left to the appendix; more discussion of related work (online IL, few-shot IL, meta-RL, meta-IL) should appear in the main paper. (R3) *Minor*: Several typos and figure issues (Fig. 4 text exceeds maze boundaries). (R3, R4)
*   **Lack of theoretical grounding:**
    *   No theoretical guarantees for the imitation ability; the reward is purely heuristic. (R4)
*   **Algorithm cost and partial observability:**
    *   Each iteration runs SAC to update the context policy, which could be costly. (R3)
    *   The method’s performance is limited when ground truth state is not available; need description of failure in partially-observed scenarios. (R3)
*   **Questions regarding evidence and design choices:**
    *   Elaborate on the claim that demonstrations provide not just task identity but the way to accomplish the task, and provide evidence that the attention network effectively regularizes policy behavior. (R3)
    *   Discuss if the handcrafted reward limits the imitator module’s ability, and whether a unified or automatic reward producer could be designed. (R4)
    *   Clearly distinguish imitator learning from meta-reinforcement learning. (R4)
