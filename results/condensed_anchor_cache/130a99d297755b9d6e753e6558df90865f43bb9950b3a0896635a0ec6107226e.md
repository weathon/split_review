## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper addresses book-length summarization (100k+ tokens) for documents exceeding LLM context windows. It identifies data contamination and lack of reliable automatic metrics as core challenges. The authors collect 100 recently-published books to avoid contamination, generate GPT-4 summaries, and obtain 1193 fine-grained human annotations using an eight-category coherence error taxonomy. They then prompt GPT-4 to classify sentences with the same taxonomy, finding precision similar to human annotators. This yields BOOOOKSCORE, a reference-free metric measuring the fraction of sentences free of coherence errors. The paper systematically evaluates hierarchical merging and incremental updating workflows across GPT-4, Claude 2, LLaMA 2, and Mixtral. GPT-4 and Claude 2 summaries are more coherent; hierarchical merging yields higher coherence but lower detail than incremental updating, a trade-off sometimes preferred by annotators.

### Strengths
- Systematically tackles an important problem with extensive, solid experiments and clear writing.  
- Rigorous attempt to avoid data contamination by using books released within the last year; authors argue LLM memorization of summaries is impossible because no summaries were found online (one reviewer notes closed‑source models might still have access via private data deals, but overall this is among the most rigorous contamination‑avoidance efforts).  
- Proposed BOOOOKSCORE metric is reference‑ and source‑free, following recently proposed best practices, offering a practical solution to the high cost and time of human evaluation.  
- Focus on evaluating coherence via precision avoids pitfalls of n‑gram overlap or embedding similarity metrics that depend on human-written gold summaries.  
- Compares two standard book‑length summarization techniques (hierarchical vs. incremental) with interesting insights about coherence‑detail trade‑offs.  
- Authors commit to releasing code and annotations, enabling future research.  
- One reviewer (rating 1) is nonetheless highly positive, calling the work a “great idea, very well executed” and a “fundamental paper” that will be a required reference.

### Weaknesses
- **Missing baseline**: No experiment without hierarchical merging or incremental updating (i.e., a vanilla LLM approach applied to chunks or truncated books). For books near 100k tokens, it would be informative to see whether these techniques introduce or mitigate coherence issues.  
- **No faithfulness evaluation**: The framework does not ask annotators to verify each fact against the original book to assess factual accuracy.  
- **Metric limitations**  
  - BOOOOKSCORE assigns equal weight to every sentence, potentially overlooking the varying importance of different parts for overall coherence.  
  - The sentence‑level score may disproportionately favor summaries with many short sentences (fewer omissions per sentence). The effect of summary length on score differences between models is not investigated.  
  - No consistency check for the evaluation metric is reported.  
- **Human annotation design concerns**  
  - Only precision is evaluated for human annotations; recall is not measured (though the authors acknowledge this).  
  - Some examples of error types (e.g., Table 1’s “mysterious man”) may be of questionable quality: it is possible the book itself leaves the question unanswered. This could be a side effect of offering monetary reward based partly on annotation count (Appendix G).  
- **Model comparison gaps**  
  - LLaMA2 is omitted from the incremental updating results in Table 2 without clear justification.  
  - The choice of p=1 for nucleus sampling effectively disables it, and the large temperature (e.g., 0.9) is unusual for experimental evaluation (temperature 0 is more common for reproducibility). Claude 2 used a different temperature, and it is unclear whether results are stable when temperature is varied.  
- **Minor issues/typos**  
  - Page 7: “Incremental… lower” should read “higher” (the intended direction is reversed).  
  - Page 8: “highly extractive” description appears to contradict the large proportion of novel trigrams, which would be more indicative of abstractive summaries.  
  - The paper’s claim that “not finding summaries online implies memorization is impossible” is too strong; closed‑source models may have been trained on private data from publishers.  
- **Reviewer questions that overlap with weaknesses**  
  - Why does hierarchical merging produce more coherent summaries than incremental updating, even though the paper earlier states that merging lacks complete context and can cause incoherence for non‑linear narratives?  
  - How was the number of “O”s in BOOOOKSCORE chosen? (Minor.)
