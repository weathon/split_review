- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 3, 6, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a two-stage Variational Information Bottleneck (VIB) framework to disentangle textual and acoustic features from self-supervised speech model representations (HuBERT, wav2vec2). The first stage isolates textual content by training a decoder that minimizes irrelevant information while predicting transcripts; the second stage extracts acoustic features (e.g., for emotion recognition, speaker identification) from the residual representation. Evaluations on ASR, emotion recognition, and speaker identification show that disentangling is achieved, and layer-wise analyses are performed. Reviewers agree the method is clearly described and some experimental analyses are convincing, but raise concerns about novelty, motivation, experimental setup, and practical impact.

### Strengths
- The disantanglement evaluation (e.g., probing experiments) is convincing and shows that the resulting representations behave as intended.
- The approach is clearly described, easy to follow, and the method itself is straightforward to implement.
- The paper includes a number of interesting analyses, such as probing to identify information available at different layers and frame-level attribution.
- The experiments in Section 6 align with prior work on layer-wise properties of SSL speech models (Pasad et al., ICASSP 2023), reflecting the method’s effectiveness.
- The motivation for the problem is clearly stated, and the paper answers key questions about the extent of disentanglement and its benefits.
- Related work is appropriately cited, giving readers context for the contribution.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and motivation:**
  - The VIB technique itself is not new; the paper does not clearly identify what novel idea it introduces beyond applying an existing framework. (Reviewer 4)
  - The core motivation – that models should not use non-textual information for text-based decisions – is not convincingly established. A simpler baseline would be to train models directly on pure transcripts (e.g., ASR + text classifier). The paper does not compare against such a baseline or explain why disentangled representations are necessary when transcript-based training could suffice. (Reviewer 1)
  - The privacy preservation motivation (e.g., suppressing speaker identity) is mentioned in the abstract and conclusion but is never actually assessed in the model or experiments. (Reviewer 2)

- **Methodology and generality:**
  - The method assumes labeled tasks for all downstream applications; it trades general-purpose self-supervised representations for task-specific ones, limiting applicability to new unseen tasks. Transferability is unclear: would this approach require a “stage 3” training process for each new task? (Reviewers 1, 2)
  - The VIB approach may not be necessary at all: one could train separate ASR, speaker, and emotion classifiers on top of the raw SSL features and inspect their outputs. (Reviewer 1)
  - It is possible to combine multiple objectives (e.g., both emotion and speaker recognition) in stage 2, but the paper does not explore this or discuss the ordering of tasks. How important is the task order? (Reviewers 2, 3)

- **Experimental setup and results:**
  - **Data usage:**
    - LibriSpeech and CommonVoice are heavily subsampled: less than 20 hours from 960h of LibriSpeech. No strong justification is given. (Reviewer 2)
    - Non-standard splits of LibriSpeech are used (to ensure equal gender/speaker representation). Standard dev/test-{clean,other} splits should be used for comparability. (Reviewer 1)
    - WER in Table 1 is computed from a mixture of LibriSpeech and CommonVoice; these are very different datasets and should be reported separately. (Reviewer 1)
  - **Performance issues:**
    - In Table 1, VIB leads to substantial WER degradation for fine-tuned models (HuBERT-FT, Wav2Vec2-FT) compared to probing. This suggests the method discards crucial textual information, which is a major concern for ASR applications. (Reviewers 3, 4)
    - The impact on emotion and speaker identification is inconsistent: improvements are stronger for large models but regressions occur on base-sized wav2vec2 variants. This sensitivity to SSL objective and model size suggests the approach may not be robust. (Reviewer 2)
    - Using a single linear probing classifier on raw SSL features gives ~50% WER for W2V2/HuBERT; only pre-finetuned models achieve reasonable error rates. This raises concerns about the evaluation setup. (Reviewer 1)
    - The WER numbers reported seem higher than typical benchmarks; the specific LibriSpeech subset (clean/other) is not clarified. (Reviewer 3)
  - **Acoustic feature prediction:**
    - Figure 2 shows a strange behavior: prosody-related features (pitch, intensity, rhythm, voice quality) are removed in the “acoustic” representation, even though these are known to be key for emotion perception. This contradicts the intent. (Reviewer 4)

- **Missing comparisons and citations:**
  - No experiments compare the proposed method with existing speech representation disentanglement approaches such as AutoVC, SpeechSplit, or FAcodec. (Reviewer 3)
  - Prior IB-based disentanglement work (e.g., MINE, Deep Variational Information Bottleneck, Contrastive Predictive Coding) is not cited despite relevance. The paper should highlight distinctions. (Reviewer 3)
  - Section 6 largely reaffirms prior knowledge about layer-wise properties of SSL models without providing new insights for informed readers. (Reviewer 4)

- **Questions that remain unanswered:**
  - Why is Whisper mentioned in the introduction (as having highly entangled representations) but never evaluated? It is trained for transcription and may have very different properties from SSL models. (Reviewer 1)
  - Can the VIB approach handle multiple acoustic tasks (e.g., both speaker and emotion identification) in a single stage? (Reviewer 3)
  - Is the ordering of stage 1 (text) and stage 2 (acoustic) arbitrary, or would swapping them change results? (Reviewer 2)

- **Contrast:**
  - One reviewer (Reviewer 3, score 6) finds the paper well-written, the approach easy to use, and the experiments aligning with prior work. However, this positive view is a minority; other reviewers are consistently more critical, particularly regarding novelty, experimental rigor, and practical significance.
