## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a reward-robust RLHF framework using Bayesian Reward Model Ensembles (BRME) to combine a nominal reward with a conservative minimum reward for more stable PPO training. Reviewers agree the problem is important and the general idea intuitive. However, there is strong disagreement on execution: Reviewer 2 (score 1) finds the paper severely lacking in theoretical and experimental rigor with numerous unsubstantiated claims, while Reviewer 4 (score 6) considers the contribution original and well motivated, and Reviewers 1 and 3 (scores 5, 5) see merit but raise concerns about novelty, effect size, and clarity. Merged points of all reviewers are given below.

### Strengths

- Addresses an important problem: robustness to reward model uncertainty in RLHF. (All reviewers)
- Simple and intuitive idea that is easy to follow. (Reviewer 1)
- Provides theoretical justifications for the algorithm. (Reviewer 1; others dispute the depth – see Weaknesses)
- Thorough evaluations across diverse benchmarks showing consistent gains. (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3)
- Ablations demonstrate that the method leads to underestimated rewards and can reduce reward hacking. (Reviewer 1)
- The combination of robust optimization with RLHF is, to the reviewers’ knowledge, novel. (Reviewer 3)
- Motivation via toy model nicely illustrates that even with perfect preference data reward modeling can be imperfect. (Reviewer 4)
- BRME seems convenient enough that a practitioner would consider using it. (Reviewer 4)
- Appropriate ablation experiments were performed (e.g., reward over-scoring vs. under-scoring). (Reviewer 4)
- Presentation is clear with descriptive figures (minor grammatical issues aside). (Reviewer 4)
- Experimental details in the appendix are thorough, supporting reproducibility (though open-source code would be valuable). (Reviewer 4)

### Weaknesses

1. **Limited novelty vs. prior work** (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 4):  
   The difference from methods in [1] and [2] is small; the paper lacks a detailed comparison and experimental evidence against them. The combination of a conservative and nominal reward is standard in the robust RL literature, and the specific contribution of BRME architecture vs. simpler ensembles is not isolated. Reviewer 4 notes the ablation in Appendix C.4 does not directly compare BRME with a simpler ensemble; the individual contributions of the reward combination and the BRME architecture remain unclear.

2. **Small performance gains** (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 4):  
   Gains are modest (roughly 1%–3% per Reviewer 1; averaged 1.1% over benchmarks per Reviewer 4). Such small improvements could lie within error bars, which are not reported. The added complexity of training multiple RMs may not justify these margins.

3. **Lack of theoretical soundness** (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3):  
   The paper contains many unsubstantiated claims (e.g., “RLHF is a key pathway towards AGI”; “Reward-Robust RLHF approaches stability of constant reward setting”; “challenge of both overfitting and underfitting in reward models”). Reviewer 2 states that every such claim should be supported by citation, theory, or empirical evidence. The mathematical analysis (Section 6.2 “Stochastic case analysis”) is considered trivial (showing RL will not learn with constant reward) and its connection to the proposed method is unclear. Reviewer 3 agrees the math does not cross the bar for “theory.”

4. **Experimental protocol weaknesses**:  
   - **Toy example** (Reviewer 2): It artificially creates a situation where the reward model is hard to learn; more details about the setup are needed in the main paper.  
   - **Main experiment details** (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3):  
     *“800 steps of PPO”* is ambiguous – are these gradient steps or data points? Why were standard fixed-epoch runs not used? Why compare performance before the algorithm has seen all training data?  
     *“1000 steps of PPO”* is very small; performance can degrade before improving later.  
   - **MMLU evaluation** (Reviewer 3): It is unclear why benchmark accuracy on MMLU is predictive of reward overoptimization or uncertainty, especially since the feedback data are not from these benchmarks.  
   - **Fig 3** (Reviewer 3): “Difference in scores” – is it computed with respect to the base model before RLHF?  
   - **Stochastic case analysis** (Reviewer 3): Its purpose is not well explained; why would training with random reward functions provide insights about the method?

5. **Methodological complexity and missing ablations** (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 1):  
   The approach requires training multiple reward models: first a Bradley-Terry RM, then an ensemble with MSE loss. Why is the MSE loss necessary? Why not an ensemble of Bradley-Terry models? The “Bayesian” label is not justified – the model uses MLE with no explicit prior; the connection to Bayes should be clarified. The uncertainty set size “n” is never defined, and an ablation on different n is missing (would too large n make the method too conservative?). The λ hyperparameter requires additional tuning.

6. **Equation 8 and reward model architecture** (Reviewer 3):  
   It is unclear how the reward equals the “mean” plus action times variance (action is a vector of tokens, reward is a scalar). Where is the first reward model used? Does it output the mean? How is the standard deviation chosen? The description in the appendix (L791) is confusing.

7. **Writing and presentation issues** (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3):  
   The paper sometimes reads as an article rather than a research paper, with unsupported sweeping statements. Several minor errors (e.g., L37 “on a preference data”; L182 loss functions written without the average, making magnitudes dataset-size dependent). Claims about prior work are sometimes inaccurate (e.g., PEBBLe’s diversity constraints vs. overfitting/underfitting; Kwon et al.’s use of an LLM as RM for game-playing). Also, the claim that RLHF led to the breakthrough of o1 models is unsubstantiated.
