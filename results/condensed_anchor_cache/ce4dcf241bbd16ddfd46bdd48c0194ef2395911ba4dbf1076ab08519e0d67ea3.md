## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates LLM agent interactions in hierarchical social environments by simulating a prison guard–prisoner scenario (inspired by the Stanford Prison Experiment). Using a custom framework (zAImbardo), the authors conduct 2000 conversations across five LLMs to study persuasion and anti-social behavior. Only three of five models maintained consistent role-playing; persuasion success varied by goal, and anti-social behavior emerged consistently, especially with abusive guard personalities, and even with neutral personalities. The paper is bold and addresses an interesting and compelling research question, with a large number of experiments and comprehensive protocols. However, reviewers raise significant concerns about experimental design, definitions, generalizability, and the strength of claims given the specific role-playing context.

### Strengths
- The problem of understanding how LLM agents act in a role-playing setting with social hierarchy is interesting and compelling (Reviewer 1 S1, Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
- The experimental design is comprehensive, comprising 2000 conversations across multiple scenarios, with clear and well-documented protocols (Reviewer 1 S2, Reviewer 3).
- The evaluation is reliable: it uses multiple metrics for anti-social behavior assessment and maintains statistical rigor (e.g., Granger causality tests, OLS regression) (Reviewer 1 S3).

### Weaknesses
- **Model scope and scaling analysis missing:** The study is confined to open-source models, explicitly excluding major closed-source models such as GPT-4 and Claude-3. There is no systematic analysis of model scaling effects (Reviewer 1 W1, Reviewer 3: GPT-4/4 Turbo may show better role-playing performance).
- **Insufficient RLHF impact analysis:** The paper lacks a comparative analysis between RLHF and non-RLHF model versions to examine how alignment training affects persuasive capabilities and anti-social tendencies (Reviewer 1 W2).
- **Experimental design limitations affecting external validity:**
    - Fixed conversation length; no variable-length conversations to assess long-term interaction dynamics (Reviewer 1 W3, W4).
    - Restriction to one-on-one interactions (one guard, one prisoner) may not capture group effects present in the Stanford Prison Experiment (multiple participants, prominent player influences) (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 1 W3).
    - Limited personality types (only two per role plus a blank option) lack justification; a more systematic approach (e.g., Five-Factor Model / Big Five) would be preferable instead of simple SPE-relevant words like "abusive" and "rebellious" (Reviewer 1 W3, Reviewer 3).
    - The setup is role-playing; therefore, forms of "anti-social behavior" are expected and not necessarily problematic. The authors seem to imply that correct behavior should cancel negative aspects, which is not a desired feature of LLMs in narrative contexts (Reviewer 2).
- **Definitional ambiguities and conceptual issues:**
    - Persuasion is defined only as the prisoner achieving their goal (final outcome), not as a process. The outcome could result from threats or other means, not persuasion per se. A more rigorous definition (e.g., from economics/game theory) is needed (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
    - "Anti-social behavior" is a generic term; what constitutes "social behavior" in a prison context? (Reviewer 2)
    - It is unclear if agents can perform environmental actions beyond dialogue. For example, how does a prisoner "escape" by just mentioning it without interruption? No concrete examples are provided (Reviewer 3).
- **Prompt suggestiveness and training set contamination:**
    - Agents are explicitly informed about the Stanford Prison Experiment (Line 199), which may lead them to intentionally mimic behaviors based on prior knowledge rather than emergent simulation (Reviewer 3).
    - Information/scripts from the Stanford Prison Experiment may be in the training sets of the LLMs; this impact is not investigated (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
- **Evaluation and analysis gaps:**
    - No fine-tuning is performed to mitigate unexpected behaviors; the paper only tests dialogue generation in a specific role-playing scenario (Reviewer 2).
    - The applicability of the toxicity tool (toxiGen-RoBERTa) in the dialogue context is not justified (Reviewer 2).
    - No ablation studies on hyperparameters, personality choices, prompt components, or risk disclosure (Reviewer 1 W4).
    - Variance observed in Figure 2 is not explained (Reviewer 2).
    - The claim that "when the guard is abusive, toxicity is always higher; when the guard is respectful, toxicity remains consistently lower" is trivial given the prompts; the authors should consider an "abusive" prisoner role instead of just "rebellious" (Reviewer 2).
- **Ethical discussion insufficient:** The ethical implications are treated simply, omitting potential misuse and demographic/cultural biases (Reviewer 1 W5).
- **Minor issues:** Softer colors recommended for readability (Page 22); stray period at the beginning of Line 282 (Reviewer 3). A historical inaccuracy: the paper refers to ELIZA as module-based, which is incorrect (Reviewer 2).
- **Claims too strong:** The authors draw very general claims from a very specific role-playing context, overstating the contribution (Reviewer 2, also implied by Reviewer 3 on feasibility of persuasion in adversarial setup). The contribution is modest relative to the venue (Reviewer 2).
