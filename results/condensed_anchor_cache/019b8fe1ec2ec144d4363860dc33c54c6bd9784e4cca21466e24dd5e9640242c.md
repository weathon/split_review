## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies the effect of random edge-dropping techniques (DropEdge and its variants DropNode, DropAgg, DropGNN) on over-squashing in Message Passing Neural Networks (MPNNs). It provides theoretical results showing that DropEdge reduces sensitivity between distant nodes, thereby worsening over-squashing. Experiments on real-world datasets confirm that while these methods improve performance on short-range tasks, they degrade performance on long-range tasks. The authors attribute this to a reduced effective receptive field that misaligns models with long-range dependencies.

### Strengths
- **Motivation and clarity**: The motivation is clear and solid; the paper is well-written, well-presented, and easy to follow (Reviewers 1,3,4). It provides good literature review and context (Reviewer 1).
- **Comprehensive theoretical framework**: The paper offers a detailed mathematical understanding of different DropEdge variants and their effect on over-squashing (Reviewers 1,2). Assumptions are reasonable (Reviewer 4).
- **Fresh perspective**: Focuses on over-squashing, a less-explored limitation of edge-dropping techniques in GNNs (Reviewer 2). The empirical study on sensitivity is well-designed and makes sense (Reviewer 4).
- **Clear explanation of trade-off**: Shows why random edge-dropping improves short-range tasks but worsens long-range tasks, with experiments aligning well with theory and intuition (Reviewers 2,4).

### Weaknesses
- **Core claim is not surprising**: It is generally clear that removing edges decreases sensitivity; graph rewiring aims to add edges for better propagation. This trade-off is also known from the over-smoothing vs. over-squashing literature (e.g., [1] Revisiting Over-smoothing and Over-squashing Using Ollivier-Ricci Curvature, ICML 2023; [2] On the Trade-off between Over-smoothing and Over-squashing in Deep Graph Neural Networks, CIKM 2023). The authors are asked to comment on how their findings differ from these works and why the claim is not obvious (Reviewer 1).
- **Lack of quantitative rigor in theory**: Lemma 3.2 and Theorem 3.1 lack quantitative descriptions, making conclusions less compelling (Reviewer 3). The proof in Appendix A1 is not clear and needs more explanation (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing comparisons and methods**: The paper only discusses DropEdge and its variants, omitting Dropout, DropMessage (which is the most advanced graph random dropping method), and SkipNode. This limits applicability (Reviewers 2,3). No exploration of non-edge-dropping methods (Reviewer 3).
- **Insufficient experiments**: Limited to node classification tasks; missing link prediction and graph classification. Datasets on long-range interaction (LRI), trees match, and peptides are missing from experiments – these are standard for over-squashing evaluation (Reviewers 3,4). The experimental setup from reference [1] (presumably Long Range Graph Benchmark) should be followed (Reviewer 2). The Spearman correlation values in Table 2 may depend on the optimal dropping probability for each dataset rather than homogeneity; e.g., homophilic datasets PubMed and ogbn-arxiv have very small optimal dropping probabilities, so the claimed conclusions may not hold for them (Reviewer 3).
- **No proposed solution or guidance**: The paper only analyzes the phenomenon without discussing how to mitigate over-squashing for these methods. The mainstream solutions for over-squashing are graph rewiring or graph transformers; no one uses DropEdge for long-range tasks anyway, so the practical contribution is marginal (Reviewers 2,3,4). One reviewer (R1) finds the work well-written and thorough but agrees the contribution is limited without a stronger novelty argument.
- **Reproducibility**: Source codes and used datasets should be provided to facilitate reproducibility (Reviewer 2).
