## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes DeCap, a diversity-enhanced and classification-aware prompt learning method for few-shot classification using synthetic images from text-to-image models. Reviewer 1 (6/4) and Reviewer 3 (6/5) view the adaptive meta-learning of prompts as a valuable innovation that reduces manual prompt engineering and improves performance across different architectures. Reviewer 2 (3/5) finds the method easy to follow but questions its practical value, arguing that commercial or open-source LLMs would produce better prompts without the genetic algorithm. Reviewer 4 (3/5) sees novelty and good qualitative results but considers the experimental validation insufficient, noting that the method is expensive and the gains are marginal when combined with recent SOTA baselines.

### Strengths
* **Method novelty** (R1, R3, R4): Learning prompts adaptively for synthetic data generation is a novel approach, using a genetic algorithm for discrete outer-loop optimization and a meta-learning paradigm to align synthetic data with downstream classification tasks.
* **Systematic overview** (R3): The paper clearly compares and summarizes different prompt-based data curation methods for T2I models, making it a valuable reference.
* **Model-agnostic improvement** (R1, R3): DeCap consistently improves zero/few-shot methods across different network architectures, as shown in experimental results.
* **Qualitative results** (R4): The paper provides good qualitative visualizations of the generated images.

### Weaknesses
* ✅ **[RS-1] Insufficient dataset diversity and number** (R2, R4; also noted by R3 regarding generalization). Evaluated on very few datasets (only three datasets plus two subsets, according to R4), and the datasets are too similar (subject, granularity, modality). R4 explicitly states that to support the claims, the method should be tested on a minimum of 3–4 additional categories (e.g., FGVC Aircraft, Food101, EuroSAT, SUN397), and ideally more. R2 adds that the limited dataset selection makes it hard to judge the paper's contribution.
* ✅ **[RS-2] Evaluation metrics are too narrow** (R1). Only top-1 accuracy is reported; R1 argues that few-shot learning requires “n-shot n-way” scenarios and metrics like precision, recall, F1 score. The absence of these metrics restricts assessment of robustness (e.g., false positives/negatives). R1 explicitly asks for a holistic evaluation including these.
* ✅ **[RS-3] Efficiency and cost concerns** (R3, R4). R3 notes the meta-learning paradigm requires substantial sampling and the pipeline contains many uncontrolled components (e.g., high freedom in T2I generation, sensitivity to hyperparameters beyond prompts). R3 asks for a discussion of running time, robustness to generation seeds, and details on genetic algorithm parameters (generations, population size). R4 questions whether performance gains justify the cost, and requests a GPU-hour comparison with other baselines (LE, CiP, FakeIt, SuS-X, CaFo) and a breakdown of DeCap’s computational cost.
* ✅ **[RS-4] Weak generalization and domain shift** (R3). The genetic algorithm optimizes prompts using accuracy on few-shot data as fitness (Equation 2), which may overfit to those specific examples. R3 recommends evaluating on domain generalization datasets like ImageNet-R to verify out-of-domain robustness. Also, if few-shot examples are not representative (e.g., all in one style), the learned prompts may not generalize. 
* ✅ **[RS-5] Small gains on newer SOTA methods** (R4). Combining DeCap with SuS-X and CaFo yields <1% improvement on average, while the gain is larger only for FakeIt. R4 suggests two possible implications: (a) DeCap may be less effective when fine-tuning pre-trained models (vs. training from scratch in FakeIt), and (b) newer works (SuS-X, CaFo) may have already addressed the challenges DeCap targets.
* ✅ **[RS-6] Missing ablations on design choices** (R4). The paper lacks ablations on why meta learning is necessary vs. optimizing prompts directly, loss function choices, or choice of meta-learning algorithm.
* ✅ **[RS-7] Datasets used for ablations are weak** (R4). Ablations (e.g., number of prompts) are performed on STL-10, which R4 criticizes as a weak dataset (resolution mismatch: STL-10 96×96 vs. T2I 512×512). R4 also notes that CIFAR and STL are far out-of-distribution for the diffusion model’s output, further weakening the evidence.
* ✅ **[RS-8] Prompt pool adequacy and fine-grained detail** (R1). The paper does not clearly show how the prompt pool is ensured to be comprehensive enough or that the most contributive prompts are consistently selected across different iterations and datasets. R1 asks for examples of consistently selected or rejected prompts and visualizations of the selection process.
* ✅ **[RS-9] Strong skepticism from a minority reviewer** (R2). R2 argues that commercial/open-source LLMs would likely provide much better prompts than the examples in the paper, without needing the genetic algorithm. R2 explicitly states the authors are responsible for proving this assumption wrong, and the examples in the paper do not convince.
* ✅ **[RS-10] Missing recent data augmentation works** (R3). The paper does not discuss or compare with image-based data curation methods (e.g., Real-Guidance, Da-Fusion) that manipulate few-shot images directly, bypassing text prompts entirely. R3 asks for a discussion and comparison to image editing/style transfer approaches, and whether they could be combined.
* ✅ **[RS-11] Effect of varying shot numbers** (R3). All experiments use 10-shot (Section 2.2). R3 suspects the strategy may be ineffective in extreme low-shot settings (e.g., 1-shot) and asks for results across varying shot numbers.
