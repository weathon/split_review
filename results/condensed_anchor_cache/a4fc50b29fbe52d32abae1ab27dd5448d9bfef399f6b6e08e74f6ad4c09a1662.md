## Merged Review

### Summary

TSI-Bench is a benchmark suite for time series imputation (TSI) using deep learning. It standardizes experimental settings with three missingness patterns, evaluates 28 algorithms on 8 datasets (air quality, traffic, electricity, healthcare) across 34,804 experiments, and analyzes downstream tasks (forecasting, classification, regression). The pipeline also adapts forecasting algorithms for imputation via a masking mechanism. The majority of reviewers (three out of four) rate the paper 3/5, citing substantial contributions to benchmarking but major flaws in usability, method coverage, and depth of analysis. One reviewer (R4) gives 5/5 but criticizes the lack of novelty and unsurprising findings.

### Strengths

- **Standardized benchmarking framework:** The paper addresses a critical need for unified and systematic evaluation of TSI models. Provides a comprehensive overview of the state-of-the-art and enables fair comparisons (R1, R2, R3).
- **Extensive experiments:** Thoroughly covers 28 methods, 8 datasets, multiple missing rates and patterns, and downstream tasks. Full result tables in appendix increase credibility (R2, R4).
- **Insightful analysis:** Results and analyses demonstrate the platform’s value and reveal meaningful insights (R1, R4).
- **Well-written and well-presented:** Clear presentation with comprehensive coverage of existing methods (R1, R4).

### Weaknesses

1. **Missing recent/imputation-specific methods:** Multiple reviewers note that generative models, e.g., VAEs, GANs, diffusion models, and other recent works like mTANs, TimeCIB (Choi & Lee, ICLR), GRIN (Cini et al., ICLR), DSPD-GP, TIDER, BiTGraph, and probabilistic imputation methods (e.g., Kim et al., ICML 2023; Alcaraz & Strodthoff, TMLR 2024) are absent. A benchmark should include a much more comprehensive comparison of modern methods (R2, R3). R2 provides specific references: [1]-[6]; R3 provides [A]-[C].

2. **Lack of hyperparameter and method-specific settings:** For deep learning methods, the paper does not provide hyperparameters or method-specific configurations (e.g., GP kernels for GP-based models, noise schedule / number of timesteps for diffusion models). Without evidence that models were (sub)optimally tuned for each dataset, it is hard to trust performance comparisons. R2 questions whether modern methods could beat older ones (e.g., BRITS is still top) if fully optimized. (R2, R3)

3. **Unsatisfactory platform usability and documentation:** The code is not user-friendly:
   - No documentation (only inline comments).
   - Missing‑rate settings are hard‑coded; users must manually edit code.
   - Algorithm list is hard‑coded; adding a new method requires modifying multiple files.
   - No clear specification of expected input/output formats for algorithms.
   - Integration of a new dataset is very challenging.
   The platform currently only supports replicating the authors’ experiments, not broad dissemination. (R1 strong criticism; this was the main reason for R1’s rating.)

4. **No inclusion of static information:** The platform does not handle static attributes (e.g., spatial coordinates for sensor networks, descriptive metadata), which are common in real‑world TSI and could improve model performance. (R1)

5. **Unnatural adaptation of forecasting methods for imputation:** Forecasting architectures are adapted by simply replacing the output layer with a masking mechanism (like masked language modeling). This does not truly reflect the core purpose of those forecasting models and may not be the most effective way to use them for imputation. (R3)

6. **Insufficient depth of downstream analysis:** The benchmark only tests one downstream model (XGBoost). For a robust evaluation, it should compare performance of downstream tasks with and without imputation across multiple types of downstream models. (R3)

7. **Limited missingness scenarios:** Missingness assumptions are restricted; the benchmark should include Missing Not At Random (MNAR), Missing Completely At Random (MCAR), and periodic missingness to assess how methods perform under different conditions. (R3) R3 asks how pre‑existing missing values in the original datasets were handled during training/testing.

8. **Lack of novel contributions or surprising findings:** The paper is better suited for a journal review. All methods and datasets are existing, and reported findings are unsurprising: (i) different missing patterns and rates influence performance; (ii) forecasting architectures can serve as imputation backbones; (iii) imputation helps downstream tasks. No novel methodological or analytical insight is provided. (R4, at odds with the other reviewers who found the analysis insightful; R4 is the minority with this stronger negativity.)

9. **Author name in supplementary code:** The submission is not fully anonymized; an author’s name appears in the code (e.g., `global_config.py`). (R1)

**Questions from reviewers overlapping with weaknesses:**  
- How were hyperparameters/method-specific settings chosen? Are optimal configurations provided for new datasets? (R2 Q2)  
- Why does BRITS outperform modern methods? Is it simply due to incomplete optimization of newer models? (R2 Q3)  
- How were pre-existing missing values in datasets handled during training/testing? (R3 Q2)
