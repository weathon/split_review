## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Dropsembles, an uncertainty quantification method for fine-tuned neural implicit functions (e.g., NeRFs, occupancy networks, SDFs). It combines Monte Carlo dropout with ensembling and uses Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) during fine-tuning to mitigate distribution shifts. Experiments on toy examples (sinusoidal decision boundary, MNIST) and a real-world lumbar spine MRI segmentation task show that Dropsembles maintains accuracy and calibration similar to deep ensembles while reducing training costs. The method is evaluated on a Convolutional Occupancy Network trained on synthetic anatomical data and fine-tuned on sparse, noisy MRI segmentations.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses an underexplored area: modeling epistemic uncertainty in fine-tuned neural implicit functions, particularly for decoder fine-tuning in cohort-based training. It is the first work to tackle this scenario. (R1, R2, R4; noted as "meaningful exploratory study" by R3)
- Dropsembles combines dropout and ensembling in a computationally efficient manner, achieving reduced training computational costs compared to deep ensembles without sacrificing accuracy or calibration. (R2, R3)
- The integration of Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) effectively regularizes fine-tuning to preserve prior knowledge and handle distribution shifts from synthetic to real data, enhancing robustness to noisy and sparse inputs. (R1, R2)
- The experimental evaluation is rigorous across synthetic and real-world spine datasets, with clear design, well-explained baselines (MC dropout, deep ensembles, deep ensembles + EWC), and thorough ablation studies (e.g., number of ensemble members, dropout rates, EWC strength). The paper is clearly written, well-structured, and places the method in context of related work. Code is provided for reproducibility. (R2, R3; R1 notes toy experiments are included)
- The approach is straightforward and versatile; it can be integrated into various neural implicit networks and adapted to different task-specific objectives. The strategy of leveraging synthetic data as shape priors while using EWC to address distribution shifts is pragmatic for scarce/noisy real-world data. (R2; related point by R4: “interesting topic” for densifying sparse shapes)
- **Reviewer contrast**: Reviewer 3 (score 8) emphasizes originality of the application despite using established components, strong clarity, and meaningful significance. Reviewer 4 (score 3) acknowledges the topic and “first work” aspect but is critical of novelty and validation.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty of Dropsembles**: The method combines dropout and ensembling in a generic way without specific adaptations to neural implicit representations (e.g., leveraging continuous nature, spatial encoding, grid-based predictions). The contribution over standard MC-dropout ensembles is not clearly detailed. (R1, R4; R4 requests “more detailed description on its contribution”)
- **Questionable motivation and effectiveness of EWC**: The dense and sparse datasets (Tasks A and B) involve the same underlying anatomical structures, which does not align with typical continual learning use cases. The improvement from EWC is marginal and not consistently significant across all metrics. The paper does not analyze when EWC provides minimal benefit or clarify the specific distribution shift being addressed. (R1, R2, R4)
- **Insufficient baseline comparisons**: The paper does not compare to Bayesian neural networks, variational inference, or established uncertainty methods for implicit representations like Stochastic NeRF (3DV 2021). This limits evidence for Dropsembles’ relative effectiveness. (R1)
- **Missing quantitative computational cost analysis**: Despite claiming tradeoffs between computational cost and accuracy, no quantitative runtime or resource comparison is provided against baselines, nor analysis of scaling with input resolution or dataset size. Both training and inference costs (latter comparable to deep ensembles) are not fully characterized. (R2, R4; R4 explicitly asks for comparison)
- **Limited experimental scope and dataset diversity**: Experiments focus solely on synthetic anatomical data and lumbar spine MRI segmentation. Broader evaluation is missing on:
  - Other tasks such as novel view synthesis, scene completion, or 3D reconstruction on general object datasets (e.g., ShapeNet, MedShapeNet). (R2, R3, R4)
  - Varying object topologies and complexities. (R2)
  - Different activation functions (e.g., SIREN) beyond ReLU; this ablation would be simple yet relevant for implicit representations. (R3)
  - Signed Distance Functions (SDFs) in addition to occupancy networks, though the method claims relevance to both. (R3)
  - Real-world data corruption beyond simulated noise and erosion (e.g., artifacts, occlusions, non-uniform noise). (R2)
- **Unaddressed spatial coherence**: The method does not incorporate mechanisms to maintain spatial consistency in uncertainty estimates (e.g., as in Stochastic Segmentation Networks, NeurIPS 2021), which is important for grid/voxel predictions in neural implicit representations. (R1 question, also context from R1’s reference)
- **Missing discussion on applicability to auto-decoder settings**: Auto-decoder frameworks (e.g., DeepSDF) are common but compatibility with Dropsembles is not discussed; there may be theoretical limitations. (R3 question)
- **Inference efficiency not optimized**: While training costs are reduced, inference costs remain comparable to deep ensembles. The paper does not propose strategies to reduce inference overhead. (R2)
- **Related work gap**: The paper should discuss more recent encoder-decoder architectures for cohort-based training using weight modulation (e.g., Wang et al. 2023, Zheng et al. 2022) to strengthen relevance. (R3)
- **Minor issue**: The term “thinned network” is nonstandard and could cause confusion. (R1)
