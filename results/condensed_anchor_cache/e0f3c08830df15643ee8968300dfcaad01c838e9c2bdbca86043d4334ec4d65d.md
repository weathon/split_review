## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper investigates the benefits of maintaining and updating edge embeddings in graph neural networks (GNNs). The authors provide theoretical separation results showing that under memory constraints, edge-based architectures can solve certain tasks with fewer layers than node-based counterparts. Empirically, edge-based models often outperform node-based models, especially on graphs with hub nodes. Reviewers acknowledge the novelty and theoretical rigor (especially the depth/memory trade-off analysis and connection to the LOCAL framework) but raise significant concerns about the practicality of the assumptions, limited empirical validation, and presentation clarity. Scores: 6, 5, 5, 5; one reviewer (score 6, confidence 2) is more positive overall, while others (scores 5, confidence 3–4) are more critical of proof fairness, experimental scope, and writing organization.

### Strengths

1. **Novel theoretical perspective** – The paper provides a fresh analysis of edge-vs-node processing in GNNs, bridging the LOCAL framework and offering depth/memory separation results that are not commonly studied in the graph machine learning field. (Reviewers 2, 3)
2. **Theoretical rigor** – Definitions, theorems, and proofs are well-structured and logically sound; the depth separation between node and edge message-passing under memory constraints is a solid contribution. (Reviewers 1, 2)
3. **Empirical demonstration of edge embedding benefits** – Experiments show that edge-based models outperform node-based counterparts in synthetic and real-world tasks, particularly in graphs with hub nodes. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)
4. **Equivalence result (Section 7)** – When memory constraints are relaxed, the two frameworks become equivalent up to an extra round of message passing, which may open new research directions. (Reviewer 3)
5. **Systematic analysis** – The paper provides a clear, well-structured investigation of edge-based GNNs, which have been under-investigated in prior literature. (Reviewers 1, 4)
6. **Accessible writing** – Despite being theory-heavy, the writing is generally clear and helps readers grasp the main ideas. (Reviewers 2, 4)

### Weaknesses

1. **Memory assumptions unrealistic for theoretical results** – The memory constraints assumed in Sections 2–6 (e.g., constant memory per embedding) are not representative of real-world settings, raising questions about the practical relevance of the depth separation findings. (Reviewers 3, 4) Specifically, the proof of Theorem 1 assumes a graph with O(N) edges (constant degree) but then uses a √n‑path graph where a hub node has degree O(n), violating the assumption. This leads to an unfair memory comparison: edge-based models use O(N) memory for the hub while node-based models are limited to constant memory, making the separation result trivial. (Reviewer 4)
2. **Limited empirical validation** – Experiments are restricted to a small set of benchmarks (graph classification/regression and image classification) and synthetic tasks. Missing are link prediction tasks, which are a natural fit for edge embeddings. Broader real-world applications and more diverse datasets are needed to substantiate the claimed advantages. (Reviewers 2, 3) Additionally, the improvement over node-based models is often marginal, questioning practical value given the extra memory cost. (Reviewer 4)
3. **Lack of fair memory/performance comparison between architectures** – The paper does not equalize total memory used by node-based and edge-based models in experiments (e.g., N * d_n = E * d_e per layer). Without such a controlled comparison, it is unclear whether the performance gains are simply due to using more parameters. (Reviewer 4) Also missing is an experiment that increases depth of node-based models to simulate edge embeddings and compares memory consumption. (Reviewer 1)
4. **Insufficient practical guidance** – The paper offers little guidance on which types of graphs (beyond hub nodes) benefit most from edge embeddings, such as density, structural motifs, or task characteristics. (Reviewers 1, 2) Similarly, the practical implications of symmetry constraints (Theorem 5) are not connected to actionable design choices. (Reviewer 2)
5. **Missing comparisons with hybrid or recent architectures** – The study only contrasts pure node-based vs. edge-based models, omitting hybrid approaches (e.g., combining node and edge features, attention on edges) that might compete or complement the edge-based paradigm. (Reviewer 2)
6. **Presentation and organization issues** – The paper is difficult to follow; the organization reflects the authors’ thought process rather than a coherent narrative. Information is duplicated (e.g., O(1) vs O(log n) statements in lines 137–138 and 381–382) and proof sketches take up excessive space in the main text. Notation is verbose, and many empirical details (hyperparameters, evaluation protocols) are missing, hurting reproducibility. (Reviewer 3)
7. **Overstatements about expressiveness** – The paper claims at different points that edge-based models are “more expressive,” “at least as expressive,” and then “equivalent” when memory constraints are relaxed. This inconsistency should be resolved, and claims about expressiveness that cannot be proven should be avoided. (Reviewer 3)
8. **Scalability and computational overhead not adequately addressed** – For dense graphs, maintaining edge embeddings requires quadratic memory in the number of nodes, and computational costs scale with the number of edges. The paper acknowledges this but does not provide detailed analysis or mitigation strategies (e.g., sparse approximations, downsampling). (Reviewers 1, 2)

*Note on reviewer disagreement:* One reviewer (score 6, confidence 2) finds the theoretical results and empirical cases compelling, while others (score 5, confidence 3–4) question the fairness of the theoretical assumptions and the strength of the empirical evidence. The highest-confidence reviewer (Reviewer 2, confidence 4) highlights the need for broader experiments and deeper discussion of scalability. The presentation is judged weak by Reviewer 3 (score 1) but acceptable by others (score 3).
