## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses deep heteroscedastic regression, where both mean and covariance of a Gaussian target are modeled per input. Since ground-truth covariances are unknown, the authors propose a self-supervised approach: pseudo labels for the covariance are derived from a local neighborhood (k-NN using Mahalanobis distance). Two losses are analyzed for fitting these pseudo labels—KL divergence and 2-Wasserstein distance—and a stable-to-optimize upper bound for the 2-Wasserstein distance is derived. Experiments on synthetic and real datasets (including UCI and image-based tasks) indicate that the method performs competitively with prior work while being computationally cheaper. The paper is generally well written and combines theoretical analysis with empirical evaluation.

### Strengths
- **S1 (R1)** A different approach from much of the recent literature in heteroscedastic regression.
- **S2 (R1)** Good balance of theoretical and empirical support/motivation for the method.
- **S3 (R1)** Code/resources will be provided to reproduce results.
- **S4 (R2)** The problem of accurate and efficient covariance estimation is relevant.
- **S5 (R2)** The idea of using the neighbourhood of a given observation to generate a signal for covariance supervision is sensible and more computationally efficient than the prior TIC-TAC framework (Shukla et al., ICML 2024).
- **S6 (R2)** Empirical results show improvement over alternative baselines.
- **S7 (R2)** Paper is well-structured and easy to follow.
- **S8 (R3)** Properly modelling heteroscedastic random variables is an important and often overlooked problem.
- **S9 (R3)** The paper is generally well written and contains enough explanations and intuitions.
- **S10 (R3)** Experiments are convincing and support the claims.
- **S11 (R3)** Arguments and derivations preceding the proposed methods are clear and convincing.
- **S12 (R4)** Mathematical derivations are generally correct and sound; graphics are informative and well readable.
- **S13 (R4)** Results on synthetic and real datasets look convincing.

### Weaknesses
- **W1 (R2)** The formulation of Claim 1 is confusing: if the true covariance is assumed known, it does not make sense to estimate it; the authors should use "Lemma" instead of "Claim".
- **W2 (R4, supported by R3)** The problem setup is not clearly stated. The paper should explicitly specify the terminology (e.g., what is the "target"?), what data are given, what is being estimated, and the precise assumptions on the distribution. The dependence of the covariance on the input \(x_i\) (i.e., \(\Sigma_y(x_i)\)) should be made prominent from the start (R3 W1). Reference Seitzer et al. (2022) is a good example of how to set the stage (R4).
- **W3 (R4, also R3 Q about Eq.3)** The mathematical derivations contain minor mistakes and lack explicit justification. Specifically, in the proof of Claim 1, check whether equation (2) is correct: the \(\ln(\cdot)\) term may be outside the square brackets, and the factor \(1/2\) changes the conclusion (R4). The transition from the original Wasserstein distance to the simplified form using trace and matrix square root in Eq. 3 needs more justification; the assumption that the means are equal must be explicitly stated and its implications discussed (R3 W1).
- **W4 (R3 W2)** The derivations are limited to Gaussian predictions—this restriction should be clearly stated in the abstract and introduction. The limitations of this assumption and how the method might generalize to non-Gaussian distributions should be discussed, especially in contexts where non-Gaussian noise is prevalent.
- **W5 (R1)** The "Related Works" section is nearly identical to that of Shukla et al. (2024). Additionally, recent relevant literature is missing: "Optimal training of Mean Variance Estimation neural networks" (Neurocomputing 2024) and "Understanding Pathologies of Deep Heteroskedastic Regression" (OpenReview).
- **W6 (R1, echoed by R2's question)** The implementation and training procedure are unclear. Questions: Is a warmup period needed for the mean estimate? Are mean and covariance learned together from the start? Can pseudo labels be incorporated into other existing methods for fitting heteroscedastic regression? Would the pseudo labels still work well when the mean model misfits (overfit/underfit)? Are there situations where the covariance conditional on the mean becomes nonsensical? (R1)
- **W7 (R2)** The UCI regression comparison is not exhaustive. The state-of-the-art method from [4] (TIC-TAC) is not reported on the UCI benchmark. Moreover, the NLL values obtained by the authors differ substantially from previous works but are similar to those in [1]—this suggests the datasets were adapted (e.g., rescaled to have variance of ten). The authors should report results on the original UCI regression dataset as used in many previous works, to enable transparent comparison.
- **W8 (R2)** The paper advertises computational efficiency compared to [1], but it is unclear whether hyperparameters (e.g., weight decay) were grid-searched. The total compute time should be compared with other state-of-the-art methods like [4] that use automatic regularization via Empirical Bayes and thus avoid grid-search.
- **W9 (R2)** It is not reported whether different values of \(\beta\) were tried for the \(\beta\)-NLL objective. Which value is chosen?
- **W10 (R2)** The main text reports MSE while NLL is relegated to the appendix; in heteroscedastic regression, NLL is often more indicative of overall performance.
- **W11 (R3 W3)** Experiments are repeated 5 times but only mean values are reported, without standard deviations. This prevents assessing statistical significance and robustness of the method.
- **W12 (R3 W4)** The last experiment uses a hybrid approach combining three different methods (including the TIC parametrization). The authors should ablate by dropping the TIC parametrization to isolate the contribution of the proposed method. Since speed and low memory are selling points, the time/memory penalty introduced by the TIC parametrization should be reported. A direct comparison with the full TIC-TAC method in this experiment is necessary. Ideally, the proposed method should be applied with pseudo-labels taken directly from the input images (rather than combined with other components).
- **W13 (R3 W5)** Some figures are hard to read because the y-axis spans several orders of magnitude; the authors should consider using a log scale.
- **W14 (R1, R2, R4 – presentation issues)** Several presentation details need improvement: citation format errors (missing parentheses on lines 152, 153 – R2); inconsistent notation for KL divergence (KL vs. \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\) – R4); the "forward KL divergence" is not defined (R4); the appendix contains typos (e.g., capitalizing "univariate" – R4). Some implementation details are unclear: How does the method relate to kernel methods or normalizing flows? (R1, R4)
- **W15 (R3 Q1/Q4)** The advantage of the 2-Wasserstein bound over KL is not fully justified. Are the authors parametrizing \(\Sigma^{1/2}\) directly to avoid computing the matrix square root? The TIC parametrization is not explained. Memory consumption values reported seem large; what do they refer to?
- **W16 (R3 Q3)** The claimed instabilities (line 482) are not apparent from the results shown; the authors should clarify where they occur.
- **W17 (R3 Q5)** The claim in lines 210–212 is not clear from the results; further clarification is needed.

**Reviewer disagreement note:** Reviewer 2 (score 5) is more critical of the comparisons, efficiency claims, and clarity of the setup. Reviewer 3 (score 8) is more positive but still raises significant concerns about experimental rigor (lack of std dev, hybrid approach) and scope (Gaussian-only). Reviewer 4 (score 6) focuses on formulation and mathematical correctness, while Reviewer 1 (score 6) points out missing related work and implementation ambiguity. All reviewers agree that the problem is relevant and the approach has merit, but the experimental and exposition gaps weaken the current submission.
