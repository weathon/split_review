## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a transformer-based end-to-end model for physiological signal emotion recognition, framing it as a multivariate time series prediction task. It uses wavelet and Fourier attention to decompose signals into time and frequency representations and a Two-Stage Attention (TSA) mechanism to capture channel dependencies. Multitask learning predicts valence and arousal. The model achieved first place in all four tracks (Across-time, Across-subject, Across-elicitor, Across-version) of the EPiC Challenge on the CASE dataset. The architecture builds upon FED-Former with TSA as the core innovation.

### Strengths
1. **State-of-the-art performance**: The method outperformed all EPiC Challenge participants in all four test scenarios on the CASE dataset (R2, R3). R3 considered this a significant contribution.
2. **Effective use of attention mechanisms**: The approach utilizes wavelet and Fourier attention to separately model time and frequency domain representations, and introduces Two-Stage Attention (TSA) to capture cross-time and cross-dimension dependencies among physiological signals (R1, R3). R3 found TSA innovative and efficient and described the solution as simple yet effective.
3. **End-to-end modeling avoids feature engineering**: The model addresses long-term temporal dependencies in an end-to-end manner, without requiring complex handcrafted features (R3).
4. **Potential practical utility**: Performance on the challenge suggests practical applicability for emotion recognition from physiological data (R3).

*Note on reviewer disagreement:* While reviewers R1 and R2 rated contribution as poor (1 out of 4), R3 initially rated contribution as good (3). After rebuttal, R3 decreased their overall rating due to limited originality and insufficient ablation, aligning more closely with the other reviewers on the weaknesses.

### Weaknesses
1. **Lack of ablation studies and missing justification for architectural choices**: The paper does not systematically ablate the contribution of each component (wavelet attention, Fourier attention, TSA, encoder/decoder configurations) (R1, R3). The replacement of standard MSAs in the encoder and the addition of frequency/time TSA in decoders lack empirical evidence and clear rationale (R1). R2 also requested an ablation study on which physiological signals contribute most to performance. Without such studies, it is difficult to assess the true contribution of each element.
2. **Insufficient comparison with existing methods**: The paper only compares against EPiC Challenge participants and lacks comparison with established time-series models or other physiological signal emotion recognition methods (R2, R3). The model’s generalizability is not tested on other affective datasets (R2). The value over simple machine learning classification models (e.g., those avoiding complex feature engineering) is not demonstrated (R1, R3). The related work section does not sufficiently discuss or benchmark against state-of-the-art beyond the challenge leaderboard (R1).
3. **Limited novelty and contribution**: The paper’s core innovation (TSA) is grafted onto the existing FED-Former framework, and the overall structure closely resembles it (R3). Two reviewers (R1, R2) found it difficult to identify clear contributions beyond fitting an existing model to a new dataset. R2 noted the lack of contribution to the “applications to neuroscience & cognitive science” area despite the submission. R1 questioned why alternative architectures were not explored. R2 asked for clarification on how the relationship between physiological signals and affective changes is captured by the model.
4. **Lack of interpretability**: The paper does not explain why the proposed method outperforms other participants or what physiological signals/features drive predictions (R2). The meaning of terms such as “Scenario level” in Table 1 is ambiguous (R2). The relationship between input signals and affective states is not clearly explained (R2).
5. **Presentation and clarity issues**: The paper includes overly detailed formulas for standard attention mechanisms, reducing readability (R1). There is a lack of illustrations and visual aids to convey the architecture (R3). Notation is sometimes unintuitive, and the explanation of sequence length and DSW embedding is unclear (R3). The methodology section describes structure without justifying the sources and rationale behind the ideas (R3). Reproducibility is hindered by missing hyperparameter details in experiment settings (R3).

*Note on reviewer disagreement:* Although R3 initially rated the paper more positively (strength in originality and contribution), they ultimately agreed with the other reviewers on the key weaknesses—limited originality and insufficient ablation studies—and revised their rating downward. This suggests a strong consensus across all reviewers on the need for more comprehensive evaluation and clearer novelty.
