## Merged Review

### Summary
Swift4D is a divide-and-conquer 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) method for dynamic scene reconstruction. It separates static and dynamic primitives using a learnable decomposition strategy with a 2D dynamic-static pixel mask computed from temporal variance. Dynamic primitives are modeled via a compact multi-resolution 4D Hash grid that transforms Gaussians from canonical space to deformation space at each timestamp. The method achieves state-of-the-art rendering quality, 20× faster training, and minimal storage (~30 MB) on real-world datasets (N3DV, MeetRoom, Basketball court). It is not an on-the-fly method and cannot handle single-view videos like DNeRF.

### Strengths
- Clear writing with detailed methodology and experiments.
- Static/dynamic decomposition reduces learning burden, storage, and training time while maintaining quality; the idea is simple, intuitive, and effective.
- Quantitative improvements over baselines in PSNR and training time, as shown in the tables.
- Innovative iterative pruning strategy (Section 3.4) that identifies isolated Gaussians (recognized by only a few views) as floating artifact sources, addressing overfitting – this insight was called valuable by one reviewer.
- First use of Gaussian splatting for explicit scene decomposition via a Gaussian attribute and alpha blending, according to one reviewer.
- Motivation is straightforward and reasonable; the explicit structure of 3DGS aids understanding.
- One reviewer (who gave a higher overall rating) particularly praised the pruning strategy as both innovative and insightful, and noted that the static/dynamic decomposition idea appropriately cites concurrent (GauFRe) and published (SC‑GS) works.

### Weaknesses
- **Dynamic decomposition via 2D temporal variance mask is problematic**:  
  * Camera motion changes pixel intensity, so standard deviation alone is a poor criterion for separating static vs. dynamic elements; unclear how the method handles strong camera motion.  
  * Textureless regions are often misclassified as static (e.g., part of cloth in sear_steak_static result in supplementary).  
  * Variance computed over the entire time sequence causes problems for long videos; the approach is sensitive to the chosen time window – short windows miss slow dynamics, long windows misclassify illumination changes.  
  * Occlusions are not accounted for, leading to inaccurate masks when dynamic objects move behind static objects.  
  * The method cannot distinguish specular reflections from actual motion.  
- **Scope and applicability limitations**:  
  * Cannot handle single-view videos (like DNeRF); offline training prevents real-time adaptation to new frames.  
  * Comparison with on-the-fly methods (e.g., 3DGStream) is arguably unfair due to different inference protocols.  
- **Sensitivity of dynamic threshold**: Fig. 7 shows that the dynamic level parameter strongly affects PSNR; no principled way to determine the optimal threshold is given, raising concerns about generalizability to scenes with different static/dynamic ratios or complex motion patterns.  
- **Insufficient comparisons with closely related methods**:  
  * Missing quantitative or qualitative comparisons with SC‑GS (Huang et al., CVPR 2024), Deformable 3DGS (Yang et al., CVPR 2024), and Gaussian-flow (Lin et al., CVPR 2024) – one reviewer (who gave a higher score) specifically called for at least comparisons with SC‑GS and Gaussian-flow.  
  * No evaluation on multi-view datasets like D‑NeRF or VRU (the VRU dataset is mentioned but no quantitative results are reported).  
  * Static/dynamic decomposition works by Liang et al. (2023) and He et al. (2024) are only discussed qualitatively, not compared experimentally.  
- **Experimental reporting gaps**:  
  * FPS of Swift4DLite is missing from Table 1.  
  * Reported training time for SpaceTimeGS in Table 1 is too long (should be under an hour); reported FPS for SpaceTimeGS is lower than expected (~130+).  
- **Artifacts in supplementary videos**:  
  * 2_baskball2.mp4 shows players vanishing from frame to frame, indicating temporal inconsistencies.  
  * VRU video results show abrupt scene changes due to segmentation processing, which should be explained.  
- **Missing relation to existing pruning work**: The temporal pruning strategy (Section 3.4) should be discussed in connection with prior pruning methods, e.g., LightGaussians (Fan et al., NeurIPS 2024).  
- **Scope and limitations not clearly defined**: one reviewer noted that the paper does not clearly state the scope and limitations of the proposed method.
