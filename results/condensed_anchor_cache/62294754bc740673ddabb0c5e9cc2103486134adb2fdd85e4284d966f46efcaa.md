## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a shield defense system to enhance the robustness of graph prompt tuning against adversarial attacks in GNNs. It identifies a vulnerability in current prompt-based methods to adversarial alterations and introduces two strategies: Direct Handling (customizing multi-defense prompts based on node-specific attributes, e.g., low-degree nodes, nodes with low central similarity within multi-hop subgraphs, nodes linked to adversarially perturbed edges) and Indirect Amplification (few-shot learning that focuses on untainted information). Three distinct graph prompts are fine-tuned with objectives of smoothness within and across prompt embeddings and consistency with unprompted embeddings. Theoretical feasibility is provided. Experiments in few-shot scenarios under various adversarial attacks (MetaAttack, Heuristic attack, Random attack, DICE) demonstrate enhanced resilience, with feature-based prompts enabling real-time adjustments.

### Strengths
- Technical innovation: the mixed multi-layer defense strategy combines feature-based prompting and real-time adjustments, offering a fresh perspective on graph prompt tuning robustness.
- Idea and structure are easy to follow.
- Interesting and timely investigation—the method offers a solution to suppress the impact of graph poisoning when the test graph structure is intentionally manipulated.

### Weaknesses
- Missing comparative analysis with existing defense strategies against adversarial attacks (R1).
- Evaluation is limited to node classification; the paper does not discuss adaptation to edge- or graph-level learning tasks or node-attribute manipulation attacks. This is insufficient given that graph prompt learning is a multi-task system (R3). The authors should extend coverage to more graph datasets and GNN learning tasks, e.g., as in [1] (R3).
- Practical applicability is not assessed—no real-world implementation or case studies (R1). Future work should include real-world scenarios.
- The evaluation metrics are incomplete; computational efficiency, scalability, and user experience are missing (R1).
- Generalizability to different attack types or diverse datasets is not discussed; the paper could elaborate on how the approach adapts to a broader range of security challenges (R1).
- Tables (e.g., Table 1, where “Pre-Training Strategies & Prompts” appear simultaneously) are confusing; a better design or alternative charts is needed (R2).
- Section 2 (Related Work) should be restructured—subsection 2.1 is unnecessary (R2).
- The whole framework lacks a figure showing the stages of prompt design and robust optimization strategies (R2). A figure would clarify the process (R2, question 2).
- In 10-shot experiments, other methods’ results approach those of the proposed method, raising the question of whether other methods could outperform with even more shots (R2, question 1). This suggests the advantage may be limited.
- The theoretical proof in Section 4.3 is not clearly linked to the main claim of robust prompt tuning; it should explain how the proposed approach improves robustness, and the connection to the algorithmic design is unclear (R3).
- The ablation study should consider the case where all three types of nodes (low degree, low central similarity, out-of-distribution) receive the same graph prompting function. Also, it is not clear whether certain node types should be weighted more during robust tuning or handled equally (R3).
- “Out-of-distribution nodes in a graph” are not adequately defined; citing (Li et al.) is insufficient. More discussion of what these nodes are and why they are relevant in adversarial attacks is needed (R3).
- Threshold values τ_degree, τ_sim, and τ_tune (Eq.15) are chosen without justification—whether empirically or via an adaptive protocol. Excluding subgraphs whose embeddings meet the condition in Eq.15 may harm utility by discarding training information (R3).
- The method is an adversarial training approach that memorizes one attack type; transferability across attacks is not demonstrated. It is unknown if training with one attack (MetaAttack, Heuristic, Random, DICE) confers resilience to the other three (R3).
