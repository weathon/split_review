## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes BaDLoss, a backdoor detection method that uses loss trajectories (per-epoch loss vectors) to identify backdoored training samples. It constructs a probe set of clean, randomly-labeled, and backdoor-injected samples, trains a k-NN detector on loss trajectories, then filters suspicious samples and retrains. Evaluated on patch, blend, warping, and multi-attack scenarios, it shows moderate success but has significant limitations.

### Strengths
- Most reviewers found the use of loss trajectories for backdoor detection novel and interesting; one reviewer noted the motivation is well-explained and the approach is technically sound.
- Detecting multi-trigger / concurrent backdoor attacks is a realistic and scalable direction.
- The detector is easy to train and works reasonably well on several attacks.
- Compared with a range of existing defenses (NC, AC, SS, FA, ABL, etc.).
- Overall performance appears to outperform these baselines in some settings.
- Addresses the important problem of dataset backdoor attacks.

- *Contrast:* One reviewer (R1) considered using loss to detect backdoors not novel, citing similar works: Li et al. "Anti-backdoor learning" (NeurIPS 2021) and Guan et al. "Few-shot backdoor defense using Shapley estimation" (CVPR 2022).

### Weaknesses
- **Method clarity & presentation:** Section 3 is hard to follow—no algorithm, pseudocode, or figure formalizes how loss trajectories are computed and used. The construction of the 500 bonafide, 250 randomized-label, and 250 backdoored probes is unclear; in particular, which specific backdoor trigger/feature is used for the probes and how its strength is chosen is not described. This lack of clarity undermines generalizability claims.
- **Threshold & hyperparameter sensitivity:** No ablation study on key hyperparameters: detection threshold, k in k-NN, and the choice/strength of the backdoor probe. The method assumes a fixed threshold, but the defender does not know the poisoning rate—making adaptive threshold tuning essential for low (e.g., 0.5%) and high (e.g., 40%) poisoning rates. Without this, the practical utility is limited.
- **Experimental limitations:**
  - Only three attack types (patch, blend, warping) are tested; missing sample-specific, optimized-trigger, frequency-domain, and more recent attacks (e.g., ISSBA). This makes it unclear if BaDLoss generalizes to all attacks.
  - Clean accuracy degrades severely in several cases: CIFAR-10 SIG, WaNet, and multi-attack all drop to ~60%, raising doubt about model functionality. The failure on the Sinusoid attack (Table 2) is not sufficiently explained.
  - Results for ABL and other baselines in Tables 1–3 appear much lower than reported in their original papers—suggesting possible unfair tuning or implementation differences.
  - Only small-scale datasets (CIFAR-10, GTSRB) are used; no evaluations on larger or higher-resolution datasets (e.g., ImageNet subset) where loss dynamics may differ.
  - No comparison with recent SOTA detection methods such as Cognitive Distillation (which can detect both training and test samples).
  - The claim that backdoor examples cause the model to “attend to a single feature” (page 2, Fig. 1) is an overclaim without evidence.
- **Practicality & real-world scenario:**
  - The one-attack evaluations set reference backdoor probes similar to the attack being tested. This is unrealistic—the defender should have no prior knowledge of the attack. The method’s reliance on predefined reference backdoor samples limits its practicality.
  - Evaluations against adaptive attacks (where the adversary knows BaDLoss and crafts evasive backdoors) are lacking. Could an adversary select poisoned samples that have various loss curves to evade detection?
  - The model architecture used is not clearly stated; it is unclear if the method works on other models. For CIFAR-10 the reported clean accuracy is unusually low.
- **Missing details & questions:**
  - How were the 250 backdoored probes crafted (what backdoor feature)? Would different probe triggers affect performance?
  - How to choose k in k-NN? Is there a systematic method?
  - How robust to high poisoning rates (10%, 20%) or very low rates?
  - What is MAP-D (mentioned but not defined)?
  - Were baseline defenses tuned on the tested attacks? If not, comparisons may be unfair.
  - Why are different poisoning rates used for different attacks/datasets? The comparison may be unfair.
  - Only training-set detection is possible; no test-time detection capability.
