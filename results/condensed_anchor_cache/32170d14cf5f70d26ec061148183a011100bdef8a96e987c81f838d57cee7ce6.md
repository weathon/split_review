## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a meta-learning framework for discovering synthetic environments (SEs) as proxies to train RL agents, avoiding direct interaction with the real environment. The authors modify prior meta-learning to improve invariance across hyperparameters and algorithms, introduce a curriculum on evaluation episode horizons, and find that learning a synthetic contextual bandit (SCB) – a reward function and initial state distribution with fixed dynamics – suffices even for complex MDPs. Experiments on Brax/OpenAI Gym environments show that policies trained in SEs transfer to the real environment, and the authors discuss interpretability and downstream applications.

### Strengths
- **Novel research direction**: Discovering environments for quick learning has potential for deploying RL algorithms and downstream tasks. [R1]
- **Simpler proxy model**: Using a synthetic contextual bandit (SCB) as a proxy is interesting when the environment is complex. [R2]
- **Environment design approach**: Learning an initial state distribution (with fixed dynamics) instead of full transition dynamics is an interesting approach to environment design. [R4]
- **Synthetic reward functions**: Using synthetic rewards to extract an optimal policy provides a ground truth often not available, and could give insights into learning dynamics of different RL agents. [R4]
- **Extensive experiments**: The paper provides thorough experiments across multiple Gym environments and ablation studies that demonstrate the method’s feasibility. [R3]
- **Reduced complexity**: Showing that training contextual bandits (SCB) is sufficient reduces the number of parameters needed and makes the framework more feasible, with potential for large-scale real problems. [R3]
- **Interpretability**: Experiments justify the choice of SCB as a proxy. [R2]

### Weaknesses
- **Missing baseline comparisons and prior work**: No clear comparison to Ferreira et al. (2022), who proposed learning synthetic environments and reward networks; the paper does not elaborate on differences or show performance differences. [R2, R4] Also, no comparison with state-of-the-art results on the same tasks, even accounting for different inner-loop policies. [R2]
- **Unproven benefit over direct training**: The claimed wall-clock improvement (faster simulation) is not substantiated with total training time (meta-learning + inner-loop) vs. direct training in the real environment. [R1] Similarly, the statement that SEs can train agents within 10k steps lacks supporting learning curves. [R4]
- **Empirical results not demonstrating practical advantage**: Figure 2 shows feasibility but not benefit over training in the real environment. Baseline underperformance on a hyperparameter distribution does not show advantage, as hyperparameter sweeps may be cheaper than meta-learning. Many design choices (hyperparameters in both loops) are not ablated. [R1]
- **Poor intuition and unclear writing**: The paper is hard to follow. Many claims are stated without intuition or justification: why learning initial state distribution is more useful than learning transition dynamics [R4]; why limiting episode length to 1 does not hurt performance (except Pendulum, no explanation) [R4]; why state transition is not needed (experimental proof but no intuition) [R3]. There is a contradictory claim about “learning a curriculum” vs. “designing a curriculum” (Section 2). [R1] The biological motivation is not clearly connected to RL. [R2]
- **Connection to value function overlooked**: The optimal reward of the contextual bandit seems equivalent to the optimal value function. The paper does not explore why meta-learning is better than using Monte-Carlo returns or learning the value function directly, nor does it show that synthetic environments provide interpretability beyond a learned value function. [R1]
- **Fundamental limitations of SCB**: A contextual bandit is stateless, so it cannot capture temporal dependencies crucial for many RL tasks (e.g., Go). The approach assumes complete observability; how to handle partial observation is not addressed. [R2] The paper only tests continuous control tasks with simple dynamics, not complex temporal dependencies.
- **Missing/poorly presented experimental details**:
  - Figure 3 omits learning curves for most ablations, showing only final dashed lines, and 95% confidence intervals are missing for some/all methods. [R4]
  - Ablation on curricula does not include “no curriculum” as a standalone option; it is only implicitly present in another row, making visual comparison difficult. [R4]
  - Figure 5 colors should be revised so synthetic curves share the same color and real curves share another. [R2]
  - Claim that episode length of one step does not affect performance (Section 4, last subsection) is not supported by any figure or table. [R2]
  - Claim that “PPO struggles with solving MountainCar” lacks a citation. [R2]
  - Choice of Brax environments instead of Gym or other popular environments is not justified. [R2]
- **No discussion of hyperparameter tuning**: How hyperparameters of the method are set and how much tuning is needed to achieve good results is not discussed. [R4]
- **Synthetic data motivation unclear**: It is not explained why synthetic data is used instead of real data during meta-learning, given that real data is required for evaluation anyway. [R1]
- **Discrepancy between figures**: For Hopper, the ablation results (Figure 3) differ from the original results (Figure 2) without explanation. [R1]
