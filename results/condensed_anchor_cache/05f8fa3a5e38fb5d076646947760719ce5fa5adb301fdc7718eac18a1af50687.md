## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces the Adaptive Visual Tokenizer (AVT), which learns variable-length token representations for images via a recurrent encoder-decoder architecture that progressively distills 2D image tokens into 1D latent tokens over multiple iterations, adaptively adding new tokens each iteration (range 32–256). The authors validate on reconstruction loss, FID, linear probing (Top-1 classification on ImageNet-100/ImageNet-1K), depth estimation, and show token specialization (57.8 mIOU with ImageNet‑S GT segmentation without explicit supervision). They demonstrate correlations between token count and image complexity, OOD familiarity, and downstream task requirements.

### Strengths
- **Originality**: Novel integration of recurrent processing with visual tokenization, extending beyond fixed‑length tokenizers (VQGAN, Perceiver, Titok) and adapting dynamic computation from NLP/sequential domains to visual representation learning. The shared 1D codebook across iterations and the optional dynamic halting mechanism are innovative contributions.
- **Quality**: Thorough empirical validation across multiple dimensions:
  - Reconstruction quality (L1, FID) competitive with fixed‑length tokenizers; linear probing 49.9% Top‑1 vs Titok’s 48.0% with a smaller architecture.
  - Systematic ablations of continuous vs discrete tokenization, network depth, training strategies, and codebook design.
  - Emergent object discovery: attention maps align with ground‑truth segmentation (57.8 mIOU) without supervision.
  - GAN loss used to enhance photo‑realism, demonstrating robust training procedure.
- **Clarity**: Clear progression from base distillation to full adaptive system; well‑structured empirical validation; effective visualizations of token specialization and attention patterns; systematic ablation organization.
- **Significance**: Demonstrates feasibility of variable‑length visual representations; opens avenues for resource‑constrained deployment and understanding how tokenization strategies affect image understanding.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited scope of comparisons and evaluation metrics**:
  - No comparisons with representation learning methods (CLIP, MoCo, DINO, MAE) or adaptive–length approaches like Matryoshka representation learning, despite noting their relevance (Reviewer 2). Missing comparison with adaptive architectures such as FlexViT (variable patch sizes) or hierarchical multi‑scale methods, making it hard to isolate the contribution of the recurrent design (Reviewer 1).
  - Evaluation is primarily on reconstruction and classification; metrics for disentanglement, robustness to adversarial attacks, or generalization to semantic segmentation / object detection are absent (Reviewer 3).
- **Mixed results against existing baselines**:
  - Table 1 shows AVT’s FID is no better than VQGAN or Titok for the same number of tokens; many baselines remain better even at AVT’s maximum token count (256). The paper attributes this to smaller training scale (ImageNet‑100 vs full ImageNet‑1K for adaptive training), but it is not clear whether longer training would close the gap (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
  - Linear probing performance is at par with baselines but often requires more tokens and recurrent processing (Reviewer 2).
- **Lack of formal analysis of token allocation optimality**:
  - No theoretical framework or bound for optimal token count given task constraints. Empirical correlations with human‑labeled complexity are interesting but provide no insight into whether token allocations are optimal (Reviewer 1).
  - How to determine the number of iterations per image adaptively? Currently set manually; could it be selected based on information entropy? (Reviewer 4)
- **Insufficient analysis of computational overhead**:
  - No detailed comparison of inference time, FLOPs, or memory usage relative to single‑pass methods (VQGAN, Titok). How does the recurrent processing overhead scale with iterations? Are early‑stopping strategies for simple images feasible? (Reviewers 1, 4). This is critical given the motivation of efficient representation learning.
- **Sensitivity to model strength**: The observation that stronger models are more sensitive to fewer tokens is not convincingly demonstrated—weaker models may simply reach a performance cap sooner, so the conclusion is not surprising. Absolute performance metrics rather than relative drops would clarify (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 3 notes this as a limitation for resource‑constrained applications and suggests mitigation strategies (distillation/regularization).
- **Unclear dynamic halting**: Figure 1 states dynamic halting is optional, but there is no discussion of when it should be enabled/disabled or whether it was used in all experiments (Reviewer 2).
- **Method complexity**: The training procedure is not simple—requires learning to reconstruct pre‑trained VQGAN tokens, joint encoder‑decoder training, and later GAN loss—making it harder to adopt and ablate (Reviewer 2).
- **Tokenization comparison**: No comprehensive analysis of continuous vs discrete tokenization trade‑offs (computational cost, memory, noise sensitivity) (Reviewer 3).
- **Presentation of ablations**: Further experiments and ablations in Section 5 are mostly textual; figures and tables would make trends and significance more intuitive (Reviewer 4). Minor confusion: footnote on line 397 referenced three pages prior; Table 1 caption lacks clear labeling of superscripts and dataset token counts (Reviewer 2).
