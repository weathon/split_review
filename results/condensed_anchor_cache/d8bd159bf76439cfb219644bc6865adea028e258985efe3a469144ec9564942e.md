## Merged Review

### Summary
WildBench introduces a benchmark of 1,024 real-world user queries selected from over one million WildChat conversations, with two automated metrics (WB-Reward pairwise, WB-Score pointwise) that use task-specific checklists, multiple baselines, structured explanations, and a length bias margin K. It achieves a Pearson correlation of 0.98 (WB-Reward) and 0.95 (WB-Score) with Chatbot Arena Elo on hard tasks, surpassing ArenaHard and AlpacaEval. The benchmark can be dynamically updated to reduce data leakage. However, reviewers raised concerns about limited novelty (one reviewer), insufficient analysis of multi-turn conversations and “wildness,” crude length penalty handling, cost comparison missing, and risks from history conversation leakage.

### Strengths
- **First benchmark for LLM evaluation with in-the-wild user queries** – data reflects real user distribution, more authentic than standard benchmarks, and can be updated over time to mitigate data leakage (R1, R2, R3).
- **Robust and debiased evaluation pipeline** – combines pairwise and pointwise metrics, uses multiple baseline models (not a single one), task-specific checklists, structured CoT analysis before scoring, and a customizable length bias margin \(K\) (R1, R2, R3). One reviewer (R2) particularly highlighted the “bunch of tricks” (pairwise + pointwise, checklist-based, multiple baselines, structured analysis, length bias margin).
- **High correlation with human judgments** – WB-Reward achieves 0.98 Pearson correlation with Chatbot Arena Elo, WB-Score 0.95, surpassing ArenaHard (0.91) and AlpacaEval2.0 (0.89). This is cost-effective compared to human evaluation (R1, R3).
- **Interesting experimental findings** – e.g., re-evaluated performance of LLaMA3-Inst-8B-SimPO (R2).
- **Task-specific checklists reduce “LLM as a judge” bias** and add interpretability; dynamic updates help prevent overfitting (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty** – one reviewer (R2, score 6, confidence 4) found the novelty “quite limited despite its richness of evaluation and wild data construction practices.” The same reviewer noted that experimental findings “offer limited novelty compared to existing benchmarks like AlpacaEval and Chatbot Arena” and that comparative performance among models remains largely consistent with prior evaluations. The other two reviewers (scores 8) did not share this concern, but the contrast is preserved.
- **Crude length penalty** – applying a uniform margin \(K\) at the dataset level fails to account for cases where longer responses are genuinely better; a per-sample treatment would be more appropriate (R2).
- **Lack of analysis from the “wildness” perspective** – the paper does not address what unique insights emerge from using wild data; how does WildBench differ from existing benchmarks in terms of what it reveals about models? (R2)
- **Multi-turn evaluation underexplored** – despite being a claimed highlight, there is no investigation of how model performance varies with different input context lengths in multi-turn conversations, which is crucial for understanding complex exchanges (R3).
- **Task quality filtering insufficiently detailed** – filtering criteria (especially for multi-turn conversations) need more clarity. The overall correlation with Chatbot Arena may not reflect performance differences between specific models (e.g., Yi-Large vs Yi-1.5-34B-Chat), and it is unclear if the benchmark can reliably differentiate models with similar overall performance but different strengths (R3).
- **Data leakage risk from public history conversations** – the test set includes multi-turn data where the history (from earlier GPT-3.5/4 responses) is publicly available; using these histories for training could still assist in answering the final question. Lack of specifics on anonymization and leakage mitigation (R3).
- **Cost comparison missing** – no detailed comparison of LLM API costs between WildBench and previous evaluation methods (R1). R1 also suggests providing a cheaper version (e.g., using 30% of samples) with a consistency check.
- **Potential bias from history conversation responses** – the history comes from earlier ChatGPT-3.5 and GPT-4, which may contain hallucinations or factual errors, possibly conflicting with the evaluated model’s knowledge (R3). Measures to filter such cases are not described.
- **Multi-turn filtering: theme consistency?** – even with turn limits, a task might contain multiple simple questions across different domains, making overall difficulty appear higher while the final question is easy. It is unclear whether additional task theme consistency checking is performed (R3).
- **Can fixed baseline models evaluate evolving models?** – since baseline models (used for pairwise comparisons) are fixed, their ability to effectively assess more advanced models as the task set evolves is questionable (R3).
- **Anonymous link not accessible** – the provided link for additional materials is inaccessible (R3).
