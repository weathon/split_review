## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies vision-knowledge conflicts in multimodal large language models (MLLMs), where visual information contradicts the model’s internal commonsense knowledge. The authors propose a human-in-the-loop pipeline to create ConflictVis, a benchmark of 374 counter-commonsense images and 1,122 QA pairs with two conflict targets and three difficulty levels. They test nine MLLMs and find they over-rely on textual input and parametric knowledge. To mitigate this, they introduce a “Focus-on-Vision” (FoV) prompting strategy. The results show FoV improves performance, but the degree of conflict and the novelty of the solution are debated across reviewers.

### Strengths
- The topic is timely and important.
- The paper studies an overlooked problem of vision-knowledge conflicts for MLLMs.
- The generated counter-commonsense images serve as a contribution to constructing such conflicts.
- ConflictVis is a novel, well-defined benchmark with rigorous human-in-the-loop validation.
- Good experiment setups, including sanity check and comprehensive question type evaluation.
- The paper is well-structured, well-written, and easy to follow.

### Weaknesses
- **Missing methodology details in benchmark construction:**  
  - It is unclear why human evaluation is required every time the pipeline is used (lines 277-278); if answers are predetermined, automatic comparison should suffice (Reviewer 1).  
  - No explanation of what makes questions harder or why multiple difficulty levels are needed (Reviewer 1).  
  - The human-in-the-loop components in Figure 2 are not clearly described (Reviewer 1).  
  - The exact prompt used in Section 3.3 is not stated (Reviewers 1, 3).

- **Incomplete experimental details:**  
  - The paper does not specify what MLLM outputs are compared against in the sanity test (presumably yes/no human annotations, but not stated) (Reviewer 1).  
  - Uncertainty calculation and aggregation (lines 301-314) are not described; details on number of samples, selection method, and sampling method are absent (Reviewer 1).  
  - Hyperparameters for reproducing Table 2 results are missing (Reviewer 1).  
  - Why Vicuna-13b was used for probability estimation rather than a larger or more powerful model is not justified; the authors should ensure the model encodes the needed commonsense (Reviewer 2).  
  - Why Yes/No questions have lower performance compared to other question types is not explained (Reviewer 2).

- **Issues with the evaluation of visual reliance:**  
  - In Section 3.3, when images are removed, questions with determiners (e.g., “Is *the baby* on the bed fixing a computer?”) lack referent; if models reject such questions and this is counted negatively, it indicates a design flaw (Reviewer 1). The experiment should distinguish reliance on contextual knowledge vs. parametric knowledge.  
  - The degree of knowledge conflict may be overstated: the initial prompts used in Section 3.3 may have poorly structured instructions (e.g., no explicit “based on the image”), so limited visual utilization could be an artifact of the prompt rather than a fundamental conflict (Reviewer 3).

- **Limited novelty and significance of FoV method:**  
  - FoV is a simple prompt adjustment (“Please focus on the visual information”) and does not introduce a novel technique as implied in the abstract; the data in Table 2 show inconsistent and only marginal improvements (Reviewer 1).  
  - The simple prompt may not generalize across all multimodal contexts or complex use cases beyond commonsense conflicts; it seems intuitive to already include “Based on the given image” (Reviewer 3).  
  - The memorization effect of counter-commonsense conflicts is relatively low and can be addressed with a simple prompt, reducing the significance of the issue (Reviewer 3).

- **Benchmark limitations and practical relevance:**  
  - GPT-4 achieves more than 90% accuracy on ConflictVis, suggesting the benchmark is not challenging enough to fully capture vision-knowledge conflicts for state-of-the-art models (Reviewer 2).  
  - The benchmark only investigates counter-commonsense conflicts, not other types such as factual or world-knowledge conflicts (Reviewer 2).  
  - The real-world relevance of counter-commonsense scenarios (e.g., a baby fixing a computer) is unclear; users unlikely to input such contrived images, limiting broader applicability (Reviewer 3). In contrast, Reviewer 1 finds the benchmark potentially useful once issues are fixed.  
  - The classification of open-ended questions as “subjective” is questionable: answers like “playing a guitar” for a person holding a paddle are objectively wrong (Reviewer 1).

- **Superficial analysis of the conflict:**  
  - The analysis attributes the problem to a long-standing known “language bias” in MLLMs, which previous works already pointed out, lacking deeper insight (Reviewer 2).  
  - No attempt is made to understand *why* MLLMs underutilize visual information; only the resource and prompting fix are offered (Reviewer 1).
