## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes FedMUD, a communication-efficient federated learning method that replaces direct transmission of model updates with synthetic tensor sequences (synthetic samples) generated via optimization to approximate the updates. It aims to reduce both uplink and downlink communication, provides theoretical convergence analysis, and reports experiments on CIFAR-10 and CRCSlide with architectures including ResNet-18, LeNet, GoogLeNet, MobileNet, ShuffleNet, claiming up to 40.98× communication reduction while maintaining competitive accuracy. The reviews are mixed: one reviewer (score 6) found the paper well‑written and the approach effective, while three reviewers (score 3) raised substantial concerns about experimental evaluation, theoretical assumptions, computational cost, privacy, and baseline comparisons.

### Strengths
- Fresh perspective on communication bottlenecks in FL by leveraging model update distillation, decoupling updates from the network architecture (R1).
- A well‑principled idea with simple but insightful theoretical analysis (R1).
- Novel observation: exploiting the server’s prior knowledge of client model parameters at the start of each round, enabling a different approach to reducing communication overhead (R2).
- Comprehensive empirical validation across multiple architectures (ResNet‑18, GoogLeNet, MobileNet, ShuffleNet) and datasets (CIFAR‑10, CRCSlides), demonstrating substantial communication reduction (e.g., 40.98× for ResNet‑18) while maintaining accuracy around 80% (R2).
- Provides convergence analysis showing FedMUD can achieve the same asymptotic rate as FedAvg (O(1/√(NT))) under specified conditions on error decay (R2).
- Implementation practicality: functions as a drop‑in replacement for FedAvg; no need to modify network architectures or training protocols (R2).
- Bilaterally efficient: addresses both uplink and downlink communication (R2).
- The paper is well‑written, concise, and easy to follow (R3, minority).
- Empirical results confirm effectiveness and theoretical findings provide insights (R3, minority).
- Well‑motivated and original idea of using synthetic samples to approximate model updates (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Experiments are very limited**: only on CIFAR‑10 and CRCSlide with small to moderate models (ResNet‑18, LeNet, GoogLeNet, MobileNet, ShuffleNet). No evaluation on larger models (e.g., ViT‑16, RN50, >100M parameters) or larger numbers of clients, which is crucial for real‑world FL deployments (R1, R2).
- **Lack of scalability investigation**: no experiments with increasing parameter sizes to compare update computation complexities and overall wall‑clock time; the paper does not explain the breakdown of (comm + local update comm + MUD compute) times, nor why local update comm time differs between methods (R1, R2).
- **Missing experimental details**: stepsize tuning for baselines is not reported; batch size B and initial rounds M are not specified; only the learning rate 0.01 for FedMUD is given (R2).
- **Inadequate baseline comparison**: 
  * Methods cited from 2017 (Top‑k sparsification) and 2020 (FedPAQ) are outdated. FedPAQ only compresses uplink, making it unsuitable for a method that compresses both directions. More recent relevant methods exist (e.g., Cheng et al. 2024). The term “TopK” usually refers to a compressor, not the entire method; “SGD with TopK” is more conventional (R2).
  * The paper incorrectly states “FedPAQ is the first federated learning quantization scheme”; quantization schemes like QSGD (2016) and SignSGD (2018) predate FedPAQ (R4).
- **Insufficient evaluation under partial participation**: only a basic comparison between FedMUD and FedAvg is presented, despite partial participation being fundamental in real‑world FL (R2).
- **Table 2 discrepancy**: FedAvg’s average data upload is comparable to Top‑k, which is unexpected since FedAvg involves no compression; this requires clarification (R2).
- **Theoretical analysis concerns**:
  * Assumption 3.4 (bounded error) is overly strong: it assumes 𝔼[||ωᵢ⁽ᵗ⁾ − ωᵢ,ₒ⁽ᵗ⁾||] ≤ Δ², but Δ can be arbitrarily large, is unknown beforehand, and is not empirically verified. The paper provides no evidence that this assumption holds in practice (R2).
  * The convergence bound includes a term O(NT L² Δ²) not affected by stepsizes η and γ, leading to divergence as T→∞ if Δ is fixed. The authors claim that if Δ decays as Δ/Tᵏ (k>1) the same rate as FedAvg is achieved, but this decay is not demonstrated experimentally (R2).
  * There is no theoretical guarantee that the generated synthetic samples sufficiently minimize the optimization in equation (2), potentially leading to irrecoverable error (R4).
- **Privacy implications not addressed**: the synthetic tensor sequences (which resemble data distillation) could leak information about local data distributions, as noted by R2 (citing Deep Leakage from Gradients, Zhu et al. NeurIPS 2019) and R3 (local data distributions may be revealed) (R2, R3).
- **Computational cost concerns**:
  * The framework requires generating m synthetic samples at both server and clients every round by solving (2), which is computationally intensive. Figure 3 and Section 5.2 show significant local computation time; with larger models the communication gains may become marginal due to increased local computation time (R3, R4).
  * The choice of LBFGS as the default optimizer for FedMUD is not justified. Comparing with baselines using a different local optimizer may be unfair; superior results could be partially due to LBFGS rather than the distillation method (R3).
  * No clarification on how wall‑clock time is measured (actual FL system or simulation) (R3).
- **Writing and presentation issues**:
  * Typos: “Aussumption” instead of “Assumption” (lines 702, 704, 728); “eta” instead of η (line 754); “SGD with TopK” phrasing (R2, R3).
  * Confusing notation: line 730 expectation taken over undefined Sᵢ; mathematical expression (11) lacks quantifier ∀t>0 (R2).
  * Equation (4) uses ζ_g⁽ᵗ⁾ = {(xⱼ, yⱼ)}ⱼ₌₁^{m_g⁽ᵗ⁾} but later refers to m instead of m_g⁽ᵗ⁾; unclear how mᵢ⁽ᵗ⁾ is chosen for clients (R3).
  * Confusion between sections 3.2 and 4 leaves reader unsure how equations (2) and (3) are used (R4).
- **Novelty claim is overstated**: the exploitation of server prior knowledge has been explored in prior works (e.g., “Adaptive Compression in Federated Learning via Side Information”, AISTATS 2024; “Wyner‑Ziv Estimators for Distributed Mean Estimation with Side Information”, AISTATS 2021) (R4).
- **Missing analysis on m (number of synthetic samples)**: how does the method ensure that the size of the synthetic samples is smaller than the model? What is the typical range of m and how is it tuned for different datasets/models? A plot of final model performance as m increases is needed (R4).
- **Additional related works could be discussed**: FedPara (low‑rank Hadamard product), Sparse Random Networks for FL, Communication‑Efficient Adaptive FL (R3).
