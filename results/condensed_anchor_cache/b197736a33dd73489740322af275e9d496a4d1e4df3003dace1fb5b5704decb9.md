## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper explores divergences between human and machine language processing by comparing MEG brain responses (from subjects reading narrative stories) with GPT-2 XL representations. Using a data-driven approach, the authors identify words that are best and worst predicted by the base LM, then employ a proposer-verifier LLM to generate hypotheses about the differences. Three hypothesized phenomena (emotional intelligence, figurative language, physical commonsense) are selected, and the base LM is fine-tuned on corresponding tasks. Fine-tuned models show improved MEG alignment for emotion and figurative tasks but not for physical commonsense. One reviewer (R1, score 6) finds this exploration interesting and notes improved alignment from fine-tuning. The other three reviewers (scores 3) find the work limited in novelty, statistical rigor, and scientific validity of the approach.

### Strengths
- The overall approach of systematically investigating divergences between LMs and human brains in a data-driven way is relatively novel and potentially promising (R1, R3, R4).
- Examining task-based language model representations (emotion, figurative, physical commonsense) and their alignment with MEG recordings is an emerging and intriguing research direction (R1, R2).
- The idea of comparing representations, identifying words with low prediction, and using an automated proposer-verifier to generate hypotheses is insightful and could be a useful tool (R3, R4).
- Fine-tuning LMs on the hypothesized tasks (emotion, figurative) leads to improved brain alignment, which is a positive empirical finding (R1).
- The writing is generally clear and the approach is well motivated (R4).
- *Minority positive view:* One reviewer (R1, score 6) is more positive about the paper’s contribution and novelty than the other three (R2, R3, R4, all score 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Statistical methodology is insufficient and not properly described.** The permutation test in Appendix F uses timepoints (destroying temporal structure) and tests the wrong null hypothesis (no relationship vs. no difference between predictors). A valid and clearly described procedure is needed (R3, R4). The fraction of significant channels is not a meaningful effect size because MEG channels are highly correlated; authors should report correlation between measured and predicted response, compare with a control model like GloVE, and compare improvement from fine-tuning to improvements from simply using a larger base LM (R3, R4).
- **MEG signal interpretation is questionable.** MEG is very noisy and captures widespread cortical activity, not just responses to text stimuli (R2). An ablation test is needed: feeding random noise or wrong MEG activity into the regression should show whether the observed correlations (Figure 2, up to 0.45) are meaningful (R2). A decoder from MEG to words could indicate whether the signal contains any text-related information (R2). The temporal progression in Figure 2 could reflect responses to visual stimuli (fixation, word appearance) rather than semantic processing (R2). A control condition with pseudowords or random character strings would help isolate language-specific activity (R3).
- **The LLM-based proposer-verifier is not a reliable scientific discovery tool.** The mechanism is vague (R2). It generates plausible-sounding explanations even when given random pairs of word sets, so there is no guarantee the top hypotheses are valid (R2, R3). Reproducibility tests are needed: would the same top hypotheses emerge from different subjects, different random seeds, different chapters of the same book, or a repeated experiment on a different day? (R2, R3). Per-subject analysis should confirm consistency across individuals (R3).
- **Data-driven motivation for fine-tuning tasks is not compelling.** The initial word-level analyses are not statistically significant (as acknowledged) and the procedures for establishing significance are unclear (R3, R4). The resulting labels (emotional, figurative, physical) feel arbitrary and may not be reliable or replicable (R3, R4). Krippendorff’s alpha values are modest, raising concerns about label quality (R3, R4). It is unclear whether the data-driven approach adds value over simply selecting three cognitively relevant tasks a priori (R3, R4).
- **No investigation of why the emotion task yields the best improvements.** The emotion task may have more training examples, be inherently more difficult, or lead to better task performance, but these aren’t analyzed (R3, R4). The paper does not evaluate whether fine-tuned models generalize well to other datasets for the same task, which would affect trust in neural prediction gains (R3, R4).
- **Methodology choices are not justified.** Why use the last layer of GPT-2? Prior work (Toneva et al. 2019, Jain et al. 2018, Oota et al. 2023) finds intermediate layers align better with brain responses (R1, R2). Why use a context length of 100? Previous studies (Toneva et al., Oota et al. 2023 Interspeech) found context sizes of 10–50 yield better alignment (R1, R2). These choices need justification or ablation.
- **Lack of clarity and missing details.** The description of the regression model in Figure 1 is incomplete: is there one model per time × channel point? (R3). How is text presented (visual or auditory)? (R3). How were train/test splits constructed (temporal proximity, same subject?)? (R3). What is the composition of the test set and were regression models retrained per subject? (R2, R3). The role of the proposer in Figure 1 is too vague (R2). The description of the proposer-verifier should be longer, including how hypotheses are validated during training (R3, R4). Figure 4 is hard to follow and needs more detail (R1).
- **Specific analysis concerns.** 
  - Figure 5: The MSE improvement values for the physical task (panel C) appear as large as those for emotion and figurative, conflicting with the conclusion that the physical task did not improve. This must be clarified (R3, R4). 
  - Authors should clarify whether the MSE improvement is reported for the model fine-tuned on the same task (R3, R4). 
  - A specificity analysis is needed: does the emotion-trained model also improve prediction of figurative words, and vice-versa? (R3, R4). 
  - Multiple comparisons correction should be stated for Figure 5 (R3, R4). 
  - Why select words rather than sentences for the proposer-verifier? If sentences are the unit of analysis, top and bottom sentences should be identified by averaging across words (R3, R4). 
- **Missing comparisons and related work.** The paper does not discuss or compare with prior work on task-based brain alignment (Oota et al. 2022, Sun et al. 2023 IJCAI, Aw et al. 2023, Sun et al. 2023 ECAI) (R1). Several citations are missing (R1).
- **Generalization and further questions.** Whether findings hold for the listening modality (e.g., MEG-MASC story listening dataset) is not addressed (R1, R2). How different tasks interact when fine-tuned simultaneously? (R2). Which layers are most affected during fine-tuning? (R1, R2). Why does the parietal region (angular gyrus) not show enhancement between 250–375 ms, despite its role in high-level semantic processing? (R2).
