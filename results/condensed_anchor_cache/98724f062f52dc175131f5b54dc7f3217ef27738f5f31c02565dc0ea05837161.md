## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper presents ConDOR, a conditional diffusion model for generating longitudinal neurodegenerative disease data (Alzheimer’s disease biomarkers from ADNI and OASIS). The method combines ordinal regression with two diffusion models – a Regional Diffusion Model (RDM) for baseline data and a Temporal Diffusion Model (TDM) for follow-up data – to capture both cohort-level trends and subject-level variability. The model is evaluated on four biomarkers (Amyloid, Cortical Thickness, FDG) against nine baseline models (GANs, VAEs, DDPM). Reviewers commend the innovation and comprehensiveness but raise concerns about theoretical justification, methodological clarity, evaluation rigor, and missing comparisons.

### Strengths

1. **Combining cohort-level and subject-level trends** – The dual-component approach (ordinal regression for population trends, kernel-based conditional distribution for individual variability) effectively balances generalized disease progression with personal characteristics. (R1, R2, R3)
2. **Innovative architectural design** – The use of two diffusion models (RDM for baseline, TDM for follow-up) and integration of ordinal regression with diffusion are novel, enabling the generation of realistic longitudinal sequences. (R2, R3)
3. **Addresses key medical data challenges** – The method targets sparse, irregular, and widely spaced observation intervals common in neurodegenerative studies, providing a tool for data augmentation and progression modeling. (R3)
4. **Comprehensive experimental validation** – The model is compared against nine baseline approaches (including GANs, VAEs, DDPM) across multiple metrics (e.g., MAE, correlation, visual plausibility) on four biomarkers from ADNI and OASIS; implementation time is also compared. (R1, R2, R3)
5. **Multi-domain extension** – The model is extended to a multi-domain setting, enhancing generalizability across different data sources. (R3)
6. **Clear comparisons and good visualizations** – The paper provides clear quantitative and qualitative comparisons. (R2)

### Weaknesses

1. **Lack of theoretical justification and unclear assumptions** – The paper does not adequately explain how the proposed diffusion model with ordinal regression theoretically improves upon existing generative models for longitudinal data, especially for irregular intervals and complex temporal dependencies (R1). Specific assumptions are questionable: (a) ordinal regression assumes monotonic progression through discrete stages, oversimplifying non-linear disease trajectories (R3); (b) temporal diffusion relies on linear interpolation for transitions, which may smooth over critical abrupt changes (R3); (c) the use of all time points (x_t, t=1..T) to train RDM – rather than only baseline x_1 – is not justified and may bias the model toward subjects with longer or more frequent records (R2). The method’s ability to condition on specific ages and disease severity during generation is not demonstrated; the diffusion models appear to take random noise as input without age/severity conditioning, contradicting the claim (R2).

2. **Methodological clarity and reproducibility** – The description of cohort-level and subject-level sampling is convoluted; the precise mechanism balancing these two components is not sufficiently detailed (R1). Critical technical details are missing: (a) whether RDM and TDM are trained separately or jointly (R2); (b) how hyperparameters λ (cohort/subject interpolation weight) and D (interpolation window size) are chosen – λ selection is unclear and no validation set is used (80% train, 20% test split), making the results in Table 1 and 2 less convincing, as the best λ may have been derived from test data (R2); (c) the full generative deployment process is not explained – it is unclear how to generate new synthetic data (e.g., conditioning on desired ages/severity) (R2). Code is not provided, compromising reproducibility (R3).

3. **Evaluation weaknesses** – (a) No confidence intervals or significance testing are reported for the metrics, weakening the robustness of the claims (R1). (b) The train-test split is ambiguous: it is not stated whether the split was performed at the subject level (keeping all observations from a subject together) or at the observation level (allowing different time points from the same subject in both training and test sets). The latter would cause information leakage and inflate performance, especially for the temporal diffusion model (R3). (c) Abnormal results are not discussed – in Table 1, DDPM shows very high variance compared to other methods; since DDPM is one of only two diffusion-based baselines, an explanation is needed (R2). (d) The choice of λ (Table 3) significantly impacts performance, but no validation approach is provided; the reported results may not reflect realistic setting where λ must be chosen without test data (R2).

4. **Insufficient discussion and missing comparisons** – (a) The discussion is weak: it does not address potential limitations such as non-monotonic disease progression, significant population heterogeneity, or scenarios where the method might underperform (R1, R3). (b) The paper compares only to generative models (GANs, VAEs, diffusion), not to traditional statistical longitudinal models (e.g., mixed-effects models, growth curve models) that are standard in medical literature and specifically designed for temporal dependencies (R3). (c) Future work directions are not included (R3).
