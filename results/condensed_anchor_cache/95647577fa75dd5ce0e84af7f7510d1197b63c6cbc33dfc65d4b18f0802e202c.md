## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces WaveDiffusion, a framework for Full Waveform Inversion (FWI) that reframes the problem as a joint diffusion process in a shared latent space. Two separate autoencoders (for seismic data and velocity maps) have their bottlenecks merged via vector quantization into a unified latent space. A diffusion model is trained in this latent space to generate seismic–velocity pairs that approximately satisfy the governing PDE without additional constraints. Experiments on the OpenFWI 2D dataset demonstrate high fidelity and diversity of generated pairs. Two reviewers (R1, R3, scores 6,6) find the joint generation perspective novel and the experiments convincing; two reviewers (R2, R4, scores 3,3) consider the dual-autoencoder part not novel and the paper’s contribution limited.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-organized, clearly written, and easy to follow (R1, R3).
- Offers a novel perspective by treating FWI as a joint generative problem of both seismic data and velocity maps simultaneously, in contrast to prior work treating them separately (R1, R3).
- Jointly generated seismic–velocity pairs are approximately consistent with the governing PDE without explicit constraints, addressing a limitation of conditional generation methods (R3).
- Extensive experiments on OpenFWI confirm the soundness of the method and show generated pairs can supplement real training data (R3).
- Results exhibit high fidelity and diversity (R1, R4).
- The diffusion process in the shared latent space is an interesting extension of dual-autoencoder approaches (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns (R2, R4, high confidence 5):** The dual-autoencoder with a shared latent space is not new. Prior work on paired autoencoders for inverse problems (e.g., https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.13314, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.13220) is not cited or acknowledged. The remaining novelty—the diffusion process in that latent space—is interesting but should have been stated as the main contribution (R2). R4 recommends focusing on the diffusion aspect (Section 3.3) and developing it into a full paper.
- **Missing comparisons (R1, R3):**
  - Not compared to any existing conditional generative methods (e.g., [1] mentioned by R3). Section 4.2.4 only compares separate vs. joint diffusion within the same model, not against external baselines (R3).
  - Comparison with InversionNet (Section 4.2.3) is incomplete and does not clearly answer which algorithm is better (R1).
- **Missing reconstruction methods (R3):** Only InversionNet is used as the data-driven solver. Results may differ with other solvers (e.g., [2] and [3] suggested by R3). Adding at least one additional solver would strengthen the evaluation.
- **Unclear how to invert actual data (R1, R4):** The paper shows generation of pairs but does not explain how to use the trained model when given a specific seismic dataset to recover the velocity model. R4 asks: “Given some data d, how do you use diffusion to find an appropriate model?” This is a key missing application step.
- **Unrealistic experimental setup (R3):** In the Gen+1% case, the generative model is trained on the same data distribution as the 1% subset used for reconstruction. If real data are small, training on a different distribution would be more realistic. R3 asks for implications on real-world applications and what happens if the generative model is trained on a different subset of OpenFWI.
- **Terminology mix (R1):** The wave equation models *acoustic* data, but the paper uses “seismic” interchangeably. These are not equivalent; acoustic is a simplification of seismic phenomena.
- **Missing details / additional questions (R1, R3, R4):**
  - Preprocessing of acoustic data and velocity models (R1).
  - Training time (CPU/GPU hours for 1000 epochs) (R1).
  - Feasibility for 3D data and implications on computational complexity (R1).
  - Whether the generative model respects symmetry (e.g., symmetric data yields symmetric velocity) (R1).
  - How the coarse-to-fine mechanism works (R4).
  - The amount of data required for the generative model vs. reconstruction methods, and why one would use a generative model if reconstruction methods already have sufficient data (R3).
- **Reviewer disagreement:** Two reviewers (R1, R3, scores 6) view the joint generation as novel and the experiments supportive; two reviewers (R2, R4, scores 3, high confidence) consider the core approach not novel and the contribution limited. This contrast should be acknowledged.
