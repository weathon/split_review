## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Norton (NOise Robust Temporal Optimal traNsport), a method to address the multi-granularity noisy correspondence (MNC) problem in long-term video-language learning. MNC encompasses both coarse-grained clip-caption misalignment and fine-grained frame-word misalignment. Norton formulates a unified optimal transport (OT) framework, incorporating video-paragraph and clip-caption contrastive losses. It uses an alignable prompt bucket to filter irrelevant clips/captions, a soft-maximum operator to identify crucial words/keyframes, and a transport-distance-based re-alignment strategy. The method is evaluated on video retrieval, videoQA, and action segmentation, outperforming state-of-the-art methods.

### Strengths

- Tackles the novel and practical MNC problem, which includes both coarse-grained (clip-caption) and fine-grained (frame-word) misalignment. This is a new challenge that has received little attention; the paper provides the first formal study of fine-grained NC for video-text learning.
- Proposes a unified OT framework that efficiently handles both granularities of noisy correspondence.
- Innovative components: alignable prompt bucket for filtering irrelevant pairs, soft-maximum operator for identifying important tokens, and a faulty negative exploitation strategy to improve clip representation.
- Self-bootstrapping: improves temporal ability of VideoCLIP without requiring additional models (e.g., DecemBert, TAN), enhancing scalability.
- Extensive experiments on diverse downstream tasks and datasets (video-paragraph retrieval, text-to-video retrieval, videoQA, action segmentation) with strong improvements over existing methods. Ablation studies and visualizations validate design choices.
- Provides a time cost table in the appendix showing computational efficiency under various settings.
- Well-written, well-organized, and easy to follow; clearly communicates core ideas.

### Weaknesses

- The paper lacks specific numerical results on handling noisy correspondence. It would benefit from experiments on synthetic noisy datasets and comparison with other noise-robust methods to provide concrete evidence of robustness.
- The performance gains are not uniform across all tasks/settings: results on YouCookII retrieval and some ablation experiments show promising improvements, while Table 2 shows mixed results across metrics. The reasons behind this disparity need clarification.
- The advantages of OT over DTW require more elaboration. The paper should include DTW as a baseline and discuss why OT is superior for this setting.
- No comparison with other OT-based methods for sequence alignment (e.g., Su & Hua 2017). Comparison and discussion of differences in performance/efficiency would strengthen the paper.
- The explanation of why OT effectively learns video-paragraph similarity could be more explicit, especially regarding how the training objective (maximizing ⟨Q,S⟩) prevents misleading the model when the similarity matrix S is inaccurate early in training.
- The rationale for using stop-gradient in transport assignment Q is unclear. Additional discussion on why this improves efficiency is needed.
- More detailed implementation specifics (hyperparameter settings, training procedures, computational resources) would enhance reproducibility.
- The method’s limitations are not discussed; the paper would benefit from acknowledging potential shortcomings or failure cases.
- Need to clarify how the value of p for the alignable prompt bucket is chosen (fixed per epoch vs. adaptive) and how sensitive performance is to different values.
- Details on handling different clip/caption lengths (e.g., random window sampling, padding) and how these affect OT computation should be provided.
- To improve readability, it is suggested that the second-best results be highlighted alongside the primary results in each table.
