## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a framework for human-AI collaboration that integrates logical rules and Theory of Mind (ToM). A rule generator and evaluator infer human goals from observed trajectories using latent rules, then a hierarchical reinforcement learning model with ToM plans robot actions. Experiments on multiple benchmarks show the method outperforms most baselines.

### Strengths
- Novel integration of logical reasoning and Theory of Mind for human-AI collaboration (all reviewers).
- Comprehensive framework combining rule generation, knowledge graph construction, hierarchical RL, and ToM (R2, R3).
- Strong empirical results on practical datasets/environments: significantly outperforms baselines in success rate and other metrics, with standard deviations reported (R1, R2, R4). Provides insights into neuro-symbolic applications (R4).
- Limitations are properly discussed (R4).
- The idea of using logical reasoning for goal inference and generalization in human-AI collaboration is novel (R1, R4).

### Weaknesses
- Experimental tasks are too simple: even Seq2Seq achieves good results (Table 1); tasks involve only simple navigation/object manipulation, deterministic settings, and limited action space, not capturing real-world uncertainty/variability of human behavior. Lacks dynamic multi-agent interactions and complex communication. Human studies are needed (R1, confidence 2). Reviewer 1 also questions whether logical reasoning specifically helps human-AI collaboration beyond general reasoning tasks, and requests analysis/experiments to separate these effects (R1).
- Related work is only in the appendix and should be in the main text. More discussion is needed comparing the proposed method to existing human-AI collaboration and neuro-symbolic literature, highlighting gaps and how the method addresses them (R1, R4; both reviewers note this).
- Definitions of entity, relation, and logic rule are inconsistent with standard knowledge graph definitions; reusing these terms causes confusion for readers with a KG background (R2).
- Inconsistency in rule examples: Rule 1 in Fig. 3 uses `Walk_to(plate)` without a person argument, while Eq. (3) defines items as `Walk_to(person, bedroom)`. Unclear which setting is used (R2).
- The rule generator and reasoning evaluator are important components but their specific designs are not shown (R3, confidence 5 – very certain reviewer).
- Iterative Goal Inference implementation is unclear; it appears to be conditional learning but the procedure is not explained (R3, confidence 5).
- Method explanation is hard to follow in places: query v = (a, s) in Sec 2.3 is not specified (a = actions? s = states?) and lacks intuitive explanation; Eq. (5) uses g ∈ f where f is a rule (not a set), which is non-standard (R4).
- Potential scalability issue: Eq. (4) includes time and location in predicates, which would generate a very large number of ground atoms. The paper does not discuss computational cost or any restrictions on the considered language/environment to avoid this problem (R4).
- Lack of direct comparison to the most related studies; unclear how the proposed approach is superior to them (R4).
- Minor presentation issues: best results are not highlighted (bold) in tables, and metrics need ↑/↓ indicators (R1, R2). Typo: double periods in Fig. 3 caption (R4). Citation format errors (e.g., “WAH Puig et al. (2020)”, and duplicate “Watch-and-help” reference) (R1). The caption term “hardness level” for Tab. 1 and 2 is confusing (meaning “difficulty” vs. “non-softness”) (R2).
