## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces PhyDL-NWP, a physics-guided deep learning framework that combines physical mechanisms with neural networks for weather forecasting and downscaling. It learns PDEs from data using neural networks and then uses those learned PDEs as a physics loss to guide the training of forecasting or downscaling models. Experiments on multiple real-world weather datasets (including ERA5) show performance gains in both tasks.

### Strengths

- The problem of weather forecasting and downscaling is important and timely, especially in the context of climate change. (R1, R3, R4)
- The idea of combining physical mechanisms with deep learning to improve generalization and performance is interesting and reasonable. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- Experiments are conducted on the high-quality ERA5 dataset and demonstrate performance gains for both downscaling and forecasting tasks. (R1) The empirical results support the claims. (R3)
- Figures 1 and 2 are helpful in explaining the steps of the method. (R2)
- The discovery of sparse, interpretable physical relationships is of high interest. (R2)
- The proposed method can be seamlessly integrated as a plug-in module into existing deep learning models. (R4) (one reviewer, R4, rated the paper 6 out of 6, being more positive overall; others gave 3, 3, 5)

### Weaknesses

- **Writing and presentation are unclear.** Symbol definitions are missing or ambiguous (e.g., what are (u,v,w) in Figure 1? Is Q the same as Q_pi? What is epsilon?). The relationship between different Q terms is not explicitly stated. (R1) Sections in the method part are not well connected; an overview at the start of Section 3 would help. (R3) The description of the downscaling approach lacks specific details on how the physics-informed component achieves higher resolution outputs (e.g., how the latent force term is parameterized and interacts with spatial/temporal components). (R4)
- **Missing recent related works and baselines.** Section 2.1 misses recent progress in weather forecasting, including GraphCast, ClimaX, and FengWu. (R1) The related work also lacks discussion of PINNs. (R1) For downscaling, the paper does not compare to models specifically designed for downscaling, such as YNet and DeepSD, or more recent super-resolution models. (R3) For forecasting, only FourCastNet is compared, which is among the least-performing recent models; more state-of-the-art methods like ClimaX, GNN-based models, PanguWeather, and Graphcast should be included to demonstrate improvement. (R3) The paper needs to more clearly articulate differences and superiority over existing works that decompose PDEs into components using neural networks. (R4)
- **Core contribution not clearly distinguished from existing PINN approaches.** The paper does not clearly articulate how it differs from the broad PINN family and what novel aspects it introduces. (R1) The unique advantages or improvements over decomposing PDEs with neural networks and capturing temporal dynamics are not explained. (R4)
- **Physical consistency is not rigorously demonstrated.** It is unclear how to measure physical consistency—does the analysis in Sec 4.3 provide more than a qualitative comparison? (R1) Since all components of the PDE are parameterized as neural networks learned from data, the physics loss only enforces agreement among these networks, not necessarily with actual physics laws. (R3) The assumption that the deep learning model is accurate enough to discover PDEs is not justified. (R1)
- **Experiments use non-standard, limited-scale datasets.** The evaluation uses regional datasets focused on China, not the full ERA5 dataset or established benchmarks like WeatherBench. (R1, R3) This makes it hard to compare with existing deep learning works and generalize results. (R1) The small scale raises scalability concerns: the physics loss is computed per grid point (x,y,t), so the number of training samples increases significantly with higher spatial and temporal resolutions. (R3)
- **Insufficient experimental details and reproducibility.** Training, validation, and test settings are not clearly described. (R1) Hyperparameter tuning, early stopping, network architectures, loss function weights, learning rate schedules, implementation of the finite difference method, and details of competitor model implementations are missing. (R2, R3) No code is shared. (R1) Ablation studies are lacking, especially to understand the importance of the physics loss and regularization loss. (R3) It is unclear what partial derivative candidate terms are used and why. (R3)
- **Several motivations in the paper are questionable.** In Section 1, the claim that deep learning models "lack the ability to generalize" is too strong; overfitting is a problem but models can still generalize. The statement about "physically inconsistent" predictions is vague. (R2) In Section 3.3, the argument that PINNs won't work for forecasting because a dense neural network lacks the advantages of more complicated architectures is misguided—the problem modeling (inputs/outputs) matters more than architecture. (R2) In Section 4.1, the claim that weather data has more non-local spatiotemporal dependencies than computer vision is incorrect (images have RGB channels and time dimension). (R2)
- **Minor issues.** Equation (7) is strange because PDE parameters are already learned and fixed—they should not appear there. (R3) The paper does not address how the model handles the inherent ill-posedness of downscaling and how the physics-informed component stabilizes the solution compared to purely data-driven methods. (R4) Computational complexity and scaling with grid size are not discussed. (R4)
