## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Language Guided Skill Discovery (LGSD), a framework that leverages large language models (LLMs) to guide unsupervised skill discovery toward semantically diverse behaviors. LGSD uses user prompts to constrain the skill search space and LLM-generated state descriptions to compute a language distance that serves as a diversity reward. Experiments on legged locomotion (Ant) and robot arm manipulation (FrankaCube) show that LGSD discovers more semantically distinct skills compared to five existing skill discovery baselines.

### Strengths
- **Novel and interesting contribution**: Using LLMs to guide skill discovery toward semantic diversity is a novel approach (R3, R4).
- **Theoretical foundation**: LGSD provides a proof that the language distance is a valid pseudometric, and the reward formulation using the representation function φ transforms the skill discovery problem into maximizing cumulative reward in the latent space (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- **Meaningful and controllable skills**: The method successfully links robot states with their semantic meaning, resulting in skills that are both meaningful and controllable via language (R2). Zero-shot language-specified goal tracking is demonstrated (R3).
- **Clear presentation**: The paper is well-written and clearly structured; Figure 2 effectively conveys the high-level idea, and Section 2.1 provides a helpful taxonomy of prior skill discovery work (R1, R4).
- **Demonstration on multiple domains**: Experiments cover both locomotion (Ant) and manipulation (FrankaCube) tasks, showing effectiveness across environments (R1).
- **Effective empirical results**: LGSD outperforms baselines in discovering semantically diverse skills; the latent space visualization in Appendix D makes skill identification easier than in the original Euclidean space (R4, R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Simple scenarios fail to justify LLM necessity**: Multiple reviewers note that the experimental tasks (e.g., “move north,” “push object away”) are too simple and could be achieved without an LLM—for example using logic gates or direct position encodings. The method would be better motivated by complex scenarios where the semantic knowledge of LLMs is truly essential (R1, R2, R3). Real-world experiments and video demonstrations are lacking (R1, R3).
- **Scalability and real-time concerns**: The method may call an LLM for every state transition to compute \(d_{lang}(s, s')\), raising questions about training speed and viability in real-time, high-dimensional environments. Generalizability and the need for expert-crafted prompts for each possible skill are unclear (R2, R3).
- **Unfair comparisons and baseline performance**: The comparison may be unfair because baselines lack the prior knowledge provided by user prompts and language embedding computation. In particular, the poor performance of LSD, CSD, and METRA on the Ant tasks is surprising and may stem from different task definitions or reward designs (R2, R3). A reviewer asks whether simply using the object’s Cartesian position with a Euclidean distance constraint (as in LSD) would yield similar results—an ablation that should be tested (R3).
- **Prompt control limits discovery**: Users must provide specific skill constraints via prompts, which raises the question of whether the method is truly “skill discovery” (R2). Too much human control may reduce the diversity and improvising nature of discovered skills (R4).
- **Coverage and language distance behavior need analysis**: Coverage in the manipulation task is worse than in locomotion, requiring explanation (R1). In Fig.7, LGSD’s coverage is less evenly distributed than CSD; it is unclear whether this pattern holds across runs (R1). The language distance metric itself is underexplored: it remains small when the object is at the origin but grows when the object is pushed. Is this distance linear in the robot state? Does it correspond to something fundamentally different from Euclidean distance on the description-level (e.g., “The distance is 4 units” vs “The distance is 6 units”)? (R3).
- **Presentation issues**: Figure 1 caption is not informative enough; Figure 2 lacks an initial explanation of \(d_{lang}\), which could confuse readers (R4). Line 319 uses undefined symbols \(s\) and \(g\) (R4).
- **Technical questions requiring clarification or ablation**: Why must φ be 1-Lipschitz continuous under \(d_{lang}\)? Would other constraints be possible? (R4). What is the dimensionality of the skill space and what benefits would higher dimensions offer? (R3). Can arbitrary directions (e.g., north-east) be achieved in the FrankCube environment? (R4).
