## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes using mesoscience concepts (compromise in competition, CIC) to explain generalization in deep learning by studying the dynamics of memorization vs. forgetting of training examples. It introduces a scale decomposition method and conducts empirical studies on small-scale CV (CIFAR, MNIST) and NLP (TREC) datasets using simple architectures (FC, CNN, TextCNN). It claims that CIC between memorizing and forgetting influences model generalizability and reinterprets regularization methods like dropout and L2 regularization.

### Strengths
- The problem is important, and the approach is simple in concept.
- The introduction of mesoscience for explaining DL phenomena is interesting and promising.
- The authors consider a variety of architectures (FC, CNN, TextCNN) – though simple, they are sufficiently different.
- The idea is straightforward to grasp; the "Dominant Mechanisms" section is well-written, incorporating numerous citations from other fields and providing helpful intuition and motivation.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of novelty and trivial findings:** The paper applies mesoscience to reach conclusions that are largely known. The tension between memorization/forgetting and the effect of regularization/dropout have been studied extensively. All reviewers found that the results offer no new insights beyond basic principles (e.g., variance reduction with larger sample sizes). One reviewer specifically noted that findings appear trivial and that the authors should clarify what is not trivial and how results generalize, especially given the emphasis on mesoscience.
- **Insufficient comparison with prior work:** The paper does not adequately engage with or compare against a substantial body of related prior work. Representative missing comparisons include: Maini et al. (2022), Garg and Roy (2023), Siddiqui et al. (2022) – methods that also study training dynamics and memorization. Other relevant work not discussed: Physics of language models Part 3.3 (knowledge capacity scaling laws), learning and generalization in overparameterized neural networks, implicit and explicit regularization effects of dropout, grokking, and memorization measures not relying on training dynamics (Feldman & Zhang 2020). This omission makes it unclear what new insights the proposed method provides.
- **Very limited experiments with cherry-picking concerns:** The experiments are limited in scope. No deep model architectures are tested; TextCNN is an unusual choice for NLP. The results appear cherry-picked: the authors use different models for different results, and there is no theorem to support generalizability. One reviewer explicitly stated that without a theorem, robust experimental findings are expected, but the current impression is that results may not generalize. Missing ablations and controlled checks (e.g., same architecture with different optimizers, learning rates, number of epochs) undermine confidence.
- **Poor clarity and presentation:** Despite the simple idea, the paper is overly verbose, complex, and unclear. Specific issues:
    - Figures (1, 2a, 2b, 5) have insufficient captions; variables (w, “(-)”) and lines (e.g., purple/black in Figure 5) are not explained. The caption for Figure 1 does not provide a high-level overview.
    - Figure 2a: number of epochs not stated; effect of batch size on number of gradient updates not considered.
    - Figure 2b: x-axis and variable w are not defined in the caption.
    - Line 157: The statement about “mimicking the integrative process of neuronal activity in the brain” is inaccurate without a supporting neuroscience citation.
    - Subsection 2.4 is difficult to follow; CIC is not formally defined (no mathematical definition), and conclusions are unclear (they appear to restate the known fact that larger sample sizes reduce variance).
    - Line 262: “stability condition” is undefined; it is not transparent whether the conclusion (“no stability condition on the element scale”) applies universally or only to the two selected points.
    - The S2 subset (size of memorized examples) is ignored despite its potential significance.
- **Missing experiments and analyses requested by reviewers:**
    - How do observations scale with model size (e.g., BERT, LLMs)?
    - Can experiments extend beyond noisy labels to spurious correlations (e.g., Nam et al. 2020)?
    - Are findings consistent across different optimizers and learning rates?
    - How robust are the results under different training lengths (epochs)?
- **Background on mesoscience lacking:** Since the concept is unfamiliar to the ML audience, a more comprehensive background section with examples is needed to make the work approachable.
- **Reference formatting:** Several references are given as “arXiv” instead of the actual venue (conference/journal); this should be corrected using resources like DBLP or Semantic Scholar.
