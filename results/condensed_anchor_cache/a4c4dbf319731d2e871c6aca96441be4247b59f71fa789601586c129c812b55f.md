## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates the durability of safeguards for open-weight LLMs via case studies on two methods (RepNoise and Tamper Attack Resistance, TAR). By replicating published work with small changes to evaluation methodology (e.g., random seeds, prompt formats, hyperparameters, using the standard Huggingface SFT trainer instead of a custom trainer), the authors show that these alterations can nullify or even reverse original claims of robustness. The study emphasizes that minor configuration differences drastically affect perceived effectiveness, and calls for more careful, constrained threat models and rigorous evaluation in future work.

### Strengths
- **Timely and important topic:** The paper addresses a critical security issue—the robustness of safeguards for open-weight LLMs—and is clearly motivated by the need for trustworthy evaluations. *(R1, R2)*
- **High empirical rigor and replication value:** The study is thorough, covering multiple factors (random seeds, prompt formatting, hyperparameters, trainer differences) that influence attack success. One reviewer (score 8) particularly highlighted the importance of replication studies for the community and praised the detailed validation, including exact comparison with the original papers. *(R2, R1, R3)*
- **Effective demonstration of fragility:** Simple modifications (e.g., changing random seeds, using the HF trainer) are shown to break claimed safeguards, underscoring how evaluation pitfalls can mislead audiences. *(R3, R1, R4)*
- **Practical implications:** The insights provide valuable guidance for researchers and policymakers on the limitations of current durability evaluations, stressing the need for testing across diverse scenarios. *(R1, R4)*
- **Clear discussion of evaluation difficulty:** The paper includes a thoughtful discussion on the difficulty of evaluating unlearning methods and the necessity for cautious claims. *(R3)*

### Weaknesses
- **No proposed solutions or improvements:** The paper identifies weaknesses in existing defenses but does not explore alternative approaches or offer new methods to enhance durability. *(R1, R4)* Suggests future work without concrete recommendations (except calling for careful threat modeling). *(R1, R4)*
- **Limited scope (two methods, one model family):** Analysis is confined to RepNoise and TAR on LLaMA-2 and WMDP, narrowing generalizability. It is unknown whether other defenses or model architectures face similar evaluation challenges. *(R1, R4)*
- **High computational cost:** The methodology requires extensive runs across random seeds, prompts, and configurations, limiting scalability for future researchers. *(R1)*
- **Cursory analysis of performance trade-offs:** The paper notes that defenses can improve safety while sacrificing other tasks but does not clearly analyze acceptable performance loss or what constitutes an effective defense in light of these trade-offs. *(R1)*
- **Ambiguities in technical details and language:** Several descriptions are vague. For example, “RepNoise trains a model to push its representations of HarmfulQA data points at each layer toward random noise” lacks formalization of loss function, noise distribution, and sampling details. Results described as “not more than a moderate amount” lack quantifiable metrics. Attack fine-tuning details (dataset composition, size, hyperparameters) are insufficiently specified. *(R2)*
- **Hyperparameter presentation hard to follow:** Hyperparameters for the replication and original studies are not always clearly presented; more tabular overviews like Table 1 are needed for all experiments. *(R2)*
- **Actionable lessons not fully drawn:** The paper could provide a checklist or summary of evaluation principles to make the lessons more directly useful to future evaluators. *(R2, R3)*
- **Reproducibility unverifiable:** One reviewer could not verify the reproducibility statement because supplementary material was not accessible. *(R2)*
- **Unfair evaluation of multiple-choice datasets?** One reviewer (R3) argued that evaluating full answers with human/LLM judges (as opposed to the standard practice of checking logits for answer letters) may be unfair; comparing logits after adding the chat template could be more appropriate. This raises a concern about the paper’s own evaluation methodology for multiple-choice benchmarks.
- **Concerning zero exact-match results in Table 2:** The reviewer noted that some exact-match benchmarks yield zero results, raising doubts about evaluation validity and whether such metrics are appropriate. *(R3)*
- **Missing analysis for other multiple-choice datasets:** It is unclear whether human evaluation and LLM judges were used for all multiple-choice datasets in the study. *(R3)*
- **Need to consider additional pitfalls:** The paper could discuss other potential pitfalls beyond those identified (e.g., differences in data ordering, optimizer choices). *(R3, R4)*
