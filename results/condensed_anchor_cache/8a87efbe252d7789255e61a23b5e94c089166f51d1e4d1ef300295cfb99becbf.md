## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents a statistical analysis of augmentation-based self-supervised representation learning by formalizing the augmentation operator and connecting it to an RKHS. It introduces the augmentation complexity and ratio trace to bound downstream error, separating the roles of model and augmentation. The four reviewers range from very positive (8,8) to moderately positive (6) to critical (5). All acknowledge the framework’s theoretical value and rigorous structure, but raise major concerns about clarity, the strength of the key assumption (isometry/soft invariance), practical relevance (bounds may be vacuous for real-world augmentation complexity), and several technical gaps. The minority reviewer (score 5) finds the paper inaccessible and questions the significance of the assumed target function form; the high-scoring reviewers are more positive but still note significant limitations.

### Strengths
- The paper gives a well-structured foundation for augmentation-based learning, with the augmentation operator motivated and its central role clearly highlighted.
- Error bounds cleanly separate the contributions of the model and the augmentation.
- The analysis is rigorous, and the appearance of spectral properties of the augmentation is intriguing.
- Presentation improves over competitors; discussions and remarks are enlightening and anticipate reader questions; literature review is adequate.
- Experiments (theoretical values for masking, empirical estimation on `wikipedia-simple`) complement the theory and justify the introduction of augmentation complexity \(\kappa\).
- The theoretical framework is original and interesting, connecting self-supervised learning to an RKHS where the target \(\mathbb{E}[g(A)|x]\) is an element of an RKHS with kernel given by the augmentation-induced joint distribution.
- The paper is generally clearly written and easy to follow; proof sketches are useful.
- The formalism of dual operators and the isometry property is elegant and builds insightfully on previous work.
- Main results (Theorems 1 and 2) go further than prior work by characterizing how much information the augmentations alone provide, decomposing error into approximation and estimation, and showing that the empirical top-\(d\) eigenspace can yield small approximation error even when \(\lambda_{d+1}\) and \(\lambda_d\) are close.
- Analysis of augmentation complexity for realistic masking strategies is novel and interesting.

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity and presentation:** The text is very dense and compressed, not accessible to a general audience, and requires major revision for readability (R1, R3). Many concepts (e.g., the distribution \(P_{AX}\), the space \(\mathcal{F}_B(\Gamma;\epsilon)\), the choice of \(d\)) need more motivation (R1, R3). Assumptions and notations are insufficiently clarified: it is not stated explicitly that \(P_\mathcal{A}\) has a density and that \(p(a|x), p(x|a)\) exist; \(A\) in \(\mathbb{E}(g(A)|x)\) is not defined; \(X\) is not defined as a random variable; the notational difference between \(p\) and \(p(\cdot,\cdot)\) is small (R1). Symbols are sometimes loosely defined or introduced before their formal definition (e.g., \(\hat{\mathcal{H}}_d\) is mentioned before \(\hat{\mathcal{H}}_\Gamma\); R3). The “proof sketch” of Theorem 1 only repeats the decomposition and could be omitted (R3). Problem description should include a high-level overview with early symbol definitions (R3).  
- **Assumption 1 (soft invariance / isometry):** The assumption is strong and not well motivated. It is difficult to interpret whether it holds for real-world functions; the RKHS membership is strict and even a small perturbation may violate it (R2, R3, R4). The assumption that \(f^*\) can be written as an expectation of some \(g\) on augmentations lacks clear justification and appears introduced for analytical convenience (R3, R4). The comparison with the source condition of Cabannes et al. could be more developed (R2).  
- **Encoder and predictor specification:** The claim that the encoder can be arbitrary is inaccurate because it must take the form (10) to be included in the RKHS (R3). The bound constraint on the final predictor (Definition 1) is not common in the literature; its use needs justification (R2).  
- **Practical relevance and vacuous bounds:** Empirical augmentation complexity for real-world data is massive (e.g., \(\kappa \approx 10^{100}\) for masked language modeling), suggesting that the bounds in Theorems 1 and 2 are likely vacuous in practice. The paper does not adequately address this limitation (R4). The dimensionality dependence in the upper bound is pessimistic, as the authors admit (R3).  
- **Comparison with Saunshi et al. (2022):** The claim that the analysis refutes the vacuity result of Saunshi et al. is misleading. Saunshi’s approximately-disjoint augmentations would lead to \(\lambda_{d+1}\approx 1\) and \(\tau\approx 1\), causing a blowup in equation (14). More discussion of this relationship (including possible connections between augmentation complexity and approximately-disjoint augmentations) is needed (R4).  
- **Comparison with RIP:** The analogy to the Restricted Isometry Property is inaccurate because RIP holds for every vector, while the isometry property bears only on a single target function \(f^*\) (R2).  
- **Technical issues:**  
  - Proposition 1, part (iv): The statement “\(g^*\) satisfies Assumption 1” is unclear (Assumption 1 is about \(f^*\)); likely equation (1) is meant. The proof appears incorrect: \(\|g_1\|^2_{P_A}\) should be \(\langle g_1, (I-\Gamma\Gamma^*)g_1\rangle_{P_A}\), and the argument seems to imply \(R(\Gamma^*)\subseteq \mathcal{F}_B(\Gamma;\epsilon)\) for all \(\epsilon\), which is questionable (R4).  
  - Definition 3: Is \(G^{-1}\) well-defined when \(d=\infty\)? (R1)  
  - Remark after Lemma 2: What is \(\|\cdot\|_n\)? (R1)  
  - How is \(d\) chosen? Equation (20) motivates a large gap between \(\lambda_d\) and \(\lambda_{d+1}\), but the result improves upon HaoChen21 and Saunshi22 for selecting \(d\); further explanation is needed (R1).  
  - Is \(\bar{\phi}\) an eigenfunction of \(\Gamma^*\bar{\Gamma}\)? How restrictive is the condition \(\hat{\phi}=\bar{\phi}\)? (R1)  
  - Lemma 9: Does it need a condition on boundedness of \(QDQ^T\) or absolute convergence of \(\sum_{i=1}^\infty \lambda_i q_i^T q_i\)? (R1)  
  - Unbounded kernels: The paper assumes \(K_X\) and \(K_A\) are square integrable, but does not prove there exist realistic augmentations for which this holds. Examples (e.g., Dirac or Gaussian augmentations) could violate this; merely claiming “we assumed they were integrable” is insufficient (R2).  
  - Equation (9) requires a regularization that depends on \(B\) and \(\varepsilon\); what happens if this is violated? (R3)  
  - Why is \(G^{-1/2}\) required in (12)? (R3)  
  - “As shown in Wang et al. (2022b)…” is an existence statement? (R3)  
  - Theorem 2: \(\overline{\lambda}_d^{-1}\) and \(\gamma_G\) may depend on \(N\); can anything be said about their convergence or boundedness? (R4)  
  - The soft-invariance condition (8) seems to imply that \(u_i\approx 0\) for \(\lambda_i<1-\varepsilon\), i.e., \(f^*\) is mostly encoded in the top eigenvalues. This is not a consequence of any known “RIP” property and should be discussed (R2).  
  - Section 5: The term “strong” augmentation is obscure; if the augmentation distribution is independent of \(x\), the complexity is 1. Is the dependency between \(A\) and \(X\) what defines “strength”? (R3)
