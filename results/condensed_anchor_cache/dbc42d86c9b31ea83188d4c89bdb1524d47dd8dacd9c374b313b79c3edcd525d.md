## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Direct Q-function Optimization (DQO), an offline reinforcement learning method that frames text generation as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) and uses the soft actor-critic (SAC) framework to directly optimize a Q-function parameterized by the language model. The aim is to improve multi-step reasoning (e.g., math problem-solving) over bandit-based approaches like DPO/DRO. Experiments on GSM8K and MATH show DQO outperforming prior methods, with particular gains on small-scale open-source LLMs like Qwen2-7B-Instruct and Gemma-1.1-7B-it.

### Strengths
1. Addresses a significant issue: sparse rewards and multi-step reasoning in LLM alignment and generation.
2. MDP formulation is more suitable for long-horizon, step-by-step reasoning tasks compared to bandit-based methods (e.g., DRO).
3. Incorporates process rewards, λ-return, and importance sampling to effectively leverage step-level feedback from offline data.
4. Theorem is presented straightforwardly; the paper is generally well-written and easy to follow.
5. Experimental results on GSM8K and MATH show promising performance, especially on small open-source models.
6. (Minority view, reviewer 4 score 5) The approach demonstrates potential for using process supervision without requiring preference-based datasets.

### Weaknesses
1. **Ground-truth reward assumption undermines the log π*(a|s) representation.** The paper assumes a ground-truth reward function (Lines 149-151), yet later uses log π* to represent the reward (Eq 8, Eq 9). Why not directly learn a policy that maximizes expected return under the ground-truth reward, making the log π* proxy redundant?
2. **Questionable transition from Eq(9) to Eq(11).** Eq(7) is a value function fitting target, not a policy optimization target. Substituting the reparameterized reward into Eq(7) does not clearly achieve policy optimization; the connection between Q-function error minimization and policy improvement is not established.
3. **Conflict in reward definitions.** Line 223 defines reward as log π_ref + r, while Eq 14 uses log(π_ref/π) + r. This inconsistency undermines the validity of the method.
4. **Unjustified use of importance sampling in value function learning.** Section 3.3 uses importance sampling (Eq 6, Eq 7) for value function learning, where it is not typically needed. The standard approach does not involve importance sampling for value estimation; a compelling justification is missing.
5. **Method heavily relies on process value accuracy, but analysis is lacking.** The paper does not analyze whether process values are fairly accessible or measurable, nor how to produce them at scale. The similarity to process-supervised reward model approaches is not clarified.
6. **Excessive formulas and presentation issues.** The paper is lengthy and difficult to follow due to many equations; suggestions include removing Eq 9 (redundant with Eq 8), moving Eq 6-7 to preliminaries/appendix, and condensing Section 3.2-3.3 by referring to original SAC papers. Minor formula errors: missing β in Eq(2), r vs \(\bar{r}\) in Eq(7), missing minus sign in Eq(11), unclear notation in Eq(12) (\(s_{h+1} \sim P(\cdot|s_h,a_h)\) vs deterministic concatenation), and Eq(14) should likely be \(r(a_{h+l}, s_{h+l})\).
7. **Experimental results not convincing enough.** For Qwen2-7B-Instruct, DQO shows <1% improvement over baselines on GSM8K and MATH. No statistical significance tests (e.g., standard error over multiple seeds) are provided. Additional experiments on other models or tasks are needed to demonstrate stronger improvement.
8. **Fairness of comparison concerns.** According to Table 8, DQO uses datasets up to four times larger than DPO (even though DPO's subset is sampled from DQO's dataset). The small performance margin when dataset sizes are similar raises questions about the claimed superiority.
9. **Lack of experimental details.** The paper does not discuss how generated/augmented responses were evaluated, the distribution of positive/negative responses in training/test data, or detailed training parameters (e.g., number of epochs for DRO and DQO). This makes it difficult to explain performance discrepancies, e.g., why the results for Qwen2-7B-Instruct differ from those reported in related work [A1] (pass rates 82.3% GSM8K, 49.6% MATH).
10. **Hard to assess benefits of MDP formulation.** Without importance sampling, DQO's performance (Table 5) is worse than DRO's (Table 4). This suggests the key factor may be importance sampling rather than the MDP formulation itself.
11. **Risk of overfitting.** The reliance on offline data and importance sampling introduces overfitting risk, especially if data does not represent diverse real-world scenarios.
12. **Potentially high computational costs.** Training involves learning Q and V functions, λ-return, and importance sampling—more complex than DRO. The paper does not discuss computational efficiency, nor compare with chain-of-thought prompting [A2] or other lightweight intermediate-check methods.
13. **Insufficient discussion on integration challenges.** While SAC, λ-return, and importance sampling are incorporated, the specific challenges encountered during this integration are not highlighted.
14. **Unbalanced data and process reward experiments missing.** Table 1 claims DQO can learn from unbalanced samples, but no experiments substantiate this. Section 4.4 presents a synthetic process reward mechanism, but more explanation and experimentation with different process score designs are needed.
15. **Minor notation/typo issues.** Eq(6) expectation should be w.r.t. \(a_h\); Line 247 should read \(V^{\pi}\); Eq(16) update should give \(G^{\lambda}_{\phi,\theta}(s_h) = G^{(H-h)}_{\phi,\theta}(s_h)\) when λ=1; Line 240-241 should reference DRO instead of DQO.
16. (From reviewer 2) Clarify key differences between DQO and process-supervised reward model approaches. (From reviewer 4) Explain why DQO with λ=1 outperforms λ=0.95, and discuss correlation between learned value function and constructed process scores during testing.
