## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies sycophancy (tailoring responses to align with user views even when incorrect) in LLMs. It finds that both model scaling and instruction tuning increase sycophancy on three classification tasks with no correct answers (politics, etc.) and on a simple addition task with objectively wrong statements. The authors propose a synthetic data intervention that reformats 17 public NLP tasks to encourage robustness to user opinions via lightweight fine-tuning. The intervention significantly reduces sycophancy on held-out prompts without harming performance on MMLU or Big-Bench Hard. Evaluations use PaLM/Flan-LLM models at 8B, 62B, and 540B parameters; the smallest model (Flan-LLM-8B) did not respond well to the intervention.

### Strengths
- Clear motivation and writing; well-structured with smooth flow; effective figures. (R1, R4)
- Lightweight fine-tuning approach that is accessible and easy to replicate across models. (R1, R3)
- Comprehensive results across three model sizes (8B, 62B, 540B) showing clear reductions in sycophancy; demonstrates that scaling and instruction tuning increase sycophancy (though novelty of the scaling point is noted as limited by one reviewer). (R1, R3, R4)
- Intervention does not compromise performance on MMLU or Big-Bench Hard, preserving existing learned knowledge. (R3)
- Synthetic data leverages openly available datasets with good variety (17 total). (R2)
- Well-fleshed limitations section. (R2)
- Convincing generalization to prompts not seen during training. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- Model selection limited to a single model family (PaLM/Flan-LLM). Missing evaluation on diverse architectures (e.g., LLaMA, Mixtral, GPT, Gemini, encoder-decoder models with different pre-training objectives) to ensure sycophancy findings and intervention effectiveness are not artifacts of this specific family. (R2: limited set; R3: single model type)
- Smallest model (Flan-LLM-8B) did not respond well to the intervention, raising concerns about scalability and applicability to resource-constrained environments. The reasons for failure are not explained, and it is unclear whether the approach could be adapted for smaller models. (R1)
- Experiments restricted to multiple-choice classification tasks with a narrow range of prompt formats (single prompt format used throughout). Does not evaluate generative settings with open-ended responses, where the model is not limited to predefined choices and metrics like repetition rate may not apply. (R1: limited to multiple-choice; R2: single prompt format; R4: missing open-ended)
- User beliefs are always explicitly stated; evaluation does not test scenarios where beliefs are implied, subtly embedded, or require inference from context, limiting real-world generalizability. (R3)
- Study on objectively incorrect statements is limited to simple addition tasks. To support the claim that models are sycophantic for objectively wrong answers, evaluation should include broader misinformation (e.g., false medical claims, fake news, conspiracy theories) beyond basic arithmetic. (R3)
- No comparison to alternative methods for reducing sycophancy, such as prompting techniques (e.g., System 2 attention). If prompting is effective and requires less effort (and may generalize more easily to open-ended questions without gold answers), the need for fine-tuning is challenged. (R4; also R2 implicitly asks about other ways without fine-tuning)
- The finding that scaling increases sycophancy is not novel (previously discussed in [1]), reducing its contribution; the more novel aspect is the instruction tuning finding. (R1)  *[Note: Other reviewers valued this insight as a strength, reflecting disagreement.]*
- Intervention requires fine-tuning, which may not be feasible for all use-cases (e.g., limited access to model weights or computational resources). (R2)
- Table 1 and Figure 1 are nearly duplicated. (R3)
- Missing ablation studies on prompt formats or filtration details. It is unclear what proportion of synthesized examples is filtered out for each model size (larger models likely retain more), and whether results are sensitive to the exact prompt phrasing. (R2: question on prompt ablation; R3: question on filtered proportions)
