## Merged Review

### Summary
The authors propose a method to automatically select high-quality, diverse subsets of multimodal instruction data for fine-tuning MiniGPT-4. They first extract heuristic features (CLIP score, answer length, etc.), cluster the data, train MiniGPT-4 on each cluster, and evaluate on four VQA datasets to obtain “genuine quality labels.” A small network (data selector) is then trained to predict these quality labels from the feature vectors. Finally, data is re-clustered and the selector picks the best 200 examples (≈6% of the original 3.4k cc_sbu_align dataset). InstructionGPT-4 trained on these 200 examples outperforms both the original MiniGPT-4 (trained on all data) and a random 200‑example baseline across several benchmarks, including VQA, MME, and MMBench, as well as GPT‑4‑based evaluation.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles a highly significant question: the impact of instruction data quality on MLLM performance, which has rarely been discussed before.
- The proposed data selection method is reasonable and reasonably novel (beyond adapting text‑only methods like InstructMining), with additional steps such as clustering for diversity.
- The method is clearly described and concise; the entire pipeline is easy to understand.
- Results show that training on only 200 selected examples can outperform the full MiniGPT-4, demonstrating the efficiency of less but high‑quality data for reducing compute while maintaining or improving performance.
- Evaluation is extensive, covering both benchmark tasks (MME, MMBench, VQA datasets) and open‑ended GPT‑4‑based evaluation.
- The authors ablate each component of the method (clustering, individual indicators, the data selector) and justify each design choice. The single‑indicator ablations (Table 5) are particularly useful for future work on data quality.
- The paper contains few grammatical errors and is generally well‑proofread.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of baselines.** The paper compares only against the full MiniGPT-4 and a random baseline. Several established data‑selection/pruning methods are not included, such as Influence Functions, Data Shapley, HyDRA, Diverse Ensembles (Meding et al., ICLR 2022), prototypicality (Sorscher et al., NeurIPS 2022), Meta‑Weight‑Net (based on meta‑learning), or even the mentioned InstructMining. Without these comparisons, it is difficult to assess the novelty and relative effectiveness of the proposed approach. (Reviewers 1, 3, 4)
- **Generality and scaling are unclear.** The method is tested only on a single dataset (cc_sbu_align, 3.4k examples) and a single MLLM (MiniGPT-4). It is unknown whether the approach would work on larger, more diverse multimodal datasets or transfer to other models/architectures. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4)
- **Evaluation limitations reduce confidence in results.**
  - Results appear to come from single runs; variance from selecting a small (200) subset could be high. Multiple random seeds or repeats would strengthen the conclusion. (Reviewer 2)
  - The GPT‑4 evaluation uses only 60 questions, which may not be enough to thoroughly test the model across diverse queries. (Reviewer 2)
  - InstructionGPT-4 performs noticeably worse than MiniGPT-4 on commonsense reasoning and OCR tasks (Table 2) and on natural relation and object localisation (Figure 3), raising concerns that the small selected subset misses important skills. (Reviewer 2)
  - The improvement over MiniGPT-4 is marginal in several benchmarks, and MiniGPT-4 itself is a weak baseline on MME and MMBench, making the gains unconvincing. (Reviewer 3)
- **Data selector & process issues.**
  - The data selector assigns the same quality label to every data point in a cluster, even though the label reflects the joint contribution of the whole subset – this is conceptually problematic. (Reviewer 3)
  - The description of the data selector’s input is confusing: during training it seems to take features of a whole subset, but during testing it seems to take features of a single data point. The paper should clarify how a single point’s feature vector (which may be high‑dimensional after concatenating embeddings from a cluster) is used. (Reviewers 1, 3)
  - The purpose of re‑clustering the data before final selection is not well justified; it would be more natural to use the predicted quality scores directly. (Reviewer 1)
  - The relationship between the validation data used to generate quality labels and the final evaluation benchmarks is unclear; why should performance on those four VQA datasets indicate performance on others? (Reviewer 3)
  - The method can be time‑consuming because it requires repeatedly training the model on validation subsets, which is especially problematic for larger datasets. (Reviewer 4)
- **Vague writing and logic gaps.** Examples include: “steer … in a particular manner” (what manner?), “more completely cover the various aspects of multimodal data quality” (more than what?), and unnumbered equations with undefined symbols (e.g., what is x in Section 2.3?). Such imprecision undermines clarity. (Reviewer 1)
- **Questions about specific features and design choices.**
  - Length alone achieves the third‑best single‑feature result in Table 5, only behind CLIP and the full set. This suggests length might be a shortcut overfitted to the test set. (Reviewer 1)
  - Why does MME performance drop sharply when moving from two to three layers in Table 5? (Reviewer 2)
  - How do you guarantee that K‑means and spectral clustering return clusters of equal size? (Reviewer 1)
  - It would be useful to see at what number of selected points the method becomes no better than random (extending Figure 7 with a random line). (Reviewer 2)
  - No evidence is provided that the data selector can be directly applied to other multimodal datasets, as claimed. (Reviewer 4)
