## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a benchmark for evaluating the zero-shot association capability of Multi-modal Large Language Models (MLLMs)—the ability to link observations to prior practice memory using an underlying concept (e.g., adjective or verb). The benchmark is constructed via an annotation-free method that transforms existing object concept learning datasets, with a data refinement step to remove confusion. Three task levels are defined: single-step association, synchronous multi-step association, and asynchronous multi-step association. Extensive experiments test open-source and closed-source MLLMs (including GPT-4V, mixture-of-experts models), various memory strategies, and human experts. Results show that all current MLLMs, including GPT-4V, perform significantly worse than humans, revealing a substantial gap.

### Strengths
* **Originality and novel perspective**: The work introduces a new benchmark specifically targeting association, an overlooked cognitive ability in MLLMs, and offers a fresh evaluation perspective. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4)
* **Annotation-free construction**: The method to construct association tasks without costly manual annotation is practical and innovative, enhancing reproducibility and usability. (Reviewers 1, 3, 4)
* **Significance of findings**: The benchmark reveals a clear and large gap between current MLLMs and human performance in association, highlighting an important direction for future AGI research. (Reviewers 1, 3)
* **Comprehensive evaluation**: A wide range of MLLMs are tested (open-source, closed-source, MoE models), along with multiple memory strategies and human expert comparisons, providing a thorough initial assessment. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)
* **Three distinct task levels**: The design of single-step, synchronous, and asynchronous associations covers different complexities of associative reasoning. (Reviewers 1, 3, 4)
* **Clarity and structure**: The paper is well-organized, with clear definitions and explanations of the association tasks. (Reviewer 3)
* **Minority positive view**: Reviewer 4 (rating 8) particularly praises the fresh approach and the innovative annotation-free method, while noting the evaluation is a good starting point. In contrast, Reviewer 3 (rating 5) is more critical of novelty and execution; this disagreement shows the work is viewed as promising but with clear areas for improvement.

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty compared to existing benchmarks**: Reviewer 3 (confidence 5) argues that evaluating association/reasoning is not entirely new; similar benchmarks like CommonSenseQA and HellaSwag already test associative and commonsense reasoning. The paper lacks a detailed comparison to these benchmarks to clearly demonstrate its unique contributions beyond the specific focus on adjectives and verbs.
2. **Missing human performance and stronger baselines**: Reviewer 2 (confidence 3) notes that Table 1 lacks human performance, which is essential to understand the headroom for improvement (especially since MLLMs already achieve ~80% accuracy on single-step tasks). Reviewer 2 also requests inclusion of more recent models like GPT-4o. Reviewer 1 (confidence 5) suggests checking correlation with existing benchmarks to understand whether this capability is orthogonal or overlapping.
3. **Clarity of task definitions and figures**: Multiple reviewers found specific parts unclear.
   * Reviewer 2 (confidence 3) finds the “deduction” task in Table 1 poorly explained; it needs an illustration similar to Figure 2 for the association task.
   * Reviewer 2 finds Figure 3 (synchronous association) difficult to understand; pseudo-code showing inputs, expected outputs, and metric calculation is needed.
   * Reviewer 2 notes that Figure 6 is not self-explanatory and its textual description is too brief.
   * Reviewer 1 (confidence 5) suggests adding more figures to explain the annotation-free transformation process from object concept learning datasets.
4. **Lack of in-depth error analysis**: Reviewer 1 (confidence 5) calls for ablation studies to determine whether limitations are due to the image encoder/resolution or intrinsic to the LLM component. Reviewer 4 (confidence 3) notes that while failure cases are analyzed, a deeper understanding of why failures occur is missing. The performance gap in Figure 3 lacks explanation, raising concerns about evaluation protocol effectiveness (Reviewer 2, confidence 3).
5. **Scope and generalizability concerns**: 
   * Reviewer 4 (confidence 3) points out that evaluation is limited to zero-shot association using adjective/verb semantic concepts, which may not capture real-world complexity. The three task levels may not capture the gradual, contextually influenced nature of human associative learning.
   * Reviewer 1 (confidence 5) questions why the study sticks to zero-shot setting (e.g., practice memory could be similar to few-shot) and why only MLLMs are tested; a text-only benchmark version is not considered. The rationale for focusing on MLLMs over other architectures is not clearly argued.
6. **Subjectivity and bias in tasks and dataset**:
   * Reviewer 3 (confidence 5) notes that association tasks can be subjective (different humans may perceive different relationships), but the paper does not address how such subjectivity is mitigated.
   * Reviewers 3 and 4 (confidence 5 and 3) raise concerns about dataset biases: no clear methodology is given for identifying or removing bias in the initial object concept learning dataset annotations; the constraint \(z_{ij} \in \{0,1\}\) in Eq. (1) could introduce bias from human evaluations; and how limitations of original datasets affect results is not addressed (Reviewer 4).
7. **Resource intensiveness and reproducibility**: Reviewer 3 (confidence 5) points out that the comprehensive experiments with multiple models and memory strategies require significant computational resources, yet the paper does not provide hardware/software specifications, making replication difficult for researchers with limited resources.
8. **Insufficient discussion of performance gap and implications**: 
   * Reviewer 3 (confidence 5) finds the discussion superficial: no exploration of practical applications, theoretical contributions to AI/cognitive modeling, or specific strategies for closing the MLLM–human gap.
   * Reviewer 2 (confidence 3) states that the significant gap between MLLMs and humans in Figure 3 is not explained, which could raise doubts about the evaluation protocol.
   * Reviewer 1 (confidence 5) notes that without correlation to existing benchmarks, it is unclear how to improve this capability.
9. **Missing experimental details on prompting and format**: Reviewer 2 (confidence 3) asks for clarification on whether images are concatenated or input separately, whether textual instructions are interleaved to ensure correct intention, and whether tasks are multi-choice QA with well-mapped answers. These details are omitted but important for reproducibility.
10. **Model selection rationale not explained**: Reviewer 3 (confidence 5) notes that the criteria for selecting specific MLLMs for evaluation are not clearly justified, and a systematic comparison with existing benchmarks to motivate the need for a new one is missing.
