## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers generally appreciate the breadth of models compared (text, expression, sequence) and the comprehensive task list, but raise significant concerns about novelty, reproducibility, methodology, and interpretation. One reviewer (R2, score 3) found no strengths and criticized missing code, data, and limited novelty. The other three (scores 5-6) acknowledge the interesting cross‑modal comparison but point to multiple weaknesses including data leakage, misleading evaluation of text models, poor performance relative to bag‑of‑words, reliance on linear probes, class imbalance, and lack of important baselines and ablations.

### Strengths
- The benchmark compares a wide breadth of models (text‑based, expression‑based, DNA‑sequence, protein‑language models), covering the multi‑modal landscape of genomic ML (R1, R4).
- The list of benchmark tasks is comprehensive (genomic properties, regulatory functions, localization, biological processes, protein properties) and incorporates many commonly used datasets (R1, R4).
- The paper is well‑organized, clear, and easy to read (R1, R3, R4).
- The topic of using different data sources to understand genes is interesting (R3).
- The benchmark may help direct research efforts toward more effective models (R1).
- **Note:** One reviewer (R2) explicitly stated the paper has no particular strengths.

### Weaknesses
1. **Data leakage / pre‑training overlap:** The benchmark does not consider similarity between pre‑training and test sets; models may have already seen test data, inflating performance (R1).
2. **Text‑model advantage may be misleading:** Text‑based models (LLMs) perform well on regulatory and protein‑class tasks (e.g., TF vs non‑TF), but their NCBI summaries often contain explicit information about these properties. The paper should analyze how much explicit information is provided rather than learned (R4).
3. **Advanced methods not better than bag‑of‑words:** LLMs, RNA‑seq models, etc., do not outperform a simple bag‑of‑words baseline on many tasks, making it unclear whether future models will improve gene function prediction (R3).
4. **Limitations of linear‑probe evaluation:** The evaluation relies solely on linear/logistic regression on pretrained embeddings, despite some models (e.g., Geneformer) being designed for fine‑tuning (R4). A linear model may not transfer to new genes or situations (R3). The paper also does not explore whether similarity metrics (e.g., cosine similarity) correlate with predictions (R4).
5. **Underserved tasks and class imbalance:** For tasks where no model performs well, the paper does not analyze dataset size or contributing factors (R1). Many binary “biological process” tasks involve only tens of positive genes out of ~20k, causing extreme imbalance. The paper lacks confusion matrices and does not confirm use of stratified cross‑validation (R4).
6. **Cross‑species issues:** The benchmark includes tasks for mouse and pig, but some models (e.g., Geneformer) are trained only on human data, introducing bias (R4).
7. **Missing code, data, and reproducibility:** No code or supplementary files are provided (anonymous GitHub link not accessible in review). A benchmark must supply reproducible code and at least sample data (R2). The paper does not compare to existing similar tools like OpenBioLink, PyKEEN 1.0, or the Open Biomedical Network Benchmark (R2).
8. **Insufficient data description and visualization:** There is no dedicated section describing data statistics, figures, or distribution of tasks (R2). The appendix should include proper citations for all data sources and links to servers (R2).
9. **Limited novelty:** The dataset is entirely sourced from public databases and merely reorganized; this contributes little new to the field (R2). R3 echoes that using gene embeddings only as linear‑model inputs does not enable discovery of new pathways or functions.
10. **Factual inconsistency:** Line 392 claims “ScGPT-H was the top performer in two different families of tasks,” but Figure 2 does not show ScGPT‑H as top performer in any task (R4).
11. **Missing experimental details:** The number of genes used in the benchmark is not mentioned (R4).
12. **Dimensionality impact unaddressed:** The effect of embedding dimensionality on linear model performance is not explored; high dimensionality may degrade accuracy (R4).
13. **Abstract too long** (R4).
14. **Missing state‑of‑the‑art models:** EVO and other long‑context protein language models are not evaluated, despite rapid growth in this area (R1).
15. **No ensemble analysis:** The paper does not test an ensemble of best models (e.g., voting) as a step toward multi‑modality, though this is suggested as future direction (R1).
