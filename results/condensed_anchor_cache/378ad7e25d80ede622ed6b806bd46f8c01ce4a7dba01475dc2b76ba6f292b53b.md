## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a benchmark of graphs with heterogeneous tabular node features and evaluates a wide range of graph and tabular models (GBDTs, GNNs, tabular deep learning) on 10+ datasets with 18 baselines. Reviewers generally agree the experiments are extensive but raise serious concerns about the novelty of the problem setting, the omission of closely related benchmarks (RelBench, 4DBInfer), and the lack of significant algorithmic contributions. Two reviewers rated the paper higher (score 5) citing the practical importance and solid experiments; two rated it lower (score 3) arguing the setting is fundamentally a relational database problem and the analyses are insufficient. All reviewers note limited novelty compared to prior work.

### Strengths
- The research topic (improving tabular ML with graph structure) is interesting and practically important.
- Experiments are comprehensive: 10+ datasets (10 according to reviewer 3; 11 tasks from 8 datasets according to reviewer 4) and 10+ baseline methods (18 methods per reviewer 3), covering GBDTs, GNNs, and tabular deep learning models.
- The paper compares a wide range of methods (R1, R2, R4) and provides enough discussion (R3).
- The paper cites a large number of related works, aiding reader orientation (R2).
- Multiple datasets combining graph structure with heterogeneous tabular data are constructed (R1).
- Overall writing and presentation are clear (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and lack of algorithmic contribution:** The concept of constructing graph datasets from tabular data has been previously suggested (R1, R3); graph methods used are not novel and have been employed in prior works such as arxiv.org/abs/2004.05718 even for heterogeneous node features (R1); the paper makes little to no algorithmic contributions and the benchmark itself is the main offering (R2, R3, R4).
- **Problem setting conflated with relational databases:** The claim that “graphs with tabular node features” differs from standard relational databases is insufficiently justified. The setting is essentially a relational database with two tables (one for the original table, one for intra-table relationships), making it a special case of existing relational benchmarks (R2). After reading Appendix C, the distinction remains unclear (R2); all graph structures constructed can be represented in RelBench (R4). The paper should have highlighted and thoroughly discussed RelBench (Fey et al., 2023) and 4DBInfer (Wang et al., 2024) in the main text instead of hiding them in a single sentence in the appendix (R2, R4).
- **Missing comparisons and related work:** The paper overlooks a recent survey on GNNs for tabular data (arxiv.org/abs/2401.02143) (R1); it also omits PyTorch Frame (Hu et al., arXiv:2404.00776), which supports encoding heterogeneous tabular data via deep encoders and can be used with PyG for downstream GNN tasks – a natural baseline to include (R4). More discussion on differences from prior works is needed (R3).
- **Task type too limited:** Only node classification and regression are considered; link prediction (a common GNN task, offered by RelBench) and multi-label classification are missing. The claim that node-level tasks are “the most common setting in modern graph machine learning” is unfair without covering other common tasks (R4).
- **Analysis is handwavy and lacks actionable guidance:** Statements like “it is important to experiment with different design choices” do not help users prune the search space of methods or architectures for a given dataset (R1). The main text should provide more details on how tabular data are represented as a graph; the absence causes confusion (R3).
- **Homogeneous vs. heterogeneous GNNs:** The paper treats homogeneous GNNs as a benefit, but extending to heterogeneous GNNs requires only a few lines of code in PyG and offers more expressiveness; no advantage of homogeneous GNNs is demonstrated (R2).
- **Verbose writing and poor motivation:** The first two paragraphs of the introduction introduce tabular and graph learning in general, which is not directly relevant to the research problem; motivation in the introduction is weak (R2). The initial paragraph in the ‘Machine Learning for Graphs’ section is excessively lengthy, hindering readability (R3).
- **Missing runtime comparisons:** The paper only compares performance scores; runtime should be compared as GBDTs can be much slower on larger datasets than GNNs and tabular models (R4).
- **Unaddressed performance drop with PLR:** The paper should explain why some models suffer from performance degradation after using PLR (R3).
- **No standardized mechanism to augment any table with a graph:** The paper does not propose a methodology to convert any table into a graph, limiting its practical novelty (R1).
