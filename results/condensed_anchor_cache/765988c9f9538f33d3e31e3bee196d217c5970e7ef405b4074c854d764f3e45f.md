## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes MERV (Multi-Encoder Representation of Videos), which leverages multiple frozen vision encoders (spatial expert DINOv2, temporal expert ViViT, image-language contrastive expert SigLIP, video-language contrastive expert LanguageBind) to create a unified video representation for Video Large Language Models (VideoLLMs). A pre-fusion projection aligns embedding sizes, then cross-attention performs spatio-temporal fusion before feeding to the LLM. Experiments on benchmarks including MSVD-QA, ActivityNet-QA, MSRVTT, TGIF, and Something-Something v2 show improvements over single-encoder baselines and prior state-of-the-art (e.g., up to 3.79% better accuracy than Video-LLaVA, 2.21% better zero-shot Perception Test accuracy than SeViLA). The authors also provide extensive ablation studies on encoder combinations, fusion strategies, and training recipes.

### Strengths
- **Well-motivated and clearly written.** The paper is easy to follow, with clear discussion of related work, method, and experiments. (Reviewer 1, 2)
- **Multi-encoder approach is effective.** Combining specialized visual encoders captures a wider range of visual information (spatial, temporal, multimodal) and yields performance gains across multiple video understanding benchmarks. (Reviewer 1, 3)
- **Extensive and well-conducted ablation studies.** The paper systematically explores feature fusion strategies (cross-attention vs. concatenation), pre-fusion projectors, encoder combinations, and two-stage training recipes (MERV vs. MERV-Full), providing insight into design choices. (Reviewer 1, 4)
- **Efficiency and ease of implementation.** The fusion module introduces minimal extra parameters and computational overhead compared to single-encoder approaches, and is straightforward to integrate. (Reviewer 1, 3; Reviewer 1 specifically claims “minimal extra parameters,” while Reviewer 2 disagrees regarding surprisingness given increased cost – see Weaknesses.)
- **Pioneering empirical framework for multi-encoder VideoLLMs.** Although not novel in methodology, the paper provides valuable empirical results answering key questions (which vision encoder to use, whether to pretrain adapters, whether to unfreeze components) in a systematic way, which is helpful given the flood of VideoLLMs with diverse training recipes. (Reviewer 4)
- **Reproducibility.** Technical details are clearly described. (Reviewer 2)
- **Performance improvements over strong baselines.** Reported accuracy gains on standard benchmarks demonstrate the potential of the multi-encoder fusion strategy. (Reviewer 3)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty.** The idea of ensembling multiple vision encoders is a well-known paradigm in machine learning and has been applied to image-based VLMs. The paper does not offer new insights beyond adapting this to video by including one video-specific encoder (ViViT); the core mechanism still relies heavily on image-based encoders applied to video frames. The performance improvement is unsurprising given increased computational cost. (Reviewer 2, 3; Reviewer 4 acknowledges the lack of novel methodology but finds the empirical contribution valuable – this contrast is noted.)
- **Feature fusion module lacks clear advantage over simple concatenation.** Table 2 shows cross-attention fusion yields nearly the same accuracy as channel concatenation. Without statistical significance testing, the added complexity of cross-attention may not be justified. The paper should compare computational cost and parameter overhead between cross-attention and concatenation. (Reviewer 3; Reviewer 2 also implies the fusion is “empirical” without in-depth analysis of feature interactions.)
- **Scalability and extensibility are unclear.** The video backbone ViViT (2021) has only a slight influence on final performance (Table 4), and Figure 5 seems to contradict its effectiveness. The paper should test with newer video backbones (e.g., Video Swin Transformer, MViTv2, VideoMAE v2) and clarify how additional vision encoders beyond the four chosen would affect performance. (Reviewer 2, 4)
- **Missing analysis on encoder specialization.** It is unclear how each encoder contributes differently depending on the task and how the fusion module weighs them. For example, Figure 3 shows DINOv2 outperforms SigLIP on MSRVTT-Who but lags behind on MSVD-Who, and Figures 4b/5 do not clearly disentangle individual contributions of contrastive methods and ViViT. (Reviewer 1, 4)
- **When to use MERV vs. MERV-Full?** The paper does not provide general guidelines for choosing between the two training recipes. (Reviewer 4)
- **Claim about weak video-language alignment in MERV recipe (L393-L394) is not verified.** How was this observed? (Reviewer 4)
- **Computational trade-offs not fully analyzed.** While Reviewer 1 claims minimal overhead, Reviewer 2 argues the performance improvement is unsurprising given increased cost. The authors should provide detailed runtime comparisons between single-encoder and multi-encoder models (e.g., inference time, FLOPs, memory). (Reviewer 3, implicitly Reviewer 2)
- **Missing details and minor issues.** 
  - Are single-encoder models in Figure 5 variants of MERV? (Reviewer 4)
  - Clarify role of positional embeddings P_e: are they to differentiate encoder features? If so, why named “positional”? (Reviewer 4)
  - Training time: L254-L255 states “24 hours with 8 L40-48GB GPUs” – is this for MERV or MERV-Full? (Reviewer 4)
  - Table 1 should add columns specifying vision encoder and LLM used for each method, and the TGIF score for Video-ChatGPT is missing (3.0, L309). (Reviewer 4)
  - L338: “3D Conv” in Table 2(a) – is it a 2D 3×3 convolution? Naming is confusing. (Reviewer 4)
  - Typos should be fixed. (Reviewer 4)
- **Would benefit from analysis with more encoders.** The paper shows gains from using four encoders; it is unclear whether further adding encoders continues to improve performance or saturates. (Reviewer 1)
