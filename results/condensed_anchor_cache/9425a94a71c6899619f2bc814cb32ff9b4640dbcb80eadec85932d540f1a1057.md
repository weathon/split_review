## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper provides a theoretical understanding of adversarial examples and adversarial training from the perspective of feature learning. It focuses on a multiple classification setting with structured data composed of robust features (sparse, resistant to perturbation) and non-robust features (dense, susceptible). A two-layer smoothed ReLU convolutional neural network is trained. Standard training (gradient descent) primarily learns non-robust features, leading to adversarial vulnerability. Adversarial training (gradient ascent to find adversarial examples, then gradient descent on adversarial examples) strengthens robust feature learning and suppresses non-robust features, improving robustness. Theoretical findings are empirically validated on MNIST, CIFAR10, SVHN.

### Strengths

- Provides rigorous theoretical analysis elucidating the dynamics of feature learning under adversarial conditions, with a clear distinction between robust and non-robust features and their impact on network vulnerability. This is meaningful for the community as a basis for extension to general architectures.
- The paper is well-structured, clearly written, with detailed related work and helpful discussions on assumptions and theoretical results.
- Theoretical findings are supported by experiments on real-image datasets (MNIST, CIFAR10, SVHN), enhancing credibility.
- Brings a new perspective on a core topic; establishing the result required major technical effort and delivered interesting insights into training dynamics.
- Provides a compelling theoretical explanation for a well-known empirical phenomenon (Ilyas et al. 2019).

### Weaknesses

- Title misleading: ‘provable robustness’ typically refers to robustness verification, not the analysis here.
- Adversarial training analysis assumes one-step gradient ascent (FGSM) for generating adversarial examples, which does not produce robust models in practice; models trained this way can be broken by stronger attacks.
- The analysis should treat epsilon as a continuous variable and show a spectrum of feature learning; Lemma 5.2 does not degrade to Lemma 5.1 when epsilon=0. Authors should clearly explain the effect of epsilon as a continuous variable on feature learning.
- The choice of smoothed ReLU is not well justified; why not softplus or GeLU? Also appears discontinuous at z=rho. What happens with other activations (e.g., standard ReLU)?
- The scaling epsilon = Theta(sigma_n sqrt(d)) is unrealistic; in practice, epsilon is smaller for higher-dimensional inputs (e.g., 8/255 for CIFAR10).
- Theoretical results rely heavily on specific assumptions (smoothed ReLU, one-step attack, patch-structured data with orthonormal features), limiting general applicability to deeper networks, standard ReLU, multi-step PGD, and more realistic data.
- It is unclear why the patch-structured data model is more realistic than previous Gaussian mixture or multi-cluster models; authors should explain or reference.
- The relationship between features and input is linear up to noise; non-linear relationships (e.g., ground-truth feature extractor) are not considered.
- Features are assumed well-separated with respect to input dimensions; cases where robust and non-robust features overlap are not analyzed.
- The data distribution is heavily stylized, especially the class signal being a scaled one-hot vector. It is unclear what the take-aways are beyond insights into training dynamics, given the stylized setting and that outcomes align with prior work.
- The framework assumes only two types of features (robust and non-robust), whereas real data may have infinite non-robust features, and not all are useful (Ilyas et al. categorizes further).
- The orthogonality assumption between robust and non-robust features is crucial for the proofs (Propositions 3.1, 3.2) but unrealistic in practice.
- The data construction models features in input space, but high-level robust features (e.g., object shape) would require many patches; it would be more appropriate to define features in a latent space.
- The definitions of robust features as “stronger” and non-robust as “denser” lack interpretability; it is unclear how these manifest in input space, and there is no empirical validation of these properties in real datasets. Under these assumptions, standard training picks denser features and adversarial training picks stronger features, assumed to be robust.
- In Proposition 3 and Theorem 4.3, what if the adversarial perturbation uses a direction v_t orthogonal to all v_i (non-robust features)? Could adversarial examples be constructed without using known non-robust features?
- The definition of “small perturbation radius” assumes two data points with distinct labels have significant differences; what if X’ is itself an adversarial example?
- The assumption that classifier F is accurate on (X,y) may not hold; parts of the theory could be impacted if F misclassifies a substantial portion.
- The analysis shows no tradeoff between standard and robust accuracy, contrary to empirical observations (Tsipras et al.). Authors should include what happens to standard accuracy in Theorem 4.4 and consider the case of (X_f, y) from D_NR.
- The analysis shows a phase transition where non-robust features are learned first, then robust features. However, some works (Tsilivis & Kempe, Singh et al.) suggest robust features are learned first. The discrepancy is not observed in real data with other activations; authors should discuss.
- Minor: line 179, ‘u,v’ should be boldfaced.

One reviewer (R4) raised stronger concerns about the realism of the assumptions, rating soundness 2 and contribution 2.
