## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes First-order Repulsive Deep Ensemble (FoRDE), a particle-based variational inference (ParVI) method that performs repulsion in the space of first-order input gradients rather than in weight or function space. The motivation is that previous ParVI methods for ensembles have not led to practical improvements over vanilla deep ensembles (DEs). FoRDE is evaluated on image classification (CIFAR-10/100, TinyImageNet, CINIC-10) and transfer learning, with comparisons to DEs and other Bayesian neural network baselines.

Reviewers agree the idea is novel and well-motivated, and the paper is clearly written. However, opinions on the empirical gains are mixed. Two reviewers (1, 3) find the results convincing (scores 8, 8), while two (2, 4) are more critical (scores 6, 6) and question the practical improvement over DEs. Common concerns include: the performance gains over DEs are often marginal (especially for in-distribution data and larger datasets), the trade-off between clean and corrupted accuracy is not resolved, there is limited evidence that input gradient diversity translates to functional diversity, additional kernel hyperparameters introduce a burden, and the computational cost (≈3× DE) is high. Reviewers also note the limited experimental scope (vision only, small-scale datasets) and missing details (corruption types, ECE bins, confidence intervals, OOD proportions). Several specific criticisms and requests for clarification/analysis are made.

### Strengths

- **Novel method and strong motivation.** The idea of repelling particles in input-gradient space (rather than weight or function space) is new and well-justified by the observation that previous repulsive ensembles have not improved over vanilla DEs. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4)
- **Excellent presentation.** The paper is very well written, self-contained, and enjoyable to read. Section 3 (main contribution) is particularly clear and informative, leading the reader step-by-step from Wasserstein gradient descent to input-gradient kernels. (Reviewers 1, 3, 4)
- **Thorough empirical evaluation.** The experiments cover accuracy, calibration (NLL, ECE), robustness to corruptions (cA, cNLL, cECE), transfer learning, and include a runtime analysis. Connections to existing methods (e.g., EmpCov prior) are drawn. (Reviewers 1, 2)
- **Comprehensive literature overview.** The paper provides a detailed overview of repulsive ensemble methods and their limitations. (Reviewer 2)
- **Identifies key trade-offs.** The discussion of lengthscale choices (FoRDE-Identity vs. FoRDE-PCA) and the connection to the EmpCov prior help explain when the method is most beneficial. (Reviewer 2)

### Weaknesses

- **Marginal and conditional performance gains.** FoRDE-Identity performs similarly to vanilla DEs. FoRDE-PCA improves corruption robustness but *significantly* reduces in-distribution accuracy. On the largest dataset (TinyImageNet) gains over DEs are marginal, and FoRDE shows lower performance than baselines on in-distribution data for some settings. (Reviewers 2, 4)
- **Missing evidence that gradient diversity drives functional diversity.** The paper does not provide empirical or theoretical proof that repulsion in input-gradient space actually increases functional diversity, nor does it show how such diversity translates to improved ensemble performance. (Reviewer 4)
- **Optimal lengthscale not resolved.** The paper acknowledges an ideal lengthscale regime exists (“between unit and inverse-square-root eigenvalues”) but offers no method to find it in practice. The trade-off between clean and corrupted performance is not sufficiently explored. (Reviewer 2)
- **Additional hyperparameter burden.** The RBF kernel and its lengthscales introduce extra tuning, and no simpler base kernel (avoiding the lengthscale problem) was considered or abandoned. (Reviewers 3, 4)
- **High computational cost.** FoRDE incurs ≈3× the training cost of DEs, which are themselves expensive. No ideas for reducing this burden are given. (Reviewer 3)
- **Small experimental scale.** All experiments are on vision datasets of moderate size (CIFAR, TinyImageNet, CINIC-10). Evaluation on larger or non-vision benchmarks (e.g., WILDS) is absent. (Reviewers 3, 4)
- **Missing details and clarity issues.** 
  * No confidence intervals are reported for corruption results (CIFAR-10/100-C). It is unclear which specific corruption types were used. (Reviewer 1)
  * Missing implementation details: number of bins for ECE, the proportion of OOD data used (e.g., CIFAR-100 for CIFAR-10 OOD detection). (Reviewer 1)
  * No reference is given for the CINIC-10 dataset. (Reviewer 1)
  * Section 3.1 does not explicitly address that the target distribution *π* is unavailable in practice. (Reviewer 1)
  * The algorithm steps (in Appendix) are not clearly linked to the equations in Section 3. (Reviewer 1)
  * The claim that mini-batch repulsion causes no performance degradation is unsupported – no comparison with full-batch repulsion is presented. (Reviewer 1)
- **Limited investigation of robustness.** Experiments on adversarial attacks are missing, even though robustness to adversarial examples is mentioned as a motivation. (Reviewer 2)
- **Reasons for weight-space repulsion failure need elaboration.** The explanation of redundancy due to over-parameterization, high-dimensional kernel difficulties, and weight symmetries could be more detailed, including discussion of the curse of dimensionality and permutation invariance. (Reviewer 2)
- **Kernel design choices.** Is the median heuristic required for PCA-based lengthscales? Could a simpler kernel (e.g., linear) avoid the lengthscale problem altogether? (Reviewer 3)
- **Supplementary recommendations.** An ethics statement and reproducibility statement should be added after the main paper. (Reviewer 4)
