## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes to disentangle rewards into a task-specific component (assumed known or learned) and a common-sense component (shared across tasks) learned via multi-task adversarial inverse reinforcement learning (AIRL). It argues that standard IRL methods (AIRL, GAIL) fail to recover useful reward functions, and that multi-task training is necessary to learn a common-sense reward. Experiments on a subset of Meta-World manipulation tasks suggest that the learned common-sense reward transfers to new tasks. While some reviewers found the problem interesting and the method simple, others identified fundamental flaws in motivation, assumptions, and experimental design, leading to a wide split in scores (5,5,1,1).

### Strengths
- The problem of recovering reward functions from demonstrations for better transfer and generalization across tasks is interesting and practically important.
- The proposed method is simple and straightforward, facilitating easy implementation and extension.
- The paper addresses the important challenge of learning environmental constraints (common-sense rewards) from multi-task data.
- Experiments on Meta-World demonstrate that multi-task training can recover a common reward that transfers to new tasks.
- The overall presentation is clear and easy to follow for several reviewers.

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation and claims about IRL are unsubstantiated.** The paper argues that IRL fails to learn correct reward functions due to its connection to GANs, but this conflates GAIL (a primal distribution-matching method) with reward-learning IRL. Dual-form IRL (e.g., MaxEntIRL, f-IRL) explicitly learns reward functions on which policies can be retrained. The paper also incorrectly attributes IRL origins and provides a shallow literature review.
- **Assumption of additive reward structure is overly simplistic.** The decomposition into common-sense + task-specific reward is arbitrary and lacks theoretical guarantees that optimizing the task-specific part alone yields the same optimal policy as the full combination (c.f. Ng et al. on reward shaping). More complex functional forms (non-linear, noise models) are not discussed, limiting applicability.
- **Main method assumes task-specific rewards are given, which is unrealistic** and changes the problem from IRL to demonstration-guided RL. In practice, the method appears to learn an auxiliary reward from multi-task demos to supplement an environment-provided task reward.
- **The unknown-task-reward extension (MT-CSIRL-LT) learns task-specific and common-sense rewards jointly without any explicit disentanglement mechanism**, making it unclear why it is better than learning a combined reward. Key baselines (e.g., AIRL on each task individually) are missing for this setting.
- **Experimental evaluation is incomplete and biased.** 
    - Comparisons are unfair: the proposed method uses both task rewards and demonstrations, while baselines use only one or the other.
    - Standard IRL reward learners (MaxEntIRL, f-IRL) and recent methods (IQ-Learn, Reward Network Distillation) are absent.
    - Experiments are limited to a few similar tabletop manipulation tasks in Meta-World; generalizability is not demonstrated.
    - The definition of common-sense rewards (target velocity, action norm) is contrived; they encode a style preference not critical for task success, and all tasks achieve near-100% success regardless.
- **Section 5.4 (unknown task reward) lacks baseline comparisons**, so the claim of effective decomposition and transfer is unconvincing.
- **Section 4.3 is titled "curriculum learning" but does not connect to any curriculum learning concept.**
- **The paper is poorly written and hard to follow, especially the experiment section.** Key details (how trajectories are sampled, evaluation protocols, finetuning steps) are unclear. The results in Table 1 and Table 2 are not clearly explained.
- **Missing theoretical analysis** on why multi-task training is necessary for learning useful common-sense rewards, as opposed to single-task or single-target transfer.
- **Unclear how MT-CSIRL works on unseen tasks**: whether the task-specific reward and demonstrations are both available, and if the common-sense reward is finetuned. Similarly, how MT-AIRL baselines are evaluated (access to task reward and demos) is not specified.
- **Potential bias from episode termination** (task solved ends episode) may cause baselines to ignore constraints, as noted in prior work on reward bias in imitation learning.
- **There is strong disagreement among reviewers** about the paper's validity: two reviewers found the approach promising (scores 5), while two others considered it fundamentally flawed due to unsupported assumptions, missing baselines, and insufficient results (scores 1).
