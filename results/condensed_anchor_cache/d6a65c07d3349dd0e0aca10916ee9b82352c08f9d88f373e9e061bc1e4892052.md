## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces PersonalLLM, a public benchmark for personalizing LLMs to individual user preferences. It provides 10K open-ended prompts, each paired with 8 responses from SOTA LLMs (GPT-4, Claude 3), and uses 10 pre-trained reward models (from a leaderboard) to simulate 1000 synthetic users via a Dirichlet-weighted sum of these reward models. Baselines include in-context learning (ICL) with historical user feedback and meta-learning across users. The paper argues that its method generates more heterogeneous preferences than persona-based prompting. Most reviewers acknowledge the potential of the dataset and the novel simulation method, but several raise concerns about the validity of the simulated preferences, clarity of concepts, presentation, and adequacy of baselines. One reviewer (score 8) is more positive, emphasizing the originality and significance of the dataset and methodology; others (scores 5, 5, 6) are more critical, questioning the realism of the simulations and the clarity of the paper.

### Strengths
- **Impactful direction and large-scale dataset**: Personalization of LLMs is an important problem. PersonalLLM provides a large-scale public dataset (10K prompts × 8 responses from SOTA LLMs) that can support future research. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4)
- **Novel method for generating diverse synthetic user preferences**: The paper proposes an original approach using a weighted combination of pre-trained reward models (sampled via Dirichlet distribution) to simulate user preferences, which is shown to produce more heterogeneous preferences than the persona-based prompting baseline. (Reviewers 3, 4)
- **Empirical validation of diversity**: The paper validates its method by showing that the top response receives a majority user vote for only ~50% of prompts (vs ~90% for persona baseline), and for ~60% of prompts, at least 5 different answers are chosen as the best by at least 1 simulated user (vs ~30% for persona). (Reviewers 3, 4)
- **Addresses data sparsity**: The paper highlights meta-learning and ICL baselines that leverage historical data from similar users, which is crucial for real-world applications with limited user-specific feedback. (Reviewers 1, 3)
- **High quality and broad use**: The dataset is built from multiple high-quality LLMs (GPT-4, Claude 3), and the paper provides a specific analysis of the dataset. (Reviewers 2, 3)
- **Clearly written overall**: One reviewer noted the paper is clearly written and argued for its significance. (Reviewer 3)

### Weaknesses
- **Validity of simulated user preferences**: 
  - Reward models are from a general leaderboard and may not capture the nuanced, individual-specific preferences of real users. The linear combination of these reward models may not reflect true user reward distributions. (Reviewers 1, 4)
  - The paper’s own statement “We do not claim our simulated personal preference models provide a high-fidelity depiction of human behavior” contrasts with the claim “in doing so we are able to simulate an entire user base” (line 76), creating confusion about the objective. The goal should be clarified (coverage vs. matching real distribution). (Reviewer 4)
  - The candidate responses generated by LLMs may not cover user-specific responses or backgrounds, limiting the ability to learn user-specific preferences. (Reviewer 1)
  - It is unclear whether the reward models are truly distinct; they may optimize for similar objectives. A similarity/embedding analysis of the reward models would help evaluate diversity. (Reviewer 3)
  - It is unclear if the LLMs used to generate the 8 responses were prompted with personas; without persona prompting they may converge on similar response styles, reducing preference diversity. (Reviewer 3)
  - A baseline with a hard choice (each user selects one of the 10 rewards instead of weighted sum) would be insightful. (Reviewer 4)
- **Missing comparisons and baselines**:
  - No comparison with other personalized LLM benchmarks (e.g., LaMP dataset) is provided, making it hard to assess novelty and contribution relative to existing resources. (Reviewer 1)
  - The persona baseline uses only 500 personas versus 1000 simulated users in PersonalLLM; results with 1000 personas should be included. (Reviewer 4)
- **Unclear definitions and formulation**:
  - What is meant by “preference” is ambiguous: is it user preference in recommender systems, an alternative to NLP tasks, or a more general concept? The relationship to recommender systems needs explanation. (Reviewer 2)
  - How the reward model is trained or built up is not explained. (Reviewer 2)
  - The difference between heterogeneous and homogeneous preferences, and how each is demonstrated, is not clear. (Reviewer 2)
  - Key terms such as ‘interaction history’, ‘preference data’, and ‘user data’ are not well defined. (Reviewer 1)
- **Clarity, readability, and presentation issues**:
  - The paper lacks an overarching figure illustrating the dataset construction logic and example samples. (Reviewer 1)
  - Several long, multi-line sentences reduce readability (e.g., line 76, line 102, paragraph citing Kirk et al. 2024). (Reviewer 4)
  - Figures are difficult to interpret in places:
    - Figure 5 (left columns) shows minimal visible difference across α values; a better visualization is needed. (Reviewer 4)
    - Figure 5 (right): entropy comparison over keywords is unclear; a formal definition or equation should be given, and the persona baseline should be included for comparison. (Reviewer 4)
    - Figure 6 (left): the dashed line (presumably zero-shot) is missing from the legend. (Reviewer 4)
    - The claim that “the top response receives a majority user vote for only about half of the prompts” (line 257) and “for roughly 60% of prompts, at least 5 different answers are chosen as the best by at least 1” (line 258) cannot be easily read from the figure; explanation is needed. (Reviewer 4)
  - In Section 4.2, how prompt and response embeddings are computed is not mentioned. (Reviewer 4)
  - Typo: Line 32 cites “Christiano et al., 2017”, not “2023”. (Reviewer 4)
- **Methodological requests and unresolved questions**:
  - Are there other sources of high-quality reward functions that could be used? (Reviewer 3)
  - Were the leading LLMs prompted with personas when generating the 8 responses? (Reviewer 3)
  - The paper states that generating diverse preferences is the goal, but random preferences would give even higher diversity yet be useless; the objective needs clearer justification. (Reviewer 4)
