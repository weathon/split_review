## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Mixture of Task Experts (MoTE), a transformer block that adds task-specific MLP experts after every other layer to generate different embeddings per query depending on the task. During training, each task is routed to a dedicated expert; after training, Weight Average of Vector Experts (WAVE) averages the expert weights to keep the backbone parameter count constant. The model is evaluated on 56 datasets across 7 tasks (in-scope and out-of-scope) and compared against instruction-conditioned baselines (e.g., TEM-Inst.). MoTE reports average gains of +1.62 NDCG@10 on retrieval, +1.54 MAP on re-ranking, and +0.65 overall, without changing inference speed or active parameters.

### Strengths
- **Task-specific parameter sets** enable distinct embeddings per task, allowing the model to better optimize representation spaces for different intents (R1, R2, R4).  
- **Innovative integration of MoE into embedding models** that addresses the memory challenge of multi-task specialization through the WAVE merging technique (R2).  
- **Robust experimental setup**: tested across 7 tasks (both in-scope and out-of-scope) on 56 datasets, with thorough ablations and analysis of training configurations (R2, R3, R4).  
- **Clear presentation** with well-chosen examples and motivating illustrations (R2).  
- **Practical parameter efficiency**: WAVE reduces memory overhead while retaining 98.2% average performance and keeps the backbone parameter count unchanged, ensuring a fair comparison with baselines (R2, R3).  
- **Ablation experiments** sufficiently cover performance variations under different settings (R3, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited innovation**: The work applies established MoE and weight-averaging techniques to embedding models without providing new theoretical insights or a novel learning mechanism. The gains, while present, are largely expected from the straightforward application of these methods (R3 – strong criticism).  
- **Incomplete methodology and poor reproducibility**:  
  - No commitment to open-source code (R3).  
  - Methodology section is very brief; missing details such as the specific loss functions for each expert, exact architecture of the MoE layer, task-aware training implementation, optimization algorithm, learning rate schedules, and hyperparameters (R3).  
  - The paper falls short of the page limit (appears incomplete), with grammar issues and citation inconsistencies (R3, R4).  
- **Marginal out-of-scope performance**:  
  - Gains on semantic textual similarity (STS) tasks are slight (Table 2) (R2).  
  - The approach does not generalize effectively to out-of-scope tasks; for example, classification, clustering, and retrieval share latent spaces, yet out-of-scope improvements are limited (R2).  
- **WAVE preserves average performance but not the absolute improvement**:  
  - Table 6 shows WAVE retains only 98.2% average performance, but the absolute gains of the full MoTE model are not well preserved. An individual task analysis is needed to show where performance drops are most significant (R1).  
  - The WAVE method may be too simplistic – simply averaging expert layers post-training – and further analysis is required to understand why it works (R4).  
- **Missing comparisons to mainstream retrieval models**: No experiments against E5, BGE, or other state-of-the-art models; no comparisons using their training data, making it unclear whether improvements come from the architecture or from the specific training data used (R4).  
- **No analysis of GPU memory scaling**: The paper lacks a clear analysis of how the memory footprint increases with the number of tasks, a critical practical concern for large-scale deployment (R1).  
- **Task-aware training provides marginal gains**: Table 3 shows task-aware training does not improve performance for most tasks; the reported improvements are minimal, raising questions about its practical utility (R1).  
- **Lack of in-depth analysis in ablation sections** (e.g., Sections 4.5 and 4.6): results are presented without discussing why certain configurations perform better or worse, limiting the insights from the experiments (R4).  
- **Hyperparameter tuning for TEM-Inst. baseline unclear**: The performance difference between TEM-Inst. and MoTE is less than 1% in Tables 1 and 2. The reviewers ask whether TEM-Inst. received comparable hyperparameter search efforts to ensure a fair comparison (R3).  
- **Evidence of task-specialization insufficient**: The paper claims existing instruction-based methods limit task-specialization, but Tables 1 and 2 show TEM-Inst. outperforms STEM, indicating multi-task learning already helps. No clear evidence is provided that the representation space is truly task-specialized, nor how it differs across tasks, nor why instructions alone cannot resolve the issue (R3).  
- **Writing quality issues**: Duplicate lines in Section 3.3 (R1, R4), typo ("texty-type" instead of "text-type", R1). The two paragraphs in Section 3.3 are repetitive, and overall readability suffers (R4).  
- **No background on weight averaging**: The paper introduces WAVE as model merging but provides no background on model merging techniques, making the work hard to follow for readers unfamiliar with the area (R3).  
- **Minority negative view**: One reviewer (R3, score 3) is notably more critical, emphasizing the lack of novelty, insufficient methodology detail, and incomplete presentation; this contrasts with the more positive assessments of R2 (score 6) and the moderate scores of R1 and R4.
