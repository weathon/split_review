## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces the retrieval problem, a reasoning task requiring a minimum number of transformer layers, with adjustable difficulty. It shows that large language models solve it under various prompting formulations without fine-tuning, but performance degrades to random guessing as complexity increases. On a minimal formulation, successful learning occurs only with an implicit curriculum. By manually inspecting attention maps, the authors identify learned circuits and show that attention heads emerge sequentially during training. Reviewers find the contribution novel and the experiments on synthetic data rigorous, but express concerns about readability, theoretical assumptions, lack of experimental validation for Theorem 1, insufficient detail on the manual reverse-engineering method and the implicit curriculum formulation, and limited generalizability to real-world data. One reviewer is more positive (score 8), one moderately positive (6), and one skeptical (5), highlighting a disagreement on the strength of empirical support.

### Strengths
- Novel retrieval problem that generalizes induction steps and introduces a conditional variant, providing a principled way to study emergent reasoning in transformers.
- Strong contribution to mechanistic interpretability; the finding that attention heads emerge in a specific sequence during training is particularly valuable.
- Clear problem statement and logical progression from high-level analysis to focused experiments.
- Experiments are rigorous; the correlation between task difficulty and required number of layers is well demonstrated.
- Visualization of attention maps gives insight into how transformers perform retrieval reasoning.
- Highlights the role of implicit curriculum in the development of retrieval mechanisms.
- Finding that LLMs fail as complexity increases fits with human intuition and underscores the importance of depth.

### Weaknesses
- **Readability**: Section 5 (theoretical analysis of information flow) is hard to follow, with dense notation (e.g., variables E, F) not clearly connected to the experimental setup. A step-by-step mapping to concrete transformer components and retrieval examples is needed.
- **Missing justification for assumptions**: The analysis heavily relies on Assumption 1 (shared token/position information required for attention) and simplifications to the transformer architecture. No rigorous justification is provided for why these hold for LLMs or how non-linearities in FFNs or multi-head interactions might break the assumption. This limits confidence in generalization.
- **Lack of experimental validation for Theorem 1**: The claim that the target embedding appears only when \(t \geq \log_3(2D)\) is not empirically verified. An experiment testing this specific relationship would strengthen the practical relevance.
- **Insufficient detail on manual reverse-engineering**: Section 8 mentions manually reverse-engineering circuits but gives no step-by-step description of the process, tools, or criteria used. This severely hampers reproducibility and independent verification.
- **Vague implicit curriculum (IC) definition**: The IC formulation, which outperforms non-IC, is not clearly defined. How target vectors are constructed, how the retrieval chain tokens are concatenated, and why IC succeeds while non-IC fails are not explained.
- **Generalization concerns**: All experiments use synthetic data. No evaluation on real-world QA datasets or more complex linguistic structures, raising doubt about whether the identified circuits and emergence patterns transfer beyond the controlled setting.
- **Limited unification**: The paper lacks a general framework that connects the retrieval task to a broader class of reasoning problems; a more complex illustrative example would help.
- **Missing analysis of width vs. depth**: The paper focuses on depth but does not explore whether the emergent ability depends more on width; one reviewer notes this as an open question.
- Reviewer 3 (minority more negative) explicitly states that Assumption 1 is “overly restrictive and conflicts with the flexibility of Transformers” and recommends it be reconsidered to better capture context-driven, non–preshared information. The same reviewer also requests validation under longer retrieval steps.
- One reviewer notes that the real-world example in the Discussion section (e.g., F5 "Relatives") could be introduced earlier to motivate the theory; also, F5's number of choices is not specified (unlike F1–F4 which have 4 choices and 25% random guessing).
