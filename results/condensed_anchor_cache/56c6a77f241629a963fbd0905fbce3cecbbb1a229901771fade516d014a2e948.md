## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies learning the full parity (k=d) and almost‑full parities (k=d‑O(1)) on the d‑dimensional hypercube using two‑layer ReLU networks trained by gradient descent. The main findings are: (1) With Rademacher initialization (weights uniform in {±1}), noisy SGD using either correlation or hinge loss succeeds in strongly learning the full parity within poly(d) samples/iterations. Adding Gaussian perturbation with standard deviation σ=O(d⁻¹) still allows learning, but larger σ prevents it. (2) For any target function, a new measure called initial gradient alignment (GAL0) bounds the accuracy achievable by noisy GD/SGD with correlation loss; if GAL0 is exponentially small, learning fails in polynomial time. Gaussian initialization yields exponentially small GAL0 for (almost‑full) parities, thus hardness. The negative result also covers almost‑full parities and is more general than the positive one. The paper thereby exhibits a sharp separation between Rademacher and Gaussian initialization for this task.

### Strengths
- **Novel positive result for full parity**: Prior work only covered constant‑k or weak learning; showing that full parity can be learned with polynomial complexity is novel and non‑trivial (R1). The proof, though relying on Rademacher initialization, is considered interesting and original (R1).
- **Interesting phase transition**: The paper demonstrates that adding small noise to the initialization makes learning significantly harder, and quantifies this via the gradient alignment measure (R1).
- **Clear separation between initializations**: The gap between Rademacher and Gaussian initialization is surprising a priori and provides a concrete example of how initialization critically impacts learnability (R3, R4, R5).
- **General negative result via GAL0**: Theorem 7 applies to arbitrary target functions and may be of broader relevance beyond dense parities, potentially offering a tool for negative learning results in other settings (R4, R5, R6).
- **Well‑written and organized**: The paper is clear and easy to follow (R4, R5).
- **Advances an active area**: It answers open questions about the learnability of full and almost‑full parities (R6).
- **Introduction of the GAL measure**: This measure may be useful for future theoretical work (R6).
- **Solid technical contribution**: The proofs are sound and build on prior techniques while adding new insights (R5).
- **Minority contrast**: Reviewer 1 is less positive (rating 5), questioning motivation and generality, whereas Reviewers 5 and 6 are more positive (rating 8), highlighting the paper’s contribution while noting some limitations.

### Weaknesses
- **Misleading scope in abstract/intro**: The positive result is proven only for the full parity (k=d), but the abstract and introduction repeatedly mention “almost‑full parities” without making the limitation clear. This caused confusion among multiple reviewers (R2, R6). R6 suggests explicitly stating “full parities” in the abstract and introduction.
- **Positive result trains only the output layer**: The network does not perform feature learning. It is unclear whether the negative results also train input weights or only the output layer, and whether failure implies a representation or optimization issue (R3). The positive result’s reliance on only training the second layer limits its generality (R3, R1).
- **Negative result requires additional noise in SGD**: The Zᵗ terms in Eq. (3) are artificial; deterministic GD is not covered, nor is standard SGD without this extra noise (R3, R4). This reduces the naturalness of the hardness result (R3).
- **Negative result only for correlation loss**: The analysis centers on the correlation loss, leaving open whether the same conclusion holds for other common losses such as hinge, cross‑entropy, or MSE (R2, R4). Reviewer 2 notes this works against the generality of the contribution.
- **Crucial poly(d) width assumption**: The positive result relies on the width being poly(d). The paper does not discuss what happens with narrower networks; it is unclear whether the phenomenon degrades gracefully (R4).
- **Fragility to initialization perturbations**: The positive result no longer holds after a small perturbation to the Rademacher initialization (σ large enough). This sensitivity raises questions about practical relevance and robustness (R1). The positive result only holds up to σ=O(d⁻¹); the sharp threshold is not resolved (abstract).
- **Unclear sample complexity and tightness**: The positive result does not specify the exact polynomial sample complexity; it is also unknown whether training the input layer could improve it (R2, R3). No lower bound is provided to assess tightness (R3).
- **Novelty and relation to prior work**: The paper claims novelty, but the emerging picture (a single fixed parity is learnable under Rademacher or all‑1 initialization, but not under Gaussian) may incrementally extend prior results (R5). The GAL measure appears related to the “Label‑Gradient Alignment” of Abbe & Boix‑Adsera (2022) and the “variance” of Abbe et al. (2022); the differences in proof techniques are not sufficiently explained (R3, R5).
- **Technical artifacts in negative result**: Corollary 1 introduces an additional Gaussian smoothing on top of an already smoothed initialization, which seems odd (R5). The form of the perturbation is also questioned: additive vs. multiplicative (R5).
- **Presentation issues**: Theorem 4 is overly long; formatting is inconsistent—target functions for positive results are defined in the text, while negative ones are stated inside the theorem (R6). Line 237 has a typo (missing xᵢ in the full parity definition) (R2). The intuitive explanation of how GAL works and why it differs between initializations is lacking in the main text (R2, R4).
- **Open questions left**: Can the positive result be extended to almost‑full parities? Reviewer 6 asks what the obstacle is. Reviewer 1 asks about complexity of learning (d‑k)‑parity—are k and d‑k essentially different? Reviewer 5 asks whether GAL can be estimated in practice with polynomial samples; if GAL is superpolynomially small, estimation may require superpolynomial sample complexity, limiting its practical use.
- **Motivation for studying full parity**: Reviewer 1 questions why learning full parity is important beyond being a challenging benchmark; every direction is equivalent, unlike sparse parity where feature learning is the focus. The paper should articulate relevance to real‑world phenomena.
