- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 3, 1, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Activation Decay (AD), a deterministic regularizer penalizing the L2 norm of penultimate-layer activations, derived from Gaussian smoothing, to flatten minima and improve generalization. Experiments are conducted on CIFAR-10, ImageNet, and NLP tasks (BERT) to demonstrate effectiveness. Reviewers generally note the topic is timely and the paper is easy to follow, but raise significant concerns about novelty, theoretical correctness, experimental rigor, and reproducibility.

### Strengths
- The paper is easy to follow and clearly presented (Reviewers 2, 4).
- The motivation to move away from stochastic regularization is important (Reviewer 2).
- The idea of regularizing only the penultimate-layer activation is interesting and could replace dropout at that layer (Reviewer 4).
- Extensive experiments across computer vision and NLP tasks are conducted to demonstrate the effectiveness of activation decay (Reviewer 1).
- The topic is timely and well suited for the ICLR community (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses

**Novelty and Related Work**
- The method appears to be a reapplication of average-direction SAM restricted to the last layer. Related work (unidentified) also suggests SAM is only needed on layer normalization layers (Reviewer 1).
- The flatness premise (flatter minima ⇒ better generalization) is treated as sufficient, but opposing works (Zhang et al., 2017) are ignored; this makes the theoretical foundation biased and insufficient (Reviewer 3, minority).
- Regularizing later layers has been explored in Baek et al. [2024]; a direct comparison is missing (Reviewer 4).
- Missing citations for ASAM [Kwon et al., 2021] (Reviewer 4), and for *Large Margin Deep Networks for Classification* and *Minimizing Layerwise Activation Norm Improves Generalization in Federated Learning* (closest works) (Reviewer 2).
- AD can only be applied to the penultimate layer, whereas dropout can be applied at multiple locations; this limits the method’s applicability (Reviewer 4).
- Reviewer 2 notes the method was already explored before and fails to expand on empirical validation; the derivations are well known.

**Theoretical Issues**
- Theorem 1 assumes parameter permutation across all layers, but AD only permutes parameters in the last layer; the theoretical justification is misaligned with the method (Reviewer 1).
- Theorem 2 does not explain why only the last layer is perturbed; similar results should hold for other layers (Reviewer 1).
- Theorem 3 gives only an upper bound for the AD loss; a two-sided bound is needed to substantiate equivalence to weight decay (Reviewer 1).
- **Critical proof error** (Reviewer 3, minority): The proof of Theorem 2 incorrectly claims that “at a local minimum, the gradient term involving the first derivatives vanishes” with respect to outputs; this only holds at a global optimum. The conclusion is therefore invalid. The paper itself acknowledges (Discussion, lines 497‑499) that no improvement is derived (Reviewer 3).
- Theorem 1 bounds the smoothed *training* loss, but the relevant quantity for generalization is the *test* loss without noise convolution; no argument connects the bound to test performance (Reviewer 3).
- Theorem 1 is simply a special case from Dellattre et al.; presenting it as a standalone contribution is not appropriate (Reviewer 3).

**Experimental Issues – SAM Comparisons**
- In Table 1, SAM uses ρ=2 on CIFAR‑10, whereas the standard ρ is 0.05 or 0.1; it is unclear whether SAM was properly tuned across experiments (Reviewers 1, 2).
- In Table 2, the optimal ρ for SAM on NLP tasks is 0.01, lower than the recommended 0.15 from Bahri et al. [2022] (Reviewer 4).
- Because SAM is explicitly designed to find flatter minima, it should not be significantly outperformed by AD; suboptimal SAM tuning likely explains the reported results, making the experiments inconclusive (Reviewer 3).
- Table 1 differences between (AD+SAM) and SAM rows are not significant given the reported standard deviations (Reviewer 3).
- The paper compares with ASAM but omits a citation for ASAM and does not compare with the original SAM (Reviewers 1, 4).
- SAM comparisons need a much finer set of ρ values for *all* experiments (Reviewer 2).

**Missing Experiments and Comparisons**
- Weight decay (WD) experiments with hyperparameter optimization are missing; AD must outperform WD in accuracy to justify replacing it, given WD has equivalent computational efficiency (Reviewer 3).
- Additional comparisons with dropout and stochastic depth are needed (Reviewer 2).
- Experiments should be extended to more complex datasets (CIFAR‑100, Tiny ImageNet) to make improvements clearer; Section 4.2 improvements are not significant (Reviewer 4).
- What are the results if parameters in layers other than the last layer are perturbed? (Reviewer 1)
- Additional datasets and architectures should be explored for the computer vision domain (Reviewer 2).

**Method Details and Reproducibility**
- The paper lacks essential experimental details; no mention of data augmentation, making the work currently not reproducible (Reviewer 2).
- Figure 1a lacks annotations, legend, and clear labels, hindering interpretation (Reviewer 4).
- In Figure 1a, the Hessian is estimated only for the final layer, mismatching the theory (Theorems 1 and 2) that considers all parameters (Reviewer 4).

**Practical Limitations**
- AD introduces an additional hyperparameter σ that must be tuned per task, reducing flexibility (Reviewer 1; Tables 1–3 show σ values change across tasks).
- The name “activation decay” is questioned: the method does not alter activation functions directly but only parameters in the output layer (Reviewer 1).
- Some claims are subjective, e.g., the batch‑size argument (Reviewer 2).

**Minority Negative View**  
One reviewer (Reviewer 3, score 1) is significantly more critical than the others (all score 3). This reviewer identifies a critical proof error in Theorem 2, states the flatness premise is biased, finds SAM comparisons invalid, and concludes that the work provides no novelty or insights. The other reviewers, while less severe, still rate the paper low (score 3) and recommend rejection.
