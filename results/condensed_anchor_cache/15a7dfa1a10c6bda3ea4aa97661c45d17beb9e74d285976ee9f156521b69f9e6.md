## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces Text2Reward, a data-free framework that automates the generation and shaping of dense reward functions for reinforcement learning using large language models. Given a natural-language goal, it produces executable reward code based on a compact representation of the environment (API summary). The method operates zero-shot or few-shot with retrieved examples, and supports iterative refinement via human feedback. Evaluated on 17 manipulation tasks (ManiSkill2, MetaWorld) and 6 locomotion tasks (MuJoCo), policies trained with generated rewards often match or exceed expert-written rewards. Real-world deployment and improvement through human feedback are also demonstrated.

### Strengths

- Addresses the important problem of dense reward specification in RL; novel application of LLMs to automatically generate reward code, simplifying reward design.
- More flexible than prior work (Yu et al., 2023): no extensive manual templating per embodiment or task; generates free-form reward functions (as opposed to sparse or templated rewards).
- Zero-shot results are strong across all environments.
- Solid results compared to oracle (expert-written) reward codes; on 13 of 17 manipulation tasks, policies match or exceed oracle performance.
- Produces interpretable, free-form dense reward codes that can use standard Python/numpy packages.
- Results on new tasks (e.g., Hopper flipping) that lack an expert reward – shows ability to create rewards for novel behaviors.
- Comprehensive evaluation: 17 manipulation tasks across ManiSkill2 and MetaWorld, 6 locomotion tasks in MuJoCo, plus real-world experiments.
- Demonstrates real-world deployment of a policy trained with generated rewards, and improvement via human feedback.
- Includes analysis of failure modes: categorization of Python runtime errors in generated code.
- Paper is well-written and free of grammatical errors.

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of qualitative analysis on source of improvements:** Why does zero-shot outperform few-shot on Turn Faucet, Open Cabinet Door, Open Cabinet Drawer? Why does few-shot fail to outperform oracle even when oracle code is in context (Lift Cube, Turn Faucet, Push Chair, Open Cabinet Drawer)? In cases where few-shot improves on zero-shot, what is the source of improvement?
- **Missing experimental details:** Only one example of human feedback (Figure 6, schematic for a task without ground truth); full traces of a generation–feedback cycle (generation, feedback, generation, feedback, generation) are needed to understand the process. Standards for human evaluation in Table 1 and Figure 4 (novel behaviors) are not specified – who conducted the evaluation? The experiments in Table 1/Figure 4 are described as zero-shot (Figure 4 caption), yet Figure 6 gives a schematic for ambiguity resolution that would imply few-shot for novel behaviors; no such experiment is shown in the paper or appendices. The choice of \(k\) for the number of few-shot neighbors is not given. For Pick Cube (Appendix D.1), the few-shot reward code is nearly identical to the oracle code except for two constants; given this small difference, it is important to know what human feedback was used – is the result just luck? And why does few-shot underperform oracle in other settings (Lift Cube, Turn Faucet, Open Cabinet Drawer, Push Chair) despite near-identical code on Pick Cube? Is the oracle code always placed in context for few-shot?
- Section 4.2 (code errors before RL) is a necessary filtering step, but a concrete example of the generation/feedback process would be more valuable in the main body than Table 2.
- **Iteration cost:** Each iteration requires training policies, which is expensive; details on the duration of the iteration loop and ideas for early evaluation are missing.
- **Benchmark contamination concerns:** Most evaluation tasks are from benchmarks released before GPT-4’s knowledge cutoff date (September 2021). MuJoCo and MetaWorld tasks have been extensively studied; ManiSkill2 tasks largely overlap with ManiSkill (released mid 2021). It is unclear whether T2R’s reward design capability transfers to truly unseen tasks in a new simulator. (Reviewer 4 further notes that GPT-4 can reasonably be expected to draw from training data containing reward specifications from those environments.)
- **Novelty of locomotion behaviors:** The “novel” behaviors on MuJoCo (e.g., Hopper back flip) have appeared in prior literature (e.g., Christiano et al., 2017). It is unclear whether T2R benefits from that knowledge existing in the LLM’s training set.
- **Task type bias:** Most manipulation tasks are of pick-and-place or opening/closing/pushing nature – the most common types in RL literature. GPT-4 may be generally adept at writing rewards for those task types. Can T2R work for non-pick-and-place tasks?
- **Few-shot reliance:** T2R appears to work best with few-shot examples. For tasks that do not belong to a benchmark family, providing few-shot examples remains difficult.
- **Lack of robustness assessment:** Only one reward function is reported per task; it is unclear whether T2R is robust to stochasticity in LLM generation. The temperature used for generation is not shown.
- **Incomplete results for MetaWorld:** There are 11 tasks in MetaWorld but only 6 results are reported. Why are few-shot examples not used for MetaWorld?
- **Unclear contribution over prior work (Yu et al., 2023):** The main claimed technical novelty is dense vs. sparse rewards and the use of standard Python code (versus pythonic code). However, Yu et al. also uses pythonic code and utilizes sparse rewards mainly due to using MPC, not because of technical limitations. The paper should clarify more specifically what other technical contributions exist beyond “Python → Python” and “MPC → RL”.
- **No baseline comparison:** The paper does not compare against any baseline method (e.g., Yu et al., 2023 + RL) to contextualize performance, despite claiming that dense free-form reward + established packages can yield superior performance.
- **No evaluation with open-source LLMs:** The method relies on GPT-4 (closed-source), raising reproducibility and cost issues. Results with smaller open-source models (e.g., LLaMa-2) would benefit the community and show broader applicability.
- **Reproducibility concern:** Reliance on GPT-4 makes results inherently irreproducible – OpenAI does not guarantee indefinite support for specific API snapshots (e.g., GPT-4-0314 deprecated by June 2024).
- **Limited technical novelty of prompting approach:** The prompting techniques are straightforward and fundamentally easy to execute manually; the paper may be more of an observational study of GPT-4’s capabilities than a technical innovation. (This limits the significance compared to e.g., chain-of-thought prompting, which was an innovation in how to query a model for general-purpose NLP.)
