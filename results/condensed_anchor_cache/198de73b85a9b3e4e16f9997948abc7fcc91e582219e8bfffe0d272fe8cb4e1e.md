## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Dynamic Contrastive Skill Learning (DCSL), a method for offline skill learning from state sequences. It uses a contrastive loss to learn a similarity function that clusters semantically similar behaviors (based on state transitions) into the same skill embedding, and dynamically adjusts skill length via relabeling based on the similarity function. The skills are used with a downstream RL algorithm (SAC) or model-based controllers. Experiments on Ant-Maze, D4RL Kitchen, and Meta-World show improved performance over baselines (SPiRL, SkiMo). The method is motivated by addressing fixed-length skills and lack of semantic grouping in prior work.

### Strengths
- Investigates core questions in skill learning, including variable-length skills and skill differentiability (Reviewer 1).
- Skills are more general and less overfitting to specific behaviors due to the similarity function (Reviewer 2).
- Dynamic relabeling allows skills of different lengths, with evidence of benefit in some domains (Reviewer 2).
- Significantly better performance than baselines on downstream tasks on multiple benchmarks (Reviewers 1,2,3).
- More robust to lower-quality demonstrations, e.g., on the PP ME dataset (Reviewer 2).
- The approach is well-motivated, addressing important challenges in capturing semantically similar skills and adapting skill lengths (Reviewer 3).
- Provides a mostly clear and understandable description of a complex method and motivation for each algorithmic choice (Reviewer 1).
- Offline skill learning is an increasingly applicable field, especially as large robotics datasets become more prevalent (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses
- Disconnect between claimed contributions and empirical verification: it is not obvious that skills capture “more semantic context” (only AntMaze analysis in Figure 6 shows some separation, but it is not particularly semantic; a clearer semantic transfer, e.g., opening different drawers with the same skill in Franka Kitchen, would be more convincing) (Reviewer 1).
- Effect of skill length relabeling appears marginally significant; no analysis showing that shorter/longer skills are assigned appropriately, or how much time is wasted without relabeling, especially for pick-and-place tasks (Reviewer 1).
- It is not clear why DCSL specifically takes advantage of the offline RL setting; it could be applied to unsupervised skill learning (Reviewer 1).
- The method requires training many components jointly (two encoders in the similarity measure, encoder-decoder in the state encoder, target state model, skill prior, policy) with multiple objectives but no hyperparameter tuning discussion; combination likely needs tuning in practice (Reviewer 1).
- Skill embedding uses two randomly sampled intermediate states per trajectory, which introduces variance; no analysis of how the number of intermediate states is chosen or its theoretical ramifications (Reviewers 1 and 2; Reviewer 2 asks about why 4 key states, Reviewer 1 asks about number of intermediate states).
- Comparisons are limited: only variants of SPiRL and SkiMo; no comparison with unsupervised skill learning methods (e.g., OPAL, Hilbert representations, unsupervised-to-online RL) or non-BC methods like offline RL; such comparisons would strengthen the evaluation (Reviewers 1 and 2; Reviewer 2 recommends Ajay et al. 2021, Park et al. 2024, Kim et al. 2024).
- No qualitative comparisons (videos) of the learned behaviors (Reviewer 2).
- Experimental and ablative studies are not fully comprehensive: only one D4RL Kitchen dataset (mixed) used, though two additional datasets exist; only one Meta-World task (pick-and-place) tested; Ant-Maze experiments limited; no ablation omitting the similarity function to isolate state-transition-based skill learning vs. SPiRL’s action-based approach; no sensitivity analysis for the similarity threshold epsilon; no detailed explanation of how target state is considered reached in continuous space (Reviewer 3).
- The paper lacks a rigorous theoretical foundation with formal mathematical derivations and analysis (Reviewer 3).
- The paper does not discuss its limitations, which is essential for understanding boundaries of applicability (Reviewer 3).
- Writing clarity could be improved: e.g., definition of “skill trajectory” (Line 248), how z′≠z is determined in continuous space for negative sampling in Eq. 5, and how skill prior is learned (Reviewers 2 and 3).
- Some reviewers note that the method is straightforward overall, but one reviewer (Reviewer 3) finds the presentation less clear, while Reviewer 1 finds it mostly clear (disagreement on clarity).
