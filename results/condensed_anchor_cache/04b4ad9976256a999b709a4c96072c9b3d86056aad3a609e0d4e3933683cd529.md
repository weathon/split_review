## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces the first web-scale Time-Continual (TiC) benchmarks for continual training of vision-language models: TiC-DataComp (12.7B timestamped image-text pairs, 2014–2022, 7 timesteps), TiC-YFCC (15M pairs, 2008–2014, 4 timesteps), and TiC-RedCaps (12M pairs, 2011–2020, 4 timesteps). Using these benchmarks, the authors create dynamic evaluation tasks (retrieval and LAIONNet-like classification) to measure temporal robustness. They show that OpenAI’s CLIP (trained up to 2020) loses ≈8% zero-shot accuracy on retrieval from 2021–2022 compared to more recently trained OpenCLIP models. They further study efficient continual training and demonstrate that a simple rehearsal-based approach (equal replay) that continues from the last checkpoint and replays old data reduces compute by 2.3–3.5× compared to training from scratch (Oracle) while achieving comparable performance.

### Strengths
- **Important problem**: addresses the critical challenge of continually training large foundation models (e.g., CLIP) without full retraining, a necessity for keeping models up-to-date. (R2, R4)
- **Large-scale time-evolving benchmarks**: provides the first set of web-scale timestamped datasets (TiC-DataComp, TiC-YFCC, TiC-RedCaps) with multiple timesteps, filling a gap in the continual learning community. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- **Dynamic evaluation design**: retrieval and LAIONNet-like classification tasks (with fixed 1000 classes to isolate new concepts) effectively capture temporal robustness and distribution shifts. (R2)
- **Insightful analysis**: shows that optimizing for old data leads to worse performance on current tasks; demonstrates that picking continual SSL strategies based on downstream tasks can lead to poor design choices (e.g., best-pool filtering). (R2)
- **Practical rehearsal finding**: simple equal replay effectively mitigates catastrophic forgetting, achieving 2.3–3.5× compute savings vs. Oracle while maintaining performance. (R1, R4)
- **Clear experimental protocol**: establishes a fair evaluation framework with ID, backward, and forward transfer metrics. (R1)
- **Compute cost perspective**: the discussion of fine-tuning cost in terms of compute MAC provides a valuable framework for researchers. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited model scope**: benchmarks focus exclusively on CLIP; should incorporate more vision-language models. (R1)
- **Outdated dataset concern**: YFCC100M years (2008–2014) may be outdated; exploring newer datasets could be more representative. (R1)
- **Necessity questioned**: if only a small number of parameters are fine-tuned (e.g., prompt tuning), catastrophic forgetting may not be a major concern; if many parameters are fine-tuned, resource limitations arise. This calls into question the need for such a large-scale benchmark. (R1 – minority view; other reviewers support the benchmark’s necessity)
- **Missing comparison with diverse continual learning methods**: lacks evaluation of EWC, progressive neural networks, dynamic architectures, Continual-CLIP, and robust fine-tuning methods (e.g., from “Robust fine-tuning of zero-shot models”). (R3, R4)
- **No in-depth analysis of VL model tuning paradigms**: ignores prompt tuning, vision prompt tuning, and parameter-efficient tuning approaches for continual learning with CLIP. (R3)
- **No fine-tuning transfer analysis**: does not examine how the continually pre-trained checkpoint transfers to standard vision benchmarks beyond simple retrieval tasks. (R4)
- **Major results/ablations not presented** (R2):
  - Results for the LAIONNet-like dynamic classification task are missing.
  - Supporting evidence for “same maximum LR works best across all runs when using cosine schedule” is not shown.
  - Claim that “warm up helps training on data from the first time step, but hurts on subsequent time steps” appears contradicted by the presented table; reviewer expected warmup to be unnecessary given good initialization.
  - Claim that “random subsampling vs. other strategies shows minimal difference” is not supported by evidence.
- **Design flaws in continual learning setup** (R2):
  - Only 4–7 timesteps are used; findings may change significantly with 20–50 timesteps (e.g., memory buffers become far smaller, gap between CL methods and -all likely larger).
  - Memory constraint issues: for TiC-RedCaps and TiC-YFCC, the “all” and “exp/equal” conditions only differ at the last timestep (both can store 2/3 of data until then), undermining comparisons. This is partially mitigated in TiC-DataComp, but the claimed 3.5× compute saving may be inaccurate (buffer is 2.3× smaller).
  - Replay buffers may not be utilized effectively: due to computational constraints (35k iterations on 128M samples), the network can only pass through 1/k of past data per timestep. Random sampling and data imbalance across timesteps likely cause the performance gap, not memory constraint alone.
- **Unexplained differences between datasets** (R2):
  - In TiC-YFCC and TiC-RedCaps, forward transfer is significantly affected, but not in TiC-DataComp. The gap between backward transfer and ID retrieval is much smaller in TiC-DataComp. The authors should analyze distribution shifts in TiC-DataComp (e.g., a plot similar to Fig 15 for YFCC).
  - Fig 1(mid) and Fig 10 present a different picture than Table 2: intuitively ID retrieval performance should be higher than backward transfer, but this is not always observed. Hypothesis: past tasks are easier; this needs explanation.
- **Insufficient discussion of related work** (R2):
  - (Cai et al., 2021) covers similar analysis (YFCC distribution shift, LR modulation, replay, web-scale training with 600K timesteps).
  - ImageNet IID findings are already detailed in “Computationally Budgeted Continual Learning: What Does Matter?” (CVPR 2023) and “One Pass ImageNet” (NeurIPS-W 2021).
  - Compute budget considerations in (Bornschein et al., 2022) and (Jang et al., 2022) are relevant but not discussed.
- **Missing details**: what is the size of the dynamic retrieval and LAIONNet test sets per chunk? (R2)
- **Minor**: the backward/forward transfer metric should cite (Lopez-Paz & Ranzato, 2017) rather than (Díaz-Rodríguez et al., 2018). (R2)
