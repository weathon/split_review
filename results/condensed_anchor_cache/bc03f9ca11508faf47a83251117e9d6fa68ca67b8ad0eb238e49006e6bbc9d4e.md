## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Bidirectional Negative Feedback (BNF), a novel LLM alignment loss that, unlike DPO and its variants, does not rely on pairwise contrastive losses and requires no extra tunable hyper-parameters. BNF establishes stable bidirectional negative feedback such that the gradient norm decreases linearly as the log-probability deviates from the reference in either direction, avoiding the unidirectional negative feedback of log-likelihood losses. Experiments on two QA benchmarks and four reasoning benchmarks (using preference training data from SimPO) show BNF achieves comparable performance to the best methods on QA while incurring a smaller reasoning performance drop (less alignment tax). BNF also works with non-pairwise data and exhibits smaller log-likelihood shifts and a lower Gini coefficient in logit shifts across tokens.

### Strengths
1. **Clear motivation and well-grounded theory**: The paper clearly identifies weaknesses of DPO variants (hyper-parameter sensitivity, instability on math tasks, unidirectional gradient feedback) and derives BNF from the goal of decreasing gradient norms as log-probs deviate from the reference.
2. **Novel and principled loss design**: BNF is well-motivated by the desire to avoid increasing gradient norms for large deviations, and it does not require pairwise contrastive losses, making it applicable to non-pairwise data.
3. **Strong empirical validation across diverse settings**: Experiments cover two QA benchmarks and four reasoning benchmarks, multiple backbone LLMs, and comparisons with strong baselines (DPO, IPO, KTO, SLiC-HF, ORPO, SimPO, CPO). BNF shows an overall favorable balance between value alignment and reasoning ability.
4. **Fewer hyper-parameters and no pairwise data requirement**: BNF eliminates the need for the β hyper-parameter of DPO and for paired preference examples, potentially making alignment more compute-efficient and applicable in settings where only individual preferred/dispreferred samples are available.
5. **Additional analysis and clarity**: The paper provides theoretical analysis of gradient behavior, visualizes logit and loss changes (Figure 2), and includes ablation experiments (Appendix D) to support its claims. The structure, equations, and charts are well presented.

### Weaknesses
1. **Training collapse definition and evidence not provided**: The paper invokes “training collapse” as a motivation but never defines it precisely, nor does it demonstrate that BNF mitigates this issue. The experiments in Appendix D do not directly address degradation during training that “training collapse” implies. *[R1]*
2. **Missing direct comparison with DPO+NLL baselines**: The paper argues that adding an NLL term to DPO-like losses leads to poor chat/QA performance, but it does not compare BNF against such baselines (e.g., DPO+NLL) on the same datasets. It is unclear whether BNF overcomes these specific limitations. *[R1, Q2]*
3. **Misalignment between motivation and experimental scope**: Section 2.3 discusses challenges specific to pairwise preference data in reasoning tasks, yet experiments do not explicitly explore training on such data. The connection between each point in Section 2.3 and a contribution or result of BNF is not clearly established. *[R1, Q3]*
4. **Claims not sufficiently distinguished from hypotheses**: Several statements are presented as conclusions but are not directly supported. For example: (a) smaller gradients for large deviations → less overfitting and reduced alignment tax (lines 496-471); (b) lower Gini coefficient is inherently desirable (it may be better to decrease probabilities of only incorrect tokens); (c) “the instability of DPO stems from … the unidirectional likelihood-derivative negative feedback” (lines 54-55) is stated as fact rather than a hypothesis. *[R1, Q4]*
5. **Rationale for the complexity of the BNF loss formula not explained**: Given that the core goal is to avoid increasing gradient norms for large deviations, it is not clear why the loss must be as complex as Equation (6), nor why the condition \(y_i \neq t_i\) is introduced in its second term. The theoretical underpinnings of that design choice and the implications of omitting it are not elaborated. *[R1, Q5; R2]*
6. **Missing analysis of BNF on mathematical tasks analogous to Figure 2**: Figure 2 illustrates challenges of creating a log-likelihood gap for mathematical tasks, but the paper does not provide a similar visualization or comparative analysis focusing on how BNF handles subtle differences between preferred/dispreferred responses in the mathematical domain. *[R2]*
7. **Performance gains over DPO are modest**: Across benchmarks, BNF’s performance is often close to DPO, raising questions about the practical significance of the improvement. *[R3]*
8. **Gemma experiments lack proper baselines**: The Gemma results use scores copied from another paper, so the comparisons are not directly controlled or reproducible. *[R3]*
9. **Statistical significance not reported**: It is unclear whether BNF’s performance differences relative to baselines are statistically significant or could be due to random variation or specific hyper-parameter settings of the baselines. *[R3]*
10. **Hyper-parameter sensitivity remains unclear**: BNF has fewer hyper-parameters, but it is unknown whether they are harder to tune or whether other baselines would close the gap with further tuning. *[R3]*
