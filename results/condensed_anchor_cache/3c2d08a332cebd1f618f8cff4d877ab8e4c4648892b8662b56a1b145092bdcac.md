## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates the relationship between effective dimensionality (computed from the Hessian spectrum) and adversarial robustness across multiple architectures (ResNet, VGG, ShuffleNet, MobileNetV2, RepVGG, YOLO), datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet), and adversarial training methods. The authors claim a near-linear inverse relationship: lower effective dimensionality correlates with better robustness. While the topic is relevant and the idea is novel to some reviewers, all reviewers agree that the empirical evidence does not convincingly support the central claims.

### Strengths
- The paper is clearly written and easy to follow (R1, R2, R4).
- The research question—finding a simple metric that correlates with adversarial robustness—is compelling and important for model selection and robust training design (R1, R2).
- The idea of linking effective dimensionality to robustness is considered novel by R2 and R3; to their knowledge, this connection has not been explored before.
- The paper presents a broad empirical investigation covering multiple architectures, datasets, and adversarial training methods, which is helpful for understanding the relationship (R3, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Empirical evidence for the claimed negative correlation is weak and often contradictory.**  
  R2 provides a detailed breakdown: for ResNet, the claimed trend appears in only 1 out of 9 plots (bottom right of Figure 2). ShuffleNet shows the opposite trend in 2/9 and no correlation in another 2/9. VGG yields the opposite trend or appears noisy in all plots where it appears. RepVGG shows the opposite trend in the bottom right plot. MobileNetV2 shows the opposite trend or noise in 4 out of 6 plots. Only YOLO consistently shows the claimed trend across its 3 plots. R1 notes that in the right column of Figure 2, several lines go up rather than down, contradicting the claim of a clear inverse trend across all datasets. R4 describes the relationship as “chaotic without clear trend.” The paper’s description of a “clear polynomial trend” (line 215) is considered overly generous (R1, R2, R4).

- **Evidence that adversarial training reduces effective dimensionality is also insufficient.**  
  In Figure 3 left, the adversarially trained model shows *higher* effective dimensionality than the standard trained model (R2). In Figure 4, when training without AWP, the relationship between effective dimensionality and robustness is not correlated in two of the three plots (center and right, looking at circle points). This suggests that the observed reduction in effective dimensionality is driven primarily by AWP (related to Sharpness-Aware Minimization) rather than by adversarial training in general (R2). R1 also found the adversarial training experiments unclear and requested more details.

- **Potential tautology or lack of theoretical distinction from existing metrics.**  
  R4 argues that effective dimensionality is inherently correlated with adversarial accuracy because both reflect the flatness of the loss landscape; the paper may be measuring the same phenomenon twice. R3 points out that effective dimensionality depends on the eigenvalues of the Hessian, which is closely related to traditional flatness measures (e.g., maximum or sum of eigenvalues). Without a theoretical explanation of why effective dimensionality should be a better or different predictor than these prior measures, the contribution remains purely observational and lacks depth (R3). R2 also notes that relevant connections—e.g., to Sharpness-Aware Minimization (Andriushchenko et al., 2023)—are missing.

- **Methodological limitations and missing details.**  
  - All experiments are run with only **one seed** (R1). This makes it impossible to assess variability or noise in the results, especially given the bumpiness of the plots. Running multiple seeds (even just three) would substantially strengthen the analysis (R1, R2).  
  - The choice of adversarial attack methods (three specific ones) is not justified, and the absence of a standard baseline like **FGSM** is questioned (R1).  
  - The paper mentions that “outliers” were removed from the data (line 265), which raises concerns about cherry-picking; the reviewers suggest showing all data (e.g., using different colors for outliers) instead of removing them (R1).  
  - The computational cost of computing the Hessian is not addressed; R1 asks whether a trick is used to scale this to larger models, and notes that only small models are used, contradicting the later claim of “production-scale” models (R1).  
  - Plot quality is criticized: legends overlap with plotted lines, and colors for the same model are inconsistent across subplots in Figure 2 (R2). Several typos and grammatical issues are noted (R1).

- **Disagreement on novelty.**  
  R1 states that the paper makes no novel contribution, while R2 and R3 consider the exploration of effective dimensionality as a robustness measure to be novel. This contrast in opinion reflects the thin line between an interesting observation and a sufficiently deep contribution (R1, R2, R3).
