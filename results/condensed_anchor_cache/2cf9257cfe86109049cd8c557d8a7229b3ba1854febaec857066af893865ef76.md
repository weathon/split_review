## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes NCSNAD (and a variant NCSNADVAL using a validation set), a semi-supervised/one-class anomaly detection method for tabular data that employs a Noise Conditional Score Network (NCSN) to learn the gradient of the log-density of normal data. The anomaly score is derived from a simplified loss function of the score network. The method is evaluated on a large benchmark of 57 tabular datasets (plus 15 additional datasets) against 49 baseline methods, reporting average ROC-AUC, F1, and ROC-PR. The authors also provide a visual interpretability example using image data.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-organized and easy to follow.
- Extensive experiments: the largest benchmark for tabular anomaly detection to date (57+15 datasets, 49 baselines), with substantial effort in reproducibility (code, readme, etc.).
- Adequate theoretical derivation and proof of the method.
- Good interpretability example (Figure 3) demonstrating the score map for anomaly localization in images.
- To the best of reviewer 2’s knowledge, the first application of score networks (a diffusion model variant) to anomaly detection.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty.** The method is a straightforward application of the established NCSN with a simplified loss function and minor adaptations (time-step embedding). Several prior works have introduced diffusion models to anomaly detection (reviewer 4 cites [1],[2],[3]); using NCSN specifically is an incremental step. Reviewer 2 considers it the first score-network application, but other reviewers note the lack of original adaptation.
- **Unfair experimental comparison.**  
  - The network architecture was optimized (Appendix A) on a subset of the benchmark, while baselines were not similarly optimized, giving NCSNAD a significant advantage.  
  - NCSNADVAL uses a labeled validation set for model selection/early stopping; other baselines were not given the same benefit. If labeled validation data is available, it could be used to train semi-supervised models directly.  
  - Several baselines have suboptimal implementations: the VAE uses a sigmoid activation in the last layer, unsuitable for standardized real-valued tabular data (should be linear). The PyOD implementation of DeepSVDD lacks critical optimizations from the original paper (Ruff et al.).  
  - The box-plot comparisons (ROC-AUC, F1, ROC-PR) are less informative than relative rankings across datasets; the average performance masks that NCSNADVAL does not achieve the best performance on the majority of datasets (reviewer 4 estimates best on a minority). The authors should provide per-dataset rankings.
- **Domain clarity and setting.** The abstract and line 55-57 incorrectly state unsupervised learning, but the method is trained exclusively on normal data (semi-supervised/one-class). The truly unsupervised setting (training set containing anomalies) is not tested. Reviewer 1 notes one-class methods typically fail under contamination.
- **Data leakage and experimental design concerns.**  
  - The MI-F/MI-V datasets come from 18 experiments; splitting these across train/val/test without respecting experiment boundaries causes cross-contamination and overestimates performance (reviewer 2).  
  - Subsampling datasets to 50,000 data points (line 224) may bias anomaly rarity and heterogeneity; it is unclear whether this is total dataset size or per split, and whether all original anomalies are kept.  
  - F-beta scores are hard to compare across datasets; average precision (shown in appendix) or adjusted measures (Campos et al., 2016) are recommended.  
  - Simpler methods (LUNAR, KPCA, GMM) achieve comparable accuracy; the added complexity and computational cost of NCSNAD is not justified.
- **Interpretability section is disconnected.** Section 5 shows interpretability only on computer vision (CIFAR-10) not on tabular data, which is the paper’s focus. The authors should either demonstrate tabular interpretability or remove this section.
- **Missing motivation and methodological discussion.**  
  - The choice of NCSN over DDPM (used by the closest related work DTE) is not explained; pros and cons of each for anomaly detection are absent.  
  - The title “Estimating Gradients” is not sufficiently reflected: the paper does not clarify how the score function (gradient of log density) is directly leveraged for anomaly detection beyond using it as a generative process.  
  - The method claims no additional prior knowledge, but it is reconstruction-based, assuming normal data can be accurately reconstructed. This prior assumption, and its limitations for complex/multimodal normal distributions, are not discussed.
- **Minor presentation issues.** Spacing too large near references (e.g., Appendix  C); y-axis labels in Figure 2 too small; x/y labels in Figure 3 not needed for images; the proposed method is not marked as “(ours)” in comparative charts, making it hard to locate.
- **Unanswered or insufficiently addressed queries.**  
  - Detailed structure of MLP2048 (layers, parameters per dataset) is not given, even in the appendix.  
  - Number of times NCSNADVAL achieves best performance across the 57 datasets is not reported.  
  - Five random seeds are used, but it is unclear whether seeds affect dataset subsampling, method initialization, or both.  
  - How unsupervised methods (e.g., PyOD) are applied in the fit/predict paradigm with separate test sets needs clarification; mixing train+test data for prediction would be incorrect.  
  - Line 59-60 states the network “learns” during testing; no network updates occur at test time – rewording needed.
