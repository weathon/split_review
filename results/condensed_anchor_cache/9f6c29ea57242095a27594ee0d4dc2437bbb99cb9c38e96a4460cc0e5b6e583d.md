- Scores: 6, 8, 8, 6, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper empirically demonstrates that multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) and MLP-Mixer models can learn in-context on synthetic regression and classification tasks, performing competitively with Transformers given the same compute budget. On classical relational reasoning tasks (match-to-sample, sphere oddball, line oddball) from psychology, MLPs actually outperform Transformers. The authors also show a transition from in-weight to in-context learning with increasing task diversity for all architectures. While the findings challenge the assumption that attention is required for in-context learning, the analysis is confined to small, stylized synthetic settings, and the definition of ICL is not made concrete. Reviewers disagree on the significance and novelty: some find it interesting and valuable, while one (score 3) argues it is not novel and not meaningful.

### Strengths
- First paper to highlight that MLPs alone (without attention) can lead to in-context learning; this challenges a common assumption (Reviewers 1, 3).
- Verification of the transition from in-weight to in-context learning with increasing task diversity, observed for all architectures (Reviewer 1).
- Clear, well-written presentation that is easy to follow (Reviewers 1, 2, 4, 5).
- Extensive and thorough experimental design for the considered synthetic ICL problems (Reviewers 1, 3).
- Introduction of psychology-inspired relational tasks (match-to-sample, sphere oddball, line oddball) provides valuable insights into Transformer limitations – Transformers perform poorly when test exemplars differ significantly from training data (Reviewer 3).
- Thought-provoking; questions common assumptions about which architectures can exhibit ICL and what ICL really is (Reviewer 4).
- Connection to related works is clearly presented (Reviewer 5).

### Weaknesses
- All experiments are in stylized, restricted synthetic settings; it is unclear whether observations generalize to realistic ICL scenarios (Reviewers 1, 4, 5). Even within synthetic settings, more complex problems (e.g., decision trees, fitting MLPs) could have been considered (Reviewer 1). Reviewer 5 finds the synthetic results insufficiently convincing to be generalizable.
- Missing experimental details: the input distribution for Section 2.1 is not specified in main text; details on how MLP and MLP-Mixer are used (e.g., concatenation order, permutation handling) are in appendix without full reference; theoretical discussion in appendix is not connected to main paper (Reviewer 1).
- Lack of discussion about *why* MLPs can be competitive and why Transformers fail on relational tasks (Reviewers 1, 3). Reviewer 2 requests analysis of functional similarity between architectures – e.g., do MLP-Mixers approximate gradient descent or mimic self-attention layers? Are readout layers at different depths computing similar solutions?
- One reviewer (score 3) argues the paper overclaims significance; MLPs are universal function approximators, so it is not surprising that they can solve these tractable problems (likely finding Bayes-optimal solutions). Another reviewer (score 3) explicitly states the contribution is not novel: it is already known that MLPs can learn set/sequence data, and the paper does not point out known disadvantages of naive MLP concatenation (lack of permutation-invariance, handling variable context lengths). This reviewer concludes the study is “not meaningful” – a clear minority view contrasted with other reviewers who find it interesting.
- The comparison is unfair: Transformers are applied autoregressively (single token prediction) while MLP/MLP-Mixer models access all data at once (non-autoregressive). A bi-directional (encoder-style) Transformer should be included for fair compute comparison (Reviewer 4). The paper does not test MLPs or MLP-Mixers autoregressively (Reviewer 4).
- The paper does not run experiments in the autoregressive setting of Garg et al. (2022) or von Oswald et al. (2023) – a standard framework for studying ICL (Reviewer 2).
- Only small toy problems are used; no real data (e.g., real regression datasets) despite the fact that similar synthetic tasks dominate the literature (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 5 requests empirical results on real-world few-shot learning data.
- The definition of ICL is not made concrete; it remains unclear why ordinary linear regression (computing a projection for a new point) would not count as ICL if the task changes per context. The paper blurs the line between “in-weight learning” and “in-context learning” (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 1 asks about previous papers that argue attention is required for ICL.
- The paper does not address the unique strength of Transformers: dynamically allocating compute/memory based on in-context length, and handling variable context lengths with a single model. Whether MLPs can also handle different context lengths is not tested (Reviewers 1, 2).
- The experiments mix the roles of expressivity and optimization; it is not disentangled whether the results are due to model capacity or optimization dynamics (Reviewer 1).
- Missing analyses: effect of varying data covariance on task diversity (Reviewer 1); applying the “meta” recursive construction where each input point is itself a concatenated set of examples from different problem classes (Reviewer 4).
