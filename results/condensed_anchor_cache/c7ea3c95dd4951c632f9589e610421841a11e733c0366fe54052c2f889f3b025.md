## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies mean-field Langevin dynamics (MFLD) for two-layer neural networks under anisotropic input data, focusing on the k-sparse parity problem. It shows that aligning prominent directions of anisotropic data with the target support improves both statistical complexity (sample size) and computational complexity (network width). A coordinate transform based on gradient covariance is proposed to make the width independent of k. Lower bounds for kernel methods are also provided.

### Strengths
- Technically solid and well written, with clear contributions and laying out of the problem setting (R1, R2, R3).  
- Extending the isotropic setting to anisotropic data is definitely interesting and important for understanding two-layer nonlinear NNs (R1).  
- The results are novel: showing how anisotropy can be leveraged to improve learning guarantees for a specific setting (R3).  
- The coordinate transformation result could be of interest for practical neural network training (R3).  
- The implication of a trade-off between statistical and computational complexity is interesting (R3).  
- Related work seems well addressed (R2).  
- Reviewer 1 is more positive overall (score 8 vs. 6,6), noting that the paper is technically solid and studies an important problem, while R2 and R3 are more cautious about significance.

### Weaknesses
- **Assumption of known matrix \(A\)**: The paper assumes \(A\) is known a priori. This is too strong and makes the problem more theoretically interesting than practically relevant, especially given the abstract’s motivation from realistic datasets. What if \(A\) is estimated from data? (R1). Could a preconditioned version of MFLD be used instead of a coordinate transform? (R1).
- **Contrived setting and lack of practical connection**: The assumptions are highly contrived and no attempt is made to link them to any practical task in a meaningful way. It is unclear why progress in this formal setting (beyond previous works) is worth pursuing for a broad conference like ICLR (R2).  
- The claim “anisotropy helps” seems too strong, specific to the k-sparse parity problem, and not a general statement for neural networks. Better motivation is needed for connections to real-world scenarios where features are not aligned with coordinate axes (R3).
- The observation that structured data can improve performance has already been made in regression settings (cited Ghorbani et al., Ba et al., etc.). The paper does not clearly articulate why the k-parity classification problem is worth studying separately from those regression analyses, weakening its impact (R3).
- **Definition of anisotropic input data** is not clearly stated. Does it exactly refer to the mathematical setting at the beginning of Sec 1.1? (R2).  
- **Applicability of regression results**: Why can existing results for regression not be directly applied to binary classification by converting to a regression problem? (R2).  
- **Niche venue concern**: The work seems very niche and may be more suitable for a specialized venue than a generalist conference like ICLR (R2).  
- **ReLU/activation generality**: The analysis uses a smooth activation (tanh). Can the results apply to nonsmooth activations like ReLU? (R3).  
- **Real-world applicability of coordinate transformation**: How applicable is the coordinate transformation to conventional neural network training for real-world datasets? (R3).  
- Minor typos: page 6, after Proposition 2: “otherwise, we *still*”; after Proposition 3: “Under this *condition*” (R1).
