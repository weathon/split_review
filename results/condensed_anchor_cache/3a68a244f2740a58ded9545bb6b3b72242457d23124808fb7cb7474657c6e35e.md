## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes VibeSpace, a pipeline that uses prompting LLMs (ChatGPT) to generate lists of similar entities and relation sentences for arbitrary domains (movies, books, songs), then learns entity embeddings via Word2Vec, and finally trains autoencoders to map between domains. Experiments show label separability in single-domain embeddings and cross-domain mapping consistency. Reviewers agree the idea is interesting and the data collection is automated, but raise major concerns about novelty, evaluation, clarity, and overstated claims.

### Strengths

1. **Interesting idea** – synthesizing vector spaces by prompting LLMs to generate entities and their relations, especially for cross-domain mappings. (R1)
2. **Fully automated data acquisition** with LLMs, achieving good quality as validated in Section 4.1 (comparison of LLM-based vs. non-LLM-based datasets). (R3; note R4 found no strengths, rating 1)
3. **Timely study** on how well LLMs can generate entities and relations of entities. (R1)
4. **Actionable pipeline** that leverages knowledge-rich LLMs for embedding extraction. (R2)
5. **Visualizations** (Figure 1, 2, 3) help the audience understand the embedding spaces and mapping consistency. (R2; though R3 questions Figure 2 interpretability – see Weaknesses)

### Weaknesses

1. **Unclear Section 3.3 (vector space mapping)** – multiple reviewers note the description of how mappings are constructed is lacking essential details. (R2, R3) 
2. **Overclaiming on cold-start problem** – the Abstract and Introduction claim VibeSpace is a “data-free solution to the cold-start problem” for recommender systems, but no experiment validates this. The Conclusion implies it is future work. (R1, R2, R3)
3. **Lack of baselines and comparisons** – no comparisons for mapping consistency (Figure 3) against existing methods; no discussion of why Word2Vec is chosen over alternatives (R2); Section 4.2 does not compare LLM-based embeddings with non-LLM-based ones (R3). Only one LLM (ChatGPT) is tested; other models like LLaMA or Vicuna should be evaluated. (R1)
4. **Unclear metrics in Section 4.2** – Tables 2–4 report F1 scores (some 0.00) without explaining how to interpret quality of separation. (R2)
5. **Lack of novelty** – the idea of extracting similar entities to enrich context has been studied before (e.g., Meng et al. 2018, weakly-supervised text classification); this work only replaces a corpus with an LLM. (R2; echoed by R4: “oversimple model presenting no insight”)
6. **Cost not addressed** – the method’s claim of removing “costly data acquisition” is questionable: calling an LLM hundreds of thousands of times may be more expensive than traditional data engineering. (R1)
7. **Popularity bias** – LLMs tend to generate popular entities; the method likely suffers from popularity bias, and the claim that niche entities are uncovered is biased because MovieLens (1M?) contains only pre-2003 movies while ChatGPT’s training data is more recent (2019). (R1)
8. **Domain overlap** – claiming movies, books, and songs are “non-overlapping domains” is unconvincing; many movies originate from books. (R2)
9. **Embedding space interpretability concerns** – Figure 2 shows points with the same label (e.g., “thrilling”) scattering and interleaving with other labels, which undermines the claim of “meaningful and interpretable positioning within vector spaces.” (R3)
10. **Missing materials** – code, datasets, and even samples of extracted entities are not provided. (R1)
11. **Purpose of Figure 1 unclear** – the figure is presented without clear explanation of what it demonstrates. (R2)
12. **Overall lack of solid experiments** – R4 (rating 1) states the model is oversimple and lacks insight, with no rigorous experimental validation.
