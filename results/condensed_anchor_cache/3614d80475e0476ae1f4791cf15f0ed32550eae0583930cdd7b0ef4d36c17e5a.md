- Scores: 8, 6, 8, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces ProverGen, a framework for generating first-order logic (FOL) reasoning datasets by combining symbolic provers (Prover9) with LLMs. It creates 1.5K evaluation examples and a larger training set. The pipeline uses a top-down approach (logic skeleton generation, then LLM-based translation to natural language) to ensure logical coherence, diverse language, and varying difficulty with distractions. Evaluations show that state-of-the-art LLMs struggle on ProverGen (e.g., ~50% on hard problems). Finetuning Llama3.1-8B-Instruct on ProverGen yields large in-distribution gains (>30%) and modest out-of-distribution gains (5-8%) on FOLIO and PrOntoQA. Three reviewers rated the paper positively (scores 8, 6, 8), praising the pipeline, natural language diversity, and the challenge posed. One reviewer (score 3) raised serious concerns about novelty, dataset quality, coverage, and significance, arguing that the dataset closely resembles ProntoQA, lacks verification of NL translations, omits some FOL constructs (e.g., binary predicates), and does not clearly reveal new insights about LLM reasoning.

### Strengths

- **Novel top-down generation framework:** ProverGen’s pipeline (background story generation, logic skeleton generation, statement translation) is the first automatic pipeline that fully encompasses first-order logic (FOL) relationships while being scalable, faithful, and providing complete reasoning chains. (R1, R3)
- **Natural language diversity and realism:** Using an LLM to convert logical forms into natural language yields linguistically diverse and realistic sentences, contrasting with template-based synthetic data pipelines. (R1, R3)
- **Guaranteed correctness:** Each example’s proof tree is verified by the symbolic prover Prover9, ensuring logical validity. (R1)
- **Challenging benchmark:** State-of-the-art LLMs struggle with ProverGen problems, even with chain-of-thought prompting (e.g., ~50% accuracy on hard problems), and the dataset includes varying difficulty levels and two types of distractions (task-irrelevant premises) to increase comprehensiveness. (R2, R3)
- **Finetuning improvement:** Training on ProverGen leads to consistent improvements on both in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks (e.g., FOLIO, PrOntoQA), with larger gains compared to previous logical reasoning datasets. (R1, R2, R3)
- **Reduction of disconnected and contradictory premises:** The top-down approach mitigates issues found in bottom-up datasets (e.g., ProofWriter) where premises may be disconnected or self-contradictory, leading to more reliable evaluation. (R3)
- **Clear presentation and reproducibility:** The paper is well-written, clearly explains how ProverGen differs from existing FOL benchmarks, evaluates multiple LLMs with standard and CoT prompting, and promises to release code and dataset. (R1, R2)

### Weaknesses

- **Limited novelty compared to ProntoQA:** One reviewer (R4) argues that ProverGen’s creation largely resembles ProntoQA, with the main difference being LLM-based translation for more natural statements. This reviewer contends that it is insufficiently demonstrated how this difference contributes novelty, what limitations or insights of LLMs are uniquely revealed by ProverGen, and that other benchmarks could not have provided these insights. (R4)
- **FOL coverage gaps:** The dataset may not cover all FOL constructs. Specifically, R4 notes that the “goal” fact is always an instantiated literal with a unary predicate (e.g., Elegant(Sawyer), Swift(jack)), and the pipeline does not include cases with binary predicates (e.g., FriendOf(Amy, Bob)) or composite facts (e.g., FriendOf(Amy, Bob) ∧ Person(Bob)). The set of deduction rules used in proof skeleton generation is not fully described; R1 asks whether rules for universal and existential quantifiers are generated, and what completeness properties the skeleton generation procedure has. (R1, R4)
- **Lack of quality verification for NL translations:** There is no quantitative assessment of whether the LLM-generated natural language statements correctly align with the ground-truth FOL rules. The universal rule translations are only checked by an LLM for commonsense consistency, which can be noisy due to hallucination. A small held-out set with manual annotations would provide a minimum quality measure. (R3, R4)
- **Potential for background knowledge exploitation:** Because the generated facts and rules are designed to be consistent with the LLM’s commonsense knowledge, models can use background knowledge as a heuristic when solving ProverGen problems, rather than performing genuine logical deduction. (R1)
- **Modest out-of-distribution generalization:** While ID performance improves significantly (>30%) after finetuning, OOD gains on FOLIO and PrOntoQA are marginal (5-8%). This raises concerns about whether skills learned from ProverGen transfer to broader reasoning tasks, and whether the dataset patterns are too specific. (R2)
- **Limited scope and lack of evaluation on general reasoning benchmarks:** The work focuses exclusively on FOL deductive reasoning, without evaluating on broader reasoning benchmarks (e.g., MMLU, GSM8K, BIG-bench). This makes it difficult to assess whether FOL improvements translate to general reasoning capabilities. (R2)
- **Unfair finetuning comparisons:** R4 points out that the model finetuned on FOLIO was trained with only 1K examples, whereas the models finetuned on ProverGen and another dataset used 50K examples. This large discrepancy prevents reliable comparison of effectiveness across datasets. (R4)
- **Insufficient analysis of domain distribution and dataset diversity:** The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the domain distribution (e.g., from WordNet categories) in the generated dataset, making it difficult to evaluate coverage and potential biases. (R2, R1)
- **Risk of indirect contradictions from distracting premises:** Although distracting premises are designed not to affect the core deduction, R3 asks whether they could introduce indirect contradictory conclusions. The paper does not address this possibility. (R3)
- **Quality control gaps:** The pipeline lacks explicit quality control steps to filter invalid or erroneous cases, particularly during logic skeleton generation and FOL-to-NL translation, given the probabilistic nature of LLMs. (R3)
- **Data contamination not discussed:** R1 notes that the paper does not discuss the disadvantage of data contamination for manually-annotated datasets, and how ProverGen relates to data contamination concerns. (R1)
- **Incomplete ablation and experimental details:** R1 and R2 mention that the paper could have explored more few-shot examples in prompts, studied case-level improvements from finetuning, and investigated whether changing the generation model (e.g., from Llama3.1-70B-Instruct to a more advanced model) affects results. (R1, R2)
- **Unverified benchmarks:** R4 notes that the training set used for finetuning experiments is not presented as part of the contribution and lacks direct verification of quality and FOL alignment. (R4)
- **Significance questioned:** R4 argues that the dataset (only 1.5K evaluation examples) merely benchmarks deductive reasoning, and to be significant it must reveal a new property that is exclusively tested in ProverGen but missing from other benchmarks. The paper does not sufficiently demonstrate such insights. (R4)

Note: While three reviewers found the paper strong, the fourth reviewer (score 3) raised substantial concerns about novelty, quality, and significance, as detailed above. These contrasting views highlight disagreement over the contribution level.
