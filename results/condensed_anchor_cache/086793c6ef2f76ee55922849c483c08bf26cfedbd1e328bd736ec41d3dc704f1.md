## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SGU-SQL, a framework that leverages structural information in user queries and database schemas to improve LLM-based text-to-SQL generation. It represents queries and schemas as graphs, links them via structure-aware encoding, decomposes the linked structure using syntax trees, and incrementally generates SQL. Experiments on Spider and BIRD show improvements over several baselines.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses a critical issue: LLMs often struggle with ambiguous user intents, complex schemas, and SQL’s strict syntax; the framework is designed to improve reliability and robustness.
- The approach is novel and intuitive, combining graph-based structure linking with syntax-based decomposition prompting, moving beyond traditional few-shot and chain-of-thought methods.
- Strong empirical results on Spider and BIRD datasets, outperforming 11 fine-tuning models, 7 structure learning models, and 11 in-context learning models.
- The method is evaluated on both open-source and closed-source LLMs, providing objective comparisons.
- The prompting strategy yields substantial improvements over naive few-shot and CoT baselines across different LLM families.
- The structure-linking mechanism reduces the input length to the LLM by filtering relevant schema elements, improving generation quality.
- The paper is well-articulated and easy to follow; experiments and ablation studies are designed with thorough understanding.
- Real-world applicability and potential for large production databases are highlighted.

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear method description:** The use of schema-linkage in SQL generation via decomposition (section 3.1) and the role of Eq. 21 are not clearly explained. The PRELIMINARIES section includes detailed equations that do not clearly connect to the method (e.g., appears to describe GNN training or prompting but lacks clarity).
- **BIRD performance not competitive:** The reported execution accuracy on BIRD is 61%, while the current leaderboard shows ~74% (and recent methods like CHASE-SQL achieve ~80%). This underperformance questions the method’s effectiveness on complex, realistic datasets.
- **Missing comparisons with recent SOTA:** Several strong recent methods are omitted: CHESS, Distillery, CHASE-SQL. Distillery suggests LLMs already handle complex schemas well without explicit schema linking or decomposition, directly challenging the paper’s core motivation. CHASE-SQL shows that enhanced sampling alone lifts BIRD performance to ~80%, indicating that output verification may be the main challenge, not schema linking.
- **Ablation studies are insufficient:** No ablation on the graph representation of the user query (vs. standard NL representation) or the database (vs. code representation like `CREATE TABLE (...)`. The “structure-aware linking” (dual-graph encoding) is a key contribution but lacks clear quantitative/qualitative ablation. “Syntax-based decomposition” is not ablated against direct SQL generation without decomposition. Table 3 in appendix only compares with a simple prompt decomposition approach that achieves comparable results (86.75 vs 87.5 on Spider).
- **Crucial tables relegated to appendix:** Table 2 (BIRD results) and Table 3 (decomposition comparison) contain key evidence but are not in the main text, hindering accessibility.
- **Not tested on Spider-2:** Spider-2 offers larger and more diverse databases that better reflect production settings; testing on it would strengthen the evaluation of scalability and real-world applicability.
- **Insufficient details on structure-linking model:** The training methodology, architecture choices, and evaluation of the GNN used for linking are not adequately described. Questions remain about the training details, the GNN’s hyperparameters (e.g., LoRA rank), and its performance alone.
- **Lack of analysis on computational complexity:** The cost of constructing graphs and linking nodes at a high level is not discussed, which matters for real-world deployment.
- **No robustness analysis for database changes:** In practice, databases evolve frequently; the framework’s components that remain relevant under such changes are not discussed.
- **Limited future direction:** The paper lacks a discussion of how the work could be extended (e.g., to other SQL dialects, to handle noisy or distributed schemas), which limits its impact on the research community.
- **Minor/Incomplete comparisons:** Many baselines in Table 1 (Spider) are not text-to-SQL specific, so the advantage over dedicated systems may be overstated. The paper does not show performance on the BIRD test split via leaderboard submission (only dev). A comparison with standard CoT and few-shot methods is requested but only partially provided.
