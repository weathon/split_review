## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes AlignDiff, a framework to augment few-shot semantic segmentation training by synthesizing images and mask annotations using pre-trained text-to-image diffusion models. It identifies three misalignments when applying diffusion models directly: instance-level (generated samples inconsistent with target task, e.g., OOD or wrong texture), scene-level (object-centric bias, unrealistic multi-object layouts), and annotation-level (pixel masks are not produced). Solutions are: 1) normalized masked textual inversion to bind a learnable word embedding to the few provided real examples; 2) copy-paste augmentation to blend synthesized instances into real scene backgrounds; 3) semi-supervised iterative refinement of the segmenter to improve coarse masks. Experiments on PASCAL-5i, COCO-20i, and FSS-1000 under generalized few-shot segmentation (GFSS) and few-shot segmentation (FSS) settings show improved novel IoU. The method demonstrates robustness even when combined with simple fine-tuning.

### Strengths

- Paper is well-written, well-organized, and easy to follow.
- Identification of three specific misalignments in applying text-to-image models to few-shot segmentation is valuable.
- Solutions, while individually simple or existing, are effectively combined and ablated extensively (both qualitative and quantitative in appendix).
- Technically sound; the method is easy to implement with any latent diffusion model.
- Experiments show significant improvement on novel categories over baselines; the method is robust, achieving on-par performance even with simple fine-tuning where other methods degrade.
- Addresses the open challenge of generating scene images with pixel-level masks using diffusion models.
- Related work is complete and discussed in detail.

### Weaknesses

- **Limited novelty**: The three main contributions are extensions of existing approaches with minor adaptations. Copy-paste is used out of the box without task-specific adaptation. The iterative semi-supervised training does not introduce innovation in the training procedure or loss function. Reviewer 4 notes the main performance gain appears to come from combining two prior papers ([B] and [C]).
- **Missing standard 1-shot evaluation**: The paper does not report 1-shot results, which is a standard setting in few-shot segmentation and common in prior work (Reviewer 3 considers this a major omission).
- **Incomplete baselines and comparisons**: DiffuMask without the authors' prompt engineering is described in the evaluation setup but missing from Tables 1 and 2. A comprehensive comparison with recent state-of-the-art methods (e.g., Xu, Qianxiong et al., "Self-Calibrated Cross Attention Network for Few-Shot Segmentation", ICCV 2023) is needed; the presented results do not demonstrate substantial improvement over existing methods (Reviewer 3).
- **Harmful categories**: Synthetic data can hurt performance for many categories (Fig. A.2). Deeper analysis is needed on failure patterns—whether categories have specific visual attributes that the diffusion model struggles to capture, and a study of performance across a spectrum from common to rare classes (e.g., iNaturalist) to understand practical applicability (Reviewer 2). The limitation section acknowledges degradation when the domain gap is large, yet few-shot segmentation is most useful for rare categories where data is hard to collect.
- **Lack of 1-shot results** (also noted above).
- **Missing evidence for "adjective" claim**: The claim that treating the learnable embedding as an adjective leads to faster and more stable convergence is not supported by evidence (Reviewer 1).
- **Computational cost**: The method is expensive—requires per-category textual inversion and iterative training of the segmentor. The paper only compares mask generation speed; a comparison of the total pipeline time with alternatives (e.g., Grounded Diffusion) would benefit the community (Reviewers 1, 4).
- **Unclear "copy-paste" details**: The process is not clearly explained (only citing another paper); sample images in Figure 2 are too dark. Questions arise: does copy-paste require a post-hoc harmonization method? How is the multi-object layout obtained with only a few examples of the new instance? (Reviewer 4)
- **Terminology confusion**: "Synthetic distribution" is misleading (usually implies simulator ground truth). "Out-of-distribution generation" is not well-defined in terms of prompts and images; this usage differs from standard OOD in uncertainty/robustness (Reviewer 3).
- **Problem setting**: The paper should clarify upfront that "few-shot segmentation" here uses only a few annotated examples for novel classes (not semi-supervised with many unlabeled examples) to avoid confusion with other literature (Reviewer 4).
- **Claimed contribution not novel**: Using a special adjective token for a specific instance was already proposed in [A], yet the paper presents it as a contribution without clear differentiation (Reviewer 4).
- **Limited analysis on FSS-1000**: Comparison on the FSS-1000 dataset is sparse (Reviewer 1).
- **Ablation on amount of generated samples** is missing and would be valuable (Reviewer 1).
- Whether compared methods in Tables 1 and 2 also use base classes (an extra source of information) is unclear (Reviewer 1).
