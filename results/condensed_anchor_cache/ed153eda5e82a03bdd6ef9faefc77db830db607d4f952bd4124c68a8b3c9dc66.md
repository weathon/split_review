## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes Self-MoA, a variant of Mixture-of-Agents (MoA) that replaces the standard practice of sampling responses from multiple different LLMs (proposers) with sampling multiple responses from a single top-performing LLM. The authors argue that MoA overemphasizes diversity at the expense of proposer quality, and that Self-MoA outperforms standard MoA across several benchmarks (AlpacaEval 2.0, MMLU, CRUX, MATH). On AlpacaEval 2.0, Self-MoA applied to a strong open-source model achieves a new state-of-the-art score and top ranking. The paper then studies the quality–diversity trade-off in a controlled setting (experts for MMLU, CRUX, MATH) and finds that end-MoA quality is more sensitive to proposer quality than to diversity. A sequential variant, Self-MoA-Seq, is introduced to handle many proposals without requiring a very long context window. The paper discusses scenarios where mixing different LLMs can still be beneficial.

### Strengths

- **First thorough study of the quality–diversity trade-off in MoA.** The paper systematically investigates how proposer composition affects final performance, revealing that low-quality proposers can hurt MoA even when diversity is high. This finding highlights important pitfalls for the community. [R1]
- **Practical advancement.** Self-MoA is an effective revision of MoA that simplifies the pipeline (one proposer model) while matching or exceeding standard MoA. On AlpacaEval 2.0 it achieves a new SoTA score, which is a significant result. [R1, R2, R4]
- **Interesting counter‑intuitive finding.** The result that duplicating the best model can outperform mixing diverse models goes against common views of LLM ensembling. [R3]
- **Sound argument and thorough analysis.** The paper uses controlled experiments, creative methods to illustrate the quality–diversity trade-off, and clear figures that complement the findings. The structure is logical and the analysis is comprehensive. [R1, R3]
- **Clear writing.** The paper is well written and easy to follow, with few errors. [R1, R2]

**One reviewer (R3) was significantly more positive**, praising the paper as a good contribution with sound arguments and thoroughness (score 6). The other three reviewers (scores 3,3,3) had more reservations, especially about soundness of claims and technical novelty.

### Weaknesses

1. **Soundness of the main claim: Is mixing different LLMs actually beneficial?**  
   - Table 3 is central to this question. The authors claim that only two MoA configurations slightly outperform or match Self-MoA (configuration “dddddd”). However, “dddddd” is the worst configuration on MMLU and in the bottom half for MATH; its overall average beats others only because CRUX (coding) shows high variance and “dddddd” excels there. Basic MoA (equal sampling across experts, “iiddmm”) beats Self-MoA on 2 of 3 capabilities. The best overall numbers come from “TaskBest”, which still uses a mixture of all agents—directly contradicting the claim that single‑model Self-MoA is superior. Thus Table 3 actually shows that mixing can be beneficial, though the authors do not acknowledge this. [R1, R3]  
   - The AlpacaEval 2.0 SoTA result (Table 2) uses a stronger base model that was not evaluated with the original MoA method, making the comparison unfair. In Table 1, where models are matched, Self-MoA improves over 3‑layer MoA by only 0.3% win rate—within the noise of the autorater (original MoA reports ±0.6). The paper does not report a second benchmark (e.g. MTBench) or show that the improvement is consistent across prompt categories, raising concerns about overfitting to a single evaluation. [R1]

2. **Limited technical novelty.** The core idea (using multiple samples from the same model and aggregating) is essentially a combination of self‑consistency and MoA. The main difference from Mixed‑MoA is an empirical study of the trade‑off, which is more correlational than causal. The sequential variant Self‑MoA-Seq is related to the layering already present in Mixed‑MoA. Several reviewers felt the paper reads more like a technical report than a research paper. [R2, R4]

3. **Missing comparisons and experiments.**  
   - No experiments with larger models such as GPT‑4o as proposer or aggregator, although Mixed‑MoA used GPT‑4o as aggregator. It is unclear whether the findings generalise to stronger base models. [R2]  
   - The inference‑scaling experiment (Figure 3) does not include a Mixed‑MoA baseline. Since the sequential aggregation method could be applied to both, this comparison is necessary to support the claim that Self-MoA scales better. [R1, R4]  
   - In Table 1, the “Individual” column is not clearly defined (is it simply prompting a single model without MoA?). The number of forward passes (12) seems inconsistent with the MoA procedure (3 layers × 5 models + 3 aggregations = 18). [R3]

4. **Causal analysis is lacking.** The paper fits linear regressions to model the relationship between quality, diversity, and performance, but does not justify the linear assumption (why not polynomial or another form?). There is no deeper causal study of the aggregator’s behaviour (e.g. attention patterns when combining proposer outputs). [R2, R4]

5. **Answering the title question incompletely.** The paper thoroughly shows *when* mixing is not beneficial, but only briefly (Section 3.2) addresses *when* mixing *is* beneficial. The supporting evidence there is minimal—only one configuration shows a small improvement, and the aggregate metric may hide task‑specific variations. [R3]

6. **Presentation issues.**  
   - Section 4 (Self-MoA-Seq) feels out of place; it addresses a different problem (context‑length scaling) without comparing to Mixed‑MoA in that setting. [R1]  
   - The paper lacks a dedicated introduction to Self-MoA for readers unfamiliar with MoA. [R4]  
   - Typo: “Table 2.1” should be “Table 1” (line 155). [R2]  
   - Grammar issues: lines 422‑423 (“we fit a linear regression to…”), line 371 (“explains 70% MoA’s performance”), line 444 (“WHEN MIXED-MOA OUTPERFORMS SELF-MOA?”). [R3]  
   - In Figure 2, the y‑axis range for MATH appears too low (1.6‑2.8); presumably a typo. [R3]

**Reviewer questions that overlap with missing comparisons:**  
- Did the authors run the Figure 3 inference‑scaling experiments with Mixed‑MoA? (R1)  
- Does Self‑MoA use only 1 layer (in Mixed‑MoA’s terminology)? (R2)  
- In Table 1, is the aggregator model Qwen1.5‑110B‑Chat? Would results using WizardLM‑2‑8x22B as aggregator follow the findings of the original MoA paper? (R2)  
- What does the “Individual” column in Table 1 mean? How are the numbers of forward passes calculated? (R3)
