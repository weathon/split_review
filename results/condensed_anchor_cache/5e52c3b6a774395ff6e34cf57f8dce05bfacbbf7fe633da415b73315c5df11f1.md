## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a novel task: using LLMs to hypothesize missing variables in partial causal DAGs for scientific discovery. A benchmark with four tasks of varying difficulty is created using existing causal graphs. Open-source and closed models are benchmarked. LLMs perform well on mediating variables but struggle with causes/effects. Some open-source models outperform GPT-4.

### Strengths
- The task is original and demonstrates how LLMs may generate causal hypotheses in scientific discovery.
- The task is orthogonal to other aspects of causality such as causal discovery.
- The benchmark allows automatic evaluation through multiple metrics and uses varying difficulty levels and knowledge assumptions.
- Experiments include finetuning, chain-of-thought prompting, and seven LLMs (both open-source and proprietary) across diverse causal DAGs from multiple domains.
- The paper provides clear definitions of the four tasks, how each is constructed and related.
- Code is provided in an anonymous repo.
- The topic is timely and important; a good benchmark for hypothesizing missing causal variables is needed.
- Tasks are relevant in causality, especially causal discovery where model complexity scales significantly.

### Weaknesses
- Real-world utility beyond evaluation with existing causal graphs is unclear; additional empirical benefits of using LLMs for hypothesis generation are not demonstrated.
- The paper does not clarify whether few-shot learning helps improve performance (a missing experiment relevant to understanding LLM capabilities).
- Methodology section is insufficiently clear and does not adequately motivate each task; tasks became clearer only upon reading the evaluation (Reviewer 2).
- There is a risk of data leakage: most datasets may already be in the LLMs’ training corpus. The authors claim the Alzheimer’s Disease dataset is new, but its ground truth is discussed in earlier papers ([2], [3] as cited in an ICLR 2024 paper [1]). This compromises benchmark validity and raises the need for analysis on whether LLMs are genuinely reasoning or memorizing causal patterns.
- The assumption that all causal relationships are known and correct is unrealistic in practice (e.g., knowing inbound/outbound edges of a mediator without knowing the variable itself). This restrictive assumption limits the benchmark’s ability to assess causal reasoning.
- The multiple-choice question answering (MCQA) options are built to be “far enough” from the true variable(s), making identification easier. It is unclear how “far enough” is defined and whether variables close to the true one are ever included; this potentially underestimates task difficulty.
- The evaluation introduces author-defined metrics (e.g., MIS) without providing their properties (e.g., interpretability, sign handling), making outcome interpretation harder.
- NDE and NIE are used as reference metrics but it is unclear why they are relevant, how they are identified (in the causal estimand sense), and how to handle sign reversal with MIS.
- Sources/sinks are conflated with mediators/confounders/colliders (graph property vs. path configuration), making interpretation of results unclear.
- Terminology issues: “causal identification” is misleading because identification refers to the causal estimand; the paper should avoid this overlap. Also, “missing” versus “unobserved” variables are not distinguished.
- Notation issues: capital V1 vs. v1 may be a typo; ⊮ in line 320 should be revised.
- Several methodological points are unclear: (i) In Task 3, is it realistic for users to provide partial DAGs with known and correct edges when causal variables are unknown? (ii) Tables 3 and 11 appear nearly identical; the metric ∆ is described differently in captions vs. Section 5.5, and it is unclear why ∆ is better than reporting performance differences directly. (iii) In Section 4.2, why are two variables removed but the LLM required to predict only one? Figure 2b does not depict two removals. (iv) What is the formal definition of “out-of-context distractors” and how does it differ from “in-context” choices? Variable names alone may provide context to the LLM.
