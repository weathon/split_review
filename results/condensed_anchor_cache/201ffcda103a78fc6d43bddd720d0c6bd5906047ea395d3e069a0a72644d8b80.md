## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses long-range interactions (LRI) in molecular graphs by introducing “Neural Atoms” – abstract nodes that cluster groups of atoms. Information is exchanged among these neural atoms via attention and then projected back to individual atom representations, effectively reducing any node pair’s interaction path to a single hop. The method is connected theoretically to the Ewald Summation for LRI calculation. Validation on three long-range graph benchmarks (graph-level and link-level tasks) shows up to 27.32% improvement in 2D and 38.27% in 3D settings. The approach is architecture-agnostic and can enhance any GNN.

### Strengths
- The problem of capturing long-range interactions in molecular graphs is important and timely.
- The method is simple, architecture-agnostic, and can be plugged into any GNN.
- It reduces long-range interactions to a single hop, avoiding the path explosion issue.
- Experiments on several datasets (covering graph-level and link-level tasks) show consistent effectiveness; performance gains of up to 27.32% (2D) and 38.27% (3D) are reported.
- The paper provides a theoretical connection to the traditional Ewald Summation, offering a physical perspective.
- Real-world case studies and visualizations demonstrate that neural atoms learn meaningful groupings aligned with interactions.
- The paper is generally well-written and almost clear everywhere (though one reviewer noted writing quality drops in Section 4).
- The authors made additional experiments and clarifications during the rebuttal, leading to a rating increase from one reviewer.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns:** The core idea of using virtual/supernodes to represent groups of atoms is not new; similar concepts have appeared in graph learning since at least 2017 (e.g., DiffPool, virtual nodes in OGB). One reviewer was more positive but acknowledged the similarity. Another noted a concurrent study with a similar pipeline. The paper could discuss how its techniques (interaction and projection) differ from these prior works.
- **Controversial claims against Transformers:** The Introduction criticizes Graph Transformers (GTs) as computationally expensive, yet the projection step uses multi-head attention. The running time experiments do not clearly show an advantage of Neural Atoms over Transformers, undermining the initial claim.
- **Limited experimental scope:**  
  - Only three relatively small benchmark datasets are used; larger and more recent benchmarks (e.g., OC20, OE62) are absent.  
  - No comparison with state-of-the-art methods specifically designed for LRI, such as Ewald-based message passing ([1] Ewald-based Long-Range Message Passing for Molecular Graphs, ICML 2023).  
  - The method was not tested on broader graph benchmarks (e.g., OGB), even though the concept of virtual nodes is applicable there.  
  - Only intra-molecular interactions are considered; inter-molecular interactions are not addressed.  
  - 3D coordinate information available for some molecules is not utilized.
- **Writing quality and clarity:** The writing worsens in Section 4, with noticeable grammar mistakes in Section 4.2. Terms like “ratio” in Table 4 and “varying proportion” on page 7 are introduced without prior explanation, giving a hasty impression.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** The number of neural atoms is a hyperparameter that requires tuning, and reviewers noted that optimal groupings lack strong theoretical justification.
- **Missing theoretical grounding:** There is no strong theory on why the learned groupings or the neural atom mechanism optimally capture long-range interactions.
- **Open questions that overlap with missing experiments/ablations:**  
  - How does running time compare to Transformers, given the use of multi-head attention?  
  - Can the method scale to very large molecular graphs?  
  - How are neural atom groupings initialized? Are they randomly assigned?  
  - Could 3D coordinates be incorporated to model distance-dependent interactions?  
  - In Figure 5, can atoms within the same neural atom share information? Is information exchange between atoms from different neural atoms actually greater than within the same neural atom?
