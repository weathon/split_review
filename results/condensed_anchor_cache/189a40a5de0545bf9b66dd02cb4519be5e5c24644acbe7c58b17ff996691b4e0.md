## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes CNMP (Common Neighbor Induced Message Passing) and its enhanced version CNMP+ to address disconnected reasoning paths in subgraph-based inductive link prediction. CNMP updates distance labels for isolated common neighbors to enable message passing, and CNMP+ reconstructs complete reasoning paths. The method shows gains on multiple datasets. Reviewers generally found the problem well-motivated and the experiments extensive, but raised concerns about missing comparisons with recent models, insufficient analysis of inductive reasoning fundamentals, questionable experimental details, and an overly narrow focus on common neighbors.

### Strengths
- The CNMP framework innovatively addresses the issue of disconnected reasoning paths in subgraph extraction, a significant hindrance for GNN-based inductive KG reasoning. The strategy of updating distance labels for isolated common neighbors is a clever solution.
- The paper is well-organized, with clear introduction, related work, and technical explanation. Figures and charts are exceptionally clear.
- The paper identifies and analyzes specific issues within existing subgraph-based methods (e.g., implementation issues in GraIL’s code where `np.max(dist)` and `np.sum(dist)` cause problems).
- The authors propose two solutions (CNMP and CNMP+) that mitigate and resolve these issues, and experiments demonstrate consistent outperformance against a range of baselines across multiple datasets.
- Intensive experiments have been conducted, including multiple datasets and comparisons, showing the effectiveness of the proposed methods.

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation and scope too narrow**: The exclusive focus on common neighbors and reasoning paths overlooks crucial information contained in non-common neighbors. Knowledge graphs contain diverse structures (tree-like, ego-graph-like) that are not simple reasoning paths; GNNs are inherently good at capturing such diverse knowledge through message passing. Constraining the model to common neighbors may unnecessarily limit its ability to leverage the full spectrum of available graph patterns. [Reviewer 1, high confidence]
- **Insufficient analysis of inductive reasoning fundamentals**: The paper does not adequately explain how the model initializes or learns embeddings for unseen entities, or how it extracts entity-independent features for inductive KG reasoning. These are critical for any inductive model. [Reviewer 1, high confidence]
- **Outdated baselines**: The compared models in Tables 3 and 4 are all published in or before 2022. The paper should compare with more recent inductive KG reasoning models such as C-MPNN, MINES, AdaProp, and InGram (ICML 2023) to demonstrate effectiveness against state-of-the-art. [Reviewers 2 and 4; note low confidence from Reviewer 2]
- **All-entity evaluation missing**: Prior works like NBFNet adopt an evaluation setting treating all entities as candidates instead of the 50-negative setting used here. Reporting results with all entities as candidates would allow a more reliable and standard comparison. [Reviewer 3, high confidence]
- **Suspicious experimental results**: Tables 16 and 17 report identical results even when the number of hops for the enclosing subgraph changes from 2-hop to 3-hop, raising concerns about correctness or reproducibility. [Reviewer 1, high confidence]
- **Questionable proof in Appendix A**: The proof is unnecessary as it presents an obvious conclusion. Furthermore, it lacks the condition that if edge (A, r, B) exists, then edge (B, r_inverse, A) must also exist; without this, in directed KGs, two nodes with a common neighbor may not actually have a connected path, undermining the proof. [Reviewer 3, high confidence]
- **Insufficient details on CNMP+ strategy**: The paper does not provide sufficient details on the CNMP+ strategy, making it difficult to understand or reproduce. [Reviewer 4] (Also related: the Q&As in the appendix are not well-integrated with the main text to clarify the model. [Reviewer 1])
- **Motivation for new datasets**: For the experiments in Section 3.5 and Table 6, fully-inductive datasets such as RMPI and InGram (ICML 2023) already exist. The authors should justify why new datasets were constructed instead of using these existing benchmarks. [Reviewer 3, high confidence]
- **Runtime analysis limited**: The runtime experiments in Appendix B.8.3 are conducted only on the smaller v1 dataset. A comparison on the larger v4 dataset would be more convincing. Additionally, it is unclear whether the reported runtime includes preprocessing time (especially for closed subgraph construction). [Reviewer 3, high confidence] (Reviewer 2 also notes lack of large-scale runtime comparison.)
- **Missing source code and dataset**: The source code and the newly constructed dataset have not been provided, despite the authors’ commitment to release them after acceptance. This hampers reproducibility. [Reviewer 3]
- **Lack of case studies**: No case study is provided to illustrate how the model performs inductive reasoning based on common neighbors, which would help verify its effectiveness. [Reviewer 1]
- **Unexplained design choice**: The paper only considers cases with hop numbers 2 and 3 for the enclosing subgraph. The authors need to discuss this choice in more detail in the motivation. [Reviewer 2]
- **Implementation vs. method confusion**: The paper focuses on issues arising from the subgraph construction implementations of certain methods (e.g., in GraIL’s code, `np.max(dist)` and `np.sum(dist)` cause issues), rather than the methods as described in their corresponding papers. This context is not clearly distinguished. [Reviewer 3] (A separate but related point: the disconnected path problem may be due to discarding isolated nodes, which is similar to a previous work (paper [2]); the authors should discuss this similarity. [Reviewer 2])

*Note on reviewer disagreement:* Two reviewers gave scores of 5 (positive; one with low confidence 1, one with high confidence 4) while two gave scores of 3 (negative; both with high or medium confidence). The positive reviewers highlighted the novelty and empirical results, whereas the negative reviewers raised fundamental concerns about motivation, analysis, and experimental rigor.
