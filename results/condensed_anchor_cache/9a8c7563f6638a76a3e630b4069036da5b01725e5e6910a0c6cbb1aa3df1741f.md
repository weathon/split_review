- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 5, 3, 1

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a time-series imputation method built upon radial basis function neural networks (RBFNN) to learn a continuous function from observed timestamps, then incorporates this into a bidirectional RNN (MIRNN-CF) with additional losses. It is evaluated on two real-world datasets (AirQuality, Human Activity) and one in the appendix (ETT) under MCAR missingness. Overall assessments vary widely (scores 5, 3, 1), reflecting strong disagreements on clarity, positioning, experimental rigor, and technical soundness.

### Strengths
- Detailed description of the complex architecture makes the paper easy to follow according to Reviewer 1, though other reviewers strongly disagree on presentation quality.
- Empirical results on two datasets suggest improvement over several competitors; on the Human Activity dataset both the MIM-RBFNN component and the full MIRNN-CF beat all baselines across missing ratios up to 80% (Reviewer 1).
- An ablation study (Table 1) is provided, showing how much performance comes from MIM-RBFNN and how much further improvement is achieved by MIRNN-CF (Reviewer 1).
- Continuous function approximation is a simple but powerful idea for certain types of time-series data (Reviewer 2).
- The problem of missing values in time series is relevant to multiple domains (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Positioning, motivation, and missing assumptions**  
  The paper criticises existing methods for strong assumptions but does not clarify its own assumptions about the missingness mechanism; experiments only consider MCAR, leaving applicability under MAR/MNAR unclear (Reviewer 3). Motivation and contribution over previous imputation works (including autoregressive ones) are not clearly stated (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 1 notes that RBF itself makes a strong assumption (value depends only on distance from a fixed point; Gaussian RBF uses exponential decay). Reviewer 2 asks how the proposed method deviates from the confounding effects of autoregressive models given it still uses RNN predictions.

- **Missing related work and insufficient literature coverage**  
  Several important imputation methods are omitted: generative approaches such as GP-VAE (Fortuin et al., 2020) and CSDI (Tashiro et al., 2021) (Reviewer 2). The paper also overlooks contributions in Gaussian processes and RBF interpolation (Wright, 2003) and should clarify how it builds upon or distinguishes from that literature (Reviewer 3). The claim that RBF networks are universal approximators lacks a supporting reference (e.g., Park & Sandberg, 1991) (Reviewer 3).

- **Presentation and clarity**  
  While Reviewer 1 found the paper easy to follow, Reviewers 2 and 3 report significant presentation issues: notation is not well-defined and consistent; the procedure for learning multiple RBFs based on residuals (Section 3.1) is hard to follow; the initialization and training of RBF components are not clearly described; Equation (11) needs clarification (Reviewer 2). The residual modeling resembles boosting but the connection is unclear (Reviewer 3). Several terms (“GRUI”, “Instability of deep learning”) and claims (about GRU-D’s practical usability) are undefined or unjustified (Reviewer 3). The discussion of complete case analysis should mention bias under non-MCAR and reduced sample size (Reviewer 3).

- **Experiments and evaluation limitations**  
  - Empirical results conflict with previously published values: SAITS’s reported performance on AirQuality is comparable to BRITS, but in Table 1 it is significantly worse; self-reported BRITS results are better than those in Table 1 (Reviewer 1).  
  - Experiments are limited to two datasets (a third, ETT, appears only in the Appendix); important baselines (GP-VAE, CSDI) are not compared (Reviewer 2).  
  - No analysis of different missingness patterns (MAR, MNAR) is provided (Reviewer 2).  
  - Confidence intervals and hyperparameter tuning are absent, making it unclear whether improvements are statistically significant or due to inadequate parameter choices (Reviewer 3).  
  - It is not explained why MIM-RBFNN trains a sequence of RBFNNs in a boosting-like fashion; a comparison against a single, more expressive RBFNN is missing (Reviewer 1), as is a comparison against Gaussian Processes (Reviewer 1).  
  - MICE is listed among baselines but does not appear in Table 1 – clarify whether the table uses MF or MICE (Reviewer 1, question).  
  - The RBF construction lacks cross-feature dependencies; sharing RBF functions across features (as done in Appendix C) may cause problems when features have very different temporal dynamics (e.g., different periodicities, common in healthcare). The method’s performance when strong inter-feature dependencies exist is unclear (Reviewer 2, questions).

- **Technical soundness concerns**  
  The methodology needs clarification to be fully assessed; the residual-based learning (boosting-like) approach is not justified (Reviewers 2, 3). Some claims are unsupported or oversimplified (e.g., description of healthcare time series, use of “Instability of deep learning”, justification for GRU-D’s limitations) (Reviewer 3).
