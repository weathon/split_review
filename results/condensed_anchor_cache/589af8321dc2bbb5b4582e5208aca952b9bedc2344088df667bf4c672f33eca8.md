## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper identifiability conditions for linear structural causal models (SCMs) with hidden confounders and Gaussian noise. It establishes that if the number of confounders is at most the number of observed variables, the SCM is finitely identifiable (at most \(n!\) models, where \(n\) is the number of observed variables). If confounders are non-Gaussian (Assumption 2) and the causal order is known, unique identifiability holds. The equivalence class is characterized via permutations, and the characteristic function of confounders is derived. The results fill a gap between the known non-Gaussian and Gaussian cases.

### Strengths
- General and fundamental result on a core causal inference problem, extending prior work (all reviewers).
- Solid theoretical contributions: Theorems 1–3 provide clear sufficiency conditions and a characterization of the equivalence set, including the number of permutations (reviewers 2, 4).
- Clear writing and proof structure; proofs are rigorous and easy to follow (reviewers 2, 4). The paper is polished with an extensive literature discussion (reviewer 3).
- Original results that fill in gaps: finite identifiability under Gaussian noise + non-Gaussian confounders (Assumption 2) (reviewer 3).
- Results are appealing and of high quality (reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
- **Scope and applicability**: The linear model framework restricts the scope (reviewer 3, score 6). Results are information-theoretic with no analysis of finite-sample perturbations; no algorithms or complexity analysis are provided (reviewers 1, 3). Assumption 2 (confounders cannot be decomposed into variables with a Gaussian component) is difficult to verify in practice; any density can be approximated by an infinite mixture of Gaussians, potentially breaking the assumption (reviewer 2). No guidance is given on verifying identifiability from real data (reviewer 2).
- **Novelty of proof techniques**: One reviewer (score 6) noted that the proof techniques are a mixture of standard Gaussian properties and characteristic function analysis, with limited novelty beyond the SCM context. Other reviewers (scores 8) found the proofs clear and rigorous, not lacking novelty. This disagreement is noted.
- **Definitional rigor**: Definition 3’s equality in p.d.f. is unrigorous; it should be stated in terms of distributions (reviewer 2).
- **Equivalence class computation**: The characterization in Sec. 6 could be related to invariant permutations of \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\), possibly simplifying the computation of equivalence classes (reviewer 2).
- **Presentation issues**: Minor grammar/punctuation issues (e.g., lines 41–42, 49). Typos: line 67 “base on”, line 200 \(\backslash\)ref instead of \(\backslash eqref\), line 387 “is illustrated by SCMs”. Some definitions (Definition 3, Definition 7, Remarks 2 and 3) could be moved to the appendix (reviewer 4). Narrative citations should remove parentheses (reviewer 2). Remarks 4 and 5 could be stated as theorems (reviewer 2). Example illustrations for Remarks 5 and 6 need more steps (reviewer 4).
- **Missing analyses / discussions** (implied by reviewer questions that overlap with criticism or missing content):
  - If only a partial causal order is given, is the SCM uniquely identifiable in some scenarios? (reviewer 3)
  - Can Theorem 2 give insight into when part of the model (e.g., a treatment effect) is uniquely identifiable even when the full model is not? (reviewer 4)
  - How do the identifiability results connect with classical results in estimating linear model parameters? Can existing methods benefit from these results? (reviewer 3)
  - Could the proof technique (characteristic functions) benefit other areas of machine learning? (reviewer 3)
