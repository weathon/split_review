## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper empirically investigates whether scaling language model size improves adversarial robustness in the classification setting. Using the Pythia model family fine-tuned on six binary classification tasks (e.g., IMDB sentiment, spam detection, Harmful/Harmless, PasswordMatch, WordLength), the authors find that without explicit defense training, larger models are modestly more robust on most tasks but the effect is inconsistent and even large models remain easy to attack. They then show adversarial training is much more compute-efficient than scaling alone, and that adversarially trained larger models generalize faster and better to unseen attacks. Finally, the offense/defense balance analysis shows parity in some settings and an advantage for offense in others, suggesting adversarial training alone is insufficient.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important gap in understanding scaling effects on language model robustness (R1, R3).
- Provides a decent empirical analysis across multiple model sizes (all Pythia sizes), attack types (random tokens, GCG), and adversarial training setups (R1, R3).
- Identifies that adversarial training is more compute-efficient than model scaling alone for achieving robustness, adding practical insight (R1, R3).
- Highlights task-specific trends in robustness, valuable for real-world applications (R1).
- Covers further analyses such as offense vs. defense compute balance and transferability of adversarial training, which are crucial for LLM safety (R3).
- Results are clearly presented with good plot layout and writing style (R4, R5).
- Experimental design is principled and findings make a succinct point about compute tradeoffs (R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Narrow model family:** The study only uses the Pythia family of decoder-only LMs. It does not test other architectures (e.g., T5, encoder-only models) or more capable decoder-only models like LLaMA, Mistral, Vicuna, Gemma, or Qwen. This severely limits generalizability (R1, R2, R4, R5). R2 notes Pythia models are pre-trained without RLHF and perform far below modern LLMs. R5 adds that Pythia hyperparameters are held constant and are not optimal for all sizes.
- **Restricted task setting:** Experiments are limited to binary classification (spam, sentiment, etc.). Modern LLMs are primarily generative; robustness trends may differ for generation tasks (e.g., jailbreaking, toxic output, hallucination). Multiple reviewers strongly urge extension to generative settings (R1, R2, R3, R4). R2 and R4 argue that classification-only evaluations miss vulnerabilities that matter in practice.
- **Insufficient attack methods:** Only two attacks are used: random token suffix and GCG (with a universal suffix). Classical NLP attacks (e.g., TextFooler, BERT-Attack, TextGrad) are not included (R2). Recent strong jailbreak attacks (Andriushchenko et al. 2024, AutoDAN, Fast Adversarial Attacks, etc.) are also missing (R3, R4). R2 calls the attack methods “naive” given the toy setting.
- **Mildly surprising findings:** Many results are not novel. That model size somewhat improves robustness aligns with prior work (e.g., Ganguli et al. 2022). That adversarial training enhances robustness is well-known; transfer protection against other attacks is also expected from the image classification literature. The final claim that adversarial training alone is insufficient is a well-accepted belief in the adversarial robustness community, not a novel insight (R4, R5). R4 states the paper “lacks a novel perspective or significant advancement.”
- **Practical impact is unclear:** The threat model of adversarial attacks on spam filters or similar classifiers may be of limited real-world importance, as production systems typically have multiple components beyond an LLM. In contrast, jailbreaking in generative models has clearer safety implications (R5). R5 also questions whether the GCG strings used would be caught by simple perplexity filters—baselines like those by Jain et al. (2023) are not compared.
- **Lack of deeper analysis:** The paper does not explore why robustness is task-dependent (e.g., why adversarial training works well on some tasks but fails on WordLength). Ablation studies are needed (R3). It also does not investigate whether robustness could be an emergent property at larger scales beyond Pythia (R3). R4 asks whether the low ASR for larger models in Figure 3 is due to inherent robustness or insufficient compute (the slopes don’t fully flatten).
- **Missing experimental details:** Key details—amount of finetuning data, how long GCG attack strings take to compute, when GCG optimization is stopped (first successful fooling?)—are not in the main text, raising reproducibility concerns (R5).
- **Limited defense baselines:** Only scaling and adversarial training are considered as defenses. Other approaches (e.g., ALUM, perplexity filters, adversarial augmentation) are not compared; the paper should acknowledge that its adversarial training results may not generalize to other variants (R1). Using scaling as a defense baseline is odd, since scaling is not typically used for that purpose (R5).
- **Conflicting reviewer confidence/agreement:** Minority reviewers (R1, R3, score 6) find the work valuable and well-presented, while others (R2, R4, score 3) consider the experiments too narrow and contributions minimal. R5 (score 5) is intermediate but echoes concerns about narrowness and impact.
