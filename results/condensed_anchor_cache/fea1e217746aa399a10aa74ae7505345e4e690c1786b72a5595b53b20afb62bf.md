- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 1, 6, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes CC-VFed, a defense against Byzantine attacks in vertical federated learning (VFL). The authors first investigate three attack methods (sign-flipping, random, permutation) and show sign-flipping is the most effective. Their defense detects malicious clients by measuring each client’s contribution to output labels via Grad-CAM. Experiments on BCW and CIFAR-10 are conducted with a single malicious client. Two reviewers rated the paper low (1 and 3) while a third was more positive (6), leading to a mixed assessment. All agree the topic is relevant but raise significant concerns about novelty, method clarity, privacy, experimental scope, and the defense’s own effectiveness.

### Strengths
- The topic of Byzantine attacks in VFL is important and has received limited attention (Reviewer 1).
- A sign-flipping attack for VFL is demonstrated to be highly threatening; this provides a baseline for evaluating defenses (Reviewers 2, 3).
- The proposed CC-VFed defense uses client contribution to output labels to counter attacks (Reviewer 2).
- The background section clearly argues that HFL outlier detection methods are insufficient for VFL and highlights deficiencies of existing VFL approaches (Reviewer 2).
- The study uses real-world datasets (BCW, CIFAR-10) and examines the impact of different model architectures on robustness (Reviewers 2, 3).
- One reviewer (Reviewer 2) found the method practical and appreciated the detailed experiments on attack severity, while noting the method’s simplicity as both a strength and a weakness.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty**:
  - The investigated attack methods (especially sign-flipping) have been extensively studied in prior work; the defense’s gradient-contribution mechanism bears strong similarities to existing approaches (Reviewer 1). The two claimed improvements over (Selvaraju et al., 2017) are not clearly justified (Reviewer 2).
  - The unique advantages of CC-VFed over other VFL defense methods are not specified; no comparison with state-of-the-art VFL methods is provided (Reviewers 1, 2).
  - The paper describes “four defense methods” but these are actually four situations within one method (Reviewer 2).
- **Method Clarity and Protocol**:
  - Step 1 of the defense requires clients to share training data (x_i) with the server, which compromises data privacy (Reviewer 1).
  - The aggregation of Grad-CAM values is unclearly described (multiple nodes vs. single node) and the defense computation lacks rigorous mathematical formulation (Reviewer 1).
  - It is unclear who determines whether the output label matches the training data label: if the client, how is mischief prevented? If the server, the server learns the labels, violating VFL privacy (Reviewer 2).
  - The contribution calculation formula needs more explanation (Reviewer 2).
  - Why must the parameter c be close to one? The paper provides no analysis or supporting reference (Reviewer 3).
- **Experimental Evaluation**:
  - Only two relatively simple datasets (BCW, CIFAR-10) are used; this may not demonstrate robustness across diverse scenarios (Reviewers 1, 3).
  - The setting assumes exactly one malicious party, making detection lesschallenging; the method’s effectiveness when the number of malicious parties is unknown (a real-world scenario) is not shown (Reviewer 3).
  - No ablation studies are included to isolate each component’s contribution (Reviewer 1).
  - No comparison with existing defense methods (Reviewer 2). Experimental environment, number of trials, and statistical significance are missing (Reviewer 2).
  - **Critical concern**: When the defense is applied against random/permutation attacks, Top-1 accuracy often drops compared to the no-defense setting, suggesting the defense itself acts as a “stronger attack” (Reviewer 3).
- **Organization and Writing**:
  - Key terms such as “Top-1 accuracy” are not explained (Reviewer 2).
  - The method lacks diagrams showing entities, their operations, and the meaning behind each step (Reviewer 2).
- **Contrast among reviewers**: While Reviewer 2 rated the paper as a 6 and found the method practical and the experiments thorough, Reviewers 1 and 3 (ratings 1 and 3) consider the novelty, privacy, and experimental evaluation insufficient, and Reviewer 3 doubts the defense’s own safety.
