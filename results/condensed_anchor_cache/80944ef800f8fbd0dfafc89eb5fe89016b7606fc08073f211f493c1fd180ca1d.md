## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper revisits classic Sliced-Wasserstein Distance (SWD) and proposes a rescaling approach based on slice informativeness, defined as alignment with the data’s effective subspace. Instead of modifying the slicing distribution, a single global scaling factor balances all slices, simplifying SWD while preserving its metric properties. Theoretical justification is given under a low-dimensional subspace assumption. Experiments on gradient flow, color transfer, and deep generative modeling show that a properly scaled SWD often matches or outperforms more complex SWD variants. The work asks whether sliced-Wasserstein is “all you need” for common learning tasks.

### Strengths
- The rescaling approach is simpler and more computationally efficient than modifying the slicing distribution (R1, R3).
- The global scaling factor can be absorbed into learning rate tuning, making it easy to implement without major model changes (R1).
- Reduces dependence on knowing the effective subspace dimension k, improving adaptability (R1).
- Solid theoretical foundation, with extensive derivations supporting the rescaling strategy (R1, R2, R3).
- The idea of rescaling 1D Wasserstein distances is novel (R2).
- Detailed experiments conducted on several machine learning tasks (R2), with convincing performance on image generation and color transfer (R3).
- Bridges the performance gap between classic SWD and more complex variants through appropriate hyperparameter tuning (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (score 8, no weaknesses) was highly positive, while the other two (scores 6 and 5) raised multiple concerns regarding experimental scope, assumptions, and clarity.
- **Insufficient experimental validation**: The experiments do not include common tasks like image classification or object detection (R2). Only visual datasets are tested; text/audio data and extremely high-dimensional data are missing (R3). MNIST and toy datasets are too small to be convincing (R2). This limits generalizability claims.
- **Assumption limitation**: The theoretical analysis relies on a low-dimensional effective subspace assumption that may not hold for complex high-dimensional data, such as text or intricate time series, making applicability uncertain (R3). One reviewer asked why the strict subspace condition can be relaxed given Assumption 4.1 (R1).
- **Unclear methodology**: How SWD is used during training is not explained clearly; a systematic model framework or detailed algorithm would help (R2). Compared methods are given only by abbreviations with no explanation (R2).
- **Lack of performance evidence**: It is not clearly shown how the proposed SWD is better than alternatives in the experiments (R2). The paper’s claim “Sliced-Wasserstein is all you need” is not sufficiently supported (R2).
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: The method depends heavily on learning rate choice; lack of detailed analysis on convergence, stability, and effective learning rate ranges raises concerns about robustness (R3).
- **Single informativeness metric**: The global scaling factor only considers alignment with the effective subspace, which may not capture task-specific slice contributions (R3). One reviewer asked what other criteria could define informativeness and whether they would offer additional benefits (R1).
- **Limited generalization to complex models**: Applicability to large-scale deep generative models, such as language models, is unclear (R3).
- **Missing details**: Important content is relegated to the appendix (R2). Computational complexity of the proposed SWD is not discussed (R2). The purpose of finding a learning rate bound for SW gradient flow and how it impacts the results is unclear (R2).
