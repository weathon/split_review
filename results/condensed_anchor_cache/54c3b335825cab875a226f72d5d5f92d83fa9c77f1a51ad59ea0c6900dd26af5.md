## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces FMBench, a benchmark to evaluate fairness of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) on medical tasks (Visual Question Answering and Report Generation) across demographic attributes (race, ethnicity, language, gender). It is built from the Harvard-FairVLMed dataset (10,000 fundus images with clinical reports and demographics). VQA pairs and summaries are generated using Llama-3.1-Instruct-70B. The benchmark assesses models under zero-shot settings, using lexical metrics (BLEU, METEOR), semantic metrics (GREEN, LLM score), and a proposed Fairness-Aware Performance (FAP) metric. Eight open-source MLLMs (7B-26B, including Llava-Med and general MLLMs like MiniCPM, InternVL) are evaluated. Findings show performance variations across demographics, limitations of traditional metrics, and that scaling does not necessarily improve performance. The dataset and code are promised upon acceptance. Reviewers note that only one dataset (ophthalmology fundus images) is used, the dataset is not yet released, and evaluations rely solely on LLM-generated content without human verification, among other concerns. Ratings range from 1 to 5, indicating disagreement over significance and rigor.

### Strengths
- Addresses a timely and important gap: fairness evaluation of MLLMs in medical AI, where biases can have serious consequences (R1, R3).
- Introduces a new benchmark FMBench for fairness evaluation across multiple demographic attributes, setting a foundation for future work (R1, R3).
- Reports interesting empirical findings: medical-specific MLLMs (e.g., Llava-Med) achieve high lexical accuracy but low semantic correctness, while general MLLMs show the opposite pattern (R1).
- The benchmark includes two tasks (VQA and RG), enabling assessment across different multimodal scenarios (R2).
- Reveals limitations of traditional lexical metrics (e.g., BLEU, METEOR) for open-ended tasks, highlighting the need for semantic-based metrics like GREEN score and LLM scoring (R2, R4).
- Proposes the FAP metric that accounts for group performance and deviation from the weighted mean, helping identify disparities (R3).
- Provides baseline comparisons across both biomedical and general-domain MLLMs, acknowledging that general models are often repurposed for medical applications (R3).
- Experimental evaluation shows performance disparities across demographic groups (especially language attributes) and that increasing model scale does not necessarily lead to gains (R4).

### Weaknesses
- Only one dataset (Harvard-FairVLMed) and one medical domain (ophthalmology, fundus images) are used, so claims about “medical multimodal tasks” are overstated; the benchmark is narrowly scoped (R1, R2, R4). The title and abstract should be reframed (R2).
- The dataset is not released for review; the authors only promise release upon acceptance (R1).
- VQA pairs and summaries are generated solely by Llama-3.1-70B without manual inspection or clear guardrails against hallucination, which is critical for a high-stakes medical domain (R1, R3).
- Both the GREEN score and the LLM score rely on the same model (LLaMA-3.1-70B-Instruct), introducing potential evaluation bias; cross-validation with multiple LLMs is needed (R4).
- The proposed FAP metric is not clearly differentiated from common parity-based fairness measures; the choice of λ is not explained, and the paper does not report which λ was used or show a plot of the deviation term alone (R1, R3).
- All experiments use temperature=0, leading to no variance reported; it is unclear whether relying on a single deterministic run is reliable (R1).
- Only one biomedical MLLM (Llava-Med) is evaluated, despite the benchmark targeting medical tasks; other biomedical models like Dragonfly-Med should be included (R3).
- Commercial MLLMs (e.g., Claude, GPT) are not evaluated, even though they are widely used by the public for medical inquiries; their inclusion would strengthen the fairness analysis (R3).
- The paper makes bold claims in Section 5.1 (e.g., “general MLLMs can surpass medical-specific ones”, “scaling is not beneficial”) based on evaluations on a single biomedical image type and only one biomedical model; broader task/model coverage is needed to support these conclusions (R3).
- Lack of few-shot and fine-tuning experiments limits the depth of the analysis (R4).
- The related work discussion misses prior fairness evaluations on multimodal datasets (e.g., Lancet Digital Health, arXiv:2403.18196) and does not address different medical imaging types (X-ray, CT, MRI) that may present distinct fairness issues (R2, R4).
- It is unclear whether the clinical reports used are clinically verified; only four demographic attributes are included, limiting assessment breadth (R2).
- Minor error: Line 147 reads “4 different demogrphy attributes” (R2).
