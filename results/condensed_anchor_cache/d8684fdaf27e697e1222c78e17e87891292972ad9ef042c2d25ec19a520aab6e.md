## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces Lean-STaR, a framework that trains language models to generate informal thoughts (natural language) before each formal proof tactic in Lean, aiming to improve theorem proving. The authors use GPT-4 to retrospectively generate thought annotations from ground-truth tactics and proof states, then fine-tune a base model (InternLM2-Math-base-7b) on this thought-augmented dataset to produce Lean-CoT. They then apply expert iteration (two rounds) to further improve the model on self-generated correct proofs. Lean-STaR achieves state-of-the-art results on miniF2F (43.4% → 46.3% Pass@64). The work is praised for its novel integration of informal reasoning into formal proving, but raises concerns about data leakage, CoT quality verification, and insufficient ablations.

### Strengths
- **Novel combination of informal thought and formal proving:** The paper is the first to interleave explicit natural-language thoughts with formal tactic generation in Lean, addressing a long-standing gap. While methods like STaR and expert iteration are known, applying them to merge informal reasoning with formal code is a valuable step.
- **Effective and simple approach:** Retrospective rationale generation (using GPT-4 to produce thoughts from state and ground-truth tactic) is simple yet yields clear performance gains. Training a separate model (Lean-CoT) for thought-augmented tactic prediction works well.
- **Sampling-based search novelty:** The proposed sampling-based search (avoiding best-first search and training a value function) is novel, elegant, and more effective in this setting. It outperforms best-first search, an interesting finding that deserves further analysis.
- **Large, useful dataset:** The CoT-augmented dataset (50,000 proofs from Mathlib + 50,000 synthetic from expert iteration) is a substantial resource for the field.
- **General framework:** The approach is model-agnostic and can be combined with other methods (e.g., autoformalization), suggesting broad applicability.
- **Strong empirical results:** Multiple experiments and state-of-the-art results on miniF2F demonstrate the method’s effectiveness.
- **Well-written and easy to follow** (most reviewers agreed, though one found presentation confusing).

### Weaknesses
- **Data leakage concerns (significant):** Multiple reviewers highlighted potential contamination. (a) GPT-4 may have been exposed to miniF2F proofs during thought generation, leaking information. (b) InternLM2-Math-base-7b may have been pre-trained on miniF2F or LeanDojo data, inflating performance. (c) Overlap between generated proofs and already-checked proofs in the miniF2F repository should be examined. The paper does not analyze these risks. A reviewer recommends testing on a newer benchmark like PutnamBench to verify generalization.
- **Unclear theoretical justification for latent-variable formulation:** One reviewer (minority, score 6) argues that the paper presents the model as marginalizing over thoughts (`p(tactic | proof context) = E[ p(tactic | thought, proof context) ]`), but in practice it trains on single thought–tactic pairs, learning `p(tactic, thought | proof context)`. This makes the comparison with direct tactic prediction unfair, as the approach receives strictly more information (the thought). The paper does not explain how expectation is handled (e.g., no Monte Carlo estimate). This reviewer also notes Figure 2 is consistent with joint modeling, not marginalization.
- **Lack of CoT quality verification:** The paper does not provide any detailed evaluation or filtering of the GPT-4-generated thoughts. Only the final pass rate is reported. No analysis of thought correctness, diversity, or alignment with expert reasoning. The prompt design (only state + ground-truth tactic) is not explored; alternative contexts (e.g., previous proof steps) are not tested. A reviewer questions the financial/computational burden of large-scale GPT-4 usage for thought generation.
- **Insufficient ablation studies:** The paper lacks ablations to isolate the contribution of thought augmentation. For example, comparing with the same pipeline (expert iteration + tactic prediction) but without thoughts. More ablations on other components (e.g., number of expert iteration rounds, search method) are needed to understand each contribution.
- **Modest improvement relative to expectations from MWP domain:** The ablation shows only a 2.5–3.3% gain (Lean-STaR vs. Expert iteration (SFT) in Table 3). Given the large gains CoT brings in math word problems, the smaller improvement in theorem proving merits explanation.
- **Presentation issues:** The paper uses confusing and inconsistent abbreviations (e.g., “CoT dataset” for thought-augmented data, “SFT model” for direct tactic prediction, “Lean-STaR” for both framework and model). “Automatic theorem proving” and “interactive theorem proving” are used interchangeably. Figure 3 is unclear (Base Dataset arrow, expert iteration loop). There are minor typos: “Table 7” should be “Table 1” (lines 372, 398); Line 449: 39.8% likely should be 34.0%. Table 1 caption should clarify whether “N” for sampling and search have different meanings.
- **Missing explanation for sampling vs. best-first search:** A reviewer asks for an intuitive reason why sampling works better than best-first search for this method, an interesting observation that is not discussed.
- **Limited expert iteration rounds:** Only two iterations are performed. The first provides larger gains; the second smaller. It is unclear whether further iterations would lead to a bottleneck or continued improvement.
- **Contradiction in GPT-4 usage:** One reviewer points out that the paper argues GPT-4 does not generate correct rationale via few-shot prompting (line 196) but then uses GPT-4 to generate the CoT dataset, which appears contradictory.
- **Minor suggestion:** The related work section could be broadened to include other ML+ITP works in other proof assistants, and specific related works on autoformalization (e.g., Jiang et al., 2023) and LLM copilots (e.g., Song et al., 2024) are recommended for citation.
