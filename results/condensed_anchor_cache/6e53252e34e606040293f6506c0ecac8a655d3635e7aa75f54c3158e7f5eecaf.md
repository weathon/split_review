- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 3, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an always-sparse training algorithm (GSE) that modifies the growth phase of prune-and-grow by confining candidate weights to those not just pruned and adding random sampling from specific distributions. The method avoids materializing dense weights/gradients. Experiments on CIFAR-10/100 and ImageNet with ResNet, VGG, and ViT show improvements over baselines. Reviewers generally find the novelty limited, the gains marginal, and the practical relevance unclear. All three assigned a score of 3.

### Strengths
- Experiments cover multiple datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet) and architectures (ResNet, VGG, ViT) with extensive baseline comparisons (R1, R2).
- The approach for searching pruned/active connections and induced sparsity distributions is sensible (R2).
- The method matches or outperforms RigL in a couple of scenarios (R2).
- The paper is well written and the argument is understandable (R3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty and incremental contribution** (all reviewers):
   - The core idea is incremental: it reuses the same pruning/growth criteria as prior methods and merely adds candidate subset selection from non-pruned weights and random sampling (R1). GSE is based on RigL, augmented with a connection sampling strategy and sparse-gradient-based activation for new connections (R2).
   - Gradient-based distributions contradict the claimed efficiency because they require full gradient computation, yielding the same complexity as RigL and no performance improvement over RigL (R1).
   - The technical tools are well known with obvious applications to this problem (R3).
   - One reviewer (R1) rates soundness as poor; two (R2, R3) rate soundness as good, but all rate contribution as poor.

2. **Marginal practical significance** (R1, R2):
   - Accuracy improvements are modest, typically 0.1%–0.5% (R1).
   - More recent always-sparse methods (Powerpropagation+Top-KAST, ITOP-RigL) significantly outperform RigL at 90% sparsity on ResNet-50, whereas GSE only matches or slightly exceeds RigL in some settings (R2).

3. **Missing experiments and ambiguous notation** (R1, R2):
   - Figure 2 only shows $\gamma$ up to 2. A comprehensive plot covering a broader range is needed; it is unclear at what $\gamma$ performance declines and why (R1).
   - How is the norm of activations/gradients computed? Summing all elements and taking the absolute value yields zero in expectation for symmetric distributions, while L1/L2 norms give different results; clarification is required (R2).
   - Notation for $f^{[l]}$, $g^{[l]}$, $h_l$, $g_l$ is confusing; the denominator $1^T |h/g^{(l-1)}| 1$ implies a matrix of size (num_layers, num_layers) (R2).

4. **Lack of practical relevance** (R3):
   - No wall-clock time analysis is provided. If the guided stochastic exploration procedure takes longer than the theoretical FLOP reduction, any benefits are moot (R3).
   - Sparsifying weight connections does not deliver actual speedups on massively parallel SIMD architectures; sparse matrix‑matrix multiplication is known to be slow in practice (R3).
   - Experiments are at small scale, and the potential advantage in reducing backward-pass FLOPs is minor because RigL only materializes dense gradients every 100–1000 steps (R2). The paper does not demonstrate value for large-scale models such as ChatGPT, GPT4, or diffusion models (R3).
   - The connection to biological neural networks is not well thought out (R3). Reviewers question whether this exploratory prototyping is sufficient for peer review and what path to acceptance exists given the insufficient novelty and lack of practical results (R3).
