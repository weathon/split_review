## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces Fairness-aware Sample Weighting (FSW), a method to address unfair catastrophic forgetting in class-incremental learning. FSW adjusts training weights for current-task samples so the average gradient vector reduces forgetting of underperforming sensitive groups (including classes). The authors theoretically analyze how gradient misalignment between new data and a sensitive group can increase loss disparity. They formulate optimization problems for fairness metrics (Equal Error Rate, Equalized Odds, Demographic Parity) and show these reduce to linear programming with polynomial-time solution. Experiments on multiple datasets demonstrate that FSW achieves a better accuracy-fairness tradeoff compared to existing baselines.

### Strengths
- Novel problem formulation integrating group fairness notions into class-incremental learning; original extension of gradient-angle analysis for forgetting to unfair forgetting.
- Clear theoretical foundation with a theorem showing how misaligned gradients can exacerbate loss disparity between underperforming and overperforming groups.
- The proposed sample weighting is equivalent to linear programming and can be solved efficiently (polynomial time).
- Comprehensive experiments across several datasets and baselines; results show FSW yields favorable accuracy-fairness balance.
- Paper is well organized, with an illustrative example and figure that help explain the challenge.

### Weaknesses
- **Binary weights issue (Reviewer 1):** The linear programming conversion implies optimal weights lie at vertices of the hypercube, i.e., 0 or 1. This changes interpretation from weighting to selection. The normalization factor during parameter update should be clarified (total vs. active samples). Visualization could show the percentage of active (weight=1) samples per group.
- **Generalization to multiple sensitive attributes (Reviewer 2):** The paper focuses on a single sensitive attribute. It is unclear how FSW handles multiple intersecting attributes (e.g., race and gender in Adult dataset); improving fairness for one attribute may worsen another or create new subgroup disparities. Theoretical guarantees for such scenarios are not discussed. An experiment or analysis on Adult/Compas with multiple attributes is recommended.
- **Quantification of accuracy-fairness tradeoff (Reviewer 2):** The paper lacks a quantified measure of the tradeoff (e.g., area under the fairness-accuracy curve, Pareto frontier). It is unclear how much accuracy is sacrificed for the reported fairness gains. A visual Pareto frontier is suggested.
- **Convergence and overfitting concerns (Reviewer 3):** No convergence analysis is provided for the reweighted objective. Reweighting may encourage overfitting to a small group of “easily” learned samples that are not representative of the full distribution. The shift in optimization landscape needs justification; convergence to a stationary point of the reweighted objective does not guarantee a desirable solution for the original problem.
- **Definition of \(m\) with zero weights (Reviewer 3):** In the demographic parity formulation, it is unclear whether samples with weight \(w_i=0\) should be counted in the calculation of \(m\) (e.g., number of samples). A clear mathematical formulation is needed.
- **Theorem 1 assumption (Reviewer 3):** The theorem assumes overperforming groups have lower loss than underperforming groups, but it is not specified whether the loss is computed on training data (susceptible to overfitting) or validation data. This affects generalization of the conclusions.
- **Inconsistency between cross-entropy loss and fairness metrics (Reviewer 4):** The paper uses CE loss to approximate group fairness metrics (Eqs. 2–4) but lacks a rigorous theoretical justification for this approximation. The relationship between CE loss and metrics like demographic parity or equalized odds needs detailed analysis, including conditions under which minimizing CE loss also minimizes fairness disparities. Empirical evidence or formal proof is recommended.
- **Theoretical analysis limited to CE loss (Reviewer 4):** The theorems only apply to CE loss, not directly to the fairness metrics that appear in the optimization objective. The gap between the theoretical framework (based on CE loss) and the algorithms (which optimize fairness losses) needs bridging. Theorems should be extended to incorporate fairness metrics, or a stronger justification for the CE proxy should be provided.
- **Missing related work on sample weighting (Reviewer 4):** The paper does not discuss active learning methods that also adjust sample weights to improve fairness (e.g., [1–4] cited by reviewer). The differences between such approaches and FSW in the continual setting should be clarified.
- **Mathematical clarity (Reviewer 1):** Equation 1 uses a dot product notation that could be improved with a transpose; the weighted gradient term \(w_l \nabla_\theta \ell(f_\theta^{l-1}, T_l)\) should be explicitly introduced before use.
- **Minor clarification requests (Reviewers 1 and 4):** Whether FSW is applied per batch or once per epoch; impact of buffer size on unfair forgetting (ablation study); inconsistency between fairness definitions in Section 3.2 and those used in experiments; sample weight variables in optimization problems not clearly defined; term “cost function” in line 241.
