## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper studies energy efficiency of on-device streaming speech recognition (ASR) using the neural transducer architecture. Through extensive experiments, it finds that most power consumption comes from loading model weights from off-chip memory, influenced by component size, invocation frequency, and memory placement. The Joiner component, despite being small, consumes more power than Encoder and Predictor due to these factors. An exponential relationship between encoder size and word error rate (WER) is observed. Using these insights, the authors propose iterative design guidelines to select which component to compress (via Adam-pruning and quantization) for maximal power reduction with minimal WER impact. Experiments on LibriSpeech and a Public Video dataset (using Emformer and Conformer encoders, respectively) report up to 47% energy savings and 29% RTF reduction at comparable accuracy. One reviewer (R1) found the paper well-written and the findings new; two others (R2, R3) found it empirically sound but limited in novelty and methodological rigor.

### Strengths

- Power consumption is a highly relevant problem for on-device ASR.
- The paper provides a systematic approach to optimizing energy efficiency.
- A large number of model sizes were tested, and experiments are extensive and logically organized (R1).
- The analysis of the Joiner component’s disproportionate power usage is interesting.
- The overall writing is clear and well-structured (R1, R2), though R2 noted a key lack of clarity on power measurement.

### Weaknesses

1. ** Lack of clarity on power consumption measurement.**  
   - It is unclear whether the power consumption results (Figures 6 & 7) are model-based estimates or real hardware measurements. No hardware platform details (specific SoC, memory types, CPU/GPU) or measurement methodology are provided. If estimated, the model parameters and validation against real measurements are absent, leaving correlation with real power consumption unsubstantiated (R2).  
   - R3 additionally notes that the experiments use a Google Pixel 5 without any neural hardware acceleration (e.g., Google Tensor G4, Apple Neural Engine). Most modern devices include such accelerators, and results on a CPU-only device may not generalize. The choice of Pixel 5 and the reliance on sparsity via Adam-pruning (suboptimal for neural accelerators) questions the relevance of the study (R3).

2. ** Incremental contribution and reliance on standard techniques.**  
   - The core methodology (profiling power per component, applying pruning and quantization) is well-known and standard; no novel compression algorithms or optimization strategies are introduced. The paper reads more like a thorough industry technical report than a research paper with significant novelty (R1, R3).  
   - Only Adam-pruning (introducing sparsity) is used to vary model size. Other methods (changing number of layers/dimensions, training from scratch, knowledge distillation) are not explored or compared. This restricts the search space and may miss better configurations for a given power budget. For neural accelerator chips, changing layers/dimensions is more relevant than sparsity (R3).

3. ** Missing specifications and comparisons.**  
   - The base model is not specified at all (encoder type, number of layers/dimensions, decoder type, joiner network details). Table 1 lacks key details (e.g., WER, beam search vs greedy, language model usage, absolute parameter count vs architecture dimensions) (R3).  
   - No comparison of different encoders (Emformer, Conformer, Zipformer) for the same dataset – Emformer is used for LibriSpeech, Conformer for Public Video, making it hard to compare across datasets (R3).  
   - The effect of the proposed heuristic is compared only against a baseline that is not clearly specified (R3 questions “the choice and performance of the baseline are not critical in this context”).  
   - No code is provided to reproduce results (R3).

4. ** Indirect evaluation of relevant metrics.**  
   - The paper plots WER, power consumption, and RTF only against *model size* (number of parameters), not against each other. The directly relevant relationships (e.g., WER vs power consumption, RTF vs power consumption at fixed WER) are not shown. Model size is not the ultimate objective (R3).  
   - Figure 4c (WER vs. encoder size) is very noisy, likely due to the variability of Adam-pruning. Averaging over multiple seeds could help. The claim of an “exponential relationship” may not hold when model size is varied via other means (e.g., changing layers/dimensions) (R3).

5. ** Speculative claims and missing analysis.**  
   - The paper claims that energy consumption is influenced by invocation frequency and memory placement, but this is not clearly demonstrated in the results (R3).  
   - No discussion of trade-offs between power consumption and model size; it is unclear whether the proposed iterative compression approach can be adapted to directly minimize model size, or whether a multi-objective optimization would be beneficial (R2).  
   - R2 notes that the WER vs model size graphs for the proposed method are slightly worse than the baseline – this is understandable given the focus on power, but the method’s efficacy for pure memory minimization is not addressed.
