## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces ZETA, a method for efficient top‑k attention that uses Z‑order curves to map low‑dimensional keys and queries into one‑dimensional space, enabling parallel sorting and reducing complexity to O(N log N). It replaces dot‑product attention with Cauchy‑Softmax and proposes reducing key/query dimensions while keeping value dimensions high, based on a theoretical trade‑off. Experiments on Associative Recall, Long‑Range Arena, and WikiText‑103 show that ZETA matches or outperforms standard attention and its variants. The method is novel and sound, but concerns remain about the evaluation scale, clarity of the Z‑order search and chunking procedure, benchmarking of speed/memory, choice of d_k, generalizability of hyperparameters, and missing LRA tasks (e.g., Path‑X).

### Strengths
1. **Novel method**: Introduces Z‑order curves for dimensionality reduction in top‑k attention, providing a fresh approach to efficient attention (Reviewer 1, 3).
2. **Theoretical grounding**: Provides theoretical justification via the Johnson–Lindenstrauss Lemma for random projection of keys/queries, with detailed proofs (Reviewer 1, 3). Investigates the trade‑off between curse of dimensionality and preservation of relative distances (Reviewer 2).
3. **Adaptive Cauchy‑Softmax**: Introduces a trainable parameter γ to dynamically adjust the receptive field, enhancing flexibility (Reviewer 2).
4. **Strong empirical results**: Matches or outperforms full dot‑product attention on Multi‑Query Associative Recall, outperforms attention and its variants on Long‑Range Arena, and works well on WikiText‑103 language modeling (Reviewer 1, 3, 4). Authors include a PyTorch implementation (Reviewer 3).
5. **Clear presentation**: Paper is well‑organized and accessible, with clear explanations of Z‑order projection and the Cauchy‑Softmax mechanism (Reviewer 1, 4). Method is considered believable (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited evaluation scale**: Experiments are run on a very small model (125M parameters). It is unclear whether the findings translate to larger models (Reviewer 2). Scaling to modern LLMs with >100k context length raises questions about hyperparameter selection, e.g., whether d_k would need to go to 2 or 1 (Reviewer 4).
2. **Unclear explanation of top‑k search via Z‑order curve**: The description of using a single binary search to identify nearest neighbors is unclear. Because the Z‑order curve does not perfectly preserve locality, multiple segments likely need to be inspected to avoid missing true nearest neighbors. The window‑centered approach is not clearly justified (Reviewer 2, 3). The chunking mechanism for causal masking is also not clearly written; pseudocode or a more detailed algorithmic description would help (Reviewer 3).
3. **Missing benchmarking of speed and memory**: Despite claiming O(N log N) complexity, the paper provides no actual runtime or memory measurements. Comparisons with standard attention, Flash Attention, or other efficient methods (e.g., SSMs) are needed, especially for long sequences (Reviewer 2, 3, 4).
4. **Narrow and insufficiently justified d_k selection**: The experiments only test d_k values of 1, 2, 3, 8, and 32. Larger dimensions (commonly used in practice) are not explored. The choice of these specific values is not justified, and it is unclear if the performance gains hold with higher‑dimensional keys and queries (Reviewer 1). The claim that smaller d_k “do not affect performance” is not well‑established (Reviewer 3).
5. **Contradictory statements about d_k and locality**: Section 3.2.1 states that lower d_k loses locality, but Section 4.4 states that lower d_k exhibits higher locality preservation. This needs clarification – specifically, whether “locality” refers to the original input space or the transformed Z‑order space, and how d_k impacts the quality of the Z‑order mapping (Reviewer 4).
6. **Unclear handling of binary expansion precision**: Equation (4) shows interleaving bits of d‑dimensional vectors, but it is not explained what precision (e.g., 32‑bit float) is used for each dimension. If d=3 and precision is 32 bits, the result is 96 bits, potentially causing overflow. The paper lacks details on how floating‑point values are converted to binary and how the precision is set (Reviewer 4).
7. **Unclear transferability of γ (Adaptive Cauchy‑Softmax)**: It is not clear whether the learned γ generalizes across tasks/datasets or is specific to the training data. No analysis or evidence is provided (Reviewer 1).
8. **Lack of formal justification for Euclidean distance over cosine similarity**: The claim that Euclidean distance is more effective for low‑dimensional top‑k attention lacks theoretical or empirical support (Reviewer 1).
9. **Limited comparison on Long‑Range Arena**: Performance is reported without comparison to state‑of‑the‑art results on LRA. Notably, the Path‑X task (a key long‑range benchmark) is not included, and the authors do not explain this omission (Reviewer 3). The paper tests d_k only on a subset of LRA tasks; a broader evaluation across tasks would strengthen the findings (Reviewer 1).
10. **Rationale for Associative Recall task**: The choice of this synthetic task is not justified (Reviewer 1).
11. **Limitations of distance preservation**: For even modest key dimensions, distances may not be well‑preserved after Z‑order projection, raising concerns about scaling to higher dimensions (Reviewer 3). The method also changes the attention similarity from dot‑product to Cauchy, so it may not serve as a drop‑in replacement for existing pretrained models (Reviewer 3).
