## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes ASMG, a novel adaptive stochastic gradient algorithm for black-box multi-objective optimization (MOO). It applies a Gaussian smoothing technique to the objectives, enabling gradient estimation with respect to the parameters (mean and covariance) of a search distribution, and uses an adaptive weighting scheme to aggregate gradients. The authors establish theoretical connections between the original MOO problem and the Gaussian-smoothed version, and prove convergence rates: \(\mathcal{O}(\log T/T)\) for convex and \(\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/2})\) for non-convex cases to a Pareto stationary solution. Empirically, ASMG is evaluated on synthetic benchmarks and a CLIP-based multi-task learning problem, showing competitive performance against an evolutionary strategy (ES) baseline.

### Strengths
- **Novelty**: The first stochastic gradient algorithm for black-box MOO with theoretical convergence guarantees, to the reviewers’ knowledge.
- **Theory**: Provides rigorous convergence proofs in both convex and non-convex settings, with concrete rates. The analysis of the relationship between the original problem and the Gaussian-smoothed problem is technically sound and interesting.
- **Significance**: Addresses an important and timely problem, as many modern ML systems (e.g., LLM APIs, multimodal models) are only accessible for inference (function queries) and may involve multiple conflicting objectives.
- **Clarity**: The paper is well-written, easy to follow, with clear explanations of the algorithm and its theoretical properties.
- **Empirical validation**: Demonstrates effectiveness on synthetic numerical benchmarks (outperforming ES in convergence speed and solution quality) and a realistic CLIP multi-task learning experiment, showing the method is not limited to toy problems.

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient baselines**: The only comparison is against an evolutionary strategy (ES). Missing comparisons with other major black-box MOO branches, such as multi-objective Bayesian optimization, multi-objective bandits, or recent gradient-based MOO methods for multi-task learning. This limits the evidence of superiority or practical gains.
- **Limited related work discussion**: The related work section only covers evolutionary methods and Bayesian optimization. There is a lack of discussion on:
  - The advantages of the Gaussian-smoothed MOO approach over traditional black-box methods.
  - Connections to recent works on MOO in learning-to-rank (KDD 2023 papers) and seminal MOO for multi-task learning (e.g., GradNorm, MGDA).
  - The technical difficulties of applying stochastic gradient approximation to black-box MOO; clarifying this would better highlight the paper’s novelty.
- **Assumption of diagonal covariance matrix**: The method assumes the covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) of the search distribution is diagonal. Multiple reviewers question how realistic this assumption is (it implies independence across dimensions) and whether there is a performance gap with a full covariance matrix. An ablation study or justification is needed.
- **Computational complexity and runtime**: The Monte-Carlo sampling used to estimate expectations adds computational overhead. The paper does not analyze complexity or provide empirical running times compared to other methods. This is especially concerning for high-dimensional parameter spaces.
- **Scalability to high dimensions**: The setting may not be suitable for very large \(d\) (e.g., deep neural network parameters). The paper lacks analysis of how performance scales with dimensionality and does not discuss the limitations or provide empirical evidence as \(d\) grows.
- **Mini-batch stochasticity**: The theoretical guarantees assume full evaluation of the objective function, but in practical settings (like the multi-task learning experiment) mini-batch approximations are used. The paper does not extend the theory to cover this stochasticity nor thoroughly discusses its potential impact on convergence and performance.
- **Minor**: The definition of Pareto stationarity (Proposition 2.3) may not be a necessary condition for Pareto optimality when \(\mathcal{X} \subset \mathbb{R}^d\) (e.g., when a global minimum lies on the boundary). A clarification would be helpful.
- **Questions about assumptions**: Reviewers also question how likely the assumptions in Section 4 (especially the convex case) hold for the synthetic problems used in experiments.
