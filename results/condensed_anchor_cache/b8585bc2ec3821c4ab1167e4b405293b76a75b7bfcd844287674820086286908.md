## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces AttributeLLaMA, a LLaMA model fine-tuned on only 100 expert-annotated attribution examples to enable evidence generation for QA tasks. The generated attributions improve performance on MMLU (>3.5%) and StrategyQA (>4.0%) and are used to analyze memorization and hallucination. Reviewers found the idea of minimal supervision interesting but raised substantial concerns about novelty, comparisons, and depth.

### Strengths
- **Interesting and effective minimal‑supervision approach**: The concept of using only 100 expert‑annotated examples to elicit attribution generation is considered interesting and appears effective (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4). One reviewer (Reviewer 1, rating 5) found the approach “impressive and effective,” while the other three (rating 3) emphasized that the empirical results are promising but not novel.
- **High‑quality expert‑annotated dataset**: The 100 annotated examples are of high quality and provide a resource for future work (Reviewer 1). Another reviewer noted the efficiency of achieving the capability with such small supervision (Reviewer 3).
- **Comprehensive evaluation on multiple QA benchmarks**: Experiments across diverse datasets (MMLU, StrategyQA, etc.) show significant gains: >3.5% on MMLU and >4.0% on StrategyQA (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4). The analysis also examines reasoning, memorization, and hallucination (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). Reviewer 1 described the analysis as “thorough”; Reviewers 2 and 3 praised the discussion of memorization and hallucination.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of novelty and missing comparisons with existing methods**: The method of prompting LLMs to generate evidence as attribution is not new; the relation to GenRead is not discussed (Reviewer 1). The paper lacks comparisons with chain‑of‑thought prompting, instruction‑following baselines (e.g., Dolly), and more recent models (LLaMa2, GPT‑4, ChatGPT) with or without attribution prompts (Reviewers 3, 4; Reviewer 1’s questions). The only baseline is a model without any attributes, which is insufficient (Reviewer 4). Contribution ratings reflect this: Reviewer 1 rated contribution 3 (good), while Reviewers 2, 3, 4 rated it 2 (fair).
- **Unnecessary two‑step process**: The separation of evidence generation and QA into two steps is not justified; the model could produce both in a single call. The approach resembles using LLMs as an external knowledge base, which has been extensively studied (Reviewer 2).
- **Insufficient justification of “minimum supervision”**: The claim that 100 examples are minimal is not substantiated. How was the number 100 chosen? Would machine‑generated (evidence, question‑answer) pairs work? Would a random set of 100 QA pairs from another benchmark suffice? (Reviewers 1, 2). The data selection and annotation procedures are not detailed (e.g., whether each expert labelled 50, how discrepancies were resolved) (Reviewer 1). The chosen data is close to the downstream tasks, so the source of improvement (attributions vs. task similarity) is unclear (Reviewer 3).
- **Confusing prompt design and evaluation**: The paper compares LLaMa with “LLaMa + AttributeLLaMa”, but AttributeLLaMa is fine‑tuned to output both evidence and answer via instruction following. It is unclear why the direct outputs of AttributeLLaMa are not compared to LLaMa (i.e., LLaMa vs. AttributeLLaMa) (Reviewer 1). Additionally, no comparison is made between generated attributions and attributions from a baseline model without fine‑tuning (Reviewer 4).
- **Scalability concerns**: The approach does not show clear performance improvements when applied to larger models, raising doubts about its practical utility and scalability (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing metrics for attribution quality**: The paper does not provide metrics to measure the quality of attributions, which would guide future work on constructing better attributions (Reviewer 3’s question).
- **No ablation on number of examples**: The impact of varying the number of expert‑annotated examples is not explored, so the trade‑off between supervision and performance remains unknown (Reviewer 3’s question).
- **Shallow analysis of memorization**: While the memorization and hallucination discussion is appreciated, it does not address implications such as overfitting or memorization of sensitive information. Overall, the study lacks deep conclusions and reads more like a technical blog than academic research; the paper still does not explain the principles of how to construct high‑quality attributions (Reviewer 3).
- **Minority reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (Reviewer 1, rating 5) considered the approach impressive, the analysis thorough, and the contribution good. The other three reviewers (ratings 3) found the paper lacking in novelty, missing comparisons, and not sufficiently insightful, indicating a substantial gap in overall assessment.
