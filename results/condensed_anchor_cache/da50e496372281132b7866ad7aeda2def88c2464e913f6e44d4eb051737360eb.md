## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Zero-shot Concept Bottleneck Models (Z-CBMs), which avoid any training on the target dataset by using a large-scale concept bank (millions of noun phrases from caption datasets) and dynamic concept retrieval via CLIP embeddings. For each input image, relevant concepts are retrieved from the bank, and sparse linear regression (lasso) is used to approximate the image’s CLIP visual feature as a weighted sum of concept text features. The final label is predicted by cosine similarity between this approximated feature and class text embeddings. Reviewers overall found the method simple and intuitive, and acknowledged that it addresses the missing supervision problem in prior CBMs. However, opinions on its novelty and significance varied widely: one reviewer (score 3) argued the approach is essentially a reimplementation of decade-old attribute-based zero-shot classification ideas using a CLIP embedding space, while other reviewers (scores 5–6) considered it a meaningful contribution but raised concerns about the evaluation metrics, interpretability of concepts, and lack of comparisons to relevant prior works.

### Strengths

- **Simple, intuitive, and straightforward method** – the idea of retrieving concepts and applying sparse regression is easy to understand (R1, R2, R3).
- **Competitive target task performance** – Table 1 shows the proposed Z-CBM outperforms zero-shot CLIP on several datasets, and Table 2 reports that a simple trainable variant surpasses previous CBMs in the same setting (R1, R3, R4, R6). However, two reviewers (R2, R5) found these results unimpressive or predictable (see Weaknesses).
- **XAI interpretability results** – Table 3 shows “impressively good” concept relevance as measured by CLIP-Score, though the validity of this metric is disputed (R2, see Weaknesses).
- **Comprehensive evaluation** – experiments cover 12 datasets, and the analysis of concept bank size (Section 4.6.2, Table 1) demonstrates that a larger concept bank improves zero-shot performance (R4).
- **First zero-shot CBM** – the paper is considered a novel contribution by several reviewers (R4, R6), eliminating the need for labeled training data compared to previous CBMs (R4, R6).
- **Tool for understanding CLIP semantics** – the method provides a way to interpret CLIP visual features through retrieved concepts (R6).

### Weaknesses

1. **Lack of novelty compared to prior attribute-based zero-shot classification** – One reviewer (R5, score 3) strongly argued that the core idea of representing an image as a weighted combination of concept/attribute embeddings in a joint space and classifying in text space dates back to works like ConSe (2014), Write (2013), Costa (2014), Objects2Actions (2015), and AwA (2013). The only differences are the use of a larger concept bank and CLIP embeddings, which do not constitute substantial novelty. R2 also noted the missing discussion of “Attributes2Classname” (ICCV 2019), which learns to represent images via linear transformations of attribute text embeddings. R6 pointed out the lack of comparison to other training-free CBM approaches such as “Visual Classification via Description from Large Language Models” (ICLR 2023). The paper’s claims of being free from additional training/data are too strong given the heavy reliance on the pre-trained CLIP model trained on gigantic datasets (R2, R5).

2. **Performance gains over CLIP are not meaningfully explained** – The paper attributes the improvement in Table 1 to a reduced modality gap in the concept-to-label mapping (text–text), but the modality gap persists in the input-to-concept mapping (image–text), and the argument lacks sufficient references (R1). More critically, R2 showed with a random matrix experiment that as the number of retrieved concepts K increases, the approximated feature approaches the original CLIP image embedding regardless of whether the concepts are semantically meaningful. This suggests that the performance is due to feature approximation rather than meaningful concept selection, and does not validate the model’s explainability improvements (R2). R1 also questioned why linear regression (which should reconstruct the original feature better) performs so poorly compared to lasso in Table 6 – this discrepancy remains unexplained.

3. **CLIP-Score metric is misleading** – R2 argued that the CLIP-Scores in Table 2 measure correlation between the image’s CLIP visual feature and the CBM-reconstructed feature. Since the concept weights are chosen to maximize this reconstruction, high scores are expected and do not necessarily indicate concept quality. The authors acknowledged this and proposed separating the CLIP model used inside the method from the evaluation model, but different CLIP models may still behave in correlated ways, making CLIP-Score an unreliable metric (R2).

4. **Missing comparisons and ablations** – Several specific comparisons are absent:
   - Construction of a concept bank using a question-answering approach similar to label-free CBM [1] (R4).
   - Direct comparison using concept sets from prior CBM works (R2).
   - Comparison to other zero-shot methods beyond CLIP retrieval (R5).
   - Ablation on the effect of hyper-parameter λ (lasso regularization) on performance, sparsity, and concept selection; the chosen λ = 1e-5 is extremely small relative to the weight vector size K ~ 1000, and it is unclear how it was tuned and whether the same value works across all 12 datasets (R2, R5, R4).
   - Ablation of the effect of concept bank construction (e.g., using domain-specific concepts vs. the generic 5M bank) (R2, R4).
   - Random matrix control: replacing concept embeddings with random matrices in Eq. 3-4 for varying K confirms the reconstruction artifact (R2).

5. **Interpretability of candidate concepts is questionable** – Retrieved concepts often contain negations (e.g., “Not maltese dog terrier” in Figure 3), which do not provide clear, human-understandable information for classification (R5, R6). The paper does not adequately explain whether “not” is part of the concept text (encoded by CLIP) or a result of negative regression weights, nor does it discuss concept granularity or the desirability of negative weights (R5, R6). Additionally, the paper lacks visual examples that illustrate how sparse regression reduces concept redundancy compared to other weighting methods (R4).

6. **Advantages over a simple interpretable CLIP baseline are unclear** – A baseline that simply retrieves concepts using visual features and uses them for interpretability (without the regression step) would similarly provide concept-based explanations, but the paper does not compare to such a baseline or discuss its differences (R1). The method’s ability to support concept intervention is also not explored.

7. **Feature space alignment concerns** – Visual and textual features from CLIP are aligned by cross-entropy loss, not strictly calibrated for L2 reconstruction. The paper does not provide a t-SNE visualization to show how well the image feature can be fitted by a weighted sum of text features (R6).

8. **Inference cost is high** – The large concept bank (millions of concepts) and test-time sparse regression significantly increase inference time compared to standard CLIP or existing CBMs (R6, R4). Figure 6 reports inference time, but a breakdown into embedding, retrieval, and regression components is needed to understand scaling behavior (R4).

9. **Hyper-parameter tuning details are insufficient** – It is not clarified how λ (and any other hyper-parameters) are tuned, nor whether the same values are used across all datasets. Reproducibility from scratch requires this information (R2). Similarly, the choice of linear regression vs. lasso in Section L426–427 is not justified beyond performance.

10. **Unaddressed directions from prior work** – The paper does not explore or discuss several aspects that have been studied in prior attribute-based zero-shot learning: weighting concepts based on target classes (class-specific selection), enforcing positive weights only, using negative concepts explicitly, and leveraging similarity values (not just binary top-K selection) in constructing the concept embedding (R5).
