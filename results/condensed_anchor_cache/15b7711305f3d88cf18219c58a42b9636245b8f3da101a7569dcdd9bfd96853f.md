## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes three backtranslation methods (on-the-fly, offline distilled, line-by-line proof analysis) to generate synthetic informal-formal pairs for autoformalization, creating the AI4Math dataset. It evaluates by fine-tuning GPT-2 124M and measuring eval loss on ProofNet. One reviewer (R3, score 6, confidence 5) finds the methodology novel, the experiments well-rounded, and the emphasis on data quality over quantity valuable for reducing computational resources. The other four reviewers (scores 3, 3, 3, 5) raise substantial concerns: narrow evaluation, weak baselines, questionable metrics, limited model scope, insufficient novelty, and lack of concrete examples. Overall, the evidence is considered unconvincing by the majority.

### Strengths
- The core idea of using backtranslation to generate more informal-formal pairs is reasonable and has proven effective in translation tasks (R2, R3).  
- The paper proposes a novel methodology focusing on data quality over quantity, which can reduce computational costs (R3).  
- Three variations are tested (online, offline distilled, line-by-line proof analysis), offering a well-rounded examination (R3).  
- Experiments are well-designed and show clear improvements over the MMA dataset on ProofNet with significantly fewer tokens (R5, R3).  
- The paper acknowledges the importance of targeted data in autoformalization and suggests new directions (R3).  
- Useful tricks are mentioned in the proposed approaches (R4).

**Disagreement on novelty**: R3 considers the methodology novel, while R2 and R4 note that backtranslation and step-by-step proof analysis are well-known in NLP and autoformalization (ProofNet already uses distilled backtranslation; line-by-line analysis resembles “Let’s verify step by step” from OpenAI and process reward modeling). The contribution may be incremental.

### Weaknesses
- **Narrow evaluation**: Only ProofNet is used; generalizability to other datasets (MiniF2F, Metamath) and real-world mathematical language is not addressed (R1, R3). Data contamination with ProofNet is a risk not discussed (R1).  
- **Limited model size**: All experiments use GPT-2 124M (R1, R4, R5). No exploration of larger or formal-specialized models (Llemma-7B, Qwen-math, DeepSeek-math, Gemini-Pro, Llama3-7b) leaves scaling behavior unclear (R1, R4, R5). Gains on a weak model do not necessarily transfer to stronger ones (R4).  
- **Unconvincing metric**: Eval loss (cross-entropy) is used to claim improvements, but it is unreliable for autoformalization (R2, R4, R5). The paper must justify why eval loss is appropriate; a Lean4 compiler success rate (pass@10) or human verification should be used instead (R4, R5).  
- **Insufficient novelty**: Backtranslation is well-known in autoformalization; ProofNet already used a distilled backtranslation technique (R4). Line-by-line proof analysis is similar to OpenAI’s “Let’s verify step by step” / process reward modeling (R4). The takeaways (quality over diversity, intermediate steps matter) are already known from prior work. The paper does not adequately differentiate its methods (R4).  
- **Weak baselines**: The main baseline, MMA, is not considered strong (rejected by ICLR’24) (R4). Other relevant works (Lean Workbook, Forml4, ProofNet, FIMO) are omitted; a comparison table would help (R5).  
- **Insufficient examples**: No concrete examples of generated data or before-and-after comparisons from AI4Math are provided (R2, R5). The template-based method lacks clarity on how informal statements are generated from formal ones (R2).  
- **No failure-mode analysis**: No analysis of which categories or types of proofs the method handles well or poorly (R1).  
- **Cost analysis missing**: A detailed cost (time, resources) comparison of the three backtranslation methods is lacking (R5).  
- **Only one method shows clear gains**: Among the three proposed methods, only GPT-4-generated offline data yields better eval loss than prior data; the other two do not demonstrate clear improvements (R2).  

**Reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms / missing experiments** (included above but explicitly listed for clarity):  
- Justify the use of eval loss and provide end-to-end metrics (Lean4 compilation success) (R2, R4, R5).  
- Compare with existing autoformalization methods before and after using AI4Math (R2).  
- Evaluate on MiniF2F and Metamath (R3).  
- Scale experiments to larger models (Llemma-7B, Llama3-7b, Qwen-math, DeepSeek-math) and formal-specialized models (R1, R4, R5).  
- Compare GPT-4 with Gemini-Pro for informalization (R5).  
- Provide concrete examples of autoformalization tasks and how they compare to baselines (R5).  
- Explore human-in-the-loop feedback during informalization (R5).  
- Provide evidence that the approach scales to larger models and datasets (R1).  
- Clarify the novelty of the techniques relative to prior work (R4).  
- Investigate data contamination with ProofNet (R1).

**Disagreement note**: R3 (score 6, confidence 5) is positive about the methodology, novelty, and results, while the other four reviewers (scores 3, 3, 3, 5) are more critical, citing the issues above. The majority view is that the paper does not provide sufficient evidence of effectiveness.
