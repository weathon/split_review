## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper introduces Adiabatic Reinforcement Learning (ARL), a wake-sleep method for continual reinforcement learning that decouples representation learning from policy acquisition. ARL uses high-TD-error transitions to drive fast policy learning during wake phases and slow representation learning via adiabatic replay (AR) during sleep phases, aiming to avoid large replay buffers. Experiments are conducted on three robotic simulator tasks based on a differential-drive model (line-following, pushing-objects, robotic pursuit). Reviewers diverge strongly: one (score 5) finds the approach innovative and the motivation clear, while two (scores 3) see promise but cite major deficiencies, and one (score 1) finds the paper too poorly written and incomplete to assess.

### Strengths

- The continual RL problem is important and the goals of the paper (fast adaptation, low memory, no task ID) are reasonable and have merit (R3). The idea of decoupling fast policy learning from slow representation learning is intuitive and well-suited to CRL (R1).
- The use of GMMs for rapid learning is novel and offers interpretability, as demonstrated in Figure 6 (R1).
- The paper’s innovative points – fast learning of new tasks, low memory footprint, and plastic representations – are impressive (R2). The motivation is very clear and the paper is easy to understand (R2).  
  *Contrast*: One reviewer (R4) found no strengths, citing that the paper is very hard to follow overall.

### Weaknesses

- **Writing, structure, and presentation**: The paper reads like an early draft with poor grammar, typos, undefined notations, and many incorrectly formatted citations (confusing \citet and \citep) (R1, R2, R3, R4). The organization is unnatural: the Methods section first details benchmarks (Sec. 2.1, with subsections covering ~2 pages) and baselines (2.2) before introducing the method (2.3) (R3). The introduction is too brief to provide a sufficient picture (R3, R4). Results are presented as two long tables with small fonts that are hard to interpret or compare (R1). Figures 4 and 5 are difficult to understand (R2). There is no algorithm pseudocode (R2, R3). The linked code repository is empty (R3). Minor formatting errors are prevalent (see R3’s list: e.g., “fig.” instead of “Fig.”, missing spaces, footnote placement).
- **Insufficient method exposition**: Several core components are not explained adequately. The adiabatic replay technique (AR, from Krawczyk & Gepperth 2024a) is not sufficiently described: what are \(p(s;\{\theta_k\})\) and \(\pi_k\)? How are they used? (R1). The paper provides no background section (R1) and does not elaborate on essential references (Krawczyk & Gepperth 2024a,b) (R4). The method section contains too much material on benchmarks and not enough on the algorithm itself (R2, R4). It is unclear whether the approach requires task boundaries or task IDs, and how it would adapt to continuous non-stationarities (R1). No ablation study is presented (R2). The specific improvements of the applied AR over the original supervised AR method are not stated (R2). The difference from Prioritized Experience Replay in DQN is not explained (R2). The use of a 25k time limit raises the question of whether partial-episode bootstrapping/time-awareness is handled (Pardo et al. 2018) (R3).
- **Limited and unconvincing experiments**: The experiments are too simple and only serve as a proof-of-concept (R1, R2, R3). They are conducted solely on a specialized differential-drive robotic simulator, not on any standard CRL benchmarks (R3). Recommended benchmarks include: gym-Minigrid, JellyBeanWorld, Procgen, MetaWorld (R1); DeepMind Control (R2); tasks from Wan et al. (2022) (R3). Comparisons with the latest CRL algorithms are absent; in particular, PT-DQN (Chung 2019) should be included (R1). The paper should also evaluate model-based RL approaches that are designed for similar CRL scenarios (R3). In Figure 6, GMM centroids for new colours overlap significantly with existing ones – why does this happen when colour is the only changing variable? (R1). The approach has not been applied to more complex RL algorithms such as SAC or DSAC (R2).
