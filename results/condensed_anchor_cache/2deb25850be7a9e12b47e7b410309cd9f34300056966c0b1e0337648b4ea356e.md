## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a defense against label-flipping attacks in federated learning for autonomous vehicles by combining PCA-based anomaly detection with SVM classification to filter malicious updates. Reviewers agree the problem is important and the paper has good structure. However, the vast majority (six of seven) rate it low (scores 1–3 out of 5) due to insufficient novelty, lack of comparisons with existing methods, trivial experimental settings (3 clients, IID data, no non-IID or AV-specific scenarios), poor threat model description, and major writing/grammar issues. One reviewer (Reviewer 2) is less critical, praising the experimental design and clarity, but still finds the contributions insufficient and the AV relevance unclear. The most negative reviewer (Reviewer 5) identifies no strengths.

### Strengths
1. **Topic relevance**: Addressing data poisoning (label-flipping) in federated learning for autonomous vehicles is a worthwhile and important security challenge (R1, R2, R3, R4, R6, R7).
2. **Anomaly detection approach**: Using PCA combined with MCSVM (Multi-class SVM) as an outlier detection method is a clear, easy-to-follow proposal that shows potential to filter compromised updates (R1, R2, R3, R6). Reviewer 2 further highlights the “clear explanations of PCA and SVM integration.”
3. **Structure and presentation**: The paper has a good logical structure, and the related work is well-contextualized (R2, R4). Reviewer 2 notes “structured result presentation.”
4. **Experimental evidence**: Experiments on benchmark datasets (e.g., CIFAR) show a 15% accuracy drop prevention at 20% poisoning, supporting initial effectiveness (R1, R3). Reviewer 2 calls the experimental design “robust” (multiple datasets, meaningful metrics), though this view is strongly contested by other reviewers.

### Weaknesses
1. **Lack of novelty / insufficient contribution**: PCA+SVM for outlier detection in FL has been well studied; the paper appears to merely combine existing techniques (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5, R6, R7). Reviewer 4 specifically references existing work: “Defending against the Label-flipping Attack in Federated Learning” by Najeeb Moharram Jebreel et al. The paper does not clarify how the attack or defense differs from prior work or what specific FL challenges are addressed (R2, R3).
2. **No baseline comparisons**: The experiments contain no comparisons with other poisoning attacks (e.g., backdoor, model poisoning, gradient leakage) or other defense mechanisms (R1, R2, R3, R5, R6, R7). Without this, it is impossible to assess the relative effectiveness or novelty of the proposed approach (R2).
3. **Trivial experimental settings**: 
   - Only **three devices** and **IID data partitions** are used (R4, R6). Non-IID settings (common in real FL) are not tested, despite the known impact on attack/defense efficacy (R2, R3, R6).
   - No ablation studies (R3).
   - Only a single experiment set is presented (R7). The results are not convincing given these limitations (R4, R5, R6, R7).
4. **Weak connection to autonomous vehicles**: The paper does not detail AV-specific data (e.g., sensor, LiDAR, camera) or explain how label-flipping manifests in AV perception/decision-making (R2, R6). It merely uses standard computer vision benchmarks (CIFAR), differing little from general FL research (R6). Figure 1 is insufficient to establish relevance (R2).
5. **Inadequate threat model and FL setting description**: 
   - The threat model is poorly described; assumptions are unreasonable—e.g., what does “training FL models without the adversarial settings” mean? How can no adversarial devices be guaranteed? (R4)
   - The source of the test set D_test and its disjointness from client data are not justified (R4).
   - The paper does not discuss FL-specific challenges of anomaly detection (decentralized detection, communication constraints, computational overhead per device) (R2). For resource-constrained AVs, the overhead of PCA+SVM scaling with number of clients is unaddressed (R2).
6. **Limited attack scope**: Only label-flipping (the simplest data poisoning) is considered. More sophisticated attacks (backdoor, model poisoning, adversarial sensor manipulation) are ignored, and their limitations are not discussed (R2, R3).
7. **Lack of mathematical formalization**: The method is described only in pure text; no formalized or mathematical description is provided (R3). Statements are unclear—e.g., what is “k” in line 191? (R3)
8. **Writing and presentation quality**: Many typos and grammar issues throughout (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5, R7). Examples: “labels.,Furthermore,” and “the robustness,of the” (R1). The paper reads like an experimental report; the experiment and proposed framework sections should be split, and excessive basic knowledge is included (R3). Current form is “far from acceptable” (R2).

**Minority contrast**: Reviewer 2 (score 5) is far more positive about experimental robustness and clarity but still agrees on the core weaknesses of insufficient novelty, no comparisons, and unclear AV relevance. Reviewer 5 (score 1) finds no strengths at all.
