## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes action-manipulation attacks against reinforcement learning in continuous state and action spaces. A white-box oracle attack (full MDP knowledge) and a black-box attack called LCBT (Lower Confidence Bound Tree) are introduced. LCBT recursively partitions the continuous action space via a binary tree to approximate worst-case actions. Theoretical bounds show that, under sublinear-regret assumptions, the white-box attack and its black-box approximation incur sublinear cost while forcing the agent toward a target policy. Experiments on three RL algorithms (DDPG, PPO, TD3) in two simple environments support the theory. Reviewers praised the theoretical contributions and originality but identified significant weaknesses in experimental scope, practical scalability, missing comparisons, lack of clarity in parts, insufficient discussion of assumptions, and omission of important details (e.g., action-space splitting method). One reviewer (score 5) was notably more critical about practical limitations than the majority (score 6).

### Strengths
- **Originality**: First work to provide theoretical guarantees on attack cost and loss for action-manipulation attacks in continuous state-action RL.
- **New attack methods**: Introduction of the white-box oracle attack and the black-box LCBT are novel contributions.
- **Interesting partitioning idea**: Using a binary tree to recursively partition the continuous action space is original within the RL adversarial setting and well-motivated.
- **Solid theoretical analysis**: In-depth cost bounds (e.g., Theorem 2) under sublinear-regret RL algorithms are interesting, novel, and have implications for robust RL.
- **Experimental support**: Results on DDPG, PPO, and TD3 somewhat support the theoretical findings; the attack appears effective in the tested scenarios.
- **Significance**: Demonstrates that action-manipulation attacks can be harmful in continuous RL with sublinear cost, highlighting a relevant security concern.

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient experimental scope**: Experiments are limited to only two extremely simple, low-dimensional environments. No comparisons with existing attack methods (e.g., Sun et al. 2020) are made, making it hard to gauge empirical effectiveness. Multiple reviewers asked for tests on more realistic benchmarks (e.g., MuJoCo). [R2,R3,R4]
- **Scalability concerns**: The binary tree method may become inefficient in high-dimensional action spaces, requiring many rounds to explore worst nodes [R1]. Constructing the tree for higher-dimensional state/action spaces could be prohibitively expensive; practical runtime and memory implications are not discussed [R4].
- **State-space discretization issue**: The black-box LCBT requires discretizing the continuous state space into \(M\) subintervals, but no method is given for a black-box attacker to find a proper discretization. This seems unrealistic in high-dimensional spaces, especially since attack cost and loss scale linearly with \(M\). [R1]
- **Action-space splitting method unspecified**: The paper does not explain how new leaves in the binary tree are generated (i.e., how a continuous subset of the action space is divided). [R1]
- **Poor clarity in Section 4.2**: The description of the LCBT algorithm is unclear and needs more intuition or an illustrative example. [R2]
- **Assumption of deterministic policies**: The analysis appears to assume deterministic policies, but most RL algorithms use stochastic policies. It is unclear whether the theoretical bounds hold in that more general setting. [R2]
- **Missing computational complexity analysis**: While asymptotic bounds are provided, practical aspects like memory requirements, actual running time on typical hardware, and sensitivity to hyperparameters (tree depth, branching factor) are not discussed. [R3]
- **Generic/inaccurate statements**: Some claims in the introduction are too harsh or unsupported, e.g., “RL algorithms for discrete state-action are no longer applicable” and the assertion that action-manipulation is less direct/efficient than other attack types without justifying why it is more challenging. [R4]
- **Target policy specification**: It is not explained where the target policy comes from or how it can be specified in a complex task. [R3]

Additionally, one reviewer (score 5) was consistently more negative about practical applicability (especially regarding scalability and discretization), whereas the majority (score 6) saw the ideas as promising but underdeveloped in evaluation.
