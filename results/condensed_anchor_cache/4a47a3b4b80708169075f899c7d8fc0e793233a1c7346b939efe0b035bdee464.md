## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies the effect of using ratings versus rankings as feedback protocols for aligning and evaluating LLMs. The authors collect 6k human annotations (ratings and rankings) and corresponding AI annotations from GPT-3.5-Turbo, using Alpaca-7b generated responses. They uncover an inconsistency problem: preferences inferred from ratings and rankings disagree ~60% of the time for both human and AI annotators. Subsequent analysis identifies annotator biases (e.g., humans rate denser responses higher but prefer accuracy in pairwise judgments). The paper also shows that the choice of feedback protocol strongly affects evaluation of aligned LLMs: models trained on rankings data are preferred under rank-based evaluation but not under rating-based evaluation. The findings highlight challenges in designing robust evaluation protocols.

### Strengths
- Essential and timely investigation of the significance of Ratings versus Rankings in collecting and evaluating preference feedback (Reviewer 1).
- In-depth analysis of feedback acquisition from humans and AI based on 6k human annotations and model predictions; reveals prevalence of feedback inconsistency and provides both quantitative and qualitative analysis of why it arises (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- Findings apply to both human and AI annotations, offering useful guidance for designing feedback protocols (Reviewers 3, 4).
- Paper is well structured and written, with extensive and carefully designed experiments (Reviewer 1).
- Demonstrates that the choice of feedback protocol has strong effects on evaluation of aligned LLMs, a practically relevant result (Reviewers 2, 4).
- Draws attention to the important problem of feedback inconsistency (Reviewer 3).
- *Note: Reviewer 1 (score 8) is notably more positive than the other three reviewers (scores 6), who have reservations about execution and novelty but still find the problem important.*

### Weaknesses
- Missing baseline for Table 2: it would be informative to show disagreement when re-annotating the same examples with a different batch of humans or with the same AI model (e.g., GPT-3.5-Turbo) at a different temperature to establish a lower bound on inconsistency (Reviewer 1).
- Lack of detailed discussion on key findings, e.g., Section 3.1 claims no difference in length between preferred and unpreferred responses despite prior work showing a positive correlation with rating; insight is lacking (Reviewer 2).
- The qualitative analysis in Section 4.2 is insufficiently documented: the annotation protocol (how many instances were sampled, how annotators were asked to explain inconsistencies) is missing. If claims about “differences in preferences of humans while annotating” are based on only 2–3 instances, the claim may not be fully supported (Reviewer 2).
- Section 5 (Alignment and Evaluation) has limited novelty: Best-of-n outperforming SFT is already known; the evaluation inconsistency is somewhat predictable given earlier discussion. The result that a ranking-trained reward model yields higher win rate under a ranking evaluation protocol is not surprising because the reward model learns to choose the response preferred in the pairwise setup. The paper does not explore whether the trends hold when feedback data is used in actual alignment training (e.g., PPO/RLHF) (Reviewer 2). This also aligns with Reviewer 3’s request to explore influence on common alignment methods (RLHF, RRHF, RLAIF), since the paper only uses reward models for Best-of-n selection (Reviewer 3).
- The evaluation inconsistency result may be straightforward: reward model performance is affected by input data format. A more informative comparison would convert rating feedback to ranking format first, then train an NLL reward model (like ranking) and compare (Reviewer 3).
- Several experimental design choices are not well justified, limiting generalizability: (a) why Dolly, User-orient, and SuperNI tasks? (b) only Alpaca-7b for generating candidate responses, only GPT-3.5-Turbo as AI annotator, only LORA Alpaca-7b as reward model, and win-rate computed only against DaVinci-003; (c) the specific prompts/instructions for both AI annotation and human crowdworkers (including the meaning of each rating score 1–7) are not provided, making it hard to assess subjectivity and consistency (Reviewer 4; also Reviewer 3’s calibration question).
- It is unclear whether the collected feedback will be released (Reviewer 3) – a missing detail affecting reproducibility.
- A suggested third protocol (instruct to give both ratings and rankings for a pair/list) is not explored, implying the study does not consider hybrid protocols that may improve calibration (Reviewer 1).
- Missing task-level analysis: how results in Figure 3 differ across tasks (Reviewer 4).
- Several typos (e.g., missing link in first paragraph of Section 3, “the humans 6k instances of annotations”) (Reviewer 4).
