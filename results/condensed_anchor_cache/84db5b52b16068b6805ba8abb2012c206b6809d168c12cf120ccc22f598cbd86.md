## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper introduces Network-based Active Inference (NetAIF), a robotic framework that combines active inference principles and random attractor dynamics to enable real-time learning and adaptation in unstructured environments. The reviewers find the core idea interesting and potentially promising for sample-efficient control, but they raise severe concerns about the clarity of the methodology, insufficient experimental comparisons, missing theoretical guarantees, and a lack of detail in the learning process. Reviewers are split: one (rating 6) finds the idea promising and validation thorough, while the other three (ratings 3, 3, 1) judge the paper poorly due to unclear exposition, no baselines, and incomplete results analysis.

### Strengths

- The integration of active inference principles with network dynamics (pullback attractor, controlled instabilities) is a novel and interesting idea, offering a fresh perspective on robotic control and potentially reducing computational overhead.
- Divering from optimization at every level and using exploration/perturbations to converge toward good solutions is an appealing concept.
- The model is claimed to be suitable for resource-constrained robotic applications, and the approach may be more sample-efficient than deep reinforcement learning.
- The paper includes accompanying videos of tracking experiments that suggest reasonable tracking performance.
- Real robot experiments on a 6-DOF arm (pose matching, target tracking, valve manipulation) are appreciated and demonstrate feasibility.

### Weaknesses

- **Unclear methodology and missing definitions:** The method is poorly explained. Reviewers had trouble understanding the state space, the quantity minimized, the objective function, the learning algorithm, and the diffusion process.  
  – Explicit definitions needed: state space, free energy landscape equation (including variable x), how it affects weights, diffusion coefficient calculation, and what dW represents.  
  – Figure 1 is unclear (architecture type? RNN? fully connected?); Figure 2 color encoding and “controlled instability” not specified; Figure 3 legend “rendering the system open” unexplained.  
  – Algorithm 1 is not self-contained: missing loop for feedback, how desired state is determined, what new_weight is, and how free energy plays a role in execution.  
  – “Controlled instability” and weight resetting are not defined; it is unclear how the system is pushed into unstable regions and what advantages/disadvantages this has over standard active inference exploration.

- **No learning process described:** Despite claiming “learning,” the paper does not present how weights are trained.  
  – Provide a separate algorithm/flowchart for learning including weight updates and optimization steps.  
  – Explain training data per task and whether Algorithm 1 is used for training; if so, how is safety ensured during training on a real robot?

- **Missing comparisons and baselines:** The paper claims improvements over deep RL and other methods, but provides no concrete comparisons.  
  – Compare NetAIF (accuracy, efficiency) against at least a well-tuned PD controller, PRM, Hybrid RRT-PRM, or other adaptive/neural control methods (e.g., PMP).  
  – The only metric shown is average planning time (Section 3.6); no quantitative accuracy or success rate comparisons are given.  
  – The tasks are simple; a PID tracking controller may suffice for the first two tasks; authors should explain why NetAIF is needed.

- **Insufficient experimental analysis and clarity:**  
  – Pose matching only shows 2-DOF joint angles, not full 6-DOF end-effector position and orientation; provide full pose results.  
  – Target tracking: what are the inputs and outputs of NetAIF? Why is a Kalman filter needed if AIF can perform filtering? Improve flow diagram with notation, inputs, outputs, and explanation.  
  – Valve experiment: only one shape shown (square), but text claims triangle, square, circle; provide analysis for all shapes and distinguish between NetAIF computations and engineering components.  
  – Lack of disturbance: tasks appear to have little to no environmental disturbance; validate performance under disturbances.  
  – How is the action space defined per task? What robot controller is used? Is gripper state part of the system state? How are constraints handled? How is desired joint pose obtained (inverse kinematics)?

- **Convergence and stability concerns:** The method introduces controlled instabilities and random perturbations, but does not address convergence guarantees or stability.  
  – Provide theoretical guarantees or empirical evidence of system stability under various perturbation conditions.  
  – How is high accuracy ensured despite constant perturbations? What mechanisms prevent divergence?  
  – Evaluate long-term performance and adaptability for highly variable environments or redundant robots.

- **Poor literature positioning and introduction:** The introduction is broad and superficial; only one active inference survey is cited.  
  – Include a comparison table with key differences from previous active inference robotics works (e.g., humanoid robot, adaptive manipulator control, end-to-end pixel-based deep active inference, deep learning perspective).  
  – Cover earlier computationally cheap adaptive/neural control methods (e.g., PMP) in related work.  
  – The paper references a companion paper (Anonymous 2024) for DRL comparisons; this information should be included here. Better to merge both works into a single paper to increase impact.

- **Presentation and writing quality:**  
  – The paper reads like a manual rather than a clear scientific paper; terms like “controlled instability,” “thoroughly explore,” “rendering the system open,” “neuron firing sequences” are vague and not supported mathematically.  
  – Claims about Least Action Principle and physics parallels are not rigorous; “appear” and “suggest” are insufficient.  
  – Figures lack detailed explanations; just titles are not enough.  
  – References: “The High Road to Active Inference” is a chapter; better to cite the book and refer to the chapter.

- **Reviewer disagreement:** One reviewer (rating 6) finds the idea promising and the validation thorough, while three others rate it 1–3 and judge the paper as insufficiently clear, lacking comparisons, and having major methodological gaps. The positive reviewer also recommends merging the companion paper and providing convergence guarantees, and notes that the work’s focus on robotics may limit its relevance to a general learning conference (ICLR).
