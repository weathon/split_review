## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies zero-shot generalization (ZSG) to unseen environments in offline RL. It proposes two algorithms, PERM (model-based) and PPPO (model-free), with theoretical suboptimality bounds. A practical instantiation based on IQL (IQL-nV) is evaluated on offline ProcGen. The paper aims to explain the failure of standard offline RL and provide the first theoretical analysis for ZSG in offline RL.

### Strengths
- Tackles an important, underexplored problem: zero-shot generalization in offline RL to unseen environment contexts. (R1, R3, R4)
- Provides the first theoretical analysis for ZSG in offline RL, with thorough and meticulous derivations; bounds on suboptimality are derived for both PERM and PPPO. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- Algorithms consider multiple environments separately, potentially improving ZSG by capturing variations across contexts. (R2)
- Empirical validation on ProcGen (Mediretta et al., 2023) supplements theory with practical results. (R1, R3, R4)
- Proposed PERM and PPPO are, to the best of reviewers’ knowledge, the first approaches with a proved bound on suboptimality in this setting. (R4)
- The paper has a theoretical focus but attempts to instantiate the ideas in a practical algorithm. (R1)

### Weaknesses
**Problem setting and assumptions**
- The problem setting requires clarification: is context information available during evaluation? If context is included in the dataset and policy is Markovian, how is this different from augmenting the state with context? (R1)
- There are no assumptions on common structure relating context to MDP; the bound in Eq. (1) (line 335) lacks a term involving the number of contexts, making generalization without structure confusing. (R1)
- The objective is the suboptimality gap w.r.t. the best Markovian policy. The best Markovian policy can be bad on every environment; a worst-case bound over contexts or a setting where context is hidden during evaluation would be more meaningful. (R1)
- The presence of an oracle for each individual dataset (line 257) is a strong assumption; it is unclear how to implement it and what estimates for Γ(s,a) are feasible. (R1)
- The requirement for accurate environment labels during training is a strong assumption that limits applicability to scenarios with noisy or missing context information. (R2)
- The counterexample in Section 4 (line 235) uses μ(a)=0, making it impossible to learn about those actions; adjusting to give positive probability would be fairer. (R1)

**Theoretical limitations**
- The suboptimality bounds rely on a large number of sufficiently varied environments; they may be loose with fewer or more similar environments, and sensitivity to diversity is not addressed. (R2)
- The parameters that adjust the degree of pessimism are not fully discussed; lack of clear tuning methodology makes the method difficult to use in practice. (R2)

**Practical and empirical concerns**
- The empirical results do not convincingly show that the proposed method outperforms simpler baselines. Behaviour cloning (BC) achieves better or similar median performance on both expert and mixed datasets (e.g., BC median better than IQL-4V on expert and mixed). The authors do not discuss this failure or how it relates to the suboptimality gap. (R3, R4)
- In Table 2, the deterministic policy result for Miner is reported, but Table 3 shows a stochastic policy variant performs markedly better. It would be fairer to report the stochastic variant in Table 2; it appears the stochastic/deterministic distinction makes the largest difference, not the additional value networks. Is the stochastic policy suggested by this work or from previous works? (R1)
- When running IQL-nV, there are more parameters and capacity; a fairer comparison would be to a 1V version with additional parameters to match capacity. (R1)
- Uncertainty estimates for mean and median performance are missing; significance of improvements is unclear. (R4)
- The baseline IQL is overly simple (treats all stages as belonging to the same MDP); a more competitive baseline (e.g., meta-learning without fine-tuning) would demonstrate the method’s merits more convincingly. (R4)
- Selection of baselines is not justified; why not compare to SOTA methods? (R2)

**Scalability and computational cost**
- PERM requires separate models/value functions for each training environment, becoming expensive as the number of environments grows. PPPO, while model-free, still requires multiple policies for different environments. This limits scalability. (R2)

**Clarity and presentation**
- The paper is difficult to follow; notation is dense and overloaded (e.g., definitions of state and state-action value functions in lines 150-153). The key contribution is unclear after several reads. (R3)
- English quality issues at times. (R3)
- Figure 2 is hard to read and lacks a y-axis label; caption does not specify what the “difference” is between. (R3, R4)
- Minor typos: “Ensure” in algorithm boxes should be “Return”; line 122 “successive feature” should be “successor features”; line 91 misses Section 2; line 355 “becomes” → “become”; line 360 remove “it”. (R1, R3)
- Section 3 should be titled “Preliminaries”. (R3)
- Line 19 claims “near-optimal policy with ZSG both theoretically and empirically”, but empirical findings do not support this. (R3)
- Code link is provided but was not used in review; it would be helpful to link it. (R3)

**Open questions and unaddressed issues**
- Line 385: Why is V^π_{i-1,1}(x_1) not achievable? (R1)
- At test-time, when IQL-nV uses n value functions and n policies (one per value function), how is the policy selected for an unseen context when n is less than the number of test contexts? (R3)
- Methods using ensembles of value networks have been used to combat overestimation (e.g., REDQ, Maxmin Q-learning); what are the parallels with this work? (R1)
- Why does IQL-4V not outperform BC? Discuss limitations in translating theoretical findings to practice. (R3)
- BC performs surprisingly well even on mixed datasets; the authors do not comment on why BC is so competitive. (R4)
