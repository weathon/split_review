## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Socrates Loss, an ad-hoc method for training calibrated selective classifiers that unifies classification and confidence calibration into a single optimization framework with an additional unknown class. It aims to overcome limitations of existing methods – CCL-SC (uses two losses, no unknown class, cumbersome) and SAT (poor calibration especially for ≤100 epochs) – and is evaluated on six image benchmarks with VGG-16 and ResNet-34.

### Strengths
- Addresses the important problem of model reliability by combining confidence calibration and selective classification into a unified, end‑to‑end training objective – a novel and interesting direction.
- The method is designed to be easy to implement and includes an unknown class, allowing the model to abstain when uncertain.
- The paper provides detailed theoretical analysis and extensive experiments on multiple datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, Food-101, etc.) and two architectures.
- Socrates Loss uses a single loss as both classifier and calibrator, avoiding post‑hoc processing or additional data.
- (Minority positive – Reviewer 3) The theoretical analysis is considered detailed; Reviewer 2 called the approach “considerable.”

### Weaknesses
1. **Poor readability and presentation** (all reviewers): The main text is not concise, lacks clear explanations (e.g., \(t_{i,y_i}\) in eq. (1), the meaning of \(E(s)\) notation, “Ep=0” in Figure 1). Figures 1–3 lack legends, consistent color schemes, and sufficient annotations; axes are incomplete. Excessive use of uppercase letters (e.g., Confidence Calibration, Focal Loss) clutters text. Reviewer 4 suggests starting each paragraph with a summary and using bullet/numbered lists for claims.
2. **Unclear motivation and rationale** (R1, R3, R4): The need for an additional unknown class is not convincingly justified. Reviewer 4 notes that selective classification can be achieved without an unknown class (SR baseline) and cites Feng et al. (2023) that the best selection mechanism should be rooted in the classifier itself. The abstract claims the lack of an unknown class is a drawback of CCL-SC, but this claim is contested.
3. **Theoretical analysis is incomplete** (R1, R2):  
   - The role of \(\Delta_{\text{reg}}\) is unclear; its dependence on \(\gamma\) makes the interpretation of each term in eq. (2) unconvincing.  
   - The derivation uses the Bernoulli inequality to simplify the loss expression, but no rigorous error analysis is provided. Cumulative approximation errors in high-dimensional spaces may affect training stability and calibration.  
   - Parameters \(\gamma\), \(\alpha_{\text{dynamic}}\), and \(\alpha_{\text{momentum}}\) are introduced without sufficient mathematical justification. The difference between \(\alpha_{\text{dynamic}}\) and \(\alpha_{\text{momentum}}\) is not clearly distinguished in the text (reviewer 2 asks if they are the same).  
   - Reviewer 3 found the theoretical analysis detailed, but this contrasts with the criticisms above.
4. **Missing comparisons and baselines** (all reviewers):  
   - No comparison with post‑hoc calibration methods such as Platt Scaling or Temperature Scaling, nor with recent works like Meta‑Cal, Mix‑n‑Match, TTA‑based calibration, or Pseudo‑Calibration (R1).  
   - No baseline of cross‑entropy (CE) training + Softmax Response (SR) for selective classification (R4).  
   - The claim that CCL-SC is the *only* method combining calibration and selective classification is overly absolute; combining a differentiable calibration metric (e.g., expected calibration error) with selective classification could achieve similar goals (R4).  
   - Comparison is limited to SAT and CCL-SC; more diverse methods should be included (R3).
5. **Experimental concerns and limited impact** (R1, R2, R3, R4):  
   - Performance improvements are often minor, and the practical advantage is unclear (R2).  
   - Socrates Loss fails to generalize well on complex datasets (Food‑101), but the cause is not explored; a clear analysis of SAT’s failure and Socrates’ success on that dataset could be the main message but is absent (R1, R2).  
   - A discrepancy exists between the reported CCL-SC performance and the original paper: on CIFAR‑10 / VGG‑16, error at 100% coverage is \(6.38 \pm 0.14\) in this paper vs. \(5.97 \pm 0.11\) in the original CCL-SC paper. The error at 100% coverage is crucial for selective classification (R4).  
   - The primary aim is to merge calibration and selective classification, yet Tables 4 and 5 show that SAT and CCL-SC outperform Socrates Loss in Expected Calibration Error (ECE) on most datasets (R4).  
   - The claim that Socrates Loss improves calibration appears largely empirical; the improvement on hard datasets may be due to the Focal Loss term (e.g., \(\alpha_{\text{dynamic}}\) approaches 0 in later stages, approximating focal loss) (R4).  
   - Only VGG‑16 and ResNet‑34 are tested; stronger architectures (ViT, ResNet‑50/101, CLIP‑based) would be valuable (R3, R4). CCL-SC has been evaluated on ImageNet; this paper does not include such large-scale experiments (R4).  
   - The process of choosing hyperparameters for each baseline is not sufficiently described, raising fairness concerns (R2).  
   - The loss curve for CCL‑SC vibrates after 150 epochs (Fig. 2); the paper does not explain whether CCL‑SC is only enabled after 150 epochs, and what happens if training starts from epoch 0 or if no special loss is used. Before 150 epochs, the brown line (likely SAT) appears better than CCL‑SC (R3).  
   - The meaning of “coverage rates” and why lower coverage yields better performance is not explained (R3).  
   - What constitutes an “acceptable ECE range” is not defined (R4).  
   - It is unclear whether ad‑hoc methods have access to test samples during training (R3).  
   - How calibration with an unknown class differs from transfer learning is not addressed (R3).  
   - Observations are not sufficiently analyzed; each table/figure needs more discussion (R3).  
   - Reviewer 2 notes that a clear explanation of SAT’s failure and Socrates’ success on Food‑101 could be the main message but is absent.
