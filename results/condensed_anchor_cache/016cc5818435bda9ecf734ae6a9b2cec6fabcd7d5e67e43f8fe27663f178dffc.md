## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a framework for provable robustness against data poisoning (untargeted, targeted, backdoor) without modifying the model or algorithm. It uses convex relaxations to over-approximate the set of all reachable parameters during gradient-based training, then bounds worst-case performance or backdoor success. The method is evaluated on a regression task, dimensionality-reduced MNIST, and fine-tuning on two vision datasets, but is restricted to fully-connected networks and vanilla SGD.

### Strengths
- Proposes a novel solution to a well-studied problem (training-time attacks), closing a clear gap in the literature by applying iterative reachability analysis to SGD. The idea is natural and has not been tried before (Reviewer 2).
- Clearly distinguished from prior aggregation-based approaches (R2).
- Expands the well-established CROWN method, providing soundness rather than a completely new relaxation (R2).
- Applicable to a very general perturbation model (bounded/unbounded inputs and labels, number of poisoned samples) (R2).
- In-depth discussion of method and limitations in Section 3.4 (R2).
- Opens avenues for future work (refining bounds, generalization beyond feed-forward, enforcing consistency between steps) (R2).
- Well-structured manuscript, beginning with high-level approach then technical details (R2).
- Well-organized and easy to follow (R3).
- Provides an alternative perspective by certifying through model parameter robustness; topic is timely (R3).
- Claims novelty for bound propagation strategy and series of proofs (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Loose bounds (major concern, raised by R1 and R3).** Both reviewers question practical utility. R3 notes that the relaxation from Eq. (6) to Eqs. (8)–(9) inflates uncertainty, leading to potentially vacuous certificates. Experimentally: Fig. 1 requires very small perturbations or few poisoned points to avoid meaningless results; flipping only 4 labels yields almost void certified bounds (R3). Appendix H acknowledges bounds are extremely loose compared to real-world attacks (R3). R1 similarly asks how useful such worst-case bounds can be. In contrast, R2 views scalability as a limitation but does not treat looseness as a major weakness, calling the work a first step in a novel direction.
- **Lack of baselines.** R2 stresses that experiments would be far more informative if prior work (e.g., aggregation-based methods) were used as baselines to demonstrate where the method fills a niche in certified-accuracy/runtime space, instead of just showing verification works for small perturbations.
- **Incorrect claim about Adam applicability.** R2 points out the claim (ll.198–199) that the procedure applies to any first-order optimizer like Adam is incorrect because it requires reachability analysis of the optimizer’s internal state; method appears limited to vanilla SGD.
- **Missing implementation and insufficient experimental detail.** R2 notes no code is provided, hindering reproducibility. Even the description of hyperparameters and setup (Appendix J) is insufficient to reproduce results. R2 strongly encourages code and experiment configurations.
- **Scalability concerns.** R2 notes the method uses small-scale models and fine-tuning rather than full training from scratch; asymptotic runtime complexity suggests it is hardly scalable to real-world settings (though considered acceptable as a first step). R3 adds that restriction to fully-connected networks is a significant limitation, as modern architectures use convolutional layers; computational cost for even these networks is a concern.
- **Attack objective formulation issues (R3).** Attacker goal is maximizing training loss (Eqs. 3–5); practical relevance would be higher if targeting test loss/generalization error. Experiments should report certified and true test performance. The backdoor definition (Eq. 5) resembles adversarial attacks more than backdoor poisoning: a backdoor should preserve clean performance and ensure backdoored data accuracy. The formulation does not enforce high accuracy on unperturbed samples, leading to likely loose upper bounds (R2 echoes this). R2 also notes the definition of targeted attacks (Eq. 4) assumes a data-independent safe set S, while safety often depends on ground truth (e.g., steering direction in PilotNet).
- **Missed relevant literature (R1).** The paper overlooks influence functions (Cook, 1977–1982; Koh & Liang, 2017) and does not explain how the approach compares in computing exact influence of poisoned data. R1 questions whether this is a novelty issue. R2 adds that prior work on parameter-level verification for Bayesian NNs (Wicker et al., 2020) is mentioned but not explained why it is inapplicable (minor).
- **Missing proofs and references (R3 minor).** Some results (Theorem 3.3, B.1, Propositions 1 and 2) are neither proved nor properly referenced. Also missing is a closed-form solution to Eq. (10) for neural networks in terms of \(\epsilon, \nu, n, m\).
- **Related work relegated to appendix (R2 minor).** Appendix A is integral and should be in the main text.
- **No wall-clock time (R2 minor).** Absence of runtime specifications makes it hard to gauge practical feasibility.
- **Questions overlapping with weaknesses:**
  - R1 asks how the method compares to influence functions (see missed literature).
  - R2 asks whether Theorem 3.3 should use \(\text{SEMin}_{m+n}\) (since perturbed features and labels can be from disjoint samples) – a technical correctness concern.
  - R2 asks why CROWN was used over more recent methods like \((\alpha,\beta)\)-CROWN, MN-BAB, given computational cost – a missing ablation/comparison.
