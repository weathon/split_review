## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes an operator (matrix) stepsize for ADMM in semidefinite programming, claimed to be tune-free and computationally cheap. The authors argue that this operator stepsize can surpass the acceleration limit of standard scalar stepsizes. Reviewers 1 and 3 raise significant concerns about scalability, fairness of comparison, experimental scope, and presentation, while Reviewer 2 is more positive, highlighting the innovation and empirical speedups.

### Strengths

- **Novelty and Innovation (R1, R2, R3)**: To the best of the reviewers’ knowledge, this is the first ADMM method adopting an operator stepsize in the context of SDP. Reviewer 2 explicitly notes it is the first parameter-free operator stepsize for ADMM, which is both innovative and practical. Reviewer 3 acknowledges the paper is well-written with some experiments demonstrating performance.

- **Empirical Performance (R2)**: Reviewer 2 reports substantial speedups compared to traditional scalar stepsize choices, alleviating SDP scalability issues. Reviewer 3 qualifies this by noting that only some experiments demonstrate performance.

- **Presentation (R2, R3)**: Reviewer 2 finds the paper well-structured and easy to follow. Reviewer 3 also states it is, in general, well-written.

**Note on disagreement**: Reviewer 2 is markedly more positive (score 6) than Reviewers 1 and 3 (both score 3), particularly regarding the practical impact and the strength of empirical results.

### Weaknesses

- **Remaining Scalability Issues (R1, R3)**: The proposed method does not address the fundamental bottlenecks of SDP. As noted by R1, the per‑iteration cost is the same as interior‑point methods (IPM): inverting an \(m\times m\) matrix \(\tilde{A}\tilde{A}^T\) costs \(O(m^3)\) time and \(O(m^2)\) memory, and unlike standard ADMM this matrix is not fixed across iterations. IPM requires only \(O(\sqrt{n}\log(1/\epsilon))\) iterations, far fewer than typical ADMM. R3 adds that the algorithm still relies on orthogonal projection and linear system solving; when both the number of constraints and cone dimension are large, even a single iteration may be infeasible. R1 asks whether the method would remain efficient for SDP problems with \(m=O(n^2)\) constraints and dense \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^T\), e.g., Lyapunov stability problems.

- **Fairness of Comparison (R3)**: The operator stepsize is adaptive (changes per iteration), yet the benchmark is an optimal non‑adaptive scalar stepsize from [4]. Given the existence of popular adaptive heuristics for choosing \(\rho\) in ADMM (Boyd et al., [1]), this comparison is not fair. R1 also questions the “tune‑free” claim: the block size of the operator \(\mathcal{S}\) (e.g., size \(m\) for \(\mathbf{1}_1\in\mathbb{S}^m\)) still requires tuning; the authors only test \(m=n-1\) on two problems, and generalization to other SDPs is unverified.

- **Insufficient Experiments (R1, R2, R3)**: The tested benchmarks are restricted to relatively simple synthetic SDP problems of moderate dimension, with \(m=O(n)\) constraints and a structure where \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^T\) is diagonal and easy to invert. R2 suggests a comparison with other state‑of‑the‑art SDP algorithms to assess overall competitiveness. R3 recommends experiments on problems with both large constraint number and cone dimension, as well as real large‑scale SDP instances.

- **Missing Analysis and Explanations (R2, R3)**: Reviewer 2 requests:
  - Additional explanation for Equation (3.2) (deriving the fixed‑point view from (2.6) is not intuitive).
  - A clear definition of variable \(\xi\) before Lemma 3.1.
  - A formal computational complexity analysis.
  - Question (also weakness): If the block size in \(\mathcal{S}\) is set to \(n\), does the method reduce to the original ADMM?

- **Presentation Issues and Notation Problems (R1, R3)**:
  - Confusing notation: \(m\) is used both for the size of the block \(\mathbf{1}_1\in\mathbb{S}^m\) in the operator \(\mathcal{S}\) and for the number of constraints in the standard dual SDP (R1).
  - The matrix \(\mathcal{S}\) has a principal submatrix that is all‑one and not positive definite (Equation 2.3). How is its inverse computed? Does the convergence guarantee of ADMM still hold? (R3)
  - Inconsistencies: dimension error (line 240), inconsistent use of \(z\) and \(Z\) (line 158), incorrect primal problem sense (line 112), inconsistent styles for minimize/arg min (line 250), undefined Frobenius norm (line 301), inconsistent figure styles (line 420).
  - Minor errors and omissions: line 81 grammar (“A solution is assumed exists”), line 102 grammar (“SDP include”), line 130 operator \(\mathcal{A}^*\) inconsistent with SDP literature, line 32 Ellipsoid method not first polynomial‑time algorithm for LP (Khachiyan’s algorithm is), line 51 review of first‑order methods for SDP is not comprehensive (e.g., missing spectral bundle method and Frank‑Wolfe).
