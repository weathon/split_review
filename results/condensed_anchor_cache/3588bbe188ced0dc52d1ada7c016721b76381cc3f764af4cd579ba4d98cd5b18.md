## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces the Foundation Acoustic model Backdoor (FAB) attack, which injects backdoors into acoustic foundation models (AFMs) using physically realizable, input-agnostic, sync-free triggers (e.g., sirens, barks). The attacker operates under a weak threat model—no knowledge of pre-training data, limited architectural details—and aims to degrade downstream task performance while preserving benign accuracy. Extensive experiments on HuBERT and WavLM across multiple tasks, triggers, and defenses (fine-pruning, input filtering) demonstrate the attack’s effectiveness in digital and physical settings.

### Strengths
- Exploring security issues in acoustic foundation models is an important and intriguing topic (Reviewer 2).
- The proposed FAB attack is task-agnostic and physically realizable, using inconspicuous, input-agnostic triggers that do not require synchronization with the audio input (Reviewer 1).
- Comprehensive evaluation across multiple AFMs (HuBERT, WavLM), tasks, triggers, and defenses (fine-pruning, input filtering) shows the attack’s effectiveness and robustness (Reviewers 1, 3). One reviewer (3) explicitly notes experimental results verify effectiveness.
- The attack addresses realistic attacker constraints: no access to pre-training data, no knowledge of downstream tasks. The method approximates missing parameters and uses pseudo-labeling to maintain benign performance (Reviewer 4).
- Language is mostly accessible (Reviewer 3, though other reviewers disagree—see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty**: Fine-tuning-based backdoor injection is not new, and FAB requires auxiliary data with a distribution similar to the target AFM (Reviewer 1). The core technical challenges are similar to prior attacks on foundation models (e.g., Badencoder, Adversarial Illusions); the paper does not clearly differentiate beyond the audio domain (Reviewer 2).
- **Unrealistic threat model**: Major AFM providers are unlikely to embed backdoors that degrade performance (Reviewer 1). The chosen triggers (sirens, oboes, flutes, barks) are prominent and easily detectable, raising questions about stealth—why not use noise-like triggers (white noise, ultrasound) that are more covert? (Reviewers 1, 3). The threat model also lacks clarity on whether the attacker knows the downstream task’s encoder architecture (Reviewer 2).
- **Limited evaluation scope**: Only HuBERT and WavLM are tested; generalizability to other prominent AFMs (wav2vec 2.0, Data2Vec) is not assessed (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing baseline comparisons**: No comparison with existing attacks (e.g., Badencoder, Adversarial Illusions) that could be adapted to the acoustic setting (Reviewer 2). The unique challenges in attacking AFMs are not justified, and the paper does not explain why previous techniques cannot be directly applied (Reviewer 2).
- **Incomplete ablation and robustness analysis**:
  - The paper assumes users will fine-tune only the last layer or use weighted sum of representations; it does not verify whether the backdoor remains robust after full fine-tuning (the “non-useful” representation may drift) (Reviewer 4).
  - The attacker is assumed to know the provider’s training procedure, which may not be publicly available; subtle differences in hyperparameters could undermine backdoor injection (Reviewer 4).
- **Outdated defenses**: The defenses tested (fine-pruning, input filtering) are not specific to self-supervised learning backdoors. More recent SSL-specific methods should be considered (e.g., SSL-Cleanse, Detecting Backdoors in Pre-Trained Encoders) (Reviewer 4). One reviewer (2) also notes a potential defense using conditional diffusion models to denoise audio, which is not discussed.
- **Missing audio quality evaluation**: The paper does not evaluate whether the trigger affects perceived audio quality (e.g., ViSQOL). Such metrics are needed to confirm the trigger remains inconspicuous, as noticeable quality degradation could alert users (Reviewer 1).
- **Poor writing and presentation**: The methods section (Section 4) is redundant and tedious, lacking mathematical details for the loss functions (L_back, L_benign) (Reviewers 2, 3). The threat model and technical explanation are difficult to follow (Reviewer 2).
- **Minority reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (3) gave a higher score (5) and found the language mostly accessible and experimental results convincing, but still raised the same concerns about trigger conspicuousness and writing. This contrasts with the other reviewers’ ratings (3,3,3) and more critical assessment of novelty and practicality.
