## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes NRGBoost, an energy-based generative boosting algorithm that models unnormalized densities for tabular data using gradient boosted decision trees (GBDTs). It extends GBDT with a local second-order approximation of the log-likelihood at each boosting iteration, analogous to XGBoost. To reduce training cost, an amortized sampling approach is introduced using a fixed-size sample pool with rejection sampling. The paper also explores bagged ensembles of DET as a generative counterpart to Random Forest. Experiments cover both discriminative and generative tasks (including data synthesis and density evaluation), showing NRGBoost can approach the discriminative performance of standard GBDTs and compete with neural network based generative models, while also providing density estimates.

### Strengths
1. **Solid formulation, proof, and demonstration** (R1): The derivation from GBDT to generative NRGBoost is natural, clear, and convincing. The amortized sampling approach is reasonably designed using properties of boosting. Experiments include repeated trials, statistical tests, and visualization analysis, with complete theoretical and technical appendix including computational time comparisons.
2. **Wide application scenarios** (R1): NRGBoost applies to both discriminative and generative tasks, distinguishing it from neural-based generative baselines.
3. **Novel and well-motivated method** (R2, R4): The boosting framework for density estimation is novel and provides refreshing insights. Incorporating rejection sampling within the boosting process is an excellent contribution that makes energy-based tabular modeling practical (R4).
4. **Clear presentation and extensive overview** (R2): The paper is well-written and easy to follow. The overview of existing tree-based generative models is extensive and contextualizes the work well. (Similarly, R1: "clear and convincing"; R4: "well-written.")
5. **Good experimental setup** (R2): Experiments have error bars and competitive baselines. The derivations are explained well with useful appendix details.
6. **Impressive generation results on downsampled MNIST** (R4): Results are convincing that NRGBoost goes beyond current state-of-the-art (ForestFlow) in generation on certain settings, while also offering density estimation. Figure 2 provides helpful insight into how the method progressively models the dataset over boosting iterations.
7. **Reference-worthy exploration of tree-based models** (R1): The paper explores generative extensions in both boosting- and bagging-based tree models, providing a reference for future research.

**Note on disagreement**: Reviewer 3 found no strengths, stating the paper is confusing and hard to follow. However, other reviewers (R1, R2, R4) consistently praise clarity and formulation.

### Weaknesses
1. **Performance gap with discriminative GBDTs** (R1): In discriminative tasks (Table 1), the gap between NRGBoost and traditional GBDTs (e.g., XGBoost) becomes more significant as data scale increases, leaving room for improvement before application in large-scale classification. (Also asked: Why does this gap exist, and is it possible to outperform discriminative baselines from the generative paradigm? – R1)
2. **Computational scalability concerns** (R1, R2): From computational effort analysis (Figure 4), NRGBoost is not computation-economical on large datasets compared to neural-network-based generative models. Data scalability is insufficiently discussed (R1). The overhead due to sampling is never quantified, making it hard to judge if performance gains are “worth it” (R2).
3. **Missing large regression and diverse datasets** (R1): The evaluated datasets (Table 5) lack large regression datasets. Discriminative performance on large regression (e.g., Microsoft, Yahoo from [1]), large-feature (Epsilon), and large-class (ALOI) datasets is not shown. (R1 question: How does NRGBoost perform on these?)
4. **Missing comparisons with deep learning generative models** (R2, R4): The abstract claims comparison with neural network models, but such baselines are missing from the main paper experiments. No DL methods have been included, so there is no evidence that DL-based generative models (e.g., normalizing flows with tractable densities) would not outperform tree-based generative models (R2). Additionally, relevant works TabEBM [4] (energy-based with TabPFN) and UnmaskingTrees [5] (GBDTs for generation and density via autoregression) are absent, making novelty and relative performance difficult to assess (R4).
5. **Unclear use case and density tractability advantage** (R2): The paper emphasizes tractability of computing densities, but no experiments illustrate the importance of this feature. The conclusion hints at applications but does not provide concrete use cases; other competitive methods exist. (Also asked: Is there a concrete use case where NRGBoost is the only/best model? – R2)
6. **Nonstandard tabular benchmarks** (R4): The tabular datasets for Section 6.2 are nonstandard; most recent papers use the TabCSDI [1] benchmark (6 datasets) or ForestFlow [2] benchmark (27 datasets). Using own datasets opens possibility of cherry-picking and makes comparison with literature difficult.
7. **Evaluation metric and baseline choices for conditional inference** (R4): In Section 6.1 (single-variable inference), using R^2 is strange compared to CRPS, RMSE, MAE as in [3]. ForestFlow supports conditioning on covariates—generating conditional samples and computing the mean would be a more appropriate baseline to evaluate conditional density quality, not just a point estimate.
8. **Clarity and writing issues** (R3, and some R4 minor): Reviewer 3 found the paper poorly written, incoherent, and with inconsistent experiments. Specific issues raised:
   - Vague terms: “mid-range consumer CPU” (Line 88), “the best generative models” (Line 89) (R3).
   - Abstract says “tabular data” but experiments include vision data (downsampled MNIST). Vision datasets differ from tabular data as images have spatial relationships. The authors’ references only use flattened MNIST, which discards spatial structure (R3).
   - Figure 2: unclear – sample size, radius, hyperparameters chosen are not explained; no description of data generation process or distribution (R3).
   - Equations appear incoherent or possibly wrong: Equation 2 is never used later; Equations 3 and 4 derivation lacks clarity (e.g., q_f appears without justification). The claim about Taylor expansion invariance and zero expectation under q_f is not explained. How maximizing Equation 5 improves f_t, how f_t is defined, and how the size of H_t in Equation 6 impacts the solution are unclear (R3).
   - Table 1: authors claim to bold the best performing algorithms but bold their own algorithm even when it does not perform best (R3). Also, mixing discriminative and generative models in one table is confusing (R3). It is unclear how the estimated unnormalized density is converted into class conditional posteriors p(y|x) for discrimination – no explanation is given (R3).
   - Bad structure: Related work appears at Section 5 (near the end) rather than early in the paper (R3). Authors later moved it but did not mention in rebuttal (R3). There is no flow diagram or pseudocode for the algorithm (R3).
   - Minor readability issues: R4 suggests splitting a long sentence and replacing “because even if” construction for clarity.

   **Note on disagreement on clarity**: Reviewer 3 strongly criticizes clarity/writing, while R1, R2, and R4 describe the paper as well-written. This is a significant divergence among reviewers.

9. **DEF section seems out of place** (R4): The methods section and experiments devoted to DET/DEF appear unrelated to NRGBoost, and DET performs worse. Suggest omitting it.

10. **Inference time not compared** (R1 question): Inference time comparison between NRGBoost and other baselines is missing.

11. **Missing NGBoost results in Table 1** (R4): Even though NGBoost does not support nonparametric probabilistic prediction, seeing its results would still be helpful.

12. **Tradeoffs with DET/DEF not quantified** (R2 question): Can tradeoffs of NRGBoost vs. DET/DEF be quantified?
