## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies feature learning in a two-layer fully-connected neural network after one gradient descent step on the first layer. By using a learning rate that scales with the sample size \(n\) as \(\eta = n^\alpha\) with \(\alpha \in (0,1/2)\), the feature matrix develops multiple separated singular values (“spikes”) whose singular vectors align with Hermite polynomials of the target function. The authors characterize the phase transitions in the spike spectrum as a function of \(\alpha\) and derive asymptotic training and test errors in the proportional limit \(n, d, N \to \infty\). The results generalize the constant-step-size analysis of Ba et al. (2022) to larger step sizes and provide a concrete description of nonlinear feature learning.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-organized, well-written, and clearly presents a technically demanding analysis (R1, R3).
- It generalizes the result of Ba et al. (2022) from a constant GD step size to step sizes scaling as \(n^\alpha\) (\(\alpha \in (0,1/2)\)), offering a more comprehensive understanding of how step size affects feature learning (R1, R2, R3).
- The identification of a sequence of phase transitions, where a spike corresponding to a degree-\(\ell\) polynomial appears when \(\alpha^2 > 1 - 1/\ell\), is a novel and clean finding that may motivate random matrix theory research (R2).
- The analysis goes beyond the NTK (lazy training) regime and provides precise, asymptotic formulas for the improvement in training loss due to nonlinear features (R4).
- The paper makes a good contribution to understanding the learning dynamics of nonlinear features, with concrete improvements over prior work (R3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Lack of generalization/test error guarantees**  
   The theoretical results focus on the training loss; the analysis does not prove test error improvement. Only an empirical example (Figure 3) for a target with information exponent \(s=1\) is shown, and the theory itself suggests that test error cannot improve when \(s>1\) (R2). R1 notes that overfitting and the bias-variance trade-off are not discussed, and R4 emphasizes that a decrease in training loss alone is not surprising and that generalization is more critical. This gap should be explicitly acknowledged and discussed in the main text (R2).

2. **Insufficient simulations and empirical validation**  
   - The experiments only consider linear and quadratic target functions; no simulations for higher-order feature learning are provided, even though Theorem 4.1 predicts such features (R1, R4).  
   - The staircase phenomenon predicted by the theory is not shown in simulations (Figure 3 Right is missing) (R1).  
   - Only one activation function is used; different activations should be tested for robustness (R1).  
   - No experiments on real-world data or deeper networks are included (R4).  
   - The way figures are presented is unclear: in Figure 2 it is not explained how the crosses (claimed theoretical predictions) are computed (R1 Q4, R2 Q1). In Figure 3 it is unclear whether the solid lines correspond to analytic predictions (and if so, what causes the fluctuations) (R2 Q2), and the thresholds for \(\alpha\) are not marked (R1 Q11).  
   - The total number of training steps for different learning rates in Figure 3 is not specified, making the comparison potentially unfair (R3).

3. **Strong and unrealistic assumptions**  
   - **Input data**: The Gaussian distribution assumption is strong and does not hold for many real-world datasets (R4).  
   - **Activation functions**: Condition 2.3 (bounded first three derivatives) and Condition 2.4 (nonzero first Hermite coefficient \(c_1 \neq 0\)) are restrictive. The paper does not give concrete examples of common activation functions that satisfy both, or discuss the implications of using activations that violate them (R4). Moreover, Theorem 3.3 assumes polynomial activation, which may contradict Condition 2.3 (R1 Q3).  
   - **Initialization**: The uniform-on-sphere initialization differs from the Gaussian initialization used in Ba et al. (2022), with no justification for the change (R1 Q2).  
   - **Learning rate scaling**: The requirement \(\eta = n^\alpha\) is not scalable to large datasets; such large step sizes are not used in practice (R3).

4. **Main text only shows training loss results for \(\ell=1,2\)**  
   The central results on training loss (Theorems 4.4 and 4.5) are restricted to \(\ell = 1,2\); the general-\(\ell\) case is relegated to Appendix L. This makes it difficult to assess the generality of the theory (R4). The paper should include the general case in the main text (R4, R1 Q10).

5. **Dependence on Conjecture 4.3**  
   Theorems 4.4 and 4.5 rely on Conjecture 4.3, which is not well justified. The authors should explain why existing techniques (e.g., from Hu&Lu 2023 or Ba et al. 2022) cannot prove this conjecture and what the specific technical hurdles are (R1).

6. **Missing limitations discussion**  
   The paper lacks a limitations section that explicitly addresses the scope of the assumptions, the absence of generalization guarantees, the unrealistic learning rate scaling, and the reliance on a conjecture (R1).

7. **Insufficient clarity on the novel technical contribution**  
   The mathematical novelty is not highlighted in the main text; the long proofs make it hard to discern where the key difficulties and new techniques lie. The authors should emphasize the specific tools and why they are non-trivial (R4).

8. **Several smaller issues that affect interpretation**  
   - The effect of the constant \(c_\star\) (magnitude of the teacher function) on sample complexity and convergence rate is not discussed (R4).  
   - Values of training and test errors are given without baseline measures (e.g., best linear model prediction risk), making it hard to assess the significance of the improvement (R4 Q2, R1 Q11).  
   - The critical regime \(\alpha = (\ell-1)/(2\ell)\) is not discussed (R1 Q5).  
   - The definition of \(c_{>1}\) below equation (4) incorrectly includes \(c_1\) (R1 Q8).  
   - Notation errors: \(\mathbf{w}_i\) is used before definition (R1 Q7); \(\mathbf{R}_0\) has a typo (R1 Q12).  
   - Lemma B.1 may require \(\|a\| = \|b\| = 1\) (R1 Q13).  
   - The claim that the sum of spikes has rank exactly \(\ell\) (Theorem 3.4) is not justified—linear independence of \(( \tilde{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\odot k}\) for different \(k\) is not obvious (R1 Q15).  
   - The formula in Appendix L includes \(c_{\*,0}\), which does not appear for \(\ell=1,2\) in (5) and (6); the term \(M\) in the summation is undefined (R1 Q16).  
   - It is unclear how Figure 2 changes as the number of training iterations increases (R4 Q3).  
   - The connection to other feature learning frameworks (e.g., Zhenmei et al. 2022) is not clarified (R4 Q1).
