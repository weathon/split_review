## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes PaPaGei-P and PaPaGei-S, the first open foundation models for PPG signals, pre-trained on 57,000 hours (20 million unlabeled segments) from three public datasets. PaPaGei-P uses a patient-contrastive objective (maximizing agreement between embeddings from the same subject); PaPaGei-S uses a morphology-aware contrastive objective (maximizing agreement between signal features across patients). The models are evaluated on 20 tasks across 10 datasets covering cardiovascular health, sleep disorders, pregnancy monitoring, and wellbeing, and compared against time-series foundation models (Chronos, Moment), a PPG-specific model (REGLE), and SSL methods (SimCLR, BYOL, TF-C). Ablation studies, skin tone analysis, model scaling, and case studies are included.

### Strengths
- **First open foundation model for PPG** – trained on public datasets, with open-source code and model weights, enabling reproducibility and further research (Reviewers 1,3,4).
- **Well-organized and technically detailed** – extensive description of data preprocessing, architecture, training, and evaluation; clear tables and figures; thorough ablation studies (Reviewer 1,3,4).
- **Practical implications** – framework applicable to cardiovascular monitoring and multiple health tasks (Reviewer 1).
- **Skin tone bias analysis** – establishes a benchmark for evaluating fairness in PPG models (Reviewers 2,4).
- **Novel PPG-specific loss functions** – incorporates physiologically-informed losses (SQI, sVRI, IPA) during pre-training, not reported in earlier work (Reviewer 4).
- **Strong clarity and presentation** – well-written, easy to follow, with helpful appendices (Reviewers 3,4).
- **Significant for health research** – open foundation models can accelerate use of PPG for health applications (Reviewer 4).
- **Model size efficiency** – outperforms models 70× larger, with best performance at 5M parameters (Reviewer 1,4).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited technical novelty** – all components (architecture, augmentations, features) are established; only the integrated loss appears new, but without clear motivational evidence. Extracting morphology features (sVRI, IPA) limits pre-training to clean datasets (e.g., VitalDB collected during surgery) and hinders generalization to noisy settings like DaLiA. (Reviewer 2)
- **Strong resemblance to prior closed-source PPG foundation model [1]** – similar methodology (loss, positive pairs, random augmentations) and evaluation across multiple health targets. The paper’s language understates this relationship; no direct comparison table with [1] on performance, dataset size, devices, or model choices. No evaluation of demographic targets (age, sex, BMI) that were reported in [1] and are missing here. (Reviewer 3)
- **Missing comparisons with task-specific supervised models** – absence of comparisons against state-of-the-art models for blood pressure estimation, sleep apnea detection, etc., makes it unclear whether the SSL approach yields better representations than highly optimized supervised solutions. (Reviewer 1,2)
- **Inappropriate baseline model** – the RF model using basic signal statistics (mean, median, percentiles) is a weak baseline. The signals are z-scored, so mean is zero and provides no information. A demographics-only predictor (age, sex, BMI, HR up to quadratic terms) outperforms PaPaGei on several downstream tasks: DBP (MAE 8.07 vs PaPaGei-S 8.71), SBP (MAE 13.56 vs all reported models), Hypertension (ROC AUC 0.78 vs all reported models), Mood Disorder (ROC AUC 63 vs all reported models). This suggests the models may rely on demographic shortcuts rather than PPG-specific features. (Reviewer 4)
- **Overlapping confidence intervals and lack of significance testing** – in Table 3, many confidence intervals overlap; e.g., for “smoker” it is unclear if PaPaGei-P is significantly better than Chronos. No p-values or significance tests are provided for any comparison (Tables 3,4; Figures 4,5,6). (Reviewer 3,4)
- **Unfair comparisons with SSL methods** – BYOL has 12M parameters vs PaPaGei’s 5M; authors state that smaller models are better for PPG data, so other SSL methods with smaller encoders might perform equally or better. Implementation details (positive pairs, augmentations, batch size, learning rate, temperature) are not specified for SSL baselines. (Reviewer 2,3)
- **Statistical features perform nearly as well as 5M model** – on several tasks, simple statistics (mean, median, min, max, percentiles) with RF have overlapping confidence intervals with PaPaGei, questioning the added value of the 5M-parameter neural network. (Reviewer 2)
- **Missing reporting of F1 scores and R²** – classification tasks likely suffer from class imbalance; AUROC alone can be misleading. Regression tasks lack R² values, limiting understanding of goodness-of-fit. (Reviewer 1)
- **Flawed claim about augmentations** – the statement that PaPaGei-S avoids augmentations that alter PPG morphology but uses Gaussian noise is contradictory, as Gaussian noise nonlinearly alters phase and morphs the waveform. (Reviewer 2)
- **Misleading claim about patient-wise contrastive learning** – “PAPAGEI-P … maximizes agreement between signals from the same subject” is already proposed in prior work (CLOCS, ICML 2021); language needs revision. (Reviewer 2)
- **Poor evidence for modeling distribution tails** – Figure 8a for AHI regression shows a regression slope much less than 1.0, indicating over-prediction for low true AHI and under-prediction for high true AHI, i.e., significant model bias. (Reviewer 4)
- **Lack of PPG-specific augmentation exploration** – augmentations are taken from inertial measurement literature; no evaluation of augmentations designed for PPG signals. (Reviewer 2)
- **No evaluation of models smaller than 5M** – the ablation on model size uses only three sizes (5M, 22M, 35M); monotonicity cannot be established, and smaller sizes (e.g., 0.07M like REGLE) are not tested. (Reviewer 4)
- **Missing discussion on why combining three pre-training datasets does not improve performance** – it is surprising that MESA alone performs best despite having the fewest subjects but most segments. Authors should hypothesize. (Reviewer 3)
- **No significance test for component ablation (Figure 5a/b)** – it is unclear whether removing sVRI loss significantly degrades performance. An appendix table summarizing performance changes across all downstream tasks would help. (Reviewer 4)
- **Skin tone analysis language unclear** – “no significant differences across all models” needs precise explanation and confidence intervals as whiskers in Figure 9. (Reviewer 4)
- **Missing train/validation loss curves** – would help understand convergence stability. (Reviewer 1)
- **Specific questions that overlap with criticisms**:
    - How can the model’s performance be improved on tasks with class imbalance? (Reviewer 1)
    - Do all out-of-domain datasets use different devices from pre-training? (Reviewer 1)
    - Have other biases (sex, age, weight, health conditions) been explored? (Reviewer 1,3,4)
    - Check the code for DaLiA processing and evaluation (Reviewer 2)
    - Why were PPG-specific augmentations not explored? (Reviewer 2)
    - Provide a table comparing dataset and training choices with prior work [1] (Reviewer 3)
    - Provide p-values for all comparisons (Reviewer 3)
    - Were models smaller than 5M evaluated? (Reviewer 4)
    - Why does combining datasets not improve performance? (Reviewer 3)
    - Why does PaPaGei-S (morphology-aware) outperform PaPaGei-P (patient-contrastive)? Investigate segment quality effects. (Reviewer 3)
