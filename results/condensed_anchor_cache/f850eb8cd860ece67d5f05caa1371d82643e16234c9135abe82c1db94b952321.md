## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies memorization in LLMs, focusing on high-complexity sequences encountered only once during training. It finds that memorization of such sequences is stable across training (contrary to expectation of catastrophic forgetting) and introduces the concept of “latent memorization”: sequences that do not appear memorized immediately after first exposure can later be surfaced via simple modifications such as adding random noise to model weights. The authors propose a diagnostic test based on cross-entropy loss to identify latent memorized sequences, and discuss implications for data privacy.

### Strengths
- **Important problem and fresh perspective.** Multiple reviewers agree the paper addresses a significant privacy concern and adds a new dimension to understanding LLM memorization. The concept of latent memorization is novel and highlights a previously overlooked failure mode.
- **Careful metric design.** Reviewers commend the relaxation of Carlini et al.’s memorization definition using Levenshtein distance and the focus on string complexity (zlib compression) to isolate true memorization from in-context pattern completion.
- **Innovative methodology.** The use of weight perturbations to reveal latent memories is considered clever and strengthens the claims. The mechanistic approach to arrive at this method is appreciated.
- **Intuitive diagnostic.** The cross-entropy loss based test for latent memorization is praised as practical and potentially generalizable to standard memorization.
- **Clarity and presentation.** The writing and visualizations are clear, making complex ideas accessible (noted by R1 and R3; R4 disagrees – see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited generalizability.** Multiple reviewers (R2, R3, R4) note the study is restricted to Pythia-1B and Amber-7B. R2 and R3 question generalizability to larger models and different architectures (e.g., those with heavier attention). R4 adds that all Pythia sizes could have been evaluated to establish trends.
- **Stationarity claim unsubstantiated.** R4 strongly criticizes the claim that memorization is stationary across training (Section 3.2). Figure 3 axes are unlabelled, the mean line is unclear, and no trend is visible at the sample level. The comparison with a random walk is not directly measured. R1 also asks about the token window size for the stationarity analysis (Fig 2a) and how many tokens a once-encountered string can survive. One reviewer (R4) states the main claim is unsupported.
- **Mechanistic explanation insufficient.** R1 points out that if a single 64-character string is encoded in weights, the odds of that small parameter fraction being disturbed are small, weakening the rationale for why training noise would activate latent memories. R3 notes the explanation lacks detail on which weights or layers store the sequences, and contrasts with Huang et al.’s finding that verbatim memory relies on distributed states rather than specific weights. R3 requests a more thorough mechanistic account.
- **Definition of latent memorization is weak or circular.** R4 states the concept is poorly defined and sometimes circular (e.g., samples recovered from an intermediate checkpoint are latent memorized, but also the other way around). R1 suggests the entire paper could be read as a critique of existing definitions, and asks why the kl-LD definition should still be preferred if latent memorization is also memorization.
- **Limited experimental scope on complexity.** R1 questions whether the claim that “simpler strings are more easily memorized” (Section 3.1) holds given that (a) it may be an artifact of early checkpoints allowing in-context learning, and (b) the kl-LD definition misses latent memorization. R4 notes experiments only cover highly complex sequences (zlib>0.8) and asks why lower complexity levels (like Figure 1) were not ablated.
- **Only syntactical memorization measured.** R4 points out that only edit distance (syntactical) is used; semantic memorization (e.g., via BERTScore) is not explored.
- **Weight perturbation method is not truly random.** R4 strongly objects to the claim that adding random noise mimics training effects, because the noise is applied to the training/chosen dataset, making it a guided guess-and-check zeroth-order optimization. This contradicts the hypothesis that simply perturbing weights with random noise produces similar effects as training.
- **Missing ablations for recovering latent strings.** R1 asks whether prompt hacking (varying the “k” part of the context) could also surface latent strings, not just weight perturbations. If not, that would be stronger evidence for the weight-encoding hypothesis.
- **Results described as anecdotal.** R4 finds Table 2 and line 305 anecdotal, and states that overall the results are unsubstantiated. R4 also notes the writing is unclear and difficult to parse (contrasting with other reviewers’ praise for clarity – this disagreement should be preserved).
- **Interaction with fine-tuning not studied.** R2 asks whether fine-tuning would mitigate latent memories.
- **Potential training-stage effect.** R1 notes that when the sequence is encountered (early vs. late in training) likely matters; overtrained models may have less capacity to retain such rare strings. This is not explored.
