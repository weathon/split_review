## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies adversarial attack strategies in cooperative multi-agent multi-armed bandits (CMA2B). In the homogeneous setting (all agents share the same arm set), the authors propose attack strategies that, by targeting just one agent, convince all agents to select a target suboptimal arm \(T-o(T)\) times while incurring \(o(T)\) attack costs under CO-UCB and other algorithms. In the heterogeneous setting (agents have distinct arm subsets), they prove a target arm attack requires linear costs and propose strategies (AAS, TAS) to force a maximum number of agents to suffer linear regrets while incurring sublinear costs by manipulating only a few target agents. Numerical experiments validate the proposed strategies.

### Strengths
1. **Well-motivated and clearly written**: The setting of adversarial attacks on cooperating bandits is well-motivated (R1, R3). The paper is well-written with clear notation, clear assumptions, a thorough review of existing literature, and helpful intuitive examples (R2, R4). Taking the model as given, the paper studies a reasonable set of questions and the contributions are sound (R4).

2. **Novel problem and analysis**: This is the first work to study adversarial attacks in cooperative multi-agent bandits (R5). The attack target for the heterogeneous setting is novel compared to single-agent bandits (R3). The result that \(o(T)\) corruptions can cause a target suboptimal arm to be selected \(T-o(T)\) times by targeting just one agent in homogeneous settings has novelty (R1). The detailed analysis of heuristics AAS and TAS shows that a large number of agents can be attacked using sublinear corruptions, which is valuable (R1). The design of AAS, TAS, and the proof of Theorem 3 are innovative and nontrivial (R5).

3. **Rigorous theoretical analysis**: The theoretical analysis is rigorous with clear proofs supporting the claims (R2, R3). The analysis covers both homogeneous and heterogeneous settings and different CMA2B algorithms (at least in the homogeneous setting) (R1). The impossibility results in the heterogeneous setting are particularly insightful, suggesting that for a goal-oriented adversary it is quite difficult to achieve their target without incurring high cost (R4). The analysis of the case when rankings are unknown to the attacker makes the attack more practical (R1).

4. **Experimental evidence**: The authors provide experimental evidence for the novelty of AAS and TAS (R1).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty for the homogeneous setting**: The homogeneous setting under CO-UCB is very similar to the results for UCB obtained in Jun et al. [2018], as every agent sees every other agent's rewards, so it effectively functions like a single-agent setting with \(M\) samples per round instead of one; the results can be modified from Jun et al. [2018] (R1). The attack strategy seems quite similar to that in Zuo [2024], with the proof being a straightforward extension from single-agent to multi-agent; the authors do not highlight any significant additional challenges, making it unclear how significant the technical contributions are (R5). The paper lacks discussion about the novelty and major technical challenges for various results (e.g., Theorem 1, Proposition 1 and 2), making it difficult to judge the significance of the contribution (R5). In the homogeneous setting where observations are shared and all agents are homogeneous, it is unsurprising that attacking one agent could misguide the rest, making the cost independent of \(M\) (R5).

2. **Restrictive and unrealistic assumptions limit practical relevance**: (a) No collision model is assumed, ignoring collisions when multiple players select the same arm simultaneously, which is standard in most MMAB studies including Boursier & Perchet [2019] and is particularly relevant in applications such as cognitive networks (R2). (b) The attacker can observe pre-attack rewards for all agents, which seems unrealistic in practice; it remains unclear how an attacker would access this information (R2). (c) In the heterogeneous setting, the attacker knows each agent's local arm set, which seems impractical (R2). (d) The attacker knows each agent's exact algorithm, which is the strongest and most limiting assumption; this could be plausible if the attacker were an agent within the CMA2B setup, but targeting a specific algorithm limits broader impact (R2). (e) The attacker knows the exact leader in the leader-follower structure (R2). (f) The attacker has prior knowledge of the reward ranking of all arms; while relaxed in Section 4.3, the attack cost becomes much larger at \(O(K\log T / \Delta_{\min}^2)\) (R2, R3). These assumptions, especially in the heterogeneous setting which is the main contribution, are highly restrictive and make the practical relevance doubtful (R2, R4). The purely adversarial, almost zero-sum type attacker model is questionable—under what context could such an attacker be expected? (R4)

3. **Proofs are incomplete, inaccurate, or require significant reader effort**: (a) In Theorem 1, "our attack strategy" is ambiguous because only vague descriptions are given and no clear mathematical definition of the attack is provided in the main text; the reader must go to the appendix (R5). (b) In Theorem 2 (page 16), the claim that "\(f\) is submodular and the greedy algorithm in Algorithm 1 gives a \((1-1/e)\)-approximation solution" is inaccurate—the paper needs to additionally prove the function is monotone and non-negative, and must cite the relevant result (R5). (c) In Theorem 3 (also Theorem 4), \(\Delta(k,k+1)\) is not defined rigorously, as the paper has not established that \(K \notin K_0\); if \(K \in K_0\), \(\Delta(K,K+1)\) is undefined. Equation (3) does not include \(T_0\); none of the equations on that page contain \(T_0\). The definition of \(g(k)\) is missing, which affects subsequent discussion (R5). (d) The results against general algorithms need refinement of assumptions. Line 1308 (Page 25) assumes that since samples from other arms always come from a distribution with mean less than the target arm, we can consider arm \(K\) to be the best arm—but if the sample distribution for other arms is changing with \(t\) (due to the attack), Assumption 2 cannot be directly claimed. In Assumption 1, it is unclear what it means that "agents maintain identical empirical means for making decisions"; the assumption may need to be refined to a more restricted class of MAP/CMA2B algorithms like mean-based algorithms (R1).

4. **Limited scope of algorithms studied**: Most results are on UCB-style algorithms or the communication model where all agents immediately share information with all others—this is a fairly limited setting because samples are effectively shared easily, making the attack easy as any attacked value gets propagated to all agents (R1). What happens with constrained communication structures, e.g., graph-structured communication as in Landgren et al. [2016]? (R1). For heterogeneous settings, an attack is only designed for one algorithm (CO-UCB). For single-agent bandits, designing attacks for basic algorithms like UCB and \(\epsilon\)-greedy is acceptable, but in the multi-agent setting, it is unclear whether an attack on CO-UCB provides insight into attacks on other algorithms—is CO-UCB as fundamental for heterogeneous multi-agent bandits as UCB is for single-agent? (R3)

5. **Experiments are limited and lack statistical rigor**: The experiments provided are fairly limited with no error bars or confidence intervals, which undermines the claim that "Both LTA and OA result in the most significant average regrets" (R1, R5). Figure 3a is unclear—what is "average regret" and why does it remain constant? The only regret defined in the paper is cumulative regret. It is also unclear how OA and LTA algorithms are performed without AAS (R3).

6. **Clarity and presentation issues**: (a) The work assumes the reader possesses extensive technical knowledge about adversarial attacks in bandits; the term "attack value" is never formally defined in the main text, and the idea behind equation (2) is unclear (R3). Parameters in Theorem 1 (\(\alpha, \Delta_0\)) are undefined (R3). (b) Table 1 is misleading—for the first two lines, it is unclear whether knowledge of the time horizon \(T\) is needed; more importantly, the third line assumes oracle knowledge of arm rankings (Theorem 3), a strong assumption, while when not using this assumption the attack cost becomes \(O(K\log T/\Delta_{\min}^2)\) (Theorem 4) (R3). (c) \( \text{UCB}_t(k) \) is introduced on page 9 line 463 without definition, and there is no reference to this quantity elsewhere in the text (R5). (d) Line 6 of Algorithm 1 uses \(\omega(k)\) without definition (R5). (e) On page 7, line 327, the phrase "is it feasible to attack this group using only a limited number of agents" is unclear—what does "using a limited number of agents" mean? (R5). (f) On page 5, line 268, the stated goal is to "leverage sublinear attack costs to misguide the maximum number of agents," but why Algorithm 3 leads to "attacking the maximum number of agents" is neither justified nor adequately explained (R5). (g) The problem could be motivated better—the introduction mentions advertisers sharing information about users, but it is unclear how practical this is given privacy requirements; more examples, references, or additional motivation would be helpful (R3). (h) Minor writing issues: line 47 "hope" should be "hopes"; line 68 "remain uncertain" is confusing since the point is addressed; line 75 "remains an unresolved issue" similar; line 101 "with its cost analysis" should be "and analyze its cost"; line 131 "observes" is confusing for pre-attack value; line 139 "collision" should be "collision model"; lines 143-152 should explain the choice for the heterogeneous model or cite works using it; lines 191,423 "no-regret" should be "sublinear regret"; line 352-361 "attack value" and \(\hat{\mu}\) never formally defined; line 362 "constant fulfills" should be "constant that fulfills"; line 424 "affected" should be "target"; line 449 "unknown" should be "known"; line 484 "when" should be "at"; line 519 "Leaning" should be "Learning"; line 707 should be \(-\hat{\mu}(K)\) instead of \(+\hat{\mu}(K)\) (R1, R3).

7. **The heterogeneity characterization is incomplete**: The attack itself technically resembles techniques from previous works (e.g., equation (2)) (R3). The difficulty of adversarial attacks on a heterogeneous multi-agent system is not fully characterized—no lower bound is provided to show how good the \(O(K\log T/\Delta_{\min}^2)\) result is (R3). It is unclear whether the target agent group used is minimal, or if fewer target agents could achieve the same effect (R3). The second claimed contribution that "linear costs are necessary to fool all agents into suffering linear regrets" appears inaccurate—this seems straightforward because the target arm attack might not be a suitable objective in a heterogeneous environment; a more accurate contribution would be to propose an appropriate "attacking goal" specifically tailored to the heterogeneous setting (R5).

8. **Inaccurate claim of being "first"**: The paper claims (line 111-112) to be "the first to study how an attacker may manipulate multi-agent cooperative learning," but ICML'20 (Boursier et al., 2020), Mobihoc'21, and AAMAS'22 already study similar problems of attacking cooperative bandit problems (R5).

9. **Potential detectability**: If agents know the distribution family of rewards, the attacker injecting strategically chosen errors might tip the agents off that something is amiss since the samples may not reflect the distribution family they know; there might be reasonable constraints to add on what can be injected (R4).

10. **\(T_0\) term is difficult to understand**: The \(T_0\) term in the heterogeneous results is confusing, and it is unclear how it is always a constant (R1).
