## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Quack, an automated framework for generating jailbreak prompts against LLMs using role-playing with four LLM agents: a Materializer, Organizer, Referee, and Advisor. It collects seed jailbreaks from the internet, builds a knowledge graph, and iteratively refines prompts by inserting a target question. The method is evaluated on healthcare-related questions across Vicuna-13B, LongChat-7B, LLaMa-7B, and ChatGPT. Reviewers agree the problem is relevant and the automatic approach is useful, but disagree on execution. One reviewer is positive overall (score 5), praising the comprehensive experiments and automatic testing, while two reviewers are critical (scores 3) citing major concerns about evaluation validity, method clarity, presentation quality, missing baselines, and limited domain scope.

### Strengths
- Automatic testing and optimization of jailbreak prompts is very useful for AI safety and red teaming, and the framework allows easy customization to different unsafe domains/questions.
- The paper provides a comprehensive experiment showing analysis of the iterative optimization process and results across multiple models and versions (Vicuna-13B, LongChat-7B, LLaMa-7B, ChatGPT).  
- The authors collected many existing jailbreak prompts and applied knowledge graphs to summarize a paradigm of eight characteristics.
- Quack uses multiple LLMs as agents, each with a specific role, enabling automatic generation of new jailbreak prompts without human manual design.
- The problem setting (jailbreaking LLMs) is new and relevant to modern ML, and evaluating a range of models demonstrates the potential of the method.
- The focus on the healthcare domain is understudied in the jailbreaking literature; the paper contributes insights into generating jailbreaks for this domain.

### Weaknesses
**Evaluation validity and fairness:**
- Baseline comparison is weak: baseline methods use all 78 jailbreak prompts collected from the internet rather than a subset of top jailbreak prompts. It is unclear if this comparison is fair, especially given the example in the supplementary (ibuprofen) where the original model did not refuse and the jailbreak produced misinformation but the oracle answer was already direct. The baseline question may not have included instructions for not following guidelines.
- The criteria for a successful jailbreak is based on a similarity score to oracle answers. It is not clear whether this conforms with human judgment (e.g., a response that differs from the oracle may not be considered a successful jailbreak by a human).
- Only healthcare questions are tested. The method claims applicability to other domains, but no evidence is provided. The paper should test on common jailbreak domains used in the literature (e.g., AdvBench from GCG paper).
- Three questions for filtering valid jailbreak scenarios (Sec 3.3) may not be sufficient.

**Missing comparisons and ablations:**
- Missing comparisons with recent automatic jailbreak baselines: GCG (Zou et al., 2023) and AutoDAN (Zhu et al., 2023). It is unknown whether Quack outperforms these methods.
- No ablation studies on the method design. For example, is a random walk in the knowledge graph necessary compared to random replacement? Are all four roles (e.g., Materializer) helpful?  
- The paper should test on Llama 2 based models (especially Llama 2 Chat with RLHF) to prove effectiveness against stronger safety alignment, since the open-source models used (Vicuna, LongChat, LLaMa) have limited safety alignment.

**Clarity and precision of method description:**
- The method is not described in detail. It is unclear how questions are inserted into scenarios (the ⊕ operation is not formally defined), how the four LLM roles interact, how success is determined, and how iteration/updating works. An algorithm or mathematical description is needed.
- The paper uses vague personifications (e.g., “Then the Advisor provides advice to the Organizer to reduce the score”). This makes reproduction difficult.
- Concepts like “role-playing,” “scenarios,” “wrong answer,” “fair suggestion,” “oracle,” “quasi-test,” and “concentration of jailbreaks” are undefined or misleading. The term “testing” is overused; the paper essentially proposes a jailbreaking method, not a testing framework.
- Notation is non-standard (e.g., $\mathcal{F}^\mathcal{Q}$ instead of $\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{Q})$, calligraphic letters without reason, $\oplus$ undefined).
- The claim that a negative response (e.g., “I’m sorry…”) is a “fair” suggestion is confusing. What makes it fair? The term “oracle” implies ground truth, which is not present in jailbreaking.

**Hyperbolic and imprecise language:**
- The paper uses self-praising adjectives such as “ingenious,” “meticulous” (three times in one paragraph), “adept,” and “innovative.” Such language should be avoided; it is for the community to judge value.
- The related work section inaccurately distinguishes between adversarial examples and jailbreaking; cited papers (e.g., Zou et al., 2023) are about jailbreaking, not AEs.

**Copy editing and grammatical errors:**
- The title contains an error: should be “automatically” not “automatic.”
- Numerous typos and grammatical mistakes throughout (e.g., “Large Language Models” capitalized incorrectly, “world-wide” should be “worldwide”, “mainstream” misused, “conducted alignment” incorrect, “model release policies… have been released” redundancy, “trials and errors” should be “trial and error”).  
- These errors force reviewers to spend significantly more time reading and detract from the scientific content.

**Specific reviewer questions that overlap with weaknesses:**
- Sec 3.3: How many of the 78 jailbreak prompts are considered effective after evaluation against the three unsafe questions? Are all 78 used for the knowledge graph or just the effective subset?
- Sec 3.4: How are weights assigned in the knowledge graph? Is this different from sampling random content for each characteristic category?
- Sec 4.4: The OpenAI libraries mentioned (0.3.0, 0.11.0, 0.20.0) were released before ChatGPT’s launch (Nov 2022) and do not support the ChatGPT API. Please clarify which models correspond to these versions in Table 4, and provide links to the model snapshots.
- Could the authors include more testing questions with oracle and jailbreak answers to help readers understand the kind of questions tested?
- Can the authors release the healthcare dataset used and evaluate on a general jailbreak benchmark like AdvBench?
- How can the Materializer guarantee oracle answers when the question contains harmful information, given that models like Llama2 and ChatGPT may refuse to answer?
- What does “invalid” jailbreak mean in the abstract? What are “quasi-tests” in the introduction?
