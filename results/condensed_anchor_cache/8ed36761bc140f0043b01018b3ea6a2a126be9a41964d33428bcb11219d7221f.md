## Merged Review

### Summary

Highway-BP is a parallelizable iterative algorithm that approximates backpropagation in neural networks with residual connections. It decomposes the gradient into a sum over all paths through the architecture, where each path passes through some number of residual blocks. By omitting terms that contain more than \(k\) expensive (non-residual) components and computing parallel gradient estimates, the method trades accuracy for speed. Empirical results on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and Wikitext103 show that \(k \leq 5\) often achieves near-backpropagation accuracy while yielding reported speedups of 2–4× over standard backpropagation. The algorithm is adaptable to ResNets, Transformers, and RNNs.

### Strengths

1. **Importance of the problem.** Accelerating training in large deep learning models is a critically important challenge (Reviewers 1, 4, 5).
2. **Novelty and originality of the idea.** The decomposition of the gradient into path-based terms and the iterative, parallel approximation is novel and intuitive (Reviewers 1, 4, 5).
3. **Clear presentation.** The mathematical derivation is well-structured and easy to follow. The training dynamics section is nicely presented (Reviewers 1, 4).
4. **Theoretical foundation.** The method is grounded in mathematical theorems and a clear derivation from the gradient decomposition (Reviewer 2).
5. **Architectural flexibility.** Highway-BP is explicitly designed for and tested on ResNets, Transformers, and RNNs, and the authors argue it can be adapted to any model with residual-like connections (Reviewers 2, 3, 5).
6. **Promising initial empirical results.** On three benchmarks (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, Wikitext103), the method achieves accuracy close to backpropagation with \(k \leq 5\), and the paper shows a consistent tradeoff frontier between speed and accuracy controlled by \(k\) (Reviewers 1, 3, 5).
7. **Reported speedups of 2–4×.** The algorithm’s parallel design is demonstrated to yield significant improvements in backward-pass wall-clock time (Reviewers 2, 5).
8. **Straightforward implementation.** The authors address practical implementation details and note that the method does not require exotic hardware changes (Reviewer 4).
9. **Extensive benchmark coverage.** The experiments cover image classification and language modeling tasks with comparisons to standard backpropagation and, for RNNs, fixed-point iteration (Reviewer 5).

### Weaknesses

1. **Limited experimental scale.** All experiments are conducted on relatively small datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, Wikitext103). There is no evaluation on large-scale datasets such as ImageNet-1K or on large autoregressive language models (e.g., LLaMA-scale). It is unclear whether \(k \leq 5\) would suffice for more complex tasks, and whether the method would maintain its advantage when memory and bandwidth constraints dominate (Reviewers 1, 4, 5). One reviewer (R5) notes that the true cost of AI is inference, not training, so trading performance for training speed may not be desirable.
2. **Far-below-expected accuracy on chosen architectures.** On CIFAR-100, the 50-layer ResNet variant used achieves only about 40% accuracy, whereas a standard ResNet50 would exceed 85% and even a ResNet20 would obtain 70–75%. The paper does not justify why such low-performing architectures were chosen and whether the proposed method’s relative performance would hold for better-trained models (Reviewer 4).
3. **Acceleration limited to the backward pass.** Backpropagation consists of forward pass, backward pass, and parameter update. The backward pass accounts for roughly one third of total time. Even a large speedup there may yield only modest overall training time reduction, especially on hardware where the backward pass is not the dominant bottleneck (Reviewer 1).
4. **No real-world speedup numbers.** The paper does not report measured end-to-end training time reductions on standard hardware. A less optimized but standard backpropagation implementation (e.g., on CPU) could have been compared. The time overhead reported in Table 3 shows that with \(k=20\) the method approaches vanilla backpropagation time, suggesting that additional read/write operations and the CumSumProd computation may negate speedups for deeper or larger models (Reviewers 3, 4).
5. **Memory consumption not analyzed.** Highway-BP requires storing additional intermediate tensors for the iterative approximation, but the paper provides no memory footprint analysis, no scaling of these tensors with network depth or input size, and no comparison with backpropagation’s memory usage. This overhead could be prohibitive for deep networks or high-resolution inputs (Reviewer 3).
6. **Hyperparameter \(k\) without guidance.** The method introduces the number of iterations \(k\) as a critical hyperparameter. No systematic procedure or heuristic is given for selecting \(k\) on a new task, which limits practical usability (Reviewers 2, 4).
7. **Missing baselines from recent optimization literature.** The comparison includes only standard backpropagation and fixed-point iteration. Other state-of-the-art accelerated-gradient or sampling-based methods are not compared, such as:
   - Huang et al. “Towards Green AI in Fine-tuning Large Language Models via Adaptive Backpropagation” (2023)
   - Wang et al. “Efficient Backpropagation with Variance-Controlled Adaptive Sampling” (2024)
   - Yang et al. “Efficient low-rank backpropagation for vision transformer adaptation” (2024)
   (Reviewer 2)
8. **No evaluation on certain modern architectures.** The authors claim generality but do not test Graph Neural Networks, Spiking Neural Networks, or Mamba (Reviewer 2).
9. **No theoretical guarantees.** There is no analysis of how approximation error varies with \(k\), no proofs for convergence or optimality, and no lower bound on the improvement over backpropagation. The paper lacks a rigorous time and space complexity analysis (Reviewers 2, 3).
10. **Unclear effectiveness late in training.** Figure 3 suggests that Highway-BP’s approximation is effective primarily in the early stages of training; by late training, the approximation may degrade. Accuracy results in Figure 2 should be presented at multiple time points to verify that final performance is not merely a result of prolonged recovery (Reviewers 3, 4). Relatedly, no experiments with varying \(k\) over a schedule (e.g., increasing \(k\) during training and finishing with true backpropagation) are presented (Reviewer 5).
11. **Questions about integration with distributed training.** The paper does not discuss or test how Highway-BP interacts with data parallelism, model parallelism, or distributed communication overhead. The added compute and memory may hinder standard parallelism strategies (Reviewers 2, 3).
12. **Exaggerated or imprecise language in the introduction.** Several phrases are vague or misleading:
   - Line 36: “transformers only defer the problem” – unclear what problem is deferred.
   - Line 41: “often involve trade-offs between speed and task performance” – a truism.
   - Line 42: “leverage the recent layers” – unclear.
   - Line 45: “significantly improves optimization” – imprecise.
   - Line 51: “derived from an original derivation” – redundant.
   - Line 92–93: “While this seems attractive, the reality differs” – could be more precise. The comment about “computing the Jacobian matrix of all layers” is misleading because Highway-BP also requires this computation.
   (Reviewer 4)
13. **Typographical and formatting errors.**
   - Line 192: “provie” should be “provide”.
   - Line 231: “\(K\)” should be “\(k\)”.
   (Reviewer 4)
14. **Question about equation (8).** The transition from equation (6) to (8) is unclear: why does \(w_i^0\) disappear? Also, the right-hand side of (8) appears inconsistent with the middle part: \(v_i^{k+1}\) is multiplied by \(K_{i+1}\) in the middle but not on the right. These need clarification (Reviewer 1).
15. **Open questions on method’s core design.** 
   - Why are intermediate losses necessary for the theoretical framework? 
   - Why is the algorithm restricted to generative and classification tasks (line 153)? 
   - Can the method be applied to tasks where the loss does not decompose in the same way?
   (Reviewers 4, 5)
16. **Minor reviewer disagreement on overall significance.** Scores range from 6 to 8. The lower-scoring reviewers (6) question generalizability beyond small benchmarks and the overall impact given the backward-pass-only acceleration, while the higher-scoring reviewers (8) are more optimistic about the method’s potential and originality. This unresolved disagreement underscores the need for stronger experimental validation.
