## Merged Review

### Summary

The four reviewers differ in overall assessment. Reviewer 1 (score 5, confidence 4) is more positive, praising the integration of facial embeddings with a unit-based vocoder and the thorough experiments, though still raising concerns about novelty and presentation. The other three reviewers (each score 3, confidences 4, 4, 5) are less enthusiastic: they find the work lacks sufficient novelty (many ideas already explored), misses crucial subjective human evaluation (MOS), has limited practical scope (very short generated speech), and exhibits unclear zero-shot claims. All reviewers agree on several presentation issues and point to missing comparisons or analyses.

### Strengths

- **Effective speaker identity preservation and domain gap mitigation** (Reviewer 1): Integrating facial embeddings into a unit-based vocoder helps preserve speaker characteristics and addresses the domain gap between visual frontend and vocoder without fine-tuning. The experiments and analysis are thorough.

- **Interesting and challenging problem** (Reviewer 2): The video‑to‑speech task is important, and conditioning on speaker identity from the visual modality is a simple yet effective idea, supported by results and example demonstrations.

- **Useful reproducibility** (Reviewer 2): Public datasets are used, and the authors commit to releasing code and models.

- **Complements previous work** (Reviewer 3): The paper provides a comprehensive supplementary exploration and further demonstrates conclusions from earlier studies.

- **Extensive experiments and ablations** (Reviewer 4): The paper includes many experimental results with ablation studies, and the model shows robustness in zero‑shot scenarios (though this claim is challenged; see Weaknesses).

*Minority more positive view*: Reviewer 1 (score 5) is notably more favorable than the other reviewers (all score 3). While Reviewer 1 acknowledges limitations, they regard the integration as effective and the experiments as thorough, in contrast to the stronger novelty and evaluation criticisms from the other reviewers.

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty is limited or incremental** (Reviewers 1, 3, 4):  
  - Extracting speaker‑identity information from faces for voice synthesis is not new (e.g., VisualVoice, Face2Speech, DiffV2S [CVPR 2023], VisageSynTalk [ECCV 2022]) and is insufficient novelty for ICLR.  
  - Using acoustic discrete units for speech reconstruction has been proposed in ReVISE (CVPR 2023).  
  - The proposed FARV largely extends Unit‑HiFiGAN by simply adding the existing image encoder FaRL (CVPR 2022); the combination is incremental.  
  - Reviewer 4 asks the authors to clarify what specific innovations their approach offers beyond combining existing components.

- **Missing subjective human evaluation** (Reviewers 2, 3):  
  - No Mean Opinion Score (MOS) listening test is provided, even though human listeners are highly sensitive to audio‑visual discrepancies and objective metrics can miss artifacts.  
  - NISQA‑MOS is primarily a metric for audio quality, not speaker similarity (Reviewer 3; see Table 2). For a speech‑synthesis task, subjective ratings are crucial.

- **Very short generated utterances** (Reviewer 2):  
  - The model produces only ~1–2 s snippets of speech; sentences are often incomplete and truncated. How well speaker characteristics are preserved over longer sequences (e.g., >2 s) is not addressed. If longer utterances were formed by concatenating short ones, would there be artifacts at boundaries? This concern directly affects the soundness score (Reviewer 2 gave soundness 2).

- **Zero‑shot claims are unsubstantiated or contradictory** (Reviewers 3, 4):  
  - The results in Table 6 show only coarse‑grained gender and general emotional reconstruction on unseen samples, suggesting a lack of clear mapping between facial images and timbre. Reviewer 3 argues the model essentially uses image embeddings as in‑domain speaker embeddings, which does not enable true zero‑shot application.  
  - Reviewer 4 questions how zero‑shot performance on LRS2‑BBC (Table 3) is claimed if the model was trained on that same dataset. The authors should clarify the training/evaluation split.

- **Mixed or underwhelming objective results** (Reviewer 1):  
  - Despite using multiple pretrained models (AV‑HuBERT, HuBERT, FaRL), performance improvements are not significant; some metrics even underperform baselines (e.g., WER on LRS3, LSE‑D on LRS2).  
  - Mel‑based vocoders after fine‑tuning show significantly better performance (Tables 1, 5).

- **Unclear or missing experiments and comparisons** (Reviewers 2, 3, 4):  
  - No comparison of image‑based speaker embeddings with traditional speaker embeddings to demonstrate whether facial images convey additional information beyond speaker identity (Reviewer 3).  
  - The vocoder should be tested with different frontend encoders; the purpose of Section 4.3.2 (“V2S frontend prediction”) is unclear (Reviewer 4).  
  - The analysis for the decline in NISQA‑MOS of Unit‑HiFiGAN when fine‑tuned (Figure 2) is not convincing—other metrics improve—and lacks discussion of trade‑offs (Reviewer 4).  
  - Reviewer 2 asks why the stopping points for training/fine‑tuning were chosen and whether there is a convergence guarantee.

- **Technical concerns and unanswered questions** (Reviewers 2, 4):  
  - Why use HuBERT to extract acoustic units instead of AV‑HuBERT, which has multimodal capabilities and might learn better unified units? (Reviewer 1 also asks this.)  
  - Equation (1) adds static image embeddings to each element of the sequence of unit embeddings; given different units/embeddings, does simple addition make sense? (Reviewer 2).  
  - Figure 1: Are predicted units used only during inference or also in training? Reviewer 1 observes that Eqs. for L_CE and L_1 suggest they are used (f(x_v) on right‑hand side). Reviewer 2 asks the same.  
  - Reviewer 2’s biggest concern: the model almost perfectly matches ground‑truth sequences word‑for‑word, despite the well‑known ambiguity of visual speech (e.g., velar stops, voiced/voiceless distinctions). How does it always predict the correct unit without longer context? (This may reflect a misunderstanding of the task but is raised as a serious concern.)  
  - In Table 3, why is the NISQA‑MOS score for Unit‑HiFiGAN so high in the zero‑shot scenario? (Reviewer 1)  
  - What does “(Choi et al., 2023a)” after every WER performance in Table 1 mean? (Reviewer 4)  
  - Are all speakers in the inference stage different from the training set for Table 1? (Reviewer 4)

- **Presentation and clarity issues** (Revision 1, 2):  
  - Introduction paragraph (Lines 32‑38) is unclear, contradicts itself, and uses the vague phrase “the generalization between synthesis stages”.  
  - Figure 1 is unclear: the Generator G structure is not detailed; the two‑stage training is not shown; AV‑HuBERT is frozen but lacks a freeze symbol; the lips and face inputs appear to come from different speakers (lips without beard, face with beard).  
  - Equations on page 4 have incorrect comma punctuation.  
  - Line 210 is missing a verb.  
  - Quote marks in Lines 447 and 476 face the wrong direction.  
  - Table 1 highlights best/second‑best systems but ignores methods that use textual information; they appear in the list but are excluded from the ranking (Reviewer 2 nitpick).  
  - The paper has a few typos and would benefit from a careful read‑through (Reviewer 2).
