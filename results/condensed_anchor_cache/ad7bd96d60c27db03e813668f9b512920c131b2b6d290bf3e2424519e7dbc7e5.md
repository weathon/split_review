## Merged Review

### Summary

DenoiseVAE is a 3D molecular pre-training method that employs a learnable, atom-specific noise generation strategy, replacing the common hand-crafted shared noise distributions. The model is structured as a variational autoencoder: a Noise Generator produces per-atom Gaussian noise distributions (diagonal covariance) for each molecule, and a Denoising Module predicts the added noise. The denoising objective and a KL regularization term encourage physically reasonable noise, and the overall training objective is proven to maximize the ELBO of the log-likelihood. The method aims to better capture force field information by adapting to the anisotropic nature of potential energy surfaces. Experiments on QM9 and MD17 show state-of-the-art performance on molecular property prediction tasks, and the learned noise patterns correlate with atomic reactivity and rigidity, offering interpretability.

### Strengths

- **Novel adaptive noise generation.** The learnable, molecule‑specific per‑atom noise distribution is a novel and physically reasonable setup compared to prior hand‑crafted uniform noise (R1, R2, R3, R4). This allows the model to respect structural and chemical differences across molecules.
- **Comprehensive theoretical grounding.** The authors derive the pre‑training objective as maximizing the evidence lower bound (ELBO) of the log‑likelihood, aligning with quantum chemistry principles (R2, R3, R4). The derivation based on potential energy surfaces offers theoretical insight.
- **Consistent state‑of‑the‑art empirical results.** DenoiseVAE outperforms existing denoising methods across all tested benchmarks (QM9, MD17) for molecular property prediction (R2, R3, R4). The improvements are consistent and the model is parameter‑efficient (R2).
- **Interpretability and physical relevance.** The learned noise patterns correspond well with atomic reactivity and rigidity, providing physical interpretability (R2, R4). The KL regularization encourages diverse conformations while preventing low noise intensity (R4).
- **Significance for conformational sampling.** The method provides an efficient way to sample conformations around a given geometry, which is valuable for quantum chemical simulations (R1). The noise generator is well‑presented with clear methodology (R1).

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of clarity on downstream task usage.** Multiple reviewers note that the paper does not clearly explain how DenoiseVAE is used for downstream property prediction. Missing details include: the architecture of the task‑specific prediction head (e.g., for force prediction), whether training is end‑to‑end fine‑tuning or feature extraction, what features (pooled EGNN outputs or latent embedding) are used, and how the generated noise distributions are leveraged after pre‑training (R1, R2). The tables are misleading – they mention “force prediction” but the 12 tasks in Table 1 are not force‑related, and the values in Table 2 appear more energy‑related (R1).
- **Insufficient training and computational analysis.** The paper omits a breakdown of the computational overhead from the Noise Generator, how training time compares to baselines, and how it scales with molecular size and dataset complexity (R3). There is no analysis of training stability (no loss curves across datasets/ seeds) or sensitivity to initialization and optimization settings (R3). The pre‑training dataset (PCQM4Mv2) is mentioned only briefly without explaining its significance or how it aids learning atom‑specific distributions (R1). Systematic ablations on dataset size or cross‑molecule‑size generalization are missing (R3).
- **Physical approximations may limit precision.** One reviewer (R4, rating 6, confidence 5) strongly criticizes that the method relies on classical approximations (Boltzmann distribution, harmonic potential, force‑field formalism) that neglect quantum mechanical effects (e.g., exchange correlation, anharmonicity). The reviewer argues this is fundamentally inadequate for precise atomic‑level computations, especially for systems with significant quantum effects. Other reviewers (R2, R3, rating 8) consider the approach a reasonable and effective approximation; this disagreement is not addressed in the paper.
- **Missing direct physical validation.** There is no benchmark of the learned noise distributions against first‑principles (e.g., DFT) simulations to confirm that the generated noises correspond to physically correct conformational ensembles (R1). The paper also does not visualize the learned energy landscape (e.g., for MD17) to demonstrate physical plausibility (R2).
- **Unanswered methodological questions.** The effect of using full‑covariance (non‑diagonal) Gaussian noise is not explored (R3). The handling of global degrees of freedom (rotations) is not discussed (R4). The range and practical extent of the learned coordinate distributions (e.g., whether they are confined to a narrow region) remain unclear (R4). Whether the model can be effectively transferred from small to large molecules (e.g., proteins) is not evaluated (R3).
