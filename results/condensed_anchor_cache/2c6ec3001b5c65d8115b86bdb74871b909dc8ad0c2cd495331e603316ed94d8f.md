## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes USLNet, an unsupervised sign language translation and generation model trained entirely from non-parallel text and video data. It uses three components: masked seq2seq text reconstruction, video reconstruction via VQ-VAE with a GPT-style decoder, and cross-modality back-translation (text-video-text and video-text-video). A sliding window aligner is introduced to handle length and feature dimension mismatch between text and video. Experiments on BOBSL and OpenASL are reported.

### Strengths
- The idea is sound and addresses an important problem: the scarcity of parallel sign language data makes unsupervised methods valuable (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- To the best of reviewers’ knowledge, this is the first bi-directional (translation and generation) sign language approach trained in an unsupervised manner (Reviewers 1, 3).
- The back-translation strategies (text→video→text and video→text→video) are novel in the sign language community (Reviewer 4).
- The paper includes detailed ablation studies and sensitivity analysis contrasting different algorithmic choices (Reviewers 2, 4).
- Reasonable comparisons to supervised baselines (e.g., Albanie 2021) and a comparison on the WMT 2022 sign language translation task (Table 6) are provided (Reviewer 2).
- The overall writing quality is good, with detailed formulation and figures (Reviewer 1). The method is generally well-described, and the motivation (leveraging non-parallel data) is clear.

### Weaknesses
- **Overall performance is very low** – BLEU-4 scores are around 0.2 on BOBSL, while supervised SOTA on the same dataset (Sincan 2023) exceeds 2 (Reviewers 2, 3). This raises questions about whether the system is “working” in a practical sense (Reviewer 3). The paper lacks in-depth analysis of why results are so poor (e.g., oracle experiments) or how the problem can be simplified to better understand paths toward improvement (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing experiments on standard benchmarks** – The model is only evaluated on BOBSL and OpenASL. Reviewers strongly recommend experiments on Phoenix‑2014T, CSL‑Daily, and/or YoutubeASL to provide more data points and allow comparison with the many existing state-of-the-art results on those datasets (Reviewers 3, 4). One reviewer noted that after rebuttal, the authors showed a benefit from using the approach as a pretraining step, but still lacked such additional benchmark evaluations (Reviewer 3).
- **Insufficient evaluation of generated video quality** – The paper provides almost no visual results in the main manuscript and only limited results in the supplementary. There is no quantitative evaluation of video fidelity with standard metrics such as FID or LPIPS (Reviewer 1). The visual quality shown in the appendix is low, likely too low to capture important hand or face information (Reviewer 2).
- **Issues with the sliding window aligner** – The assumption that text and video are monotonically aligned is questionable; in sign language, only video and glosses have a monotonic relationship. This may be a factual error in the aligner’s design (Reviewer 4). The description of the aligner is also difficult to follow (Reviewers 1, 4). No comparison to existing text-to-video aligners (e.g., the phoneme-to-pose aligner used in text2video) is provided (Reviewer 1).
- **Limited comparisons and missing discussion** – The baseline from Albanie 2021 is the only method for quantitative comparison, yet no detailed explanation of its differences and similarities to USLNet is given (Reviewer 1). The relation of the back‑translation procedure to cycle consistency in unpaired image‑to‑image translation or dual learning is not elaborated (Reviewers 1, 4).
- **Questionable assumption about word order** – The paper cites Sutton‑Spence & Woll to claim that translated sign language follows English word order, but this is not validated on the specific datasets used (Reviewer 2).
- **Lack of standard practices to boost performance** – The model trains exclusively on BOBSL; using pretrained LLMs for the text encoder or higher‑resolution / key‑point‑based (e.g., SMPL) video inputs might capture more nuance (Reviewer 2). One reviewer expected the unsupervised pretraining to be followed by supervised fine‑tuning to demonstrate benefit, similar to MMTLB (Chen et al. 2022) (Reviewer 4). The paper does not frame the approach as a pretraining method with a final supervised step (Reviewer 3).
- **Minor formatting issue** – Equations 10–12 are typeset inconsistently with the rest of the paper (Reviewer 1).
