## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper presents LLM-TSE, a target speaker extraction (TSE) method that uses text prompts encoded by a large language model (LLaMA 2) as extraction cues. The text input describes speaker properties (e.g., gender, language, loudness, distance, transcription snippets). The text embedding is fused with an optional pre-registered voiceprint embedding to generate a mask for a standard encoder‑mask‑decoder extractor. Prompts are generated from templates expanded by ChatGPT‑3.5‑Turbo. Experiments on simulated datasets show competitive SI‑SDR using text alone and new state‑of‑the‑art when text and voiceprints are combined, compared primarily with TD‑SpeakerBeam and two other models. Reviewers disagree strongly on novelty and experimental scope (scores range from 3 to 8).

### Strengths
- The paper is well written, clear, and easy to follow. (Reviewers 1, 4)
- The method is innovative in that it enables natural language interaction to guide target speaker extraction, moving beyond fixed voiceprints. (Reviewer 1: “innovative which enables the interaction of text prompt and target speaker extraction”; Reviewer 2: “novel paradigm”, “significant advancement”)
- Text prompts provide enhanced flexibility: they can be used alone without pre‑registered voiceprints, incorporate a wide range of perceptual cues through natural language, and can direct extraction or suppression of a speaker. (Reviewer 2)
- Achieves improved separation performance (SI‑SDR) compared to the baseline TD‑SpeakerBeam. (Reviewers 1, 3; note that one positive reviewer claims SOTA, while others are less convinced about the comparison scope.)
- Leveraging a large language model (LLM) allows the framework to handle flexible instructions and rich semantic information from natural language descriptions. (Reviewers 2, 3)
- Text cues can provide complementary contextual information that enhances robustness when enrollment conditions differ from test conditions. (Reviewer 2)
- Broadens applicability to scenarios where pre‑registered voiceprints are unavailable, thus addressing privacy concerns and reducing dependency on voiceprints. (Reviewer 2)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited baselines and missing comparisons (multiple reviewers):**  
  - The paper compares with only two other models (TD‑SpeakerBeam and one more), making the claim of “state‑of‑the‑art” a stretch. (Reviewer 2)  
  - Text‑guided TSE falls under multimodal speech extraction; existing multimodal methods that use text, image, or sound modalities as cues (e.g., Ochiai et al., Interspeech 2022 – “universal sound selector”; Ohishi et al., ACMMM 2022 – “ConceptBeam”) are not cited or compared, yet they have similar architectures and could be adapted to text. (Reviewers 1, 4)  
  - Because the proposed method itself is essentially a simple baseline (feature embedding injection), a direct comparison with a simpler baseline (e.g., one‑hot representations of the same attributes) is needed to demonstrate the specific benefit of the LLM. (Reviewers 3, 4)

- **Marginal technical novelty (multiple reviewers):**  
  - Using auxiliary embeddings to generate a mask is common practice (e.g., Liu et al. 2022; Oishi et al. 2023). The integration of text via concatenated embeddings is a natural extension, not a fundamental new design. (Reviewer 3)  
  - If the prompts are restricted to a handful of template‑based attributes (gender, language, loudness, etc.), such information could be provided as one‑hot vectors, raising the question of what the LLM truly adds beyond a more complex encoding. (Reviewer 3)  
  - The overall network structure (Figure 3) is nearly identical to the universal sound selector [Ochiai+ Interspeech2022]; no special tricks for handling text are introduced. (Reviewer 4)

- **Limited and impractical cue design:**  
  - The text prompts used (gender, language, far‑near, loudness) are not typical cues for real‑world cocktail‑party scenarios. When all speakers share the same gender or speak the same language, those cues become useless – the task becomes either trivial (if speakers differ on those attributes) or unsolvable (if they do not). A more practical and challenging set of attributes is needed. (Reviewer 1)  
  - The framework cannot handle more complex perceptual cues (pitch, emotion, timbre, age, topic of conversation) and is constrained to simple, predefined attributes. (Reviewer 2)

- **Evaluation concerns:**  
  - Experiments are performed only on simulated data; performance on real‑world noisy conditions with multiple concurrent speakers is not evaluated. A real‑world test set (e.g., ICASSP 2023 Deep Noise Suppression Challenge) should be used. (Reviewer 2)  
  - No psychoacoustic analysis related to human auditory perception is provided. (Reviewer 2)  
  - The ablation study (Table 1) compares variants (audio+text, text‑only, audio‑only) but does not explain how the model handles missing modalities during inference given the concatenated embeddings. (Reviewer 3)

- **Conceptual and practical limitations:**  
  - The method focuses on extracting a single target speaker only, not multiple speakers. (Reviewer 2)  
  - Large language models have high computational cost, and their outputs can be brittle, biased, or unreliable – robustness to such issues is not verified. (Reviewer 2)  
  - There is no analysis of the effect of using a pre‑trained speaker embedding module (e.g., d‑vector) for the audio cue encoder, which is a natural alternative to the proposed text‑only or combined approach. (Reviewer 1)

- **Unanswered technical questions (from ablation and design):**  
  - In the ablation: if the model is trained with both modalities, how is the absence of one modality (e.g., text‑only or audio‑only) handled at inference? (Reviewer 3)  
  - What is the specific difference between the audio‑only variant of LLM‑TSE and the existing TD‑SpeakerBeam? (Reviewer 3)
