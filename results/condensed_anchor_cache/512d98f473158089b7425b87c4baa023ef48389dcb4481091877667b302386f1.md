## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a multi-layer sparse autoencoder (MLSAE) trained on residual stream activation vectors from all layers of a transformer, and analyzes how latents activate across layers using Pythia models of different sizes. The main finding is that individual latents are often active at a single layer for a given token, but the layer can differ across tokens; the variance of activation distributions over layers is about two orders of magnitude greater when aggregating over tokens than for a single token. The paper also examines the effect of model size, SAE sparsity, and width, and applies tuned-lens transformations as a preprocessing step.

### Strengths
- **Novelty and relevance**: Training a single SAE on activations from multiple layers is a novel contribution to an important and understudied problem in mechanistic interpretability (R1, R4). Using TunedLens as preprocessing is also novel (R4).
- **Sound experimental setup**: Experiments use Pythia models of varying sizes (70M–1.4B), explore different expansion factors and sparsity levels, and include ablations with and without tuned lens, making the work reproducible (R1).
- **Clear writing**: The paper is well-written, understandable, and anticipates design questions (e.g., Section 5) (R1, R2, R4).
- **Quantitative analysis**: Provides analysis of fraction and variance of latents across model sizes and layers (R3).
- **Relevant research direction**: The problem of studying SAE latents across layers is important for AI safety and understanding transformer representations (R2, R4).

### Weaknesses
*Note: One reviewer (R1) was notably more positive (score 8, raised from 6 after discussion) but still raised several of the same concerns as the other reviewers (scores 6). R3 had lower confidence (2).*

- **Lack of direct comparison to existing SAEs**: No quantitative or qualitative comparison to standard single-layer SAEs trained independently per layer. Multiple reviewers call for: comparing MLSAE to single-layer SAEs on the same layer (e.g., reconstruction error, cosine similarity of latent representations), and evaluating whether MLSAE provides additional value over applying a single-layer SAE across all layers (R1, R4). R1 considers this the biggest weakness; R4 notes that standard SAE quality metrics (variance explained, cross-entropy loss when inserted into the model) are absent, making it unclear if the MLSAEs are decent.
- **Limited evaluation scope**: Only tested on Pythia models and top‑k SAE architecture; other model families or SAE variants (e.g., Gated SAEs) are not explored (R4). Figure 6 is incomplete compared to other figures (only 70M and 160M, TunedLens only for 70M) (R4).
- **Insufficient link to mechanistic understanding**: The analysis is primarily statistical (fractions, variances) and does not explicitly connect to deeper insights into how transformers work or what the results imply for future interpretability research (R2, R3). R3 asks why SAE was chosen over simpler methods like PCA/t-SNE and how the method helps understand transformer mechanisms. R2 suggests clarifying the motivation and future directions.
- **Computational cost and reproducibility**: Training time and memory requirements of MLSAE compared to training multiple single-layer SAEs are not quantified (R1, R2). No statement on whether source code will be published (R2).
- **Potential redundant latents**: Given that many latents are active at only a single layer, it is unclear whether the MLSAE has learned the same latent multiple times for different layers rather than truly distinct features (R4). R4 asks for investigation into redundancy. R2 notes that the single-prompt heatmap (Figure 21) may show a bimodal distribution that could be an artifact of the sorting procedure, and lacks a colorbar.
- **Unclear or missing technical details**: How layers are sampled for mini-batches during training is not specified (R2). Notation issues: Eq. (3) variance definition is ambiguous (R2); inconsistency in using \(\mathbf{x}\) for a single vector and a sequence of vectors (R2); Eq. (9) uses two symbols for the same thing (R2); L245 ratio is reversed; L353 expectation notation is incorrect (R2). “Expected value of the layer” should be clarified to “average layer index” (R2). R4 asks the source of pre-trained TunedLenses.
- **Information loss from normalization**: Normalizing histograms of activations over layers loses information about relative activation frequencies of different latents and the number of layers each latent is active in; measuring the number of activated layers may be more interpretable than variance of layer index for some purposes (R2).
- **Hypothesis motivation not strong**: The hypothesis that features “switch on” and remain active at later layers is not well-motivated; R2 asks for the intuition behind it, and whether it is based on SAEs trained on only the last layer. R2 also notes that the claim at L301 about mean cosine similarity increasing for larger models does not hold for smaller models.
- **Simple method, limited new insights**: R1 notes the method is simple and the paper does not offer many additional insights beyond the surprising single-layer localization result; other reviewers echoed that the analysis stays at a statistical level (R2, R3).
