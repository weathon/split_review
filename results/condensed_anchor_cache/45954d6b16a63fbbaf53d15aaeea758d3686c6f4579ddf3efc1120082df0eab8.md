## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper systematically evaluates whether LLMs (GPT-4, LLaMA 3, Gemma models) can generate explanations for ML models that meet established XAI criteria. Using Adult Income classification and California Housing regression, the authors measure 11 explanation properties (accuracy, selectivity, fidelity, completeness, contrastness, certainty, degree of importance, consistency, stability, robustness, comprehensibility) and compare against conventional XAI methods (LIME, SHAP, DiCE). Results indicate that LLMs struggle with identifying influential features, maintaining consistency, and accurately representing model behavior. The paper suggests LLMs may be better suited as “translators” of conventional XAI methods than as primary explainers.

### Strengths
- Novel and important research question: evaluating LLM-generated explanations against established XAI criteria – of strong interest to the field. (R2, R3, R4)
- The evaluation spans eleven XAI properties, providing a comprehensive, holistic assessment. (R1, R4)
- Attempts to define distinct evaluation criteria and makes useful connections between LLM capabilities and standard XAI methods (R2, R3). Includes detailed prompts in appendix (R2).
- Actionable insight: LLMs may be better used as “translators” of conventional XAI outputs rather than as primary explainers (R1).
- Some salient results: LLMs underperform on generating counterfactuals, capturing underlying reasoning of model decisions, and produce less accurate predictions than simpler classification models (R2).
- Clear introduction with well-identified knowledge gap (R2). Paper is well written and easy to follow (R3). (Note: Reviewer 2 strongly criticized presentation, creating a contrast.)
- Authors motivate their evaluation strategy and explicitly address shortcomings of the study (R3).
- Collating diverse explanation-quality dimensions in one place is useful (R4). Initiates discussion on where LLMs might fit within an explanation system (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Severe statistical inadequacy.** Only 1% of test sets are used (261 samples for Adult, 207 for California Housing). With 14 features, a binary classification task, and medium effect size, a minimum of ~2000 samples would be needed. No significance testing is performed; reported differences may be random. Stability is evaluated on just 20 instances, making conclusions statistically invalid. (R1)
- **Conceptual flaws in evaluation framework.** Using cosine similarity between LLM outputs and LIME/SHAP as ground truth for “completeness” and “degree of importance” is unsound. LIME and SHAP are approximations with known limitations (instability, misleading with correlated features); using them as ground truth creates circular evaluation. The work lacks a formal theoretical framework for what constitutes a “complete” explanation and does not use multiple complementary metrics, domain expert validation, or causal analysis. (R1, R3) One reviewer questions why baseline XAI methods were not first evaluated for faithfulness before being used as benchmarks (R3).
- **Insufficient implementation details and reproducibility concerns.** Missing: ML model hyperparameters and training details; LLM API settings (temperature, top-p); code or detailed algorithms for metric calculations; data preprocessing steps (handling of categorical variables, scaling). Prompts in Appendix A.3 lack information about how they were developed, tested, or optimized. (R1, R2) Prompts contain grammatical errors and inconsistencies (e.g., “do not use implement code” vs. “do not use any code such as Python”; “there should an inner dictionary” vs. “There should be an inner dictionary”), raising concerns about experimental rigor (R2). Typo in line 075: “ability of human’s how an AI system” (R2).
- **Lack of theoretical justification.** No formal framework explains why LLMs should be capable of understanding and explaining ML model decisions, how LLM architectures relate to explanation generation, what properties a valid explanation should satisfy, or how to verify explanation correctness. (R1, R3) For tabular data where models (decision trees, linear regression) are inherently interpretable, it is unclear whether verbal explanations are more helpful than standard XAI outputs; no psychological evidence supports this (R3).
- **Ignored closely related prior work.** The most prominent and closest work, “Kroeger, Nicholas, et al. ‘Are Large Language Models Post Hoc Explainers?’ arXiv preprint arXiv:2310.05797 (2023),” is not cited or discussed. (R1)
- **Poor clarity and organization.** The long list of 17 metrics in the literature survey is confusing; it is unclear whether it was compiled by the authors or from prior work and should be explicitly attributed. (R2) Definitions of key terms (e.g., Novelty, Accuracy, Robustness) are unclear and inconsistent—some refer to properties of the explanation, others of the explanatory method. (R2) The 1:1 mapping between LLM explanations and benchmark methods is only given later, buried in results text; a table would improve clarity. (R2) The “future work” paragraph is not connected to the paper’s own findings. (R2)
- **Limited scope and discussion.** The study is restricted to two small, old tabular datasets. The Adult dataset (income threshold) has known problems documented in Ding et al. 2021 (NeurIPS), and the authors do not justify its continued use. (R2) The broader role of LLMs within an explanation system (conversation, customization, integration with tools like TalkToModel) is not explored. (R4) One reviewer notes that using opaque LLMs to explain models raises fundamental trust issues (R4).
- **Mixed confidence in the negative result.** Two reviewers (R1, R2) find the study’s execution too flawed to draw firm conclusions; a carefully designed study finding the same negative result would be more convincing (R2). The other two reviewers (R3, R4) are positive but acknowledge limitations, suggesting the paper is a useful first step. (Contrast: scores are 1, 3, 5, 5, soundness ratings 2,2,2,2 – all low.)
