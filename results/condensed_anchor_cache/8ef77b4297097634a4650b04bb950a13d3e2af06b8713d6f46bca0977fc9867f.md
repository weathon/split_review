## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces PaLoRA (Pareto Low-Rank Adapters), a method for Pareto Front Learning (PFL) that augments a neural network with task-specific low-rank adapters and continuously parameterizes the Pareto front in their convex hull. It also proposes a deterministic sampling schedule for preference vectors that evolves during training, gradually shifting from general to task-specific features. Experiments on multiple benchmarks (cityscapes, NYU, MultiMNIST, SARCOS) show that PaLoRA achieves over 20× memory reduction compared to previous PFL methods (e.g., PaMaL), outperforms PFL baselines, and scales to larger networks, though performance is sometimes comparable to standard Multi-Task Learning (MTL) methods.

### Strengths
- Well-organized and clearly written paper.
- The idea of decoupling the network into a general feature extractor and task-specific low-rank adapters is reasonable and novel.
- Significant memory efficiency: PaLoRA reduces memory overhead up to “cityscapesPaLoRA ratio / NYUPaLoRA ratio”-fold compared to competing PFL baselines, and achieves over 20× reduction in memory usage over prior PFL work, which is impactful for large networks.
- The deterministic sampling of the preference vector λ (including the annealing strategy that moves from the simplex center to faces) is a well-motivated improvement over random sampling, leading to faster convergence and more stable training.
- Comprehensive experiments across multiple datasets and tasks show superior performance over existing PFL methods (PaMaL, etc.), with rich visualizations that illustrate the underlying ideas.
- The method scales to larger numbers of tasks (demonstrated on SARCOS) and outperforms PaMaL while using fewer parameters.
- Discussion sections highlight faster convergence of PaLoRA and show that different adapters learn distinct task-specific features.

### Weaknesses
- Missing comparisons and discussions with other low-rank / multi-LoRA methods for multi-task learning (e.g., [1-4]), MoE-like methods, and general adaptive weight learning approaches. The paper should clarify how PaLoRA’s Pareto front parameterization differs fundamentally from simply applying multiple LoRA adapters, and whether it offers advantages in training stability or convergence speed over these alternatives.
- No experiments or quantitative validation demonstrating that PaLoRA improves the consistency of mappings from preference space to objective space (the paper claims prior methods have “inconsistent mappings” but does not measure or compare this aspect). Additionally, the visualization of the sampling schedule in Fig. 2 is unclear: it needs to explicitly show how λ evolves during training and provide a metric (beyond visual inspection) to evaluate the quality of the mapping.
- Lack of ablation on key hyperparameters: effects of α, r (rank), and Q (“number of adapters per task”?) are not studied. Reviewer 4 specifically asks for results at different ranks for the LoRA modules. Sensitivity analysis is needed to understand robustness and generalization.
- Missing standard deviations / error bars for runs in Table 1 and for MultiMNIST/MultiMNIST-3 results. Given observed variance, reporting variance is necessary.
- The practical value of constructing the full Pareto front is not convincingly demonstrated. While having multiple trade-off models is a theoretical contribution, the paper would be stronger by showing a practical use case (e.g., adapting to OOD scenarios). For MTL, one model per task combination may suffice.
- The contribution of the fixed/annealed schedule over a simple even-sampling strategy is not empirically validated. The claimed disadvantage of random sampling (tight cluster) may be due to the distribution used rather than randomness itself; experiments comparing annealed vs. even-sampling are missing.
- The motivation for the annealing schedule (generic then specialized features) does not consider layer-wise heterogeneity. A body of work (e.g., Zhang et al. 2024, Yang et al. 2024) applies layer-wise coefficients; applying the schedule chronologically instead of per-layer may be less principled. Discussion or comparison with such methods is warranted.
- Memory overhead explanation vs. MTL baseline is unclear: if only LoRAs are trained, why does PaLoRA have higher memory than standard MTL? Also, for M>1, does the method use gradient accumulation? Clarification needed.
- Missing comparisons in terms of GFLOPS, memory, and speed at inference stage (only training efficiency is discussed).
- “Functional Diversity” in Sec 4.2 is not defined or quantitatively evaluated; the term needs explanation.
- Figs. 3(b) and (c) lack or have few baseline methods, making it difficult to assess relative performance; baselines should be consistent across subplots. Also, Fig. 3(b) should have improved legends.
- Additional dataset (e.g., UCI Census-Income) would strengthen experiments.
- For the SARCOS dataset, it is unclear if PaLoRA is compared to PaMaL after training longer (e.g., >100 epochs until convergence); such a comparison is important to show final performance.
