## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes the Contextual Interaction Attack (CIA), a multi-turn jailbreak that uses an auxiliary LLM (Wizard-70b) to automatically generate a sequence of benign prompt-response pairs, which gradually guide the target model to produce harmful content. The approach leverages the autoregressive nature of LLMs to build a context semantically aligned with the attack target. Experiments on 3 open-source and 3 closed-source LLMs report ≥90% ASR on two open-source models and ≥80% on two closed-source models, with results competitive to prior attacks. The authors also evaluate transferability across models and test against perplexity-based defenses.

### Strengths
- The topic is important: jailbreaking demonstrates LLMs are not robust to multi-turn interactions.
- The paper is well-structured, clearly written, and easy to follow; methodology presentation is clear.
- Extensive empirical validation across multiple open- and closed-source LLMs (including advanced models), with a comprehensive and systematic assessment of attack success.
- A good amount of ablation studies justify the design choices; the method uses no optimization against a specific model.
- The work inspires from prior context-aware attacks but is black-box and does not require model-specific tuning.
- Reviewer 2 (rating 6) highlights the insightful mechanism analysis and finds the experiment abundant and convincing; the overall research direction is interesting.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty**:
  - The core idea closely resembles the Crescendo multi-turn jailbreak (Russinovich et al., 2024), which also starts with an innocuous query and uses in-context learning to incrementally guide harmful generation. The paper’s implementation (e.g., starting prompt “Write a paper on the history of [harmful target]”) mirrors Crescendo’s approach. The authors must elaborate how their method diverges or improves upon Crescendo.
  - The method of generating benign prompts via few-shot prompting another LLM is similar to PAIR (Chao et al., 2023). The key differences are not clearly stated.
  - Other multi-turn attacks (CFA, Chain of Attack, Speak Out of Turn, Imposter.ai, MART, RedQueen) are already established. The paper lacks a concrete comparison or discussion of how CIA differs from these, especially CFA and the contextual attacks in [2] (CFA: arXiv 2408.04686).
  - The attack design is simple—essentially prompting an auxiliary LLM to mimic a human jailbreaker. The novelty relative to recent multi-turn work is not well elaborated.
- **Experiments**:
  - Experiments are limited to a subset of the AdvBench benchmark (50 prompts). Benchmarks like HarmBench and SorryBench offer more balanced, less noisy alternatives and should be included.
  - No comparison with the prevalent multi-turn attack Crescendo, nor with other recent multi-turn attacks (Chain of Attack, Speak Out of Turn, Imposter.ai). The paper would benefit from including these baselines.
  - The transferability experiments (Table 2) feel contrived. (1) Attacks like PAIR do not assume a threat model requiring transfer of instance-level outputs; it is an unfair comparison. (2) Closed-source models have specific defenses against popular attacks like GCG, making transferability measurements unfair. Authors should report results from the original attack papers (which show stronger transferability against GPT-3.5 and GPT-4) where possible.
  - The open-source models tested are somewhat outdated (Vicuna, LLaMA-2-7b) and not claimed to be robust. Testing on more recent, well-defended models (e.g., Llama 3/3.1, larger models with refusal training) would strengthen the work.
- **Soundness of Evaluation**:
  - The human judge setup is insufficiently detailed: the exact number of examples used to compute jailbreak percentages, the number of human evaluators, and whether the judge was independent of the authors with a single/double-blind setup are not specified. No correlation with an automated LLM judge (e.g., HarmBench classifier) is provided.
  - The claim on page 1 line 45 that “jailbreak prompts generated by automated attacks often do not perform well when transferred to other LLMs” contradicts the citation of GCG (which is transferable). The poor transferability observed here may be due to model-specific defenses, not a general property of automated attacks.
  - Perplexity-based defenses are designed against GCG-style attacks and are not expected to detect harm. The paper would benefit from testing against general defenses such as DERTA, Llama Guard, or robustly trained models like CYGNET.
  - Testing against fine-tuned adversarial prompt detectors (e.g., Prompt-Guard-86M, Llama-Guard-3-8B) is missing.
- **Missing Details and Clarifications**:
  - Section 3.2 claims responses to preliminary questions are used to tailor subsequent prompts, but further detail on how responses influence the attack progression is needed.
  - The use of Wizard-70b as the auxiliary model when attacking weaker models (Vicuna, LLaMA-2-7b) is odd—why is a stronger model needed to attack a weaker one?
  - Provide an illustrative example supporting the claim that “compared to harmful information provided by the user (exogenous input), LLMs tend to perceive their own generated content (endogenous output) as safer.”
  - Explain why the ASR against Claude-2 is much lower than other models; are there distinctive behaviors of Claude-2?
- **Presentation**:
  - Related work section cites only 3 jailbreaking papers; the rest are on background (in-context learning, autoregressive LMs) that should be assumed knowledge. More contextualization relative to prior jailbreak work is needed.
  - Figure 1 contains a typo: “Repharse” should be “Rephrase”.
