## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies domain generalization (DG) by deriving upper bounds on the excess risk of Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) and minimax lower bounds in three settings: a fully worst-case setting, and two constrained settings with bounded Integral Probability Metric (IPM) distance or bounded density ratio between domains. The paper claims that ERM achieves optimal convergence rates, suggesting that no method can substantially outperform ERM in these general-purpose DG settings.

### Strengths
- Compiles multiple structural assumptions of similarity between domains (overlap and IPM distance) and works out upper and lower bounds on what is statistically achievable. (R1)
- Addresses the important problem of domain generalization and offers an interpretation of why ERM performs competitively on DG benchmarks like DomainBed. (R2, R4)
- Provides theoretical results that quantify the difficulty of generalizing to unseen domains. (R3)
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow, with multiple new generalization bounds that explain the competitive performance of ERM. (R4)
- Presents a lower bound on the minimax excess risk. (R5)

### Weaknesses
- **Overly broad and unsurprising results**: The theorems rely on standard Rademacher complexity machinery (symmetrization, triangle inequality), yielding general but uninformative convergence rates. The main rate \(1/\sqrt{mn} + 1/\sqrt{n}\) simplifies to \(1/\sqrt{n}\) (since m > 1), depending only on the number of domains, which is unsurprising given the i.i.d. domain assumption. (R1, R3) The claim that the minimax excess risk scales as \(\Omega(1/\sqrt{mn}+1/\sqrt{n})\) from lower bound terms is not rigorously justified (e.g., the step that the difference of two \(\Omega\) terms is still \(\Omega\) in equation (11) is not true in general). Furthermore, the \(\Omega\) notation hides many constants, making the “matching scaling” claim of limited value. (R2)
- **Problem formulation deviates from mainstream DG**: The paper minimizes the expected population risk over randomly sampled target domains (Eq. 5). This is equivalent to ERM on i.i.d. domains and is known to be vulnerable to out-of-distribution data. Domain generalization is typically formalized as a minimax optimization (e.g., Sagawa et al., 2019), which yields a different optimal model. (R4) The paper’s in-expectation view also contrasts with causality-inspired works (e.g., IRM) that aim for worst-case performance over interventions. (R1) The i.i.d. domain assumption is too restrictive in practice; proper assumptions about the data-generating process are critical for characterizing DG (e.g., “An Information-theoretic Approach to Distribution Shifts”, “On learning invariant representations for domain adaptation”, “The risks of invariant risk minimization”). (R5, R3, R4)
- **Rademacher complexity does not explain deep learning generalization**: For modern deep networks, the capacity is large relative to sample size, so the Rademacher complexity term is near 1, making the bounds vacuous. (R4, R5) The theoretical results do not involve algorithm architecture. (R5)
- **The minimax lower bound assumptions are unrealistic**: The proof of Theorem 4 assumes the set \(\mathcal{P}\) includes all possible distributions over \(X \times Y\). In practice, \(X\) and \(Y\) share causal relationships across domains, so labels cannot be arbitrarily selected when \(\mathcal{P}\) is restricted (see Eq. 42→43). Thus the lower bounds may not apply to practical learning settings. (R4)
- **No explanation for known counterexamples**: Strong evidence exists (e.g., Colored MNIST (CMNIST) from Arjovsky et al.) where ERM performs worse than well-motivated DG algorithms. The paper does not explain why its results do not apply to such settings. (R4, R5)
- **Missing comparisons and actionability**: It is unclear whether the upper bound in Theorem 5 matches the lower bound in Theorem 6 (e.g., the dependence on \(\sigma\) may not be optimal). (R3) The actionable insights (e.g., choosing regularization strength) are predicated on the in-expectation view and do not address the difficulty of choosing regularization in a worst-case setting (Gulrajani & Lopez-Paz show that peeking at test set leads to overestimation of IRM). (R1)
- **Technical contribution is limited**: The results are rooted in pioneering work on ERM (e.g., “Rademacher and Gaussian complexities”, “Foundations of Machine Learning”). (R5)
- **Questions raised that overlap with criticisms**:  
  - Can we estimate the predictor at a better rate if \(E[Y|X]\) is the same across domains? (R3)  
  - Does the upper bound in Theorem 5 match the lower bound in Theorem 6? (R3)  
  - What terms are hidden in the \(\Omega\) notation? (R2)  
  - How would results change under a minimax formulation or if \(\mathcal{P}\) is restricted? (R4)  
  - Can \(\sigma\) be interpreted as the variance of the domain distribution rather than an IPM? (R3)
- **Reviewer disagreement**: Two reviewers rated 5 (accept-level), while three rated 3 (reject). Among them, R2 (score 5) identified a rigorous flaw in the scaling claim; R3 (score 5) found the results too general and uninformative; R1, R4, R5 (score 3) were unconvinced of the main claim, with R4 and R5 explicitly stating the paper is overly assertive and not capturing fundamental DG aspects.
