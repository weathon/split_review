## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Ferumal flows, a kernelised normalising flow paradigm that replaces neural-network-based coupling layers with kernel-based transformations, aiming for parameter efficiency and better performance in low-data regimes. The approach is evaluated on 2D synthetic data and five tabular benchmark datasets, with additional results on Kuzushiji-MNIST in the appendix. Reviewer opinions are mixed: two reviewers (scores 8) find the idea novel, theoretically sound, and empirically convincing on tabular/low-dimensional data; one reviewer (score 6) appreciates the low-data strengths but questions competitiveness on complex distributions and images; one reviewer (score 5) finds the concept interesting but notes missing comparisons, unclear exposition, and a lack of image experiments. All reviewers agree on the need for code release, medical data evaluation, and stronger evidence on high-dimensional data.

### Strengths
1. **Novel and interesting concept**: Integrating kernels into normalising flows is a simple yet effective idea that addresses data/parameter hunger of neural-network-based flows (R1, R2, R3, R4).
2. **Strong theoretical foundation**: Proposition 3.1 shows that model complexity scales naturally with dataset size, avoiding over-parameterisation (R2, R3, R4). The representer theorem argument is mostly sound (R4).
3. **Convincing results in low-data regime**: The proposed method clearly outperforms FFJORD in Table 3 when only 500 samples are available, demonstrating data efficiency (R1, R3). Parameter efficiency is explicitly quantified (R1).
4. **Good exposition**: The paper is well-written, with clear equations, tables, and figures given appropriate space (R3, R4). The mathematical idea is conceptually simple and requires no strong leap of faith (R4).
5. **Suitable benchmark for tabular data**: Experiments on five real-world tabular datasets are convincing and demonstrate the method's appeal for low-dimensional data (R4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Missing code repository**: No link to code or models is provided, hindering reproducibility and further exploration (R1).
2. **No evaluation on medical data**: The paper motivates its method with medical applications (Section 1 and 5.4) but never applies it to a medical dataset, limiting practical relevance (R1, R3).
3. **Incomplete baselines in Figure 1**: The figure lacks results from methods like FFJORD (or others), making the comparison incomplete (R2).
4. **Missing experiments on 2D spiral dataset**: The method should be tested on the spiral 2D dataset, a standard benchmark for normalising flows (R2).
5. **Lack of image dataset evaluation in the main paper**: The main paper does not include any image experiments; only Kuzushiji-MNIST results appear in the appendix. A minority reviewer (score 5) argues that the model should be evaluated on MNIST, CIFAR, and CELEBA, especially when compared with Glow (R2). Another reviewer (score 6) notes that the qualitative samples from Kuzushiji-MNIST (Figures 4–6) are poor, and the method’s applicability to complex, high-dimensional distributions remains unclear (R3).
6. **Poor performance on discontinuous data**: FFJORD achieves better results on the checkerboard dataset (Figure 3 in supplementary), suggesting the kernelised flow struggles with sharp discontinuities. This raises concerns about modelling capacity for complex, multimodal distributions (R3).
7. **Outperformed by FFJORD on several tasks**: In Table 1 and Table 4, the proposed method is significantly outperformed by FFJORD (which uses more parameters). It is not explored whether increasing the number of kernel basis functions or feature-space dimensionality can close this gap (R3).
8. **Lack of experimental details for 2D toy data**: Table 1 does not specify which neural-network-based model is used as a baseline, nor does it report the number of training samples. This makes the comparison difficult to assess (R3).
9. **Unclear exposition of the method**: Section 3.1 is too terse; equation (1) is introduced too quickly without a simple example to illustrate the idea. The role of kernels in coupling layers is not sufficiently illustrated (R2).
10. **Misleading statement about the representer theorem**: After Proposition 3.1, the claim that the solution is not necessarily unique because the objective lacks strictly convex regularisation is inaccurate. The classical representer theorem (Scholkopf et al. 2001) does not require convexity, and uniqueness is not guaranteed even with strongly convex regularisation (R4).
11. **Over-parameterisation via depth**: The number of layers \(L\) remains a hyperparameter and can still lead to over-parameterisation, partially undermining the argument about natural scaling with dataset size (R4).
12. **Missing related work**: The paper omits recent kernel-based probability density models such as PSD representations (NeurIPS 2021), Sampling from arbitrary functions via PSD models (AISTATS 2022), and Squared Neural Families (NeurIPS 2023), which are also applicable in low-dimensional/tabular regimes (R4).
