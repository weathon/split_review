## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes the first generative self-supervised learning (SSL) method for hypergraphs, centered on the novel pretext task of *hyperedge filling*—predicting a missing node from an incomplete hyperedge. A theoretical analysis connects this task to node classification under a homophily assumption. Based on this task, the authors introduce HypeBoy, a three-stage pipeline (augmentation, encoding, filling) using UniGCNII as the backbone encoder. HypeBoy addresses issues of overemphasized proximity, dimensional collapse, and non-uniformity/alignment. Experiments on 11 benchmark datasets show that HypeBoy outperforms 16 baseline methods, and ablation studies demonstrate the contribution of each component.

### Strengths
- Identifies and fills a clear gap in hypergraph literature: designing generative SSL tasks for representation learning. The hyperedge filling task is intuitive and well-suited to hypergraph structure.
- Provides sound theoretical analysis supporting the intuition that hyperedge filling improves node classification when hyperedges satisfy homophily (motivated by stochastic block models).
- Comprehensive experimental section with thorough ablation studies (Tables 3, 6) showing the individual contribution of each component (feature reconstruction warmup, projection head, hyperedge filling).
- Empirically examines numerical characteristics of learned representations (e.g., proximity), drawing insights beyond raw performance.
- Effectively identifies and addresses the problem of overemphasized proximity through augmentation (masking) and dimensional collapse through a two-stage training scheme that reduces reliance on projection heads.
- The hyperedge filling task can obtain more accurate node features, helping downstream tasks.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited backbone diversity**: All SSL results use UniGCNII as the backbone. Experiments with other backbones (e.g., HNN, MLP) are needed to demonstrate the method’s general advantages. (R1)
- **Dependence on warmup**: Hyperedge filling alone (v1 in Table 3) is the worst variant, and performance drops noticeably when the feature reconstruction warmup is omitted. This raises questions about the standalone effectiveness of hyperedge filling and whether it must be bound to the other components. (R1, R4)
- **Missing baseline**: Include plain UniGCNII (without any of the three components) as a line in Table 3 for reference. (R1)
- **Generative SSL justification**: The paper does not convincingly justify why a generative approach (especially hyperedge filling) is superior to other self-supervised methods for hypergraphs, given known issues like mode collapse and training difficulty. A more rigorous argument beyond empirical results is needed. (R2)
- **Lack of architectural novelty**: The method applies existing encoders/decoders (UniGCNII, HNN, MLP) and a projection head inspired by Deep Sets, without introducing novel architectural designs. This limits the perceived originality and impact. (R2)
- **Scope restricted to homogeneous hypergraphs**: The paper focuses on homogeneous hypergraphs and does not discuss embeddings for hyperedges or address heterogeneous hypergraphs, which are prevalent. This omission significantly restricts applicability. (R2)
- **Questions requiring clarification**: 
  - How would the choice of hypergraph neural network architecture affect performance? The analysis does not relate to architecture. (R3)
  - In the hyperedge filling proof (F2), the step size γ appears not to affect the theoretical result, but in practice it likely matters. Could the authors clarify? (R3)
  - Would edge filling on a clique-expanded graph produce different results than hyperedge filling on the original hypergraph? (R3)
  - What are the real-world applications of the hyperedge filling task? (R2)
  - Why were Gaussian, Bernoulli, and binomial distributions chosen for the method instead of more conventional attention or neural network strategies? (R2)
  - The homophily assumption is defined for hyperedges as each hyperedge containing mostly same-category nodes. How does this definition apply to hypergraphs, and do all datasets in experiments satisfy the assumption? (R4)
  - Why does Section 5.1 use 1% of nodes for training, when most related work uses fixed splits or 5/10 nodes per class? (R4)
- **Minor**: It would be useful to test whether other baseline SSL methods benefit from the same augmentation (masking) scheme used in HypeBoy (Table 6). (R1)
- **Contrast among reviewers**: While Reviewer 3 found no major criticisms and rated the paper highly (8), other reviewers raised the concerns above (ratings 5, 5, 6). The most positive reviewer particularly appreciated the intuition and theoretical analysis, but the more critical reviewers questioned the novelty, justification, and generalizability.
