## Merged Review

### Summary
LiFlow frames accelerated molecular dynamics (MD) for crystalline materials as conditional generation of atomic displacements using flow matching. A Propagator generates displacements; a Corrector locally fixes unphysical geometries. An adaptive prior based on the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution accounts for chemical and thermal conditions. The model is benchmarked on 25-ps trajectories of lithium diffusion across 4,186 solid-state electrolyte (SSE) candidates at four temperatures. LiFlow achieves Spearman rank correlations of 0.7–0.8 for lithium mean squared displacement (MSD) on unseen compositions, generalizes from short trajectories to larger supercells and longer simulations, and obtains speed‑ups of up to 600,000× vs AIMD and 400× vs MLIP. Reviewer scores are 8, 8, 8, 5, 3 (four reviewers considered the work strong; two expressed significant concerns about novelty, missing comparisons, and limited generalisation).

### Strengths
- **Novel and timely approach.** The application of flow matching to accelerate atomic transport in Li-ion SSEs is innovative and addresses a key computational bottleneck in energy‑storage research (R1, R2, R3, R4).  
- **Sound methodology with strong physical priors.** The model elegantly incorporates physics knowledge: a Maxwell–Boltzmann-based adaptive prior, careful enforcement of symmetry invariances/equivariances, and a Corrector for stability. Ablation studies validate each component (R1, R3, R4).  
- **Impressive computational acceleration.** Speed‑ups of up to 600,000× relative to first‑principles MD and 400× relative to MLIP‑based simulations enable dramatically larger length/time scales (R2).  
- **New publicly available dataset.** The release of a large dataset of Li‑diffusion trajectories across 4,186 SSE candidates is a valuable contribution to the material‑science community (R3, R5).  
- **Clear and well‑organized presentation.** The paper is clearly written, with thorough notation and well‑structured sections, making the method accessible (R1, R5). Citations are comprehensive (R5). Experiments are performed nicely and yield decent results (R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing comparisons with existing ML‑based MD acceleration methods.** The paper compares only to AIMD and MLIP, not to other surrogate‑model approaches. Several reviewers explicitly cite TimeWarp (Karras et al. 2023), F3LOW (Li et al. 2024), diffusion models [3], variational methods [4], normalizing flows [5], and – most closely – Fu et al. (2023) which also studied Li‑ion diffusion with a predictor+corrector approach (R1, R2, R5). Such comparisons are essential to establish the claimed improvements.  
- **Novelty is disputed.** Several reviewers note that key components have been used in prior work: flow‑based generative surrogates for MD (TimeWarp, F3LOW), predictor+corrector design (Fu et al. 2023), two‑set edge construction (Hsu et al. 2024), equivariant GNNs (PaiNN), and physics‑inspired priors. The modifications to PaiNN (equivariant flow fields, Maxwell‑Boltzmann prior) are seen as necessary adaptations rather than unique innovations (R2, R5). Two reviewers find the work more suitable for a chemistry/materials journal than for ICLR as a methodology paper (R5).  
- **Generalisation to other systems is unexplored.** The model is only shown on Li‑diffusion in crystalline SSEs. Applicability to amorphous materials (e.g., amorphous silicon, LiPON electrolytes), biomolecules (e.g., alanine dipeptide), or other chemical systems is not addressed. Reviewers suggest testing on datasets such as MD17 or OC20 (R1, R2).  
- **Limited evaluation metrics.** The primary metrics (MSD and RDF) are high‑level aggregates. The MSD and RDF may obscure inaccuracies in individual atomic positions; a per‑step MAE would be more direct. RDF is averaged over a portion of the simulation, potentially masking discrepancies during equilibration (R4).  
- **Issues with extrapolation and hyperparameter sensitivity.** The model struggles to generalise to lower temperatures, where rare events are poorly captured. The prior scale requires careful tuning; the paper lacks a sensitivity analysis or automated tuning strategy (R2).  
- **Readability and presentation concerns.**  
  - The paper exceeds the page limit and relies heavily on the appendix (e.g., subsection lines 455–466 refers only to appendix figures), making it hard to assess core contributions without flipping back and forth (R4).  
  - Organisation of experimental results (placement of Tables 1 and 2, unclear subsections) disrupts flow. Figures 2 and 3 could be merged; a model figure is absent (R2).  
  - Several dense passages assume specialist knowledge – the introductory atomic‑transport context (line 169–176), the RBF expansion and unit‑vector description (lines 245–246), and evaluation metrics – need more background/link to seminal works (R3).  
- **Design questions about the Corrector and GNN.** The Corrector’s added value beyond a well‑trained Propagator is not clearly justified. Could the Corrector be a simpler one‑step conditional model instead of a full flow (R4)? Does the Corrector maintain global translation/rotation invariances (edge construction is skipped in Appendix D.2) (R1)? Model performance is sensitive to the internal GNN architecture (Table A1); the choice of PaiNN vs. alternatives is not fully motivated (R1).  
- **Risk of physically fictitious dynamics.** The paper acknowledges this risk but does not quantify or propose systematic mitigation (R2).  
- **Minor presentation issues.** A lattice matrix lacks intuitive description (line 71); the footnote on line 107 may have a typo (t vs τ); variable σ_S is defined but not used in Eq. 10; language in lines 331–332 suggests MSD is computed between configurations rather than for a single one; the purpose of Table 3 is unclear (R4).
