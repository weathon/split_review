## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes LEAP, a black-box discrete prompt learning method that does not require a predefined label vocabulary. LEAP uses an alternating optimization strategy to jointly learn discrete prompt tokens (via Gumbel-Softmax parameterization and variance-reduced policy gradient) and a learnable mapping matrix that projects LLM output tokens to categories. Theoretical convergence guarantees are provided. Experiments on GPT2-XL, RoBERTa-large, and Llama3-8B across several classification datasets show that LEAP outperforms baselines like BDPL and SSPT. The method addresses a practical challenge in the Language-Model-as-a-Service scenario.

### Strengths
1. **Novel scenario and straightforward method**: The idea of learning a token-to-label mapping to handle missing label vocabulary is interesting and addresses a practical challenge in black-box prompt learning (LMaSS direction). The alternating optimization combining Gumbel-Softmax and a mapping matrix is clearly presented.

2. **Theoretical analysis**: The paper provides convergence guarantees for the proposed optimization algorithm, which strengthens the reliability of the method.

3. **Practical relevance**: Tackles the real-world scenario where no pre-defined label vocabulary is available and model internals are inaccessible.

*Note: One reviewer (score 5) found the contribution stronger and the presentation clear, while three other reviewers (score 3) had significant reservations about the experimental scope and practical significance.*

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited to classification tasks**: The method is evaluated only on text classification (at most 4 classes, e.g., Table 5). In the LLM era, prompt optimization for generation tasks (e.g., question-answering) is more desirable; adapting LEAP to generation tasks is missing. Most real-world applications involve language generation without predefined categories.

2. **Questionable practical significance of the "label-free" setting**: For LLMs, tasks are generally generative, and most labels can be expressed as text (e.g., Amazon Books ratings can be converted to strings and tokenized). The claim that label vocabulary is missing is not convincingly demonstrated—are label names used in the optimization or not? The paper does not clearly define "label-free" (e.g., Section 5.1 is not addressed) and omits discussion of related work exploring this setting.

3. **Weak experimental baselines and model scale**: Missing comparisons with more recent optimization methods like BBTv2 and GDFO. Experiments use only relatively weak LLMs (RoBERTa-large, GPT2-XL, Llama3-8B). Black-box prompt learning typically targets stronger models (e.g., GPT-4o, Llama3-70B); it is unclear if LEAP will work at that scale.

4. **Trivial improvements on several datasets**: On datasets like BOOK, CoLA, and QNLI, improvements over baselines are marginal. Significance tests are recommended but not provided.

5. **Lack of details and unclear presentation**:  
   - Initialization of the learnable mapping matrix \(M\) and its sensitivity to different initializations are not discussed.  
   - Figure 1’s \(B_Y\) and the loss function (line 180) are not clearly explained—what is the objective?  
   - The paper should reframe the label-free scenario more clearly to avoid confusion.  
   - Computational resource requirements for scaling to larger models/datasets are not addressed.
