## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Discrete Distribution Networks (DDN), a generative model that approximates distributions via hierarchical discrete distributions. At each layer, the network generates K candidate outputs; during training, the output closest to the ground truth is selected and fed back as input to the next layer. Optimization uses a reconstruction loss and a split‑and‑prune algorithm together with chain dropout, residual learning, and leak choice. At inference a random selection at each layer yields a sample. The model is demonstrated on CIFAR‑10, FFHQ‑64×64, CelebA, and MNIST for unconditional generation and for conditional tasks (inpainting, colorization, denoising, super‑resolution, CLIP‑guided editing). Quantitative results are reported for CIFAR‑10 and FFHQ.

### Strengths
- **Novelty**: All reviewers acknowledge the novelty of autoregressively feeding a selected output through layers. Reviewer 2 calls it “novel to the best of my knowledge”, and Reviewer 3 states it is “a whole new method, very different from any existing generative models” and finds it elegant.
- **Practical techniques**: The split‑and‑prune algorithm is clever and allows training with non‑differentiable selection (Reviewers 2, 3). Additional tricks (chain dropout, residual learning, leak choice) help optimization (Reviewer 2).
- **Conditional generation without gradients**: The model can be conditioned on external signals without requiring gradients – a useful property (Reviewer 2).
- **Visualization**: Hierarchical generation can be visualised (Figures 8, 18), which is enlightening (Reviewer 3).
- **Simplicity**: Reviewer 1 notes the method is simple and intuitive.
- **Applications**: Showcased for inpainting, colorization, denoising, super‑resolution, and CLIP‑guided editing (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing theoretical analysis**: Reviewer 3 points out the lack of a formal proof that the hierarchical sampling process (including split‑and‑prune) converges to the target data distribution under reasonable assumptions, and that approximation error from discrete sampling is not analysed. Reviewer 1 similarly finds the method not theoretically attractive because it does not explicitly model probability distributions.
- **Quantitative performance lags behind baselines**: Reviewer 1 criticises that the method performs worse than simple baselines like DCGAN, does not compare against VQ‑VAE despite claiming it is unsatisfactory, and does not compare to modern approaches like diffusion models. Reviewer 2 and Reviewer 3 also note the low quantitative results and limited comparison, though they accept it as a proof‑of‑concept. Reviewer 1 is more critical, calling it “not empirically useful”.
- **Finite output space**: Reviewer 3 raises a fundamental concern: unconditional generation has at most K^L possible outputs, suggesting the model may memorise training data rather than learn a true generative distribution. A calculation is given: MNIST (70k images, 28×28, ~55 MB) vs an EDM model with 62 M parameters. This bounded capacity raises questions about scalability to high‑dimensional datasets.
- **Scalability and cost**: Reviewer 2 questions whether DDN can scale to larger datasets (FFHQ‑256, ImageNet‑256) and notes the expense of generating multiple samples in parallel across layers. Reviewer 3 also stresses that experiments are limited to low‑resolution datasets (CIFAR‑10, MNIST, CelebA, FFHQ‑64×64). Reviewer 2 asks about memory requirements compared to baselines.
- **Connection to existing work**: Reviewer 1 identifies similarity to a VQ variant of diffusion models (arxiv 2111.14822) that runs discrete diffusion on a latent space with a codebook, and criticises the paper for failing to explain what is different and novel. Reviewers 2 and 3 do not raise this issue, so there is disagreement on the degree of novelty relative to prior discrete methods.
- **Missing comparisons and details**: Reviewer 2 requests: parameter counts of baselines (DDN benefits from more layers, but baselines should have similar counts), a comparison between a single‑shot DDN and the default DDN allowing the same number of refinements, and memory requirements. Reviewer 1 also notes the paper fails to justify design choices and does not compare against VQ‑VAE.
- **Presentation issues**: Reviewer 2 points out specific problems: Figure 9 has large white space, Table 2 is placed on page 8 while ablations are discussed on page 10, and Figure 5 shows an experiment not addressed in the main text. Reviewer 1 gives a low presentation score (2/4), while the other reviewers are less critical (3/4 and 4/4).
- **Experimental scope**: All reviewers agree that experiments are limited to low‑resolution datasets. Reviewer 2 and Reviewer 3 view this as acceptable for a proof‑of‑concept, but Reviewer 1 finds it insufficient. Reviewer 3 further notes that the model may essentially be a compressed tree‑based storage of all possible outputs, raising concerns about generalization.
- **Reviewer disagreement**: Reviewer 1 is significantly more negative (score 5, soundness 2, presentation 2, contribution 2), arguing the method is neither theoretically attractive nor empirically useful and fails to properly connect to existing discrete diffusion models. Reviewer 2 and Reviewer 3 are more positive (scores 8, soundness 3‑4, presentation 3‑4, contribution 3‑4), praising the novelty and potential while acknowledging the quantitative and scalability limitations.
