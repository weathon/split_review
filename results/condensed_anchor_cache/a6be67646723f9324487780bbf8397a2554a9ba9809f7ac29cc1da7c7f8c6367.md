## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Adversarial Latent Feature Augmentation (ALFA) to mitigate bias in classification by perturbing latent features into unfair regions (where misclassification rates for certain groups are high) and then fine-tuning the last layer. The method uses an adversarial fairness objective based on covariance (Zafar 2017) to generate perturbations, and the authors provide a theoretical framework and experiments across multiple datasets (Adult, COMPAS, German, Drug, Wikipedia, CelebA). Reviewers find the idea novel, well-motivated, and the paper generally well-written, with thorough experiments and code. However, they raise significant concerns about distribution mismatch between upsampled training and imbalanced test data, incomplete baseline comparisons, unclear implementation details for the perturbation δ, and insufficient theoretical justification. One reviewer (R2, score 8) is notably more positive, particularly commending the motivation, connection to Equalized Odds, use of Sinkhorn, Pareto curves, and code. The other three reviewers (scores 6) share the concerns, with R3 providing the most critical technical evaluation.

### Strengths
- **Novel approach and motivation**: The idea of using adversarial fairness attacks on a pretrained model to identify and augment unfair regions in latent space is interesting, counterintuitive, and novel (R1, R2, R3, R4). The concept of “unfair region” is an interesting extension of adversarial learning for fairness (R1).
- **Well-written and organized**: The paper is easy to follow, with a clear motivation and a convincing, easy-to-follow Algorithm 1 (R1, R4).
- **Thorough experiments**: Experiments span diverse datasets, modalities, and backbone networks, demonstrating effectiveness in improving Equalized Odds while maintaining competitive accuracy (R3, R4). The use of Pareto curves (Figure 4, 5) instead of singleton points helps understand fairness-accuracy tradeoffs (R2).
- **Theoretical support**: The objective L_fair is well-defined and linked to Equalized Odds in the appendix; the proof overview is solid (R2). Theoretical results are intuitive (R4).
- **Empirical choices**: Using Sinkhorn over Wasserstein is a clever choice that sidesteps computational issues (R2).
- **Code and reproducibility**: Clean, well-written code is provided (R2).
- **Flexibility**: The method can be adapted to different fairness constraints (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Distribution mismatch (upsampled training vs. test)** (R3): Fairness enforcement is performed on artificially balanced demographic data (Np = 4·max(n00,n01,n10,n11)), but the test distribution can be imbalanced. The theoretical fairness guarantee (ΔEOd(θp) ≤ ΔEOd(θ)) is shown on the upsampled distribution, not the original. This raises concern that being fair on upsampled training data does not imply fairness on the original test distribution, especially for highly unbalanced combinations of A and Y. The method achieves Demographic Parity proportional to Equalized Odds on this specific case, contradicting known incompatibility and losing the essential dependence between Y and A (R3).
- **Incomplete baseline comparisons** (R3): On Adult and Wikipedia, ALFA achieves Equalized Odds violations no better than 0.1, while existing approaches (Zafar et al., 2013) reportedly achieve EO violations close to 0.03 under the same test conditions on Adult UCI (20% test split). This suggests the experimental evaluation may not fully represent SOTA capabilities. R4 also notes that using adversarial learning to identify high-error regions is not novel (Lahoti et al., ARL) and recommends adding ARL as a baseline and discussing how ALFA handles imbalanced demographic distributions vs. ARL. The claim that ALFA is more computationally efficient than ARL needs justification, especially since ARL can be applied to latent representations with a linear adversary. ARL also operates without demographic information; the authors should discuss how ALFA can reduce reliance on demographic data (R4).
- **Technical gaps in perturbation generation** (R3, R4): The methodology lacks sufficient technical explanations for how δ is generated. Specifically:
  - How is δ maximized? Is it a neural network trained via gradient descent? Does it take X_p as input? (R3)
  - What mathematical constraints (e.g., Lipschitz) ensure proximity in latent space? (R3)
  - The proof of Theorem 3.2 relies on the assumption that samples from high-error regions influence gradient updates, which depends on the success rate of the fairness attack; the authors did not demonstrate that the attack can fully construct R_unfair (R4). The assumption of piecewise linear functions on real data should be discussed; the constants C, C0, C1 require clarification (R4).
  - Additional experiments are suggested to show the success rate of the fairness attack on real datasets (e.g., predicting accuracy of a sensitive attribute classifier in these regions and comparing with ground truth R_unfair) (R4).
- **Loss function balancing** (R3): Equation (5) uses equal weighting between the balanced (upsampled) and unbalanced distributions. When fairness enforcement is not needed (α large), half the focus remains on D(X_p,Y_p,A_p), which may differ from the test distribution. Without a hyperparameter to control this split, maintaining task accuracy is questionable. This equal weighting might explain why ALFA shows lower accuracy in unfair regions of Figure 4, and why the blue curve (ALFA) is largely absent in unfair regions (high α) except for Adult UCI. Did other datasets also show poor performance at high α? (R3)
- **Only single adversarial pass** (R3): The method uses a single pass of the two-step process (attack then fine-tune), unlike traditional adversarial learning with iterative updates. Why? (R3)
- **Clarity issues in experiments** (R4):
  - In Figures 4–6, some baselines have fewer tradeoff points; the number of parameters controlling the fairness–accuracy trade-off should be provided.
  - In Figure 2(a), different perturbation levels δ would better illustrate the magnitude of the fairness attack on the corrected boundary.
  - In the new Figure 8, the corrected model has better fairness for α=1 than for α=0, which is counterintuitive. Intermediate α values (e.g., 0.5) are missing; the role of α in controlling fairness needs explanation.
- **Misalignment of unfair region definition with metric** (R1): The unfair region is defined by misclassification rate, but the fairness metric used (Equalized Odds) considers only positive predictions. This raises a concern about the method’s consistency.
- **Performance drop on benign data** (R1): Adversarial perturbation may hurt performance on fair regions, and while benign data is retained during fine-tuning (Eq. 5), other mitigation strategies are not discussed.
- **Theoretical framework not clearly presented** (R1): The abstract highlights theoretical contributions, but the main body lacks clear presentation of the theory.
- **Scalability concerns** (R2): How does the method scale with input feature dimensions and latent space dimension in high-dimensional settings? No experiments address this.
- **Lack of Sinkhorn vs. Wasserstein comparison** (R2): Is there a performance gain from Wasserstein over Sinkhorn, and could it be feasible for smaller datasets (German/COMPAS)? An additional analysis in Appendix D would be insightful.
- **Limited analysis of covariance effect** (R2): How does the covariance of label and sensitive attribute affect effectiveness? Under what regimes is the method particularly effective? What about covariate shift from train to test? Table E in the appendix is only for training data.
- **Need for actionable fairness control** (R4): The method fails to efficiently control the fairness-accuracy tradeoff; practitioners require tools to satisfy specific fairness levels. The paper’s statement “without compromising accuracy” should be updated to reflect the tradeoff (e.g., “improves group fairness while maintaining accuracy as much as possible”).
