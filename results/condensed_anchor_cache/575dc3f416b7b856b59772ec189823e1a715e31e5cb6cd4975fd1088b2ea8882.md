## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper extends *learning to defer* (L2D) to handle missing expert annotations and control workload distribution among experts and the AI classifier. It proposes a probabilistic mixture‑of‑experts formulation using the Expectation‑Maximization (EM) algorithm; a constrained optimization in the E‑step enforces workload balance. Evaluations on synthetic and real‑world datasets show competitive or better performance than baselines under various missing rates.

### Strengths
- Addresses a realistic and under‑studied problem: missing expert annotations in L2D, which is relevant for real‑world applications (e.g., medicine) and reduces annotation burden.
- The probabilistic framework and EM application in this setting are novel and sound.
- Workload management is promising for ethical, practical, and regulatory constraints; the ablation study reveals that imbalanced workload can lead to over‑reliance on high‑performing experts, while even distribution may be desirable in practice.
- The controllable workload formulation simplifies evaluation of accuracy‑coverage ratios compared to existing methods that require post‑hoc adjustments.
- Experiments show that reducing the workload of highly accurate (often overloaded) experts only slightly decreases accuracy and can yield higher accuracy when expert performance is inconsistent between training and test sets.
- Topic is comprehensively addressed; all relevant predecessor methods are included.
- Paper is well‑written and easy to follow.

### Weaknesses
- **Scalability to many experts:** The E‑step complexity grows linearly with the number of experts, making the approach impractical for large annotator pools. Clustering is suggested but introduces a hyperparameter (number of clusters) and requires selecting a clustering algorithm; it may fail when experts are diverse (large number of clusters). Alternative dimensionality‑reduction or hierarchical clustering approaches are not discussed, nor is their computational cost analyzed.
- **Ambiguous notation and missing clarifications:** The data generation process and L2D objective (page 3) are unclear – in particular, whether **y** is ground truth or prediction, why **y** depends on expert annotations **t**, and how Eq. 1 computes log‑likelihood with hard labels from deterministic look‑up tables.
- **Brief and insufficient result discussion:** The reasons behind performance gains (e.g., why probabilistic L2D surpasses the best human expert even with 70% missing rate, especially at low coverage) are not explained. On MiceBone, the AUC‑coverage curve increases with higher missing rates; the paper mentions inconsistent expert behavior, but the mechanism by which missing annotations help is not elaborated. The possible regularizing effect of down‑weighting an expert (balanced workload) is only hinted at.
- **Assumption of static expert performance:** The model treats expert expertise as fixed, ignoring fast changes (e.g., fatigue) or slow changes (e.g., learning). It is unclear how the framework could be adapted to dynamic scenarios.
- **Trade‑off in deferral criterion:** The paper does not discuss whether deferring fewer cases or deferring only the most uncertain cases is preferable – an important consideration in high‑stakes domains like healthcare.
- **Missing practical details:** Source code is not provided (minor); experimental results could be strengthened by repeating runs to estimate uncertainty and by reporting runtimes of proposed method versus baselines.
- **Minor presentation issues:** No “Conclusion” section; “slightly‑similar” is an odd phrase; “perfect balanced workload” statement is questionable (equal distribution is only ideal if all experts are equally competent); formatting (e.g., “50 %” instead of “50%”); Table 1 results have four decimal digits without justification of such precision. The reference to Meehl (1954) cannot support claims about modern machine‑learning models.
- **One reviewer found no significant weaknesses**, but noted the minor issues above; the other reviewers all flagged the scalability and discussion shortcomings.
