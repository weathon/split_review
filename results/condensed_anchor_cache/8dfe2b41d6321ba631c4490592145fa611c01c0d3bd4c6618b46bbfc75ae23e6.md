## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a diffusion-based uncertainty-aware framework (UA-P2E) for converting PPG signals to ECG signals, intended to improve cardiovascular diagnosis. By generating multiple candidate ECG waveforms per PPG input, the method aims to capture the inherent uncertainty of the ill-posed conversion. While the theoretical foundation and multi-solution approach are novel, reviewers raise concerns about synthetic data reliance, limited and missing baseline comparisons, insufficient dataset diversity, and a fundamental limitation regarding whether PPG contains sufficient information for reliable ECG reconstruction.

### Strengths
- Originality and novelty: The work is the first to apply a diffusion-based, multi-solution approach to the PPG-to-ECG conversion, generating multiple possible ECG signals instead of a single deterministic solution. This directly addresses the ill-posed nature of the task.
- Strong theoretical foundation: The paper provides a mathematical proof of the optimality of the Expected Score Classifier (ESC), including detailed equations (Theorem 3.1), which reassures credibility.
- Comprehensive evaluation: Extensive experiments across 11 cardiovascular conditions, with multiple performance metrics and ablation studies, demonstrate the model’s generalizability and robustness. Uncertainty quantification adds to interpretability.
- Clear writing and presentation: The paper is well-structured with relevant tables, figures, and intuitive explanations, making the methodology easy to follow.
- Uncertainty quantification: Explicit modeling of conversion uncertainty enhances interpretability and practical reliability.
- Potential for wearable ECG: The proposed approach could extend the capabilities of existing PPG-based wearable monitoring solutions.

### Weaknesses
- Dependence on synthetic PPG data (Reviewer 1, strong concern): The evaluation heavily relies on synthetic PPG data, especially for augmenting the CinC dataset (Section 5.2). The generation process and its validation are insufficiently detailed; no thorough analysis of differences between real and synthetic PPG signals (beyond classification performance) is provided. This limits confidence in real-world applicability.
- Insufficient baseline comparisons (Reviewers 1, 2, 3): Only two baselines (CardioGAN and RDDM) are compared (Table 1). The paper does not justify why other relevant state-of-the-art methods—especially recent diffusion-based or generative approaches (e.g., ArXiv:2309.15375, 2012.04949, 2204.11795, 2101.02362)—were excluded. Moreover, the direct implementations of CardioGAN and RDDM are not used to benchmark classification performance, only signal reconstruction; classification metrics from those baselines are missing, making comparisons possibly unfair due to different training procedures.
- Potential for synthetic artifacts (Reviewer 1): The model may produce hallucinations or unrealistic ECG features. There is no quantitative assessment of morphological fidelity or frequency content (e.g., power spectral density comparison) to rule out artifacts.
- Limited dataset diversity (Reviewers 1, 2): The study uses only paired PPG-ECG from CinC and MIMIC-III. No dataset from wearable devices (despite the motivation) or from different clinical settings, sensor types, or demographic groups is used. The impact of data acquisition protocols and patient demographics is not explored.
- Missing details about the CinC database (Reviewers 1, 2, 3): The paper does not specify which exact database from PhysioNet's CinC collection was used. The rationale for choosing the 11 specific anomaly types is not clarified.
- Missing data summary (Reviewer 2): The number of samples used for training/testing the PPG conversion and classification models, along with class distributions, is not provided.
- Insufficient number of random seeds (Reviewer 2, strong concern): Only 3 random seeds are used to generate ECG signals from PPG. Given the stochastic nature of diffusion models, it is unclear whether 3 seeds capture the full variability; the reported standard deviation may be across seeds or across samples, and its statistical significance is not discussed.
- Potential train/test contamination (Reviewer 2): The paper does not state whether segments from the same recording appear in both training and test sets, which could overestimate generalization.
- Overfitting risk not addressed (Reviewer 1): The complexity of diffusion models relative to dataset size raises concerns; no explicit measures against overfitting are discussed.
- Suboptimal classification performance (Reviewer 3, minority negative view): Figure 3 shows that PPG-derived ECG classification significantly trails original ECG classification. This suggests that the required information for detecting certain arrhythmias may be inherently missing in PPG, raising a fundamental limitation: if PPG signals lack sufficient information, generating ECG cannot recover it. The paper’s classification results also appear suboptimal compared to state-of-the-art ECG classification models.
- Need for additional classification metrics (Reviewer 2): Only AURC is reported for classification; due to class imbalance, metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score should be provided to improve clinical relevance and enable fair comparison with other works.
- Missing analysis of specific conditions beyond AF (Reviewers 2, 3): Appendix G focuses only on Atrial Fibrillation, which is relatively easier to detect; results for other conditions are needed. The feasibility of detecting pacing rhythm from PPG is questioned.
