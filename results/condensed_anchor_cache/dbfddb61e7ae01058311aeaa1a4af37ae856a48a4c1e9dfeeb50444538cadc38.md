## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper addresses federated zeroth-order optimization and proposes FZooS, which uses trajectory-informed Gaussian process (GP) surrogates for gradient estimation and an adaptive gradient correction (inspired by SCAFFOLD) to reduce disparity between local and global updates. The algorithm aims to improve query and communication efficiency. Theoretical convergence is given for strongly convex, convex, and non-convex cases. Experiments include a synthetic task, federated black-box adversarial attack, and federated non-differentiable metric optimization. The overall assessment is mixed: one reviewer (score 5) found the work novel and clearly presented, while two others (score 3) acknowledged theoretical and experimental contributions but raised substantial concerns about assumptions, computational cost, and fairness of comparisons.

### Strengths
- Novel combination of orthogonal ideas: approximating local gradients via the posterior mean of a GP (Shu et al. 2023), gradient correction from SCAFFOLD (Karimireddy et al. 2020a), and random Fourier feature approximation (Rahimi & Recht 2007). (R1)
- Clear organization and motivation for each algorithm component. (R1, R3)
- Strong theoretical analysis covering strongly convex, convex, and non-convex cases; promising convergence results. (R1, R2)
- Adaptive gradient correction technique is interesting on its own and could benefit the federated optimization community. (R3)
- Zeroth-order method not based on finite differences or directional derivatives, providing a novel perspective for federated learning. (R3)
- Experiments on multiple real-world tasks demonstrate improved query and communication efficiency over existing zeroth-order baselines. (R1, R2)

*One reviewer (score 5) was particularly positive about the novelty and clarity; the other two (score 3) also valued the theory and results but noted important limitations.*

### Weaknesses
- **Assumptions and problem formulation not standard for federated learning**  
  The problem is restricted to \([0,1]^d\) with \(|f_i(x)|\le1\), whereas most federated optimization works use \(\mathbb{R}^d\); the paper does not justify this restriction. (R2, R3)  
  The algorithm then updates parameters in \(\mathbb{R}^d\), creating a mismatch. (R2)  
  The assumption that each local function \(f_i\) is drawn from a Gaussian process with \(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\) noise is uncommon in federated optimization; it is not verified in the experiments, and no comparison to federated Bayesian optimization is provided. (R3)  
  No explanation is given for when an average of GP samples would be strongly convex or convex. (R3)

- **Computational and storage costs are high but not directly compared**  
  Storing the history of iterates requires \(O(RTd)\) bits per client, which becomes prohibitive for large \(d\) (e.g., millions). (R1)  
  Inverting a kernel matrix of size up to \(RT \times RT\) at every iteration is computationally heavy. (R1, R3)  
  The numerical results are only plotted against the number of communication rounds, ignoring vastly different per-round computation; the paper should include total runtime or FLOPS comparisons. (R1, R3)  
  Algorithm 2 communicates a vector of size at least \(1/\epsilon^2\) per round, while existing zeroth-order methods often only need two scalars (function value and random seed) when \(Q=1\); the total communication cost for FZooS in the convex case is \(MR=1/\epsilon^5\) – it is unclear how this compares to existing methods. (R3)

- **Partial client participation not addressed**  
  The analysis and experiments assume full client participation. In realistic partial-participation settings, each client would have fewer stored iterates, and the distances between iterates would be larger, likely exacerbating the gradient approximation error. (R1)

- **Bounded heterogeneity assumption still present after gradient correction**  
  Theorem 2 retains a dependence on the heterogeneity bound \(G\), which seems to defeat the purpose of the gradient correction (SCAFFOLD removes such dependence). (R1)

- **Adaptive gradient correction lacks theoretical justification**  
  The paper does not compare the convergence rate of the proposed adaptive correction (with variable \(\gamma\)) against the standard SCAFFOLD correction (e.g., \(\gamma=1\)). A clearer discussion of the benefit of the adaptive choice is needed. (R1)  
  The setting of \(\gamma\) requires knowledge of \(G\) (the heterogeneity bound); how to estimate \(G\) in practice is not explained. (R1, R3)

- **Theoretical results need clarification**  
  The convergence error \(\epsilon\) is never formally defined (e.g., is it \(F-F^*\) for convex and \(\|\nabla F\|^2\) for non-convex?). (R3)  
  The statement that “\(\rho<1\) is likely to be satisfied” is ambiguous: does this hold with high probability or under additional conditions? It also affects the validity of Theorem 2, which only holds with constant probability. (R3)  
  Linear speedup with respect to the number of clients \(N\) is not observed in Theorem 2 (the number of rounds \(R\) does not decrease as \(N\) increases). (R1)  
  Dependence on dimension \(d\) appears only in the convex case and not in the strongly convex or non-convex cases; also, the dependency \(M=O(d^2)\) makes communication comparable to a second‑order method. Can this be improved? (R1, R3)

- **Experiment scale is small**  
  The experiments use only 5–7 clients; the results would be more convincing with larger systems (e.g., 50–100 clients). (R2)

- **Missing details and unclear implementation choices**  
  Assumptions for Theorem 2 are scattered and should be collected in one formal environment. (R1)  
  It is unclear how FedProx and SCAFFOLD are adapted to the zeroth-order setting – are standard query-based gradient estimates used? What are their iterative local updates and global aggregation in terms of model parameters? (R1, R2)  
  FedProx’s poor performance is not explained; tuning its \(\mu\) parameter should make it at least as good as FedAvg. (R1)  
  The “query around \(x_r\)” step in Algorithm 2 is not connected to the computation of \(\mathbf{w}_{r,T}^{(i)}\), which does not depend on \(x_r\). (R1)  
  The paper states that \(x_r\) should be sent first and then \(\mathbf{w}_r\); does this order matter? (R3)  
  How exactly is zeroth-order optimization used in the federated black-box adversarial attack? (R1)  
  Minor issues: use of “so” at sentence start, typo “urther” (should be “further”) on page 7. (R3)
