## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a pretraining scheme to reduce LLM training costs by using patch-level training in the initial stage, where multiple consecutive tokens are aggregated into a "patch" as the fundamental text unit. The model is trained on shorter sequences of patches to predict the next patch, then switches to standard token-level training. Experiments on model sizes from 370M to 2.7B parameters show training cost reduction of up to 50% with little to no performance degradation on perplexity and downstream tasks.

### Strengths

1. **Important and timely problem**: Reducing LLM training costs is a critical research question; the motivation and improvement objective are clearly stated.
2. **Simple, novel, and easy to implement**: The method introduces no extra parameters and is straightforward to incorporate into existing training pipelines. Several reviewers noted its potential for broad adoption in the community and industry if validated at scale.
3. **Clear and reproducible description**: The paper provides adequate details to reproduce the work.
4. **Robust cost savings across settings**: The 50% cost reduction is consistently reported across multiple model sizes (370M–2.7B) and a range of NLP tasks, with minimal or no performance loss compared to token-level training.
5. **Extensive ablations and analyses**: Experiments explore the fraction of patch-level training (λ), patch size (K), model size, context length, alternative patch computation methods (e.g., averaging vs. concatenation), and both streaming and multi-epoch training scenarios.
6. **Thorough evaluation**: Wide coverage of benchmarks to evaluate final model performance.

### Weaknesses

1. **Scalability concerns**: All experiments are limited to models under 3B parameters. Multiple reviewers note that it is unclear whether the method maintains stability and convergence at larger scales (e.g., several billions), where issues such as increased gradient variance or the need for different optimization strategies may arise. A minority reviewer (Reviewer 1) was particularly concerned that the optimal hyperparameters (λ, K) may not transfer and that the paper lacks discussion on training dynamics at scale.
2. **Tokenizer dependence not explored**: The optimal λ and patch size K may be sensitive to the tokenizer (e.g., vocabulary size and subword segmentation). The interaction between patch size, vocabulary size, and the resulting information density within each patch is not analyzed. The paper does not discuss how patch-level training affects handling of subword tokenization, out-of-vocabulary words, or morphological variations.
3. **Missing practical speed measurements**: No actual speed improvements (e.g., tokens per second) are reported on real hardware. Since the model still computes all token projections and the expensive softmax over the vocabulary, it is unclear whether the speedup comes solely from reduced sequence length or other factors (Reviewer 2). This measurement would be valuable to quantify the practical benefit.
4. **Unclear conclusions from architecture ablation**: In the architecture ablation, there are observations suggesting that subsequent token-level training may not work well in some use cases. This should be better highlighted to avoid overselling the method (Reviewer 2).
5. **PPL analysis confounded by exponential scale**: Tables 3 and 4 report perplexity differences (e.g., ~11 vs. ~10, then ~8 vs. ~7). PPL is an exponential function of cross-entropy, so the actual entropy difference between these values is not linear; the authors are encouraged to report entropy instead for that analysis (Reviewer 3).
6. **Context length ablation limited to small model**: Line 266 concludes that context length in patch-level training has little effect (KT performs as well as T), but this is based only on a 370M model. It is uncertain whether higher-capacity models would better exploit longer contexts. A comparison on a larger model is needed (Reviewer 3).
7. **Lack of positional information within patches**: Equation (3) appears to treat a patch as a bag of tokens without order. Does the training objective have any notion of which token position is being predicted within a patch? Contrasting with an ordered setting would be informative (Reviewer 3).
8. **Multi-stage gradual decrease of K not considered**: Section 3.5 analyzes a two-stage procedure (K>1 then K=1). A more general multi-stage setting where K is progressively decreased (e.g., starting very large and gradually reducing) could be explored (Reviewer 3).
9. **Unclear implementation of output head for next-patch prediction**: Line 156 states "we maintain a single output head and make its prediction cover all tokens in the next patch." It is unclear whether this implies a vocabulary size of K×|V| or another mechanism (Reviewer 4). Additionally, the notation in Equation 3 uses ‘p’ for both probabilities and patches, which should be disambiguated.
10. **Linear mapping for patch embedding conversion**: In Section 3.7, token embeddings are aggregated into patch embeddings via averaging (or other linear operations) and a linear mapping is used to convert back. It may be that a more powerful transformation (e.g., additional transformer layers) would yield better results, and this ablation has not been performed (Reviewer 4).
11. **Mechanistic understanding**: Additional analysis/ablations to explain why patch-level training can sometimes improve performance (e.g., regularization effect) would strengthen the paper (Reviewer 4).
