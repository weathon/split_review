## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes MetaAgent, a framework that automatically generates multi-agent systems based on a finite state machine (FSM). Given a task description, MetaAgent designs agents (names, prompts, tools) and configures an FSM with states (each linked to an agent), condition verifiers, and listeners. After initial design, it iteratively refines the system using self-generated test queries. The FSM supports state traceback and is claimed to be more suitable for tool-using. Experiments are conducted on three tasks: ML-bench (machine learning benchmark), six software engineering tasks, and Trivial Creative Writing (NLP). Results show MetaAgent outperforms other auto-designed methods and matches or surpasses human-designed systems. Ablation studies assess the roles of tool usage, iteration, and state traceback.

Reviewer ratings: 5, 6, 3, 3. Two reviewers (R1, R2) are more positive; two (R3, R4) are more critical.

### Strengths

1. **Addresses important, timely problem.** The paper tackles automating the design of multi-agent systems without heavy human-coded task-specific implementation, which is key to generalization across diverse tasks (R1, strengths #1; R3, strength #1).

2. **Innovative combination of components.** The framework effectively merges initial agent design, an FSM for task execution, and self-iteration via LLM-generated test queries. This combination is comprehensive and adaptive (R1, strength #2). R2 notes the logic is clear and advantages over prior work are well explained (R2, strength #1).

3. **FSM enables state traceback and self-refinement.** Introducing FSM for multi-agent systems brings robustness through error-handling and backtracking (R2, strength #2; R1, strength #2 mentions traceback). R4 also finds applying FSM to agentic workflow interesting and intuitive (R4, strength #1).

4. **No external training data required.** MetaAgent refines itself without carefully handcrafted test data or in-bag training (R2, strength #3). This is seen as a practical benefit.

5. **Preliminary experimental results show promise.** The method performs better than other auto-designed frameworks and achieves competitive results against human-designed systems across coding and NLP tasks (R1, strength #3). Experiments cover ML bench, software development, and NLP tasks, providing a reasonable testbed (R4, strength #2). R2 notes significant gains e.g., on ML bench versus AutoAgents (R2, question 3).

### Weaknesses

1. **Limited experimental scope and generalization concerns.** Only two coding tasks and one NLP task are evaluated, which may be subject to data leakage (common ML datasets likely seen in LLM pretraining). Stronger evidence of generalization requires broader benchmarks like AgentBench and SWE-bench (R1, weakness #1). The six software engineering tasks lack discussion of source, data collection, and formulation, and their evaluation criteria (e.g., naive test-pass conditions) are simplistic, making comparisons questionable (R3, weakness #2). R1 also notes a missing agentless baseline (hard-coded procedural LLM solver with tools) in all tasks (R1, question 2). *Reviewer disagreement: R1 and R2 are more positive about the results, while R3 and R4 question the experimental rigor.*

2. **Lack of technical details and reproducibility.** The implementation is insufficiently described:
   - It is only inferred that FSM generation and iterative refinement are done by prompting an LLM, but the model used (e.g., GPT-4o version) and settings (in-context examples, hyperparameters) are not stated (R3, weakness #1; R4, weakness #2).
   - How agents are designed (prompting or training strategy) is unclear (R3, weakness #1).
   - Test case generation and corner case creation lack specifics (“how is ‘edge’ defined?” – R2, question 1).
   - In software development tasks, how a test is determined as passed is not specified (R4, weakness #2).
   - Figure 2’s implementation of core modules (prompting vs. workflow) is ambiguous (R4, weakness #2). Reproducibility is hindered.

3. **Unclear motivation and questionable contribution of FSM.** The paper’s motivation—addressing lack of tool support, external data, and inflexible communication—does not clearly explain why FSM is suitable for solving these problems. The overall system feels ad-hoc, with FSM being only one component among many (tool-using, self-iteration, condition verification) that could be decoupled. The ablation study (section 4.3) confirms that removing any of these three modules harms performance, reinforcing the view that the framework is over-designed and trivial (R4, weakness #1). R3 questions why FSM is better than non-deterministic approaches like AutoGen (which uses an LLM chat manager for dynamic workflows) and calls for an intrinsic evaluation comparing automatically generated FSMs to expert-designed ones (R3, weakness #1). R2 asks a related question: line 453 says tool-using is a crucial part of FSM—why? (R4, question 1). *These points are echoed by multiple reviewers.*

4. **Dependence on the base LLM’s capability.** The method heavily relies on the designer model to decompose tasks and assign agents at an appropriate level of granularity; errors there propagate to the whole system (R2, weakness #1). R4 also notes the prompts in Appendix D are complex and specific, raising concerns about stability to prompt changes (R4, weakness #5).

5. **Scalability not demonstrated.** The case study shows only two or three states, raising concerns about scaling to more complex tasks (e.g., real-world software engineering with large repositories) (R1, weakness #2). No runtime stats (e.g., number of agents, states, backtrace frequency, iteration rounds) are provided to understand the generated systems’ complexity (R1, question 3; R2, question 4 asks about applying to other questions in the same domain).

6. **Missing comparisons.** Baselines such as EvoAgent are cited but not included in ML/SWE experiments (R3, weakness #2). The agentless baseline (hard-coded procedural solver with tools) is missing from all experiments (R1, question 2).

7. **Writing and presentation issues.**
   - In-text citation style is incorrect (use of `\citet` vs `\citep`), multiple typos and grammar problems impede understanding (R3, questions 1–3). Examples: missing parentheses in lines 39–40 (R2, weakness #2), “We” in line 300 (R2, weakness #2), “interation” (R3), etc.
   - Figure 1 is hard to follow: it involves agents (e.g., product manager, information collector) not explained in the main text, and its correspondence to section 3.2 is unclear (R4, weakness #3). Figure 2 lacks a clear breakdown of presented information (R4, weakness #3). Table captions should be at the top, not bottom (R3, question 3).

8. **Lack of inference cost analysis.** MetaAgent introduces extra inference for agent design, self-iteration, condition verification, etc. No systematic discussion of cost is provided (R4, weakness #4). R1 also asks for runtime stats (backtrace, iteration frequency) (R1, question 3).

9. **Questions on methodology details.**
   - How are edge cases created in test case generation? (R2, question 1)
   - How do states refine when encountering mistakes? Do they change instructions or tools? (R2, question 2)
   - Why does MetaAgent perform significantly better than AutoAgents (0.0) on ML bench? (R2, question 3)
   - How can MetaAgent be applied to other questions in the same task domain without re-refining? (R2, question 4)
   - Is the method stable under minor prompt changes given the complex appendix prompts? (R4, weakness #5)
