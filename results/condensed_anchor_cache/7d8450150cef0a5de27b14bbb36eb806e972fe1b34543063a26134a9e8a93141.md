## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a meta-learning framework that recommends compression methods (pruning, quantization) for deep neural networks under given resource, performance, and efficiency constraints. It uses a compression accuracy predictor and, when limited test data is available, diffusion models to generate synthetic samples. Evaluated on CIFAR-10 with several architectures and compression algorithms, the top recommendation achieves a 1% drop in average accuracy loss vs. the optimal exhaustive choice, compared to 25% drop for a single best compression method. Reviewers find the idea intriguing but raise major concerns about limited novelty, missing comparisons to existing approaches (Bayesian optimization, DNAS, OFA, recent SOTA), lack of hyper‑parameter consideration, poor scalability to large datasets, high training cost (8 days), outdated compression methods, and presentation issues.

### Strengths
- The concept of using a generative model (diffusion) to augment meta‑learning when no unseen test samples are available is considered promising (Reviewer 1; though Reviewer 1 notes this may be less important in practice where data can be obtained).
- The motivation is clear and the paper is easy to read (Reviewer 2).
- The overall idea (learning a compression accuracy predictor to recommend methods) is intriguing and has potential to become a strong contribution (Reviewer 3).
- The formulation as an optimization problem solved via boosted decision trees can provide a solution even when new data are not accessible (Reviewer 4).
- Empirical results show good performance on CIFAR-10, with only a 1% accuracy loss vs. exhaustive search (Reviewers 1, 4).
- Theoretical proof of the meta‑learning optimization is provided (Reviewer 4).
- The evaluation covers a diverse range of architectures and a large training dataset is created for the predictor (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty** – the paper does not propose a new meta‑learning algorithm nor a new compression method; the main novelty appears to be the application of meta‑learning to model compression, which needs experimental evidence showing it is better than existing approaches such as multi‑objective Bayesian optimization [1], DNAS, or OFA (Reviewer 1).  A second reviewer (Reviewer 4) describes the method as “a simple machine learning problem, which is easy to prove in a certain hypothesis”.
- **Missing contextualization** – it is unclear how this work compares to [1] (which yields an entire Pareto frontier for architecture + compression hyper‑parameters) (Reviewer 1).
- **Unclear choice of accuracy predictor** – the paper does not explain why this specific predictor design is chosen, nor does it vary compression strategies in experiments, limiting understanding of generalizability (Reviewer 2).
- **Generalizability to modern architectures** – the experiments do not include transformers or other recent modeling techniques; it is not clear how the results would hold for newer architectures (Reviewer 2).
- **Scalability concerns** – Fig. 7 shows poor performance on large‑scale datasets (significant gap vs. exhaustive search), raising doubts about robustness and scalability (Reviewer 4).  Additionally, the training cost is high (8 days), which may not save time compared to directly applying SOTA compression methods (Reviewer 4).  A third reviewer asks whether the construction overhead becomes excessive for larger datasets like ImageNet‑1000 (Reviewer 3).
- **Hypersensitivity to ignored hyper‑parameters** – the work only considers target sparsity and quantization levels, but the learning rate, batch size, and number of epochs used during fine‑tuning after compression can drastically affect final performance; these are not considered (Reviewer 4).
- **Simplistic treatment of pruning and quantization** – the paper considers pruning and quantization separately and merges them with a simple criterion, ignoring real‑world serial interactions (e.g., quantization before pruning vs. reverse order) (Reviewer 4).
- **Outdated compression methods** – the tested pruning and quantization methods are out‑of‑date; other categories like low‑rank matrix and tensor decomposition are missing; no comparison with SOTA compression techniques published after 2021 (adaptive quantization, structured pruning, tensor‑train decomposition) is provided (Reviewer 4).
- **No comparison with recent SOTA compression methods that directly compress models on unseen datasets** – this makes it difficult to assess the competitiveness of the proposed approach (Reviewer 4).
- **Presentation issues** – the introduction lacks citations (except the first paragraph), hindering comprehensibility (Reviewer 3).  Figure 2a shows a discrepancy: metadata extraction lacks information from the compressed classifier, contradicting the main text (Reviewer 3).  The title “Meta Compression: Learning to compress Deep Neural Networks” is misleading because the paper focuses on *choosing* compression methods, not learning to compress itself (Reviewer 3).
- **Unclear meta‑features** – the paper does not elaborate on what the “meta features” are (Reviewer 3).
- **Static ERM strategy** – one reviewer was confused about what exactly is the static ERM strategy (Reviewer 1, question).
- **Heuristic information guarantee** – the method relies on heuristic information from existing data; it is not guaranteed to work well on a new dataset compared to directly compressing and tuning on that dataset (Reviewer 4, question).
- **Disagreement on positive aspects** – while Reviewer 1 finds the generative model idea a great solution for limited data, they also note it is less important in real‑world settings because new data can usually be obtained. Reviewer 4 is more critical overall and confident in the assessment (confidence 5), whereas Reviewers 2 and 3 are moderately confident.
