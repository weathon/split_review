## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces PREF-RMAB, a restless multi-armed bandit model with pairwise preference feedback instead of scalar rewards, motivated by applications like app marketing and CPAP treatment. The authors propose the Direct Online Preference Learning (DOPL) algorithm, which uses a reference arm and reference state to convert preference probabilities into relative reward measures, applies an LP-based index policy, and achieves a theoretical regret bound of \(\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T\ln T})\). Numerical experiments compare against baselines MLE_WIBQL, MLE_LP, and others. The problem setup is considered novel by three reviewers (2,3,4), but reviewer 1 finds it incremental and very similar to duelling bandits with state transitions.

### Strengths
- First work to integrate preference feedback (motivated by RLHF) into the RMAB framework (R2, R3).
- DOPL achieves \(\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T\ln T})\) regret with theoretical analysis – the first such bound for RMAB with preference feedback (R2, R4).
- Clever transformation: expressing the reward of any arm/state as a function of preference probability relative to a reference arm and reference state, and using an intermediate comparator to infer preferences without direct comparison, reducing complexity to that of direct reward observation (R3).
- The relaxed LP-based direct index policy addresses computational intractability (R2).
- Lemmas 4 and 5 are particularly interesting and have potential to inform future algorithmic design (R4).
- Some proof steps are novel and not typical in bandit/RL literature (R1).
- Writing is clean and easy to follow (R2); however, this is contested by R1 and R4 (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and motivation**: The problem setup is incremental and very similar to duelling bandits with state transitions (R1). A fundamental question remains whether a preference-based model can outperform direct reward estimation in the tabular MDP setting studied, where rewards can be estimated through multiple sampling (R3). Moreover, the real-world examples (app marketing, CPAP treatment) are unconvincing: e.g., user state transitions in app marketing do not satisfy the memoryless Markov property, and time tracking is ambiguous (R4).
- **Writing and presentation**: 
  - The introduction is too wordy; more discussion of related literature is needed (R1).
  - Notation issues: use of \(\epsilon\) for failure probability instead of common \(\delta\) in bandit/RL theory (R1). Eq. (2) notation is confusing (R4). The objective function in Eq. (6) is hard to follow; \(\mu^{\pi}\) and \(\mu_n\) should be defined first (R4).
  - The main theorem (Theorem 1) appears too late (Page 8); suggest rearranging Sections 4 and 5 (R1). Lemmas 4 and 5 are more important than Lemmas 1 and 2 and could be made propositions (R4). Lemma 2 could be improved by defining \(Q_n(s)\) first (R4).
  - More detail on the composition of the preference matrix \(\mathbf{F}\) would improve clarity (R4).
  - Minority view: R2 found the writing clean and easy to follow, contradicting the above presentation criticisms.
- **Algorithm efficiency**:
  - Estimating the whole preference matrix \(\mathbf{F}\) in DOPL requires large computational cost; a thorough discussion of computational complexity is needed (R2).
  - Algorithm 3 (in Section C.3) makes inefficient use of data: at each step, \((B-1)\) random duels produce many comparison results, but only the comparison with a single intermediate column \(j\) is used to infer preference between \(s_n\) and the reference state. This wastes available information. Strategic selection of duel pairs could improve performance (R3).
  - The important results (Lemmas 4 and 5) are based on Lemma 1, which uses only a single reference arm and a single reference state. In the RMAB setting, the decision maker selects \(B\) arms each time slot; the use of a single reference arm seems inconsistent (R4).
- **Theoretical concerns and potential errors**:
  - The proof of Lemma 6’s use of Lemma 11 to argue term0 is negative is unclear; \(\pi^*\) and \(\tilde{\pi}\) do not appear aligned (R3).
  - In the proof following Eq. (49), the inequality \(\sum_{k=1}^K \sqrt{1/Z_n^k(s,a)} \leq \sqrt{Z_n^K(s,a)}\) appears incorrect because a counterexample with no revisits yields \(K > 1\) (R3).
  - The statement “\(\sum_{n=1}^N \sum_{(s,a)} Z_n^T(s,a) \leq NT\)” (lines 1398-1399) should instead be equality \(= T\); otherwise, subsequent inequalities may not hold. Similarly, the sentence from lines 1503-1505 causes confusion (R3).
  - In Eq. (4), the balance between \(\epsilon=o(1)\) (leading to arbitrarily large confidence width) and \(\epsilon=\Theta(1)\) (very small probability) is not discussed; guidance on setting \(\epsilon\) would be helpful (R4).
  - Reviewers did not fully check proof correctness. R1 noted some novel steps but did not verify correctness; R3 raised multiple potential errors (see above).
- **Experiment discussion**: The baselines MLE_WIBQL and MLE_LP fail to achieve sublinear regret; a detailed discussion on why these algorithms underperform would provide valuable insights (R2).
- **Regret scaling**: The regret bound scales with \(\sqrt{|S|}\) (size of state space), which may be inefficient for large state spaces (R3).
- **Minor**: In Eq. (3), \(\pi^{opt}\) is defined as the solution of (1) with scalar rewards, but the footnote states regret is with respect to preference feedback – this appears contradictory (R3). In Eq. (46), the suboptimality decomposition does not include the step index \(h\) within an episode, raising clarification questions (R3).
