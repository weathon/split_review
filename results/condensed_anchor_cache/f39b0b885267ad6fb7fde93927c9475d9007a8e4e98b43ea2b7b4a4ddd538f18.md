## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Mask-DPO, a fine-grained factuality alignment method that uses sentence-level factuality masks in DPO to prevent learning from incorrect sentences in chosen responses and avoid penalizing correct sentences in rejected responses. Experiments show significant improvements on in-domain (ANAH test: Llama3.1-8B-Instruct 49.19% → 77.53%, surpassing Llama3.1-70B-Instruct's 53.44%) and out-of-domain (Biography FactScore: 30.29% → 39.39%) datasets. The authors also study generalization via scaling topics vs. questions and hypothesize an internal knowledge graph. Scores: 6,6,6,8,6. One reviewer (score 8) was notably more positive, praising simplicity and strong results; the others (score 6) found the method novel but had several reservations.

### Strengths
- **Novel fine-grained masking method:** Incorporates sentence-level factuality to denoise preference learning, addressing a key limitation of response-level DPO (R1, R3, R4, R5). The mathematical formulation is clear and well-supported (R1).
- **Strong empirical improvements:** Significant gains on factuality metrics for both in-domain and out-of-domain datasets, with Llama3.1-8B-Instruct surpassing the 70B model on ANAH (R2, R4). Results are consistent across multiple model families.
- **Insightful analysis of scaling:** Shows that scaling the number of topics is more effective than scaling the number of questions for factuality alignment, providing practical guidance for data construction (R2, R3).
- **Interesting hypothesis and proof-of-concept:** The proposed internal knowledge graph hypothesis and experiments (topic clustering, best-of-N analyses) offer a plausible explanation for generalization and open new research directions (R2, R3, R4, R5). R5 specifically found the hypotheses well-founded.
- **Simple and straightforward design:** The method is easy to implement and could be extended to other metrics beyond factuality (R4, R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Sentence-level annotation issues:** The method assumes sentences are atomic factual units, but sentences can contain mixed true/false information (e.g., clauses with conflicting facts). This can lead to inaccurate masks and biased preference scores, especially when sentence segmentation or fact placement varies (e.g., 10 short sentences vs. 3 long sentences) (R3, R4, R5). The authors do not discuss mitigation for such cases.
- **Insufficient comparison with other methods:** Only compared to vanilla DPO; missing comparisons with recent preference optimization methods that use finer granularity (TDPO, DPOP, SePO) (R1, R2) and with factuality-specific approaches (knowledge retrieval, verification) (R2). Missing references to long-form factuality work (Wei et al., 2024; Jeong et al., 2024) (R3).
- **Concerns about response coherence and quality:** Masking sentences in training responses may disrupt overall coherence, as preference learning is applied to incomplete responses. The paper focuses on factuality but does not evaluate potential degradation in fluency, completeness, or harmful generation (R3). Standard benchmarks (e.g., math reasoning, toxicity) are lacking (R1).
- **Weak theoretical and empirical support for the internal knowledge graph hypothesis:** The hypothesis lacks rigorous theoretical grounding (e.g., link to representation learning) and direct evidence (e.g., visualization of graph structure, graph-based metrics). The proof-of-concept experiments (ablation, best-of-N) are indirect and do not convincingly validate Corollary 2 – it is unclear if the same conclusions would hold if the graph were defined over questions rather than topics (R2, R5). Use of alternative embeddings (Table 5) is not justified (R5). The description of the proof-of-concept (embedding details, clustering procedure, selection of near/far groups) is insufficiently detailed (R3).
- **Missing experimental details and robustness checks:** No statistical significance tests or bootstrap resampling reported (R1). Temperature hyperparameter is fixed at 0.8; no ablation across temperature values (R1). The ranking/pairwise selection process and prompts used are not fully described (R3). The ablation in Table 3 is unclear – it seems to test generator model quality but does not separate out distribution effects already known from DPO literature (R4). No evaluation on shorter QA-style tasks; experiments are limited to long-form generation (R5). Typos and grammatical errors (e.g., “inaccuracies facts”, “un-preferred”, “reward hackers”) (R2, R4). The name “Mask-DPO” is too generic and not specific to factuality (R4).
