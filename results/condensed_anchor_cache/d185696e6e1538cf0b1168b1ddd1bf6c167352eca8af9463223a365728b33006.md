## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a theoretical analysis of pre-trained representation learning for decision-making from video data (without actions). It studies two settings: iid observation noise, and harder exogenous (temporally correlated) noise. Three methods are analyzed (autoencoding, temporal contrastive learning, forward modeling) in a block MDP framework. Upper bounds are proved for temporal contrastive learning and forward modeling under iid noise, showing polynomial sample complexity for downstream RL. A lower bound shows that with exogenous noise the sample complexity can be exponentially worse than learning from action-labeled trajectories. Experiments on GridWorld and ViZDoom partially validate the theory. The work is generally seen as novel and well-written, though some theoretical gaps and experimental limitations are noted.

### Strengths
- First theoretical analysis of principled representation learning from videos for RL (Reviewers 1, 4).
- Well-organized and clearly written (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).
- Experiments on two visual domains (GridWorld and ViZDoom) support theoretical findings (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- Assumptions are thoroughly explained and justified (Reviewer 4).
- Multiple representation learning approaches compared: VQ-VAE (autoencoding), temporal contrastive learning, forward modeling, and ACRO (action-labeled baseline) (Reviewer 4).
- Addresses a significant problem of broad interest to the RL community (Reviewer 4).
- Interesting theoretical conclusion that exogenous noise makes video pre-training exponentially harder than trajectory-based learning (Reviewer 2).
- Empirical results shed light on relative performance of temporal contrastive methods versus models that reconstruct images (Reviewer 2).
- Experiments follow good practice (Reviewer 3).
- Interesting research questions (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
**Theoretical gaps**
- No theoretical analysis is provided for autoencoder-based approaches (only empirical). Missing formal treatment of representational capacity, conditions for useful representation learning, effect of latent space dimensionality, and risk of learning spurious correlations (Reviewer 1).
- The observation that temporal contrastive learning fails under exogenous noise is only empirical; a formal proof or rigorous analysis of why the contrastive loss is more susceptible than forward modeling is lacking (Reviewer 1).
- The analysis is restricted to fixed representations trained on video alone; no analysis of fine-tuning or catastrophic forgetting (Reviewer 1).
- Theorems are not clearly connected to their interpretations. For Theorem 1, it is unclear what α (introduced as a bijection mapping) means, why a one-to-one mapping is needed, and whether φ*(x)=s assumes access to the true state. The deduction that the upper bound allows learning without online episodes scaling with ln|Φ| is not obvious from the theorem statement (Reviewer 3).
- For Theorem 2, the interpretation of the lower bound is also unclear from the theorem itself (Reviewer 3).
- Several definitions are confusing: Section 3 says “learn a decoder φ...” but this is an encoder; “endogenous state φ*(x)” is undefined; “ACRO achieves optimal performance across all tasks” is an overstatement (optimal not reached or known) (Reviewer 3).
- Abstract claims “proving our theoretical findings” via experiments; empirical evaluation can only illustrate, not prove (Reviewer 3).
- Missing analysis of whether the exponential hardness persists if the Margin Assumption (Assumption 3) also holds for exogenous noise transitions (Reviewer 1, Question 2).

**Experimental gaps**
- No experiments with varying levels of iid noise (analogous to Figure 6 for exogenous noise), making it hard to compare method degradation under the two noise types (Reviewers 1, 2).
- Experiments do not directly demonstrate the exponential sample complexity gap predicted by theory; instead they show gradual performance degradation (Reviewer 2).
- Evaluation limited to only two environments; more environments would strengthen validation (Reviewer 4).
- Experiments do not explore different types of exogenous noise (e.g., structured noise, noise correlated with agent’s actions) (Reviewer 2).
- Comparison between representation learning methods lacks sufficient detail to explain why some are more robust to exogenous noise (Reviewer 2).
- Some experimental results appear contrary to the theoretical claims: Figure 4 shows forward modeling and VAE handling exogenous noise, contradicting the lower bound; only in Figure 6 with very high noise does forward modeling fail (Reviewer 2).
- The experimental section lacks clearly stated hypotheses directly testable from the theoretical claims, making interpretation ambiguous (Reviewer 2).

**Clarity and typos**
- Section 4.2: “remembering all of them can easily overcome the network’s capacity focusing on the agent’s state can better help the future predictions” reads awkwardly (Reviewer 2).
- Typos: “probne” should be “prone”; “noisy-free” should be “noise-free”; missing parentheses: above Equation 2 and in Assumption 3 (Reviewers 2, 4). Justification for Assumption 3 has missing parenthesis in “P_for” (Reviewer 2).
