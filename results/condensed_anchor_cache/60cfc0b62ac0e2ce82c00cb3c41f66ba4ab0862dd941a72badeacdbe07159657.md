## Merged Review

### Summary
The authors propose a physics-informed weakly supervised learning (PIWSL) approach for training machine-learned interatomic potentials. Two additional loss terms are introduced: the Physics-Informed Taylor-expansion-based Consistency (PITC) loss and the Physics-Informed Spatial-Consistency (PISC) loss. The PITC loss generates approximate training data via first-order Taylor expansion of the energy, and the PISC loss encourages conservative force fields. The method is independent of the specific MLIP model and is tested on ANI, TiO₂, and rMD17 datasets, primarily with training sizes of 100–1000 samples. The approach is designed to be useful when only energy labels are available, and aims to improve accuracy in low-data regimes.

### Strengths
- The method is novel and physically motivated, addressing self-supervised/weakly-supervised strategies for MLIPs that are not very common.  
- The approach can be easily integrated into existing MLIP frameworks and is independent of the model architecture.  
- The writing is clear and the motivation is well presented.  
- Pre-training and self-supervised methods are important for MLIPs because reference data (especially forces) is computationally expensive.  
- The approach consistently shows better results in the presented experiments (e.g., energy and force errors often reduced by a factor of two on small datasets).  
- One reviewer (rating 8) finds the ideas inspiring and notes that the method can be useful when forces are unavailable at the reference level (e.g., complete-basis-set extrapolation).  
- The method could be applicable to training tasks with sparse data and reduces the need for pre-training with large datasets.

### Weaknesses
- **Small and unrepresentative datasets:** All experiments use extremely small datasets (100–1000 samples). Multiple reviewers note that in the current MLIP field, large datasets (OC20, MPTrj, SPICE, full ANI-1x, Materials Project, MD22) are standard. The improvement from PIWSL appears to decrease as dataset size increases (Fig. 2). On such small datasets, any form of regularization may improve performance; non-deep-learning models like sGDML (Table A9) already outperform all tested baseline and PIWSL models by a large margin.  
- **High absolute errors:** Even with PIWSL, energy errors remain above 10 kcal/mol in most cases (Table 1). The improvement of roughly 1 kcal/mol still leaves the models with huge errors, reflected in poor molecular dynamics simulation stability. Training models on larger, more common datasets would be needed to show that PIWSL truly helps in realistic applications.  
- **Insufficient MD validation and stability:**  
  - Simulations are run only for 3–6 ps (NVE ensemble), which is extremely short. Even simple molecules like aspirin show poor stability. Models are barely usable.  
  - One reviewer asks why NVE was chosen over NVT, which would allow better comparison with works like Fu et al. (2023). Another notes that NVE conservation is not guaranteed; the authors should test actual energy conservation.  
  - The potential energy curve of the C–H bond remains badly represented with and without PIWSL (error >1 kcal/mol for bond increase in plot d). The argument that PIWSL helps is weak when it still fails on such a basic curve.  
- **Missing comparisons and questionable baselines:**  
  - The authors should compare against models that take gradients of the energy to obtain conservative forces (since that is standard). It is unclear why PIWSL’s additional losses are needed when forces are available.  
  - The “NoisyNodes” baseline is not a valid comparison because it is designed only for equilibrium structures; DeNS would be a better alternative.  
  - The tested models (Equiformer, eSCN) are tuned for OC20; on molecular datasets, models like MACE often achieve errors <1 kcal/mol with only supervised energies and forces. Showing that PIWSL improves such better models would be more compelling.  
- **Training cost vs. convergence fairness:** PIWSL increases computational time significantly (2–3×). The authors stopped training after a predefined number of epochs. A fair comparison would either reduce epochs for PIWSL or increase epochs for the baseline, or use wall-clock time as stopping criterion. No learning curves are provided to verify convergence of the baseline.  
- **Overclaiming:** The statement “strong evidence of the effectiveness of PIWSL applied to bulk materials” (line 361) is based only on a TiO₂ dataset; this does not represent bulk materials broadly (e.g., Materials Project).  
- **Generalizability concerns:** The method may introduce biases or limitations when applied to complex systems with diverse chemical environments. Tests are limited to rMD17 and small molecules; no evaluation on large molecules (MD22) or realistic material datasets.  
- **Practical value:** Despite theoretical appeal, the improvement on the common rMD17 benchmark is minimal. The added complexity is not justified by the modest error reductions, especially compared to simpler data-driven approaches.  
- **Minority reviewer (rating 8) also identifies weaknesses:** Even the most positive reviewer notes that the C–H bond curve is still poor and that training time increases significantly. They suggest finding a “sweet spot” where PIWSL handles low data and conventional approach fails, and showing that both succeed with more data to demonstrate efficient data handling.
