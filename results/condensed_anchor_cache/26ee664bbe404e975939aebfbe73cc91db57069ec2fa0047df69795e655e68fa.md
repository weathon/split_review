## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces DeSGDA, a framework for domain adaptation in spiking graph networks (SGNs). It combines three components: degree-aware personalized spiking representation (thresholds based on node degree), adversarial feature distribution alignment across domains via membrane potential, and pseudo-label distillation from consistent predictions. The authors provide theoretical generalization bounds and experiments on benchmark graph datasets (e.g., PROTEINS, molecular/chemical graphs) showing DeSGDA outperforms baselines in accuracy and energy efficiency.  

### Strengths
1. **Pioneering problem setup and motivation** – First work to address domain adaptation in spiking graph networks, a previously neglected area.  
2. **Degree-aware spiking representation** – Customizing spiking thresholds based on node degree is an interesting and effective idea that enhances representation expressiveness.  
3. **Comprehensive evaluation** – Extensive experiments on multiple datasets with many competitive baselines (graph domain adaptation, spiking baselines) demonstrate the model’s superiority.  
4. **Theoretical analysis** – Provides generalization error bounds for the proposed domain adaptation framework.  
5. **Clear presentation** – Well-structured, fluent writing, and logical flow make the paper easy to follow (noted by multiple reviewers).  
6. **Pseudo-label design** – The approach to generate effective pseudo-labels across different distributions is clever (highlighted by Reviewer 4).  

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty** – The core techniques (adversarial distribution alignment, pseudo-labeling) are standard in domain adaptation; the contribution is largely combining them with spiking neural networks. Several reviewers noted the technical novelty is weak (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).  
2. **Experimental inconsistencies raise reproducibility concerns** – Reviewer 3 reported multiple mismatches:  
   - In Figure 2, for PROTEINS P1→P0 with GIN, the performance is ~0.75–0.76, but Table 1 reports 84.6, Table 9 reports 78.4, Table 12 reports 84.3. Similar mismatches exist for other settings.  
   - Figure 3 does not include a label for DeSGDA (the legend is missing).  
   *Reviewer 3 lowered confidence and rating (5) due to these issues; other reviewers gave 6.*  
3. **Unclear motivation for spiking in graph domain adaptation** – The paper lacks a strong explanation of why spiking neural networks are beneficial for this task, especially since accuracy often matters more than energy efficiency in molecular/chemical/protein data (Reviewer 1). No experiments on EEG data despite mentioning it in the introduction.  
4. **Degree-aware assumption insufficiently justified** – The assumption that higher-degree nodes trigger spikes more easily holds only for summation-based aggregation; it is unclear whether it extends to weighted aggregation methods (e.g., GAT, GCN). Additional justification is needed (Reviewers 1, 3). Additionally, other structural/feature factors beyond degree may affect domain shift.  
5. **Missing comparisons and analyses** –  
   - Why does the simple WL subtree kernel often outperform well-designed graph domain adaptation methods (Tables 1, 2)? Further explanation is needed.  
   - What is the effect of replacing the spiking backbone with standard GNNs (e.g., GIN, GCN) while keeping the adversarial alignment and pseudo-labeling?  
   - The paper should compare training time and memory consumption against graph domain adaptation methods, not just energy efficiency.  
6. **Definition missing** – The threshold parameter \(V_{th}\) is not defined (Reviewer 1).  
7. **Limited experimental scope** – All experiments are binary classification; the model’s performance in multi-class scenarios or more complex domain shifts is not evaluated.  
8. **No analysis of why DeSGDA outperforms specific graph DA methods** (e.g., DEAL, CoCo, A2GNN) – It would be beneficial to explain how the spiking-specific components (degree awareness) provide an advantage over standard graph domain adaptation.  
9. **Minor presentation issues** – Figure 1 is described as too simplistic (Reviewer 4). A theoretical analysis specifically for the degree-aware component (beyond overall bounds) is suggested as future work (Reviewer 3).
