## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates whether maximum softmax probabilities (MSPs) from chat-fine-tuned LLMs are calibrated on multiple-choice QA, finding they are consistently miscalibrated (overconfident) across 14 models. However, MSPs still predict answer correctness: wrong answers tend to have smaller MSPs than correct ones, and this predictive power correlates strongly with QA accuracy, while calibration error does not. A proof-of-concept abstention method using MSP thresholds (requiring only ~20 labeled examples) improves performance. The paper distinguishes calibration from correctness prediction and covers many models. Reviewers overall found the question interesting and the findings useful, but raised substantial concerns about experimental details, presentation, and scope.

### Strengths
- Investigates a well-motivated and important question: whether MSPs of chat-fine-tuned LLMs are calibrated and whether they can predict correctness.
- Studies 14 LLMs, enabling broader generalization than prior work.
- Clearly differentiates between calibration and correctness prediction, deriving distinct conclusions about their relationship to QA accuracy.
- Shows that even though models are miscalibrated, practical correctness prediction is possible, and demonstrates an efficient abstention method using small labeled data.
- The scope, definitions, and empirical results are presented with remarkable clarity (Reviewer 4’s positive assessment).

### Weaknesses
- **Experimental design flaw (probability extraction):** The method of extracting the probability of the final answer token (A/B/C/D) from the output string is problematic. When the model first generates rationale (e.g., “1+1=2, thus the answer is B”), the probability of “B” given the preceding rationale is almost deterministic, artificially inflating confidence and directly explaining the observed overconfidence. A product of token probabilities would be more appropriate (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing details on binary classifier:** The paper does not specify what model is used for the binary classification of correctness, nor what inputs it receives (e.g., just MSP/logits, or also the question and response?). This is a critical omission (Reviewer 2).
- **Presentation severely undermines clarity:** The introduction is 4 pages and mixes results with insufficient methodology. Key concepts (MSP, calibration error, AUROC, threshold dependency) are introduced without definition or explanation. The structure is confusing (e.g., results appear in multiple subsections before methods are detailed). The paper would benefit from a traditional structure and clearer motivation for each analysis (Reviewer 3).
- **Scope limited to multiple-choice QA:** The study only applies to constrained multiple-choice settings. It is unclear how findings extend to free-text generation, and the abstention method’s thresholding would be difficult to generalise to variable-length or open-ended answers (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Lack of comparison with other abstention methods:** The MSP-based abstention is not compared to existing methods that train LLMs to abstain (e.g., those cited in Section 2). Even if those require additional fine-tuning, a comparison would contextualise the practical advantage (Reviewer 4).
- **No inclusion of base (non-finetuned) models:** Including corresponding base models would provide insight into how finetuning changes probability assignments and calibration, and what the binary classifier learns (Reviewer 2).
- **Reported QA accuracies are low and inconsistent with literature:** For example, Llama 3 8B achieves 55.8% on Winogrande, but the Llama 3 paper reports 75.7%. The paper’s numbers need explanation (e.g., different prompting, dataset version) (Reviewer 3).
- **Unnormalized probabilities:** The probabilities of the multiple-choice options do not sum to 1 after extraction. Normalising them might affect calibration and should be considered (Reviewer 2).
- **Weak correlation between model size and AUROC explanation is circular:** The claim that larger models only have better AUROC because they are better overall, given the correlation between AUROC and model scores, does not establish causality (Reviewer 3).
- **Motivation for using max logit is unclear:** Max logit is not a probability and has no notion of calibration. Its inclusion in the analysis is not justified (Reviewer 3).
- **HellaSwag is not a QA dataset:** It is a commonsense NLI/cloze task. The phrasing should be adjusted (Reviewer 3).
