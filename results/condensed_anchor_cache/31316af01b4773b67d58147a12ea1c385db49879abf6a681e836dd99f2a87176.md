## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces the Arithmetic Constraint Satisfaction (ACS) benchmark, a 405-item dataset designed to evaluate the ability of LLMs to verify whether numerical constraints (e.g., summation, counting, multiplication) are satisfied in agent responses to open-ended (NORA) requests. The dataset spans four domains (meal plans, daily schedules, cardio workouts, strength workouts) and is generated through a multi-step LLM-based pipeline with manual cleanup, followed by human labeling. The paper evaluates several proprietary (GPT, Gemini) and open (Llama, Mixtral, Mistral) models, reporting that GPT-4o achieves 97% accuracy. Analysis includes error categorization and multiple prompt types. The authors propose that the generation pipeline could also serve as a protocol for automatic evaluation.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses an important real-world need: evaluating whether LLM-based agents satisfy user-specified constraints in complex, open-ended scenarios.
- The restriction to arithmetical constraints with well-defined ground truth sidesteps significant problems of ambiguity present in many prior evaluation benchmarks.
- Broad model coverage is provided, including both proprietary (GPT, Gemini) and open (Llama, Mixtral, Mistral) families.
- Analysis goes beyond raw accuracy with F1 scores, error categorization, and multiple prompt types.
- The dataset will be released, which is good practice for reproducibility.
- Section 2.1 offers a thorough, step-by-step overview of the dataset generation process.
- The paper is presented in a clear, easy-to-follow manner with suitable examples (noted by Reviewers 1 and 4; Reviewer 2 and Reviewer 3 find the writing unclear at points).
- The authors are honest about their design choices and limitations, reflecting a scholarly approach.
- A non-trivial amount of work has gone into compiling the dataset, which is a valuable community resource.

### Weaknesses
- **Dataset saturation and limited difficulty**: GPT-4o achieves ~97% accuracy, essentially saturating the benchmark. Reviewers 2 and 4 argue this makes it unclear whether the dataset can still drive progress or validate improvements. Reviewer 1 also notes that most modern LLMs do well on simple arithmetic reasoning, so the results are not surprising.
- **Small dataset size**: The benchmark contains only 405 items, which several reviewers (2 and 3) consider too small for a robust evaluation benchmark.
- **Unclear focus and missing framework details**: The introduction mentions an automatic evaluation framework, but its components (scoring system, reprompting logic, cutoff determination) are not described in the body. Reviewers 2 and 4 question whether the paper is a benchmark paper or a framework paper; if the latter, substantial analysis of how the framework improves performance is missing.
- **Error analysis is incomplete and inconsistent**: The largest error category is “other.” The classification is confusing: counting is categorized as “reasoning” while multiplication is not, and information extraction is also labeled reasoning. Reviewer 2 recommends removing the “reasoning” label from Table 2 and providing clearer definitions and examples of each error type.
- **Lack of realistic grounding**: The dataset is automatically generated using LLMs as user proxies, with no validation of how well it reflects real user requests. Reviewers 2 and 4 emphasize the need for justification of the chosen domains and evidence that the benchmark captures constraints encountered in practice.
- **Missing baseline: LLM with tool access**: Several evaluation steps (counting, arithmetic) are natural candidates for tool use. Reviewer 4 notes that an LLM with tool access is an immediate missing baseline, especially for open-source models that trail GPT-4o.
- **Limited constraint diversity**: The dataset tests only one constraint at a time and only simple arithmetic operations. Reviewers 1 and 3 suggest extending to implicit constraints (e.g., scheduling conflicts) and Boolean/logical constraints.
- **Writing clarity issues**: Reviewers 2 and 3 note unclear passages. Examples include: the initial purpose of the paper (whether LLMs generate or evaluate responses is ambiguous until midway), typos (“if” → “is” in Line 105; double periods; “decribes” in Line 168), excessive use of nonstandard acronyms (WOV used only three times after introduction), and unclear phrasing such as “keywords that represent the constraint as being satisfied” (Line 287-289).
- **Manual steps in dataset generation reduce reproducibility**: Reviewer 3 points out too many manual steps in the pipeline, making it difficult to fully replicate.
- **Insufficient justification of task difficulty**: Claiming problems are “elementary school level” (Line 298) is not justified and does not meaningfully characterize LLM performance, as models often fail on simple grade-school tasks.
- **Many important analyses dismissed as future work**: Intermediate numerical evaluations, deeper F1 analysis, and other key aspects are deferred, weakening the current contribution (Reviewer 2).
- **Reproducibility and annotator details**: Information about human raters (annotators, inter-annotator agreement) and actual prompt examples across all domains are missing (Reviewer 2).
- **High-level motivation and impact unclear**: Reviewers 2 and 4 question the ultimate path of this work – what new understanding does it provide, and what are the concrete takeaways for advancing LLM constraint-satisfaction evaluation?
