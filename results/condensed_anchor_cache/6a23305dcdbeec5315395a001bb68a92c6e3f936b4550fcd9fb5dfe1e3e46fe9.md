## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes DRoP (Distributionally Robust Pruning), a dataset pruning method that selects class-specific pruning ratios based on error rates to reduce classification bias. It uses random pruning within classes, motivated by theoretical analysis on a mixture of Gaussians. Experiments on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, TinyImageNet, and WaterBirds with VGGs and ResNets (up to ResNet50) show that DRoP improves worst-class accuracy and distributional robustness compared to existing pruning methods, though average accuracy sometimes slightly declines.

### Strengths
- **Novel and concise method**: DRoP is simple, straightforward, and effective (R1, R3). Its simplicity potentially increases accessibility (R3).
- **Theoretical analysis**: A detailed theoretical analysis on a 1-D mixture of two Gaussians aligns well with the proposed method, explaining the trade-off between worst-class and average risk (R1, R2).
- **Comprehensive evaluation**: Systematic experiments across multiple datasets (CIFAR, TinyImageNet, WaterBirds) and network architectures (VGG, ResNet) are convincing and cover a wide range of scenarios, including imbalanced and group distribution robustness (R2, R3).
- **Key finding**: The study reveals that existing data pruning methods all exhibit significant classification bias, a previously overlooked issue (R3).
- **Limitations discussion**: The paper includes a sound discussion of limitations and future work (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Title and framing**: The title “DRoP” could be confused with model pruning; adding “Dataset Pruning” would help (R1). The term “classification bias” is never explicitly defined, and “robustness” may be better framed as “fairness” (R1).
- **Paper flow & related work**: The related work is scattered throughout the paper; a consolidated section after the introduction with categories like “Data Pruning Methods,” “Robustness and Fairness,” etc., would improve readability (R3). Without a standalone related work section, it is hard to assess novelty relative to recent advances (R3).
- **Limited model scale**: Experiments are restricted to small models (VGG-16/19, ResNet up to ResNet50). Larger models (e.g., ResNet101, ConvNeXt-B, ViT) are more robust to distribution changes and might not show gains with DRoP (R1).
- **Mixed performance on average accuracy**: Random+DRoP reduces classification bias but generally shows slightly worse average accuracy (Figure 5). While Strategy+DRoP mitigates this, the trend is not clear (R2). One reviewer (R2) notes that the experimental trends are not always striking and aggregate scalar numbers across densities would help.
- **Interpretation of correlation in Figure 6**: The claim that a higher negative correlation between class accuracy and class density indicates more robustness is questionable. Ideally, accuracy should have zero correlation with class size for a model invariant to class size (R2).
- **Missing task-specific comparisons**: For imbalanced datasets and group distributional robustness, comparisons with methods from those fields (e.g., [1,2] for imbalanced, [3,4] for group robustness) are omitted (R3).
- **Instability at high sparsity**: In Figure 9 (leftmost plot), DRoP is significantly less stable than other methods when dataset sparsity exceeds 50% (density < 0.5) (R1, Q1).
- **Inferior worst-class performance at high density**: In some settings (e.g., ResNet18 + TinyImageNet at 90% density), Random+DRoP yields the second worst worst-class accuracy, only better than Forgetting. The paper does not explain why DRoP underperforms at high density (R3, Q).
