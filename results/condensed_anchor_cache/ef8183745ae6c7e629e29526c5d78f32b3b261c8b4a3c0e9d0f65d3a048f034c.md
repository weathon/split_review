## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes augmenting Foundation Policies (FPs), specifically the Hilbert (FB) architecture based on successor measures, with memory models (RNNs, Transformers, state-space models) to handle novel dynamics at test time. The memory module replaces the state in the feature function and policy. Evaluation on POPGym (memory benchmark) and ExORL (unsupervised RL) shows that GRU-augmented FB (FB-GRU) outperforms memory-free FPs and approaches a supervised baseline in zero-shot generalization to unseen tasks and dynamics. The code is provided.

### Strengths
- The topic is timely and relevant: scaling RL toward generalist agents via foundation policies.
- Experiments are conducted on multiple representative benchmarks (POPGym, ExORL/DMC).
- The idea is well-implemented and straightforward; the paper is generally well-structured and easy to read.
- The authors provide their implementation and highlight changes from prior FB papers, making contributions reproducible.
- Figure 2 clearly illustrates the architecture.
- The proposed method shows good zero-shot generalization performance, with GRU notably improving adaptability.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty** is limited: simply adding a memory module to the existing FB (Hilbert) backbone is trivial and has been widely investigated; replacing a state with a temporal history is expected to improve performance.
- **Weak baselines and missing comparisons**: Only the vanilla FP (no memory) is compared. The authors do not compare to stronger methods such as FDM (which outperforms FB in some ExORL settings) or to memory-augmented versions of HILP (the state-of-the-art FP using successor features). Why FB was chosen over these alternatives is not justified.
- **Linear reward assumption** limits scalability: assuming reward is linear in state features restricts expressivity, especially for complex/large-scale problems, which contradicts the goal of a generalist foundation policy.
- **Experimental evaluation is underwhelming**: Test-time generalization only covers slight variations in agent mass and damping coefficient. Occluded DMC tasks are near random and lack precise specification (details had to be inferred from code). The authors claim “entirely new environments not encountered during training,” but this is not convincingly demonstrated. Evaluation environments (POPGym subset, few DMC tasks) are relatively simple; harder environments (e.g., Jaco, Humanoid, pixel observations) are missing.
- **Hyperparameter tuning is opaque**: It is unclear why GRU outperforms S4 and Transformer. No hyperparameter ablation or sweeping details are provided; GRU may have received more tuning. This undermines the comparison between memory architectures.
- **Only 5 random seeds** are used, which may cause high stochasticity (e.g., Figure 4 shows FB-TF having higher interpolation than training performance, likely due to noise).
- **Scalability concerns**: The method assumes RND trajectories are highly diverse, which may not hold in complex environments (e.g., Montezuma’s Revenge). Limited resources prevented testing on such tasks.
- **Missing results and details**: HILP results are absent from Section 4.4. Maze results are missing for several goal positions. Baseline FB-GRU is only trained on Cheetah for Table 6; Walker and Quadruped results could be added. The meaning of interpolation/extrapolation ratios in Section 4.3 is not defined in the text.
- **Unsubstantiated claims**: The statement that “FPs are not equipped to deal with a change in dynamics” (line 1) is counter-intuitive, as foundation models aim to generalize. The claim about in-context RL (line 2) is questionable since meta-RL tasks are typically out-of-distribution. Statements about “most performant” (lines 239, 263) lack supporting references.
- **Computation cost**: For continuous action spaces, computing the policy involves an inner optimization loop over actions (Eq. 2); the cost of optimizing over continuous \( z \) at test time is not discussed.
- **Minor presentation issues**: Missing commas (e.g., L193), confusing use of “x” as multiplication (L223), and references do not follow ICLR bibliography style.
- **Unanswered questions**: Why are certain memory models (e.g., Diagonalized S4) chosen over newer ones (Mamba, Mamba2)? Is \( z \) fixed per environment once sampled (as in B.1.1)? Why are there no HILP results in Section 4.4 and no Maze results for all goal positions?
