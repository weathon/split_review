## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SCR-CCR, a self-learning compositional representation method for zero-shot Chinese character recognition. It uses a Character Slot Attention (ChSA) mechanism to learn compositional components directly from data, bypassing human-defined radicals or strokes. Recognition is performed by comparing slots between input and example images. Experiments on handwritten and printed Chinese datasets show improvements over baselines, with visualization indicating interpretable component learning. Generalization to Japanese and Korean characters is demonstrated. The paper is clearly written overall, but reviewers raise significant concerns about the zero-shot setup (use of example images), counterintuitive performance trends, insufficient comparisons, and lack of ablations.

### Strengths
1. The self-learning paradigm autonomously learns compositional slots from data, bypassing human-defined radicals or strokes, which is a novel application of object-centric representation learning to zero-shot Chinese character recognition. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
2. The two-stage pretraining-inference process with Character Slot Attention effectively maps each character image to a group of slots, enabling recognition by slot comparison. (R1, R3)
3. Experiments on handwritten (HWDB) and printed (CASIA, Synth-print) datasets show promising zero-shot recognition results, outperforming existing methods in most settings. (R1, R3, R4)
4. Visualization experiments demonstrate that the learned slots capture interpretable components of characters, reflecting structure. (R1, R3)
5. The method generalizes to Japanese and Korean characters without finetuning. (R1, R3)
6. The paper is clearly written and logically organized, effectively communicating contributions. (R4; R2 disagrees, noting the paper is not written clearly in parts.)

### Weaknesses
1. **Zero-shot setting and example images issue**: Multiple reviewers question the fairness and practicality of using example images for unseen classes. The slot-matching stage requires 10 example images per test character, which violates standard zero-shot learning principles (unseen classes should not have accessible examples) and makes comparisons with baselines unfair. (R1, R4). R1 also asks whether these example images are printed or of a seen style (Fig. 2 appears to show handwritten characters of the same style as test images). R4 notes this resembles template-based matching and may be impractical for rare characters.
2. **Performance decrease with larger training sets**: Reviewers consistently note that in HWDB experiments (Table 1), accuracy decreases as training set size increases (from 500 to 2755 characters), which is counterintuitive and casts doubt on validity and scalability. (R1, R2, R3). R2 points out that the opposite trend for printed datasets requires explanation. R3 suggests exploring this phenomenon further.
3. **Lack of comparison with strong baselines and state-of-the-art methods**: Comparisons with state-of-the-art methods for Japanese and Korean recognition are missing (Table 3), and accuracy on Korean is very low. (R1, R3). The reported CCR-CLIP results are far below those in the original paper (~90%), indicating possible implementation issues or unfair comparisons. (R1). R4 recommends zero-shot experiments in natural scenes (e.g., CTW dataset) to support generalization claims.
4. **Need for ablation on slot attention vs. standard attention**: The core innovation relies on slot attention, yet no ablation compares it to standard attention mechanisms. It is unclear whether slot attention provides unique benefits beyond a normal attention block. (R1, R2). R2 specifically asks why slot attention can learn radical-similar information if it is just normal attention.
5. **Interpretability limitations**: Learned slots detect components but do not encode their combination order, so it is unclear how the method captures character structure in an interpretable way. (R2). R2 also asks about the slot initialization claim (line 322): if all input images share the same initial states, slots tend to have the same order; what happens with different orders and how to process that?
6. **Motivation insufficiently justified**: The claim that radical- and stroke-based methods have issues is not backed by experimental analysis. The paper does not sufficiently explain why autonomous slot learning is superior to human-defined decompositions, as both are object-based approaches. (R1, R2). R1 questions whether the approach is necessary if radical/stroke methods perform well in zero-shot scenarios given full use of available data.
7. **Missing non-zero-shot experiments and scalability concerns**: The method is not evaluated in a non-zero-shot setting to demonstrate generality and superiority over traditional methods. (R3). R3 also asks whether performance could further decline with even larger training sets and whether the method extends to Chinese text recognition. R2 queries the role of IDS (ideographic description sequences) in training – if IDS is removed, what is the recognition accuracy?
8. **Minor issues**: 
   - In Eq.7, Λ^k is not defined or explained. (R3)
   - In Figure 5, the x-axis for number of slots should use integer units. (R3)
   - The paper is not written very clearly in some parts (e.g., regarding slot initialization and order). (R2)
