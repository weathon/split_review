## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Neural Lighting Priors, a learned surface emission model for indoor scenes that combines neural fields, voxel-based latent codes, and semantic features with differentiable path tracing. Given multi-view observations and explicit (assumed ground-truth) geometry, the method decouples spatially varying lighting and material parameters through a learned parametric emission model and a test-time optimization. The approach is trained on an extended version of the 3D-FRONT synthetic dataset and evaluated on both synthetic and real-world scenes. It targets lighting-aware applications such as relighting, virtual object insertion, and light editing, and claims to work with fewer views than previous methods.

### Strengths
- **Novel combination of learning-based priors and test-time optimization.** The design leverages learned priors (neural field conditioned on voxel features and semantic embeddings) for initial lighting estimation, followed by a NeRF-like test-time refinement using physics-based differentiable path tracing. This enables plausible reconstruction even from sparse multi-view inputs.
- **High-quality lighting reconstruction and relighting.** The method demonstrates quantitative gains over several baselines in relighting and virtual object insertion tasks, especially under sparse-view settings. The use of path tracing produces realistic shadows and high-order lighting effects, and the learned decoder regularization helps reduce overfitting during optimization.
- **Voxel-based emitter sampling for noise reduction.** The integration of a voxel grid to guide emitter sampling improves efficiency and reduces noise in path-traced renderings.
- **Valuable dataset contribution.** The paper extends the 3D-FRONT dataset with photorealistic, physically based indoor lighting (using Lambertian and texture-based lighting), which will be released to the community.
- **Broad applicability.** The method supports dynamic lighting applications (e.g., AR/VR) by enabling control over emitter colors and intensities and allowing virtual object insertion with consistent illumination.

### Weaknesses
- **Reliance on ground-truth geometry is a critical limitation.** The strongest and most repeated concern across reviewers. The method assumes perfect, explicit scene geometry, which is itself a challenging problem to obtain—especially from sparse views. Without evaluating on estimated geometry (e.g., from MonoSDF or similar), it is unclear how the method would behave with noisy or incomplete geometry. Reviewers 1, 3, and 4 all flagged this as undermining the claim of “limited views,” since precise geometry is rarely available in practice. One reviewer (R1, score 3) considered this the biggest weakness, contrasting with others (R2, R3) who were more positive about the overall novelty but still raised the issue. R4 additionally noted that this makes the comparison with baselines that infer geometry from a single image unfair. The authors are urged to discuss plausible real-world scenarios where geometry from sparse views could be obtained, and to provide experiments with estimated geometry.
- **Limited material model (Lambertian only).** The method assumes purely diffuse (Lambertian) materials, whereas several strong baselines (FIPT, MILO, IRIS) model specular components. This restricts the method’s ability to recreate specular reflections and may break the view-synthesis loss in scenes with complex lighting. R1 considered this a crucial omission and called for adding a specular term and re-running comparisons (e.g., Fig. 9). R2 also noted the limitation for scenes needing complex material representations. The paper itself uses GGX in its dataset (line 269), creating an unexplained discrepancy (R4).
- **Outdated and incomplete baselines.** Comparisons with recent state-of-the-art methods are missing or insufficient. FIPT, a strong baseline with publicly available code and datasets since early 2023, is only compared in Fig. 9 and not in other quantitative or qualitative evaluations. R1 and R4 both pointed this out. R4 also noted that the geometry advantage makes comparisons unfair, and recommended evaluating without ground-truth geometry. Additional related works (Wang et al. CVPR 2023, ECCV 2022; Nimier-David et al. texture-space differentiable rendering) should be discussed and compared where applicable.
- **Insufficient ablation studies.** Several key ablations are missing:
  - Effect of test-time optimization versus pure pre-trained inference (R1).
  - BRDF estimation quality (no separate BRDF results or comparisons) (R1).
  - Emission estimation quality (no separate emission maps or masks to verify that emitters are correctly identified and non-emitters suppressed) (R1).
  - Performance with and without semantic embeddings or voxel representations.
  - Evaluation on complex and real-world scenes. The only real-world results are limited, and a standard benchmark (e.g., the FIPT real-world benchmark) is available but not used (R1). R3 also emphasized the synthetic-only training may not generalize to real-world imperfections.
- **Computational cost and practical deployment.** The approach is computationally intensive due to iterative path tracing, limiting real-time applicability (R2). The reliance on many samples per pixel (2048 at test time, line 319) while using only 1 bounce and 1 sample per training step (line 195) creates confusion and raises questions about smoothness in results (R4). The voxel sampling strategy and its relation to the neural field need clearer explanation (R2).
- **Writing and clarity issues.** Several reviewers found the exposition lacking in important details:
  - **Material model discrepancy:** The paper states only Lambertian materials are used (lines 247-248) but the dataset description (line 269) mentions GGX. No explanation is given for how this mismatch is handled (R4).
  - **Ray tracing settings:** The text describes 1 bounce and 1 importance sample (line 195), yet the supplementary videos appear smooth and Fig. 11 shows results with many samples. The distinction between training and testing ray counts (2048 per pixel at test) needs clarification (R4).
  - **Emission model Eq. (7):** It is unclear whether \(L_e\) directly outputs image color, and whether semantic masks are used to restrict emission to light source regions (R4). R1 also questioned how “control over the emitters” works when the model outputs dense emission, not discrete emitters, and how failures (e.g., false positive emitters) are handled.
  - **Terminology inconsistency:** “Scene reconstruction” should be “view synthesis”; “geometry prior” in Table 1 is ambiguous (R1). Overall, the methodology section lacks sufficient detail on the “why” behind design choices (R4).
- **Overclaiming the problem difficulty.** R4 argued that assuming accurate geometry substantially simplifies the lighting optimization, making the sparse-view achievement less impressive and the comparison with methods that do not have such geometry priors unfair. The claim of “limited views” is weakened by the unrealistic assumption of full geometry.
- **Lack of discussion on generalization to mixed lighting or outdoor scenes.** R2 asked about generalization to mixed natural/artificial lighting and adaptation to outdoor scenes with volumetric transport; these points are not addressed.

*Note on reviewer disagreement:* One reviewer (R1, score 3, confidence 5) was significantly more critical, particularly regarding the reliance on GT geometry, incomplete comparisons, and limited BRDF, while others (R2 and R3, both score 6; R4 score 5) were more positive about the novelty and integration of techniques but still identified many of the same weaknesses.
