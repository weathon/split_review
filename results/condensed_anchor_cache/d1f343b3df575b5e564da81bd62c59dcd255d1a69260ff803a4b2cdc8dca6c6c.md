## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a two-phase Data Augmentation guided Decoupled Knowledge Distillation (DADKD) framework for low-resolution fine-grained image classification. The first phase uses SnapMix to generate composite images and corresponding labels; the second phase performs knowledge distillation to minimize differences between high-resolution (448×448) teacher features and low-resolution (128×128) student features. The method is evaluated on Stanford-Cars, FGVC-Aircraft, and CUB-200-2011, achieving 88.19%, 78.98%, and 80.33% accuracy respectively.

### Strengths
- The target problem (low-resolution fine-grained classification) is meaningful and worth exploring.
- The technical design is straightforward and easy to follow (Reviewer 4); the idea of combining data augmentation with knowledge distillation is inspired by the drawbacks of deep networks on discriminative features at low resolution.
- Experimental results validate effectiveness, with significant performance gains over baselines (Reviewers 1, 4).
- Evaluation uses three well-known benchmarks, with comparisons to baselines and some recent approaches.
- Ablation study on CUB-200 demonstrates the benefit of the data augmentation and knowledge distillation modules (Reviewer 2).
- The paper provides interpretability via Grad-CAM visualizations (Reviewer 2).
- **Minority positive view (Reviewer 3, rating 5):** This reviewer considered the contribution and presentation good, noting that the submission provides a valuable implementation of the idea.

### Weaknesses
**1. Lack of novelty and insufficient motivation**
- The framework is largely an integration of existing techniques (data augmentation via SnapMix + decoupled knowledge distillation) without providing clear insight into how the guidance works. The guidance claimed by the name is unclear; the two phases are used separately.
- The novelty is incremental: teacher/student with high- and low-resolution inputs and a knowledge distillation loss is not uncommon in prior low-resolution or knowledge-distillation works (Reviewer 4).
- The addition of an attention module in SnapMix and two hyperparameters (in the loss) lacks reasonable and insightful motivation (Reviewer 1). Extensive prior work (e.g., hard example mining, focal loss) has explored different loss weights.
- The focus should have been on the novel components – semantic percentage maps (SPM) and the TCKD/NCKD losses – but these are not critically analyzed (Reviewer 2). The roles of ρₐ, ρ_b, TCKD, and NCKD are not experimentally validated and justified (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4). How α (Eq.2) and ρ (Eq.3) are computed or determined is unclear (Reviewer 4).
- Motivation for the two modules is not fully explained in the introduction (Reviewer 3).

**2. Insufficient and missing experimental comparisons**
- The claim of state-of-the-art performance is overstated. Many recent and relevant methods are not compared:
  - Low-resolution FGVC methods missing: Cai et al. (Pattern Recognition Letters 2019), Yan et al. (IEEE TCSVT 2022), Yan et al. (Pattern Recognition 2022) (Reviewer 4).
  - FGVC methods with higher accuracy on standard benchmarks are omitted, e.g., for CUB-200: SR-GNN (Bera et al., TIP 2022) 91.9%, CAP (Behera et al., AAAI 2021) 91.8%, Stacked LSTM (Ge et al., CVPR 2019) 90.4%, BARM 89.5%; for Cars: SR-GNN 96.1%, CAP 95.7%, AutoAug 94.8%, GPipe 94.6%, DCL 94.5%; for Aircraft: SR-GNN 95.4%, CAP 94.9%, Hierarchical Multi-Scale 94.9%, Graph-based high-order 94.3%, category-specific semantic coherency 93.8% (Reviewer 2).
  - Many of these methods use input size 224×224 with single-stage training and significantly outperform the reported results (>90% vs. 80–88%) (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4). The paper does not justify or discuss this discrepancy.
  - Knowledge distillation for low-resolution: (Zhu et al., 2019) also transfers knowledge from high-resolution teacher to low-resolution student; comparison is needed (Reviewer 3).
  - The teacher network's performance itself is not reported (Reviewer 1).
  - PMG and API-Net (Table 1) are both before 2021; more recent FGVC methods from 2022/2023 should be compared (Reviewer 4).
- Many important references on FGVC and low-resolution FGVC are missing from the related work section; a separate subsection for low-resolution FGVC is needed (Reviewer 4).

**3. Limited ablation and hyperparameter analysis**
- The ablation on the loss weight (Eq.5) uses too narrow a range; larger α or different ratios should be explored (Reviewer 4). How parameters α (Eq.2) and ρ (Eq.3) affect performance is not systematically studied (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4).
- No ablation or justification for ρₐ, ρ_b, TCKD, NCKD individually (Reviewer 2).

**4. Model capacity, complexity, and resolution issues**
- Missing analysis of model capacity and computational complexity (Params, GFLOPS, per-image inference time) compared to other SOTA models (Reviewer 2).
- The input resolution: teacher uses 448×448, student uses 128×128. Is 128×128 truly low-resolution? Many works use 224×224 as standard input; experiments at 64×64 resolution are needed (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3). Also, the statement “performance of models trained on high-resolution fine-grained images cannot be guaranteed when applied directly to low-resolution” needs proof/justification (Reviewer 2).

**5. Writing and clarity**
- The paper has many typos and grammatical errors (e.g., “it blend”, “go a” for “goes”, “Resne34”) that make it hard to follow, especially Section 3.2 (Reviewer 1). (Note: Reviewer 4 found it well-written; reviewer viewpoints differ.)
- The content is not sufficient; more experiments or clearer method descriptions could be added (Reviewer 3). The related work section is not well-written or extensive (Reviewer 4).
