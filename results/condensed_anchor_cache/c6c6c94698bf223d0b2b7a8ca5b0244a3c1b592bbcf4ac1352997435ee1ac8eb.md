## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper presents DiLu, a framework that integrates large language models (LLMs) into autonomous driving for knowledge-driven, human-like high-level decision making. DiLu consists of four modules: Reasoning (LLM-based decision making via chain-of-thought prompting), Memory (store and retrieve successful experiences using similarity-based retrieval), Reflection (self-correct unsuccessful experiences via LLM feedback and store corrected versions), and Action (map decisions to discrete actions). Experiments are conducted in the Highway-Env simulator and compare DiLu against a reinforcement learning baseline (GRAD) and several ablations. The framework is novel, well-motivated, and demonstrates interesting capabilities, but the evaluation, generalization claims, and real-world feasibility are contentious.

### Strengths
- **Innovative integration of LLMs** for knowledge-driven autonomous driving, shifting from purely data-driven methods to a paradigm inspired by human driving. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- **Well-motivated and clearly explained framework** with reasoning, memory, and reflection modules; the continuous learning flywheel (storing both successful and corrected experiences) is a key contribution. (R2, R3)
- **Strong generalization ability** is demonstrated across varied environments (changing number of lanes and traffic density) relative to the RL baseline. (R1; note disagreement below in Weaknesses)
- **Simple and has potential for real-world application** (minority positive: R2, score 8, contrasts with lower scores from R1,R3,R4).
- Plans to open source provide a **good foundation for future work**. (R3)
- **Extensive experiments** investigating each component, including the effect of memory size and correction. (R3)

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient evaluation** – only one baseline (a self-trained RL method, GRAD) and oversimplified metrics (collision rate). Need comprehensive metrics (time to completion, lane keeping, driving smoothness) and comparisons to more advanced baselines (state-of-the-art RL, imitation learning, rule-based methods, other LLM-based works like GPT-Driver). Without a strong RL baseline optimized on Highway-Env, it is unclear if gains are from the method or from the baseline’s weakness. (R1, R4; echoed by R3’s request for more baselines)
- **Generalization claims are not convincing** – the experiments only vary number of lanes and traffic density, still in a very similar Highway-Env setting. With a large memory, the task may reduce to copying the most similar retrieved experience. Need more diverse and challenging scenarios (intersections, pedestrians, aggressive agents, different traffic rules, new road geometries). (R3, R4)
- **Real-time feasibility and computation overhead not addressed** – querying an LLM for every decision (even with few-shot) introduces latency and reaction-time bottlenecks that are critical for autonomous driving. No discussion of computation speed, whether videos are sped/slowed, or how to handle real-time constraints. (R1, R4)
- **Dependence on LLMs raises unresolved reliability and safety issues** – no analysis of LLM biases, incorrect outputs, or consistency; the reflection module relies on self-assessment (the LLM judging its own decision) which is insufficient for safety-critical driving. Need external validation or a rule-based safety layer. The robustness to noisy sensor data, unexpected events, and varying road conditions is not examined. (R1, R2)
- **Memory module description is unclear** (Section 3.2) – the process of storing experiences, key-frame sampling, and how similarity retrieval maps scenarios to memory stores needs more detail and a dedicated figure. (R2, R3, R4)
- **Overstated claims** – “first to instill knowledge-driven capability” is misleading given prior work (LINGO-1); the claim that human driving is knowledge-based is not supported by the cited literature. (R2, R4)
- **Language is vague and overly broad** – e.g., “marginal performance of data-driven methods” lacks citations; “formidable and expensive” is unclear; many arguments are hard to follow. (R4)
- **Use of two different GPT versions not justified** – why was GPT-3.5 used for reasoning and GPT-4 for reflection? (R2)
- **Reflection (correction) experiments show only marginal improvements** (Table 1), warranting more investigation into why corrected experiences do not boost performance strongly. (R3)
- **The 5 human-crafted experiences are not explained** – what they contain and why they are needed. (R4)
- **Details from Johnson et al. 2019 for vector similarity are missing**, making the paper less self-contained. (R4)
- **Figure 7a could be replaced by a table** for clarity. (R4)
- **The paper would benefit from exploring how LLMs could assist planning stacks** (e.g., scene understanding, prediction) rather than directly outputting discrete actions, which could increase applicability to current systems. (R3)
- **Motivation for using an AI agent system specifically for autonomous driving** (distinct from other embodied tasks) is not sufficiently elaborated. (R1)
- **Text-based representation of driving experiences may lose nuanced real-world information** about sensor noise, temporal dynamics, and road conditions. (R1)
