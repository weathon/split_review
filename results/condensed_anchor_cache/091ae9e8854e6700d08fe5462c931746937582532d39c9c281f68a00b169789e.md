## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper challenges the assumed superiority of pretraining in Genomic Foundation Models (GFMs) by comparing seven pretrained models against their randomly initialized counterparts across various genomic benchmarks. The authors find that randomly initialized models can match or even surpass pretrained GFMs in finetuning and feature extraction, and that pretrained models fail to capture clinically relevant mutations. The reviewers note the ambitious scope but have significant concerns about the strength of the evidence and claims.

### Strengths
- **Interesting and timely perspective**: The paper questions whether current pretraining strategies provide real benefit in genomics, which is an important area of research (R1, R3, R4).
- **Comprehensive experiments across many GFMs and tasks**: The study includes multiple models (Mistral, DNABERTv2, etc.) and benchmarks, providing a broad view (R1, R2, R3). R2 notes the experiments are comprehensive except for a few points.
- **Mutation sensitivity analysis is a valuable contribution**: The observation that pretrained models are insensitive to single-nucleotide variants and fail on ClinVar (though limited to one gene, BRCA1) is interesting and could have clinical implications (R2, R3).
- **Clear and well-written presentation**: The paper is easy to follow (R1, R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Finding 1 (“random models match or surpass pretrained”) is not well-supported**:
  - Figure 1 is misleading because it compares models with vastly different parameter counts (e.g., 450k vs. 500M) without controlling for architecture, so a large random model beating a small pretrained model does not show pretraining is unhelpful (R2).
  - When comparing pretrained vs. random versions of the same model (Figure 2), pretrained models perform better in most cases, which actually suggests pretraining is effective (R2). The improvement is more significant on more distinguishable datasets (e.g., histone) and less on promoter/enhancer where all models achieve near-identical performance (R2). This indicates the benchmark (NT) may not be suitable for differentiating models (R2). Reviewers suggest using more discriminative benchmarks (e.g., HyenaDNA species classification, DNABERT-2 GUE, Caduceus Genomics Benchmark) (R2).
- **Insufficient analysis of model behavior**:
  - No explanation is given for why specific models (e.g., Mistral) perform better on mutation sensitivity, or why random models outperform pretrained on certain tasks (R1, R3).
  - The paper does not explore alternative pretraining objectives, loss functions, or tokenization strategies that might better capture biological complexity (e.g., for detecting single-nucleotide variants) (R1). One reviewer questions whether model-specific optimizations like vocabulary adaptations or alternate pooling strategies would change the results (R1).
  - The mutation sensitivity analysis lacks a direct comparison between random and pretrained models on the same tasks (R3). R2 would like the same feature extraction experiments on the NT benchmark subsets (e.g., histone) to better compare finetune/feature extraction.
- **Limited scope of key experiments**:
  - The ClinVar experiment uses only one gene (BRCA1), limiting generalizability (R3).
  - The paper does not test on many established genetic tasks, and the conclusions are drawn from a possibly narrow set of benchmarks (R2, R4). For example, the NT benchmark uses 1000G instead of the better NTv2-multispecies (R2). Finetuning and feature extraction experiments use different datasets, making direct comparison difficult (R2).
  - The paper disregards one of the largest GFMs (EVO), which has the largest pretraining scale (R4).
- **Overly broad claims**: The conclusion that “pretraining does not promise performance” is too strong given the evidence limitations (R2, R4). The findings largely reiterate known limitations of pretraining without providing new insights or approaches (R4). One reviewer (R4) questions the entire premise, noting that it is not that pretraining doesn’t work, but that gene sequences have not found a more appropriate pretraining strategy. Another reviewer (R2) is similarly critical, while a third reviewer (R3) is more positive but still acknowledges the need for additional comparisons.
