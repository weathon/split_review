## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a relative fairness notion for comparing two sets of decisions (human or algorithmic) by testing demographic parity between them. When decisions are on different data, it trains an ML model to impute the missing decisions. Experiments are conducted on UCI Adult income and SCUT‑FB500 face beauty datasets.

### Strengths
- The notion of relative fairness is considered novel by one reviewer (R3), though others question its novelty.
- The paper offers a natural perspective on measuring fairness across different decision‑makers, bypassing the need for “true labels” (R1, R2).
- The paper uses real datasets in its evaluation (R1).
- The paper is fairly easy to follow (R3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Motivation and added value of relative fairness**  
   - The paper does not adequately justify why relative fairness (difference of statistical parities) is needed over simply reporting absolute statistical parity for each decision set. If both sets are biased, relative fairness near zero may indicate consistent bias, but this implication is not explored (R3).  
   - The choice of demographic parity as the metric is not justified; its limitations (e.g., ignoring legitimate correlations with the sensitive attribute) are not addressed, and the framework’s adaptability to other notions (e.g., equal opportunity, predictive parity) is not discussed (R1).  
   - One reviewer asks whether absolute fairness is a sub‑routine of relative fairness (R2).

2. **Novelty and contribution**  
   - The idea of relative comparison is seen as similar in spirit to individual fairness and counterfactual fairness, with insufficient differentiation (R1).  
   - The label‑imputation algorithms (biased/unbiased bridge) are described as a straightforward application of existing techniques; no advantage over prior imputation methods is demonstrated (R1).  
   - The theoretical result (Proposition 4.5) is weak and appears tautological under the very restrictive assumptions (R1).  
   - The claim that ML trained on human decisions inherits bias is not novel; it is already the motivation for much fairness‑in‑ML work (R2).  
   - The related‑work section needs to be more cohesive and clearly position the paper (R2).  
   - **Disagreement**: One reviewer finds the notion of relative fairness novel (R3), while others find it lacks novelty (R1, R2).

3. **Mathematical formulation and assumptions**  
   - Definition 4.1 relies on “equality in distribution” without specifying the source of randomness (e.g., whether X is random) or over which values of the sensitive attribute it should hold (R1).  
   - Assumption 4.2 is unjustified: Equation 6 assumes normality for the difference of two arbitrary classifiers without any theoretical basis; the large‑sample assumption contradicts the need for label imputation (if samples are large, the raw statistics could be compared directly) (R1).  
   - Proposition 4.5’s correctness is unclear: does equality of means alone suffice for equality in distribution? The proof is not rigorous (R1).  
   - Minor issues: “mean” and “variance” in Definition 4.3 should be “empirical mean/variance”; Section 4 should state earlier that Y is binary {0,1}; in Eq. 9, \(\overline{f}\) is undefined (R1).

4. **Flaws in the experimental design and methodology**  
   - **Gap between motivation and setup**: The paper motivates relative fairness by avoiding the need for true labels, but the experiments simulate settings where true labels are available (e.g., Step 0 in Section 3 and all experimental setups). This undermines the core motivation (R1).  
   - **Data‑generation details**: The injection procedure equations are unclear (e.g., \(\overline{Y}_1 = \overline{Y}_0\), how \(\lambda\) is applied separately for sex and race, what \(z(a)\) means). The description is insufficient for reproduction (R1).  
   - **Bias propagation in ML imputation**: Using an ML model trained on \(\{X_0,Y_0\}\) to impute decisions for \(X_1\) propagates and potentially amplifies the biases already present in \(X_0,Y_0\). The results are highly sensitive to the choice of \(f\) and its implicit biases, which is not addressed (R2, R3).  
   - **Alternative experimental design**: Instead of splitting one dataset, the paper should use distinct groups of decision‑makers (e.g., demographically/ideologically different) on the same data, or run human‑subject studies (R2).  
   - **Dataset concerns**: The beauty dataset (SCUT‑FB500) raises ethical questions; the Adult dataset is assumed to be 100% fair trivia, but this is unverified and unrealistic (R2).

5. **Presentation and writing issues**  
   - Examples in the paper are non‑complementary and do not support the methodology; a single intuitive running example should be used throughout (R2).  
   - Writing is inconsistent about whether the two decision sets are from the same or different datasets (the experiments actually use a single dataset split) (R2).  
   - Experimental results are presented in dense tables; figures would improve readability (R1).  
   - Multiple hypothesis testing (e.g., across groups or datasets) is not accounted for (R1).  
   - Grammar: title should be “Testing”, “decisions sets” → “decision sets”, “what are absolutely fair decisions” → “what absolutely fair decisions are”, “present” → “presence”, “Homan and others” should cite by first author, “least beautify” unclear (R1).  
   - Tables 3 and 4 are hard to read; the meaning of values in brackets (presumably p‑values) is not explained (R3).
