## Merged Review

### Summary

Reviewers describe the paper as proposing adversarial attacks on language model activations and studying scaling laws linking the number of controlled activation tokens \(a\) to the maximum number of target tokens \(t_\mathrm{max}\) that can be predicted, with \(t_\mathrm{max} = \kappa a\). They note empirical validation across several LLMs and a metric \(\chi\) (attack resistance) between 16 and 25. The paper compares activation-level attacks to token-level attacks and motivates practicality via retrieval and multi-modal models. Reviewers find the hypothesis interesting and the work novel, but raise concerns about clarity, practicality, and supporting evidence.

### Strengths

- **Originality and novelty**: The paper is considered the first in-depth study of activation-level adversarial attacks on LLMs (R3; also acknowledged by R1 and R4).
- **Interesting hypothesis**: The core hypothesis relating input-output dimensionality mismatch to attack success is intriguing (R4).
- **Empirical scaling law**: The linear scaling between controlled activations and target tokens is validated on several different language models (R4; R1 notes it allows exploration in larger input-output regimes than previously studied in computer vision).
- **Quantified comparison to token attacks**: The comparison between activation and token attacks, though not surprising given higher granularity, is good to have quantified (R3).
- **Clear writing and presentation**: The core ideas and experiments are clearly presented and explained (R3); the paper has a clear writing style with hypotheses explicitly stated (R3).
- **Practical motivation**: Strong justification of real-world applicability via retrieval and multi-modal setups (R3; R2 also acknowledges importance due to LLM deployment).
- **Significance for adversarial ML**: The paper makes a compelling case for using LLMs and activation-level attacks as a flexible test bed for adversarial attack theory (R3).
- **Inherent characteristics**: The attack reveals inherent characteristics of LLMs (R2).

**Note on reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (R3, score 8) was notably more positive, emphasizing strong originality, clarity, and practical significance, while others (scores 6) found the work interesting but with significant concerns.

### Weaknesses

- **Practicality of the attack threat model is questionable**:
  - The assumption that an attacker can arbitrarily modify activations without limit (unlike classic adversarial attacks constrained to an \(\epsilon\)-ball) is unrealistic in many scenarios (R1, R4). In multi-modal models, a more realistic attack surface would be altering pixels in [0,255] rather than arbitrary feature values (R4).
  - It is unclear how an attacker could inject adversarial perturbations into benign user-generated activation values (R2).
  - The attacker may need full model access to compute perturbations, whereas real LLMs are typically accessed via API without model weights (R2).
  - The paper's examples of retrieval and multi-modal models involve concatenating activations from different inputs, not adding perturbations as in an attack (R2).
  - Reviewers request: analysis of realistic constraints on activation modifications in retrieval/multi-modal settings; experiments with bounded perturbations within an \(\epsilon\)-ball; more explicit discussion of limitations of the unconstrained attack model (R4).

- **Lack of supporting evidence for the core hypothesis**: The paper states "The core hypothesis is that the ability to carry a successful adversarial attack depends on the ratio between the dimensions of the input and output spaces," but no supporting evidence from literature is provided (R1).

- **Limited attack methodology**: The study uses only variants of iterative gradient attacks and FGSM, which were proposed early in computer vision literature. It is unclear if this is comprehensive enough for such scaling law studies (R1). Additionally, keeping perturbations unbounded may not be standard for LLM attacks (R1).

- **Clarity and presentation issues**:
  - The formulation and measurement of \(t_\mathrm{max}\) lack clarity. The paper describes random sampling of context tokens \(S\) and target tokens \(T\) but does not specify the number of \((S,T)\) pairs used to determine \(t_\mathrm{max}\), the distribution of \(t_\mathrm{max}\) across samples, or how variability affects the linear scaling relationship. Different samples (e.g., natural language vs. random tokens) may yield different attack performance (R4). Reviewers request: specify pair count, provide error bars/confidence intervals, analyze variation for different target sequences, and discuss impact on linearity (R4).
  - The theoretical derivation of the scaling law is presented in a large, hard-to-read paragraph with insufficient detail. The paper would benefit from a step-by-step derivation including assumptions and limitations, and a more explicit connection between theoretical and empirical results (R3).
  - The narrative flow is non-linear; readers need to skip ahead or read back to understand the significance of earlier results (e.g., the log2 value at L153). Clearer transitions and signposting are needed (R3).
  - Speculative statements, such as L204 "although adversarial training probably changes it" and L222-223 about effective dimensionality of embeddings, should be moved to a future research section rather than presented in the main text (R3).

- **Typos and technical errors**:
  - References to Algorithms at Lines 262, 267, 315 are incorrect (R1).
  - Line 499: incorrect use of Big-O notation; \(O(100)\) is equivalent to any constant \(O(k)\) (R1).
  - Line 283: broken sentence: "probability can be seen in e.g. Figure 3a and Table 1 refers to the ..." (R4).

- **Figure and appendix quality**:
  - Some figures (Figure 1(b,e), Figure 3, Figure 5) should be included as vector PDFs rather than raster images for fidelity; current format causes loss of detail and small font sizes hinder readability (R3).
  - The appendix appears incomplete, with Sections C, D, E empty (R3).

- **Missing discussion of related concepts**: The metric \(\chi\) may be related to generalized degrees of freedom (Gao and Jojic 2016) or effective dimensionality (Maddox et al 2020); this connection is not explored (R3). Also, the effect of adversarial training on \(\chi\) is not examined (R3).

- **Missing utility explanation**: The paper does not fully explain why LLM activation values are a useful attack target; only two prior works on modifying activations are cited in the introduction (R2).
