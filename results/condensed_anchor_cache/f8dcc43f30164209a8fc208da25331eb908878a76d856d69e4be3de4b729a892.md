- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 5, 8, 3, 6

## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a novel method to learn cost functions for algorithmic recourse using LLMs (GPT-4o). It queries an LLM to label pairwise comparisons of recourse actions as low- or high-cost, then trains a cost function (tree-based or MLP) on these labels via a Bradley-Terry model. The approach considers four desiderata: feature cost, relative cost, dependent cost, and fair cost. Chain-of-thought prompting is used to improve LLM responses. A human study compares LLM and human cost judgments, showing moderate alignment. The paper evaluates on Adult, German Credit, and HELOC datasets.

### Strengths
- The research question is interesting and of practical relevance; obtaining realistic cost functions for recourse is an important and under-explored problem (Reviewers 1,2,3,4).
- The proposed methodology is refreshingly original and promising for designing cost functions in practice (Reviewers 1,2,3). Reviewer 2 particularly praises it as "refreshingly original" and wants to see it accepted.
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow overall (Reviewers 1,4). No major technical flaws are apparent (Reviewer 4).
- The novel application of LLMs to generate recourse-specific data is timely and interesting (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses

**1. Evaluation of the cost function is insufficiently grounded.**
- The paper lacks clear criteria for evaluating cost functions; the claim of "high-performing" remains subjective and the evaluations in Table 1 and Figure 3 appear ad-hoc and unmotivated (Reviewer 1).
- The comparison baselines are too limited: only L1-norm on Adult is used as a baseline (Reviewer 1). It is unclear how much the LLM helps relative to manually designed cost functions, which might be feasible for small datasets (Reviewer 1).
- The accuracy comparison in Table 1 (tree vs MLP) is difficult to interpret: the relative performance of the model class seems orthogonal to the goal of learning a cost function; the key result is simply that pairwise comparisons can be learned (Reviewer 2).
- In Section 4.3, the choice of recourses for comparing MLP and L1 costs may be biased: by picking only different recourses, the comparison may force a difference that might not exist (Reviewer 3).
- The method of pairing recourses in a graph (Section 3.2) merits more justification (Reviewer 3).

**2. Human–LLM comparison study is weak and raises concerns about reliability.**
- The study uses only 6 questions per dataset (18 total) and a limited number of participants, all AI researchers, which may not be representative (Reviewers 3,4). Selection of examples is not clearly justified and may have been cherry-picked (Reviewer 3,4).
- The independence test used (Chi2) is not an appropriate measure of agreement; it only tests dependence, not agreement direction. Inverse correlation would also yield dependence. Standard agreement measures such as Cohen's kappa should be used (Reviewer 4).
- The description of the test is unclear: Figure shows large p-values indicating dependency, which contradicts standard Chi2 interpretation (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 4 asks for more details on the test.
- The LLM was unsure 49% of the time, meaning almost half the comparisons were essentially random. The authors claim this is acceptable because humans also pick randomly when unsure, but they provide no evidence that humans were equally uncertain in the same cases (Reviewers 2,3). This high uncertainty rate threatens the feasibility and reliability of the method (Reviewer 2). It should be explicitly addressed (Reviewer 2).
- The analysis of rejected pairs (LLM uncertain) and their effect on results is missing (Reviewer 4).
- Reviewer 4 asks what the user study scores would be without undecisive cases.

**3. The advantage of incorporating constraints via prompts is questionable.**
- The "custom prompt" experiment (Section 4.2) encodes desired cost behavior directly in the prompt, then recovers that behavior in labels. This is circular: specifying the cost function upfront and then using the LLM to reproduce it does not demonstrate added value. The paper’s goal is to *learn* cost functions, not recover known ones (Reviewer 2). A more realistic evaluation would involve searching over prompts without including the answer to best match human intuition (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 2 questions the bearing of these comparisons on the use of LLMs to learn recourse cost functions.
- Explicit optimization-based procedures (e.g., Ustun et al., 2019) can directly incorporate constraints with guarantees, whereas the LLM provides no such guarantees. While interesting that constraints can be included via prompts, it is not a unique advantage (Reviewer 4).

**4. The LLM fails to automatically model feature dependencies, which is the most challenging aspect.**
- The most valuable contribution would be the LLM's ability to model causal relationships and mutual feature dependencies automatically, saving manual effort. However, results for Desideratum 3 in Table 1 show that the LLM does not capture these dependencies without explicit specification in the prompt. Manual specification of all dependencies still seems required, raising doubt about practical advantages over existing direct constraint specification (Reviewers 3,4). Reviewer 4 asks why the LLM doesn't seem to have implicit knowledge about feature dependencies, and for an example where it uses background knowledge to reason about dependencies.

**5. Application of the learned cost function within recourse frameworks is not demonstrated.**
- The case study uses a brute-force approach to find recourses. It is unclear how the learned cost function integrates with popular recourse frameworks that rely on optimization (SGD), manifold-based (e.g., FACE, Poyiadzi et al., 2019), or latent space methods (e.g., Pawelczyk et al., 2020). Efficiency and ease of integration remain unaddressed (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 4 asks for additional discussion on how the learned cost function can be integrated in recourse frameworks.

**6. Data contamination risk and missing references.**
- The paper uses only highly popular datasets (Adult, German Credit, HELOC) that are likely memorized by GPT-4o (see arXiv:2404.06209). This threatens validity. High-impact work on LLMs for tabular data uses recent or non-public datasets (Reviewer 1). The authors should consider alternatives like Folktables (Reviewer 1).
- The related work section omits recent LLM applications for tabular data, including feature engineering, classification, and generation (Reviewer 1 gives specific references to Narayan et al., NeurIPS 2023; Hegselmann et al., PMLR 2023; Borisov et al., arXiv:2210.06280). These should be cited to contextualize the contribution.

**Minor weaknesses:**
- Use of Latin phrases like "ad libitum" is unnecessary; clearer terminology like "relative cost criterion" is preferred (Reviewer 1).
- The paper does not state how the 49% uncertain cases affect the subsequent training; additional discussion is needed (Reviewers 2,4).
- Reviewer 3 asks why no specific algorithm was used to find recourses (only permutations), why recourses are paired in a graph, and what the baseline is in Section 4.2 (ground truth evaluation unclear).

**Contrast in reviewer opinions:** Reviewer 2 rates the paper highly (score 8) and finds the method "refreshingly original" and "promising". Reviewer 3 is very critical (score 3), especially regarding the unconvincing experimental evaluation and weak human-LLM comparison. Reviewer 1 (score 5) and Reviewer 4 (score 6) acknowledge technical soundness but question practical advantages and evaluation rigor.
