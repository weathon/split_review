## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Direct Self-Distillation (DSD) for diffusion models, a method that removes the static teacher from progressive distillation by having the model distill itself using two consecutive sampling steps. Reviewers acknowledge the problem's importance and note that DSD reduces GPU compute (2 vs 3 network evaluations per update and no need for two model copies) and achieves faster convergence with fewer parameter updates (4K–5K) than previous online distillation. However, all reviewers raise substantial concerns: the distilled model's FID scores are far from the teacher (e.g., LSUN in Table 2) and degrade at 2–4 sampling steps; standard benchmarks (CIFAR-10, ImageNet 64×64) are missing; the clarity and organisation of the manuscript are poor; and the evidence that DSD is superior to existing methods (PD, TSD) is insufficient. These weaknesses collectively undermine the contribution.

### Strengths
- The problem of distilling diffusion models is important (Reviewer 1).
- Self-distillation removes the static teacher, simplifying progressive distillation, and enables data-free distillation (Reviewer 2).
- Converges faster than PD, achieving comparable image quality with fewer network evaluations per parameter update (2 vs 3) and fewer total parameter updates (4K–5K vs thousands). In Table 2, DSD with 8 steps and 4K updates outperforms teacher-student distillation at similar updates (CelebA HQ, LSUN Bedroom) (Reviewers 2, 3).
- Reduces GPU compute: fewer evaluations per update and no need to maintain two model copies in memory (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Weak performance**: The distilled model’s FID scores are high and not close to the teacher (e.g., LSUN results in Table 2). At 2–4 sampling steps DSD deteriorates quickly. On CelebA, the original DDIM (10 steps) achieves FID 17.33, while training-from-scratch experiments (Figure 3) show FID ~100 after 30k–60k steps; it is unclear whether self-distillation gains persist with longer training (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Insufficient comparisons**: Standard benchmarks (CIFAR-10, ImageNet 64×64) are missing, preventing comparison with recent progress. The paper does not convincingly show that DSD can match PD’s FID with more parameter updates or achieve better FID when training from scratch. A table comparing FID, sampling steps, and NFEs for DSD and PD is needed. The paper also does not explore the orthogonal combination of DSD (with more steps) and PD (Reviewers 2, 3).
- **Clarity and presentation**: Notation (z_t) is introduced without context (page 2); Progressive Distillation lacks an introduction and the distinctions between TSD and PD are unclear. The three-level nested loop (Algorithm 2) is poorly explained. Figure 2’s x‑axis is not clarified (why small denoising steps are used initially). The claim that “teacher and student diverge” in distillation is questionable. The paper needs thorough proofreading and reorganization (Reviewers 2, 3). Minor issues: citations should use brackets (Reviewer 1); grammar error in abstract (Reviewer 3); notation inconsistency between Eq. 1 (z) and earlier sections (x), and the choice of loss (||ẑ_{t−1} − z_{t−1}||²) over the standard noise prediction is not justified; “S” is undefined in Table 1 (Reviewer 3). Combining Figures 3 and 4 would save space (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing literature**: Distillation methods developed after PD, recent data-free distillation and online‑teacher approaches, and diffusion samplers without retraining are not cited (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing parameter comparison**: The number of parameters in the distilled model versus the original model should be reported to demonstrate benefits in model size and resource requirements (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing implementation detail**: It is not stated whether DSD maintains an exponential moving average (EMA) copy of model weights during training/fine-tuning (Reviewer 3).
