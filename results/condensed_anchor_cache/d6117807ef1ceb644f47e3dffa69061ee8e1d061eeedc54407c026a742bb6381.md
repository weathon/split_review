## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper studies identifiability of latent variables under general nonlinear noise. It provides conditions (nondegeneracy, variability, structural sparsity) for element-wise identifiability with only two environments (reducing from O(n)), extending to additive noise (Theorem 2), nonlinear distortions (Theorem 3), and causal DAG recovery (Theorem 4). While the generality is theoretically interesting, reviewers raise major concerns about clarity, novelty, assumption strength, and practical relevance.

### Strengths

- General noise condition without parametric assumptions on noise or mixing function, reducing required environments from O(n) to 2 (R1). This generality is theoretically interesting (R2).
- Nonparametric assumptions nicely set up, creating a framework where proofs seem relatively straightforward (R4). Applied to a wide variety of special cases yielding strong results in each; if questions addressed, would be a strong contribution (R4).
- Able to identify causal DAGs of the form in Figure 5 by leveraging equivalence between mixing function and DAG (R1).
- Motivations and results clearly presented despite theoretical focus; assumptions and extensions explained in detail; main result effectively extended to Theorems 2, 3, 4 (R3).
- Problem of going beyond additive noise is important and not well studied (R5).

### Weaknesses

- **Presentation and clarity**: Paper does a poor job explaining why assumptions lead to strong identifiability (R1). Where the variability assumption is used in the proof of Theorem 1 is not referenced (R1). The statement that most identifiability results need O(n) environments (lines 226-230) doesn’t explain how this approach avoids that requirement; the example gives no insight because it involves only two parameters and two environments (R1). A proof sketch would be more useful than some examples (R1). The mapping of assumptions between this work and Kong et al. (2022) in the proof of Theorem 1 is unclear; steps from Kong et al. should be rewritten explicitly so anyone can follow (R2). The main challenge and novelty of proving Theorem 1 vs. previous works is not clearly articulated (R3, R5). The proof appears to be a direct combination of Kong et al. and structural sparsity (Moran et al. 2022); it is unclear what new proof techniques were needed (R5). The problem reduces to block identification; existing partial identification frameworks (e.g., Kugelgen et al. 2021, Ahuja et al. 2023, Kong et al. 2023) may already cover these settings, yet authors overclaim about being first with general noise (R5). Should contrast proof technique and provide intuitions on differences (R5).

- **Assumptions strong and unclear**:
  - Structural sparsity is extremely strong; paper could be more realistic when discussing assumptions – many references to real-world data types that may not follow this framework exactly (R4).
  - Nondegeneracy precludes linear functions, which is understandable (R4).
  - "Variability exists" is unclear: it is much stronger than in Kong et al. (2022) – Kong et al. require for any set A there exist two domains such that two integrals are not equal, whereas this paper requires there exist two domains d1,d2 such that for any set A the two specific integrals are not equal. Moreover, it is not clear what these domains are in the given model (R2). Example 1 only provides a set A of specific structure, but the assumption requires it hold for arbitrary A (R2). Technically, the assumption is not satisfiable because A = Z x E is not disallowed (since Z, E are not subsets of themselves), so p(A|d1)=p(A|d2)=1 identically, violating it – possibly a typo (R4). For the variability assumption, why are rectangular sets excluded? Where is it used in the proof of Thm 1? (R4).
  - The latent variables z and noise e are symmetric with respect to the generating mechanism – what stops noise from following the same assumptions? How to ensure the algorithm recovers z instead of e? (R2)
  - Theorems require “sufficiently large” dataset; unclear what qualifies as large – not specific sample complexity, possibly only asymptotic (R3).
  - Are the 2 domains assumed labeled? (R4). In Eq (30), what is the sparsity constraint on \hat{F} and where is it listed in the assumptions? (R4). Eq (35) – why is this without loss of generality? (R4).

- **Relation to existing work**: Missing citation of important results, e.g., Peters et al. (2014) on causal discovery with additive noise – need to specify limitations and importance (R2). Definition 1 (element-wise identifiability) is only cited with two papers by the same author – unclear how commonly used (R3). Theorem 2 (additive noise) generalization appears trivial via deconvolution argument from Khemakhem et al. – if similar assumptions, does not go beyond existing identification literature (R5). The generative model in Section 4.2 is a specific case of Theorem 1 – what is the new contribution? (R2). Theorem 3 combines models from Thm1 and Thm2; could be a corollary rather than separate result (R5).

- **Practical relevance and experiments**:
  - Overselling applications: claims about medical imaging, finance, dependable patient monitoring (line 302) are unrealistic without considering finite-sample estimation error; identifiability is necessary but far from sufficient; need upfront about limitations (R1).
  - Experiments unconvincing: synthetic simulations appear well-specified (normalizing flow) – not realistic (R1). MNIST and FashionMNIST examples expose unreliability – e.g., MNIST latents interpreted as height and “right slope” but clearly entangled with stroke width (rightmost columns use thinner strokes); FashionMNIST latents are unclear. Limitations are buried in the appendix (R1). No experiments for Theorems 2-4 (both representation identification and causal discovery) (R5). In synthetic data, need more details on split between latent and noise factors; what is the “base” experiment? (R4).

- **No proposed method**: Paper provides only identifiability guarantees but no method to identify the latent variables z. Guarantees up to element-wise invertible transformation are restrictive because the transformation can be very complex and infeasible to learn (R2). Why no empirical comparison to existing methods? (R1). If general noise can be treated as an additional latent (as suggested in the question), why not compare? (R1).

- **Writing**: Verbose in many places; examples given without citations (e.g., lines 165-188, 298-308, 357-369) (R5).
