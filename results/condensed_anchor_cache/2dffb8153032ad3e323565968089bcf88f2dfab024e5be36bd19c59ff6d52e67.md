## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes PEARL, a framework for learning positional encodings (PEs) on graphs by applying message-passing GNNs to random or basis node inputs and aggregating the outputs. The key insight is that GNNs can be viewed as nonlinear functions of eigenvectors, enabling the generation of PEs without explicit eigendecomposition. The method claims to satisfy four criteria: stability, expressivity, scalability, and genericness. Theoretical analysis covers stability, sample complexity, and expressivity. Empirical evaluations on graph regression and classification benchmarks show competitive performance compared to eigenvector-based PEs (e.g., SPE) with significantly lower computational complexity.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important problem: learning efficient and effective positional encodings for graph representation learning.
- Innovative approach: using message-passing GNNs as nonlinear mappings of eigenvectors to generate PEs, avoiding costly full eigendecomposition.
- Good motivation: identifies four key properties (stability, expressivity, scalability, genericness) and attempts to satisfy them jointly.
- Scalability: worst‑case forward complexity O(N^2) without high preprocessing; experiments show one or two orders of magnitude lower complexity than eigenvector‑based methods (Tables 1, 2).
- Empirical performance: achieves results comparable to SPE on benchmarks (e.g., ZINC) while being much faster and memory‑efficient.
- Simple plug‑in: the method is easy to implement with standard GNN components.
- Consideration of both random (R‑PEARL) and basis (B‑PEARL) inputs for different scales, with sample size analysis in Figure 2.
- Good performance on ZINC dataset (noted by Reviewer 4).
- Rigorous theoretical analysis of sample complexity for R‑PEARL (Theorem 4.3).
- Well‑written and clearly presented (noted by Reviewers 2, 5).

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical gaps and errors**  
  – Proposition 3.1 assumes a symmetric graph shift operator (GSO) for spectral decomposition, but the random walk matrix used in Eq. (2) is not symmetric. This undermines the theoretical justification for non‑symmetric GSOs (Reviewer 2, Major 1).  
  – Equation (3) does not correctly represent all MPNNs: an MLP from ℝ^k to ℝ^k does not trivially extend to a graph signal of dimension ℝ^{n×h}; correct representation likely involves matrix multiplications from the right with learnable matrices (Reviewer 1).  
  – Proposition 3.1 appears trivial: since graphs can be bijectively mapped to eigenvectors, eigenvalues, and node features, any graph function is a function of eigenvectors, so the choice of MPNN over other models is not justified (Reviewer 4).  
  – Remark 5.1 is incorrect: in Huang et al., α_i are analytic sequence‑to‑sequence mappings, while here they are real‑valued and operate on each eigenvector independently, making the approach less expressive in the frequency domain (Reviewer 1).  
  – Proposition 4.3 is unclear (the variable m is undefined) and appears to be a direct corollary of results from Gama et al. (Reviewer 1).  
  – Most theoretical results (Theorem 4.3, Propositions 4.1, 4.2, 4.3) are direct corollaries of previous work; contributions to stability and expressivity analysis are limited (Reviewers 1, 4).  
  – Theorem 4.3’s proof (Formula 45) includes the maximum degree of the graph, which is later absorbed into a constant β. This is problematic for large graphs with high‑degree nodes, requiring more samples (Reviewer 4).  
- **Permutation equivariance only in expectation**  
  – The claim of permutation equivariance for Eq. (6) is correct only in expectation over random inputs; the actual method with a single sample of noise is not equivariant, a known limitation similar to Sato et al. (2020) (Reviewers 1, 4).  
- **Novelty and positioning**  
  – The method appears to be a variant of SPE where the equivariant sequence‑to‑sequence function is replaced by a point‑wise function on eigenvectors; the novelty is primarily the use of random inputs. It is unclear whether this improves or decreases expressivity, stability, or generalization (Reviewer 1).  
  – The paper is not well‑integrated into existing literature; it does not clearly differentiate from cycle‑counting methods or other spectral approaches (Reviewer 1).  
  – Missing comparisons with highly related works that also use randomization for expressivity and PEs: Random GNN and PF‑GNN (Reviewer 5, Weakness 3).  
  – The claim that SPE can be efficiently implemented by B‑PEARL with lower complexity (line 463) is not rigorously justified – more analysis of computational graphs is needed, not just empirical numbers (Reviewer 2, Major 2).  
- **Experimental limitations**  
  – Only four datasets are used; additional datasets are needed to strengthen validity (Reviewer 4).  
  – The backbone GNN for PEARL is fixed across all experiments; experiments with different GNN backbones should be added to demonstrate broad applicability and ensure fair comparison (Reviewer 4; suggests protocol from [2]).  
  – The claimed expressiveness improvement is not empirically demonstrated, e.g., by identifying graphs not distinguishable by standard GNNs; comparisons with Random GNN and PF‑GNN would help (Reviewer 5, Weakness 4).  
  – Out‑of‑distribution (OOD) generalization benefits are claimed due to stability, but Table 3 shows that stable methods like SPE and R‑PEARL do not consistently improve OOD‑size performance; the connection is not empirically supported (Reviewer 2, Major 3).  
  – The experimental setup may not follow standard practice: for ZINC, a parameter budget of 500K is customary; it is unclear whether this was used (Reviewer 1).  
  – Scalability claims lack direct evidence: no experiments on larger graphs or speed measurements are provided (Reviewer 1).  
- **Method design and evaluation**  
  – No loss function biases the learned encodings toward meaningful positional information (e.g., orthogonality constraints); this may lead to less discriminative or interpretable encodings (Reviewer 5, Weakness 1).  
  – It is unclear why eigenvectors are used instead of random features for small graphs; a direct comparison of performance and stability between the two input types on small graphs would be beneficial (Reviewer 5, Weakness 2).  
  – The use of a 9‑layer MPNN is not justified; depth impact is unclear, and given similarity to power iterations, a discussion of convergence properties is needed (Reviewer 2, Major 4).  
  – Ablation studies on different initialization strategies and pooling functions beyond mean pooling are limited (Reviewer 3, Weakness 4).  
  – Limited discussion of practical applications and real‑world scenarios (e.g., drug discovery, social networks) (Reviewer 3).  
- **Minor issues**  
  – Line 133‑134: definition of F_{l‑1} is unclear; should be F_l for X^{(l)} (Reviewer 2, Minor 1).  
  – Line 293: seems to refer to Prop 4.1, not 4.2 (Reviewer 2, Minor 2).
