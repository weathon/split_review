- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 1, 3, 1

## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces Regularised Jump Models for joint regime identification and feature selection in financial time series, adding regularization penalties (L0, Lasso, Ridge, Group-Lasso) to the Jump Model objective, analogous to regularised K-means. The method is compared to Standard and Sparse Jump Models via a simulation study, with hyperparameter tuning via clustering stability. Reviewers find the problem relevant and the adaptation sensible, but raise major concerns about missing motivation and related work, insufficient clarity in notation and algorithmic details, lack of real-data evaluation, reliance on an external paper for simulation details, unconvincing results (no clear advantage over baselines), and an overall incremental contribution relative to existing work.

### Strengths

- The paper identifies a relevant problem in Jump Models for financial time series and proposes a regularisation extension that, with further development, could offer useful insights (Reviewer 2).
- The proposed Regularised Jump Models adapt the Regularised K-means approach to the Jump Model framework, aiming to improve interpretability and accuracy over the Standard and Sparse Jump Models (Reviewer 4).
- The inclusion of multiple penalty terms (L0, Lasso, Ridge, Group-Lasso) is noted as a positive aspect (Reviewer 1).
- One reviewer found the contribution of the paper clearly presented (Reviewer 3), though other reviewers strongly disagreed about overall clarity.

### Weaknesses

1. **Missing motivation and related work**: The paper jumps directly into model definitions without providing an overview of related work, motivation, or current state of research. This makes it difficult for readers to understand the unique contributions and distinguish them from existing models. Several key comparisons are omitted, such as with Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) and the latest regime-switching model RHINE (SIAM SDM2024), and alternative feature selection methods for time series (Reviewers 2, 3, 4). The paper does not explain why Regularised K-means is specifically optimal for adaptation into the Jump Model framework (Reviewer 4).

2. **Poor presentational clarity and incomplete exposition**:
   - Numerous typos throughout the manuscript (Reviewer 1).
   - Multiple mathematical notations are undefined or confusing: e.g., \(\bar{\mu}\) in Eq. (1.2); \(\mathbf{Y}\) vs \(\mathbf{y}_t\); cluster \(C_k\); \(\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\) vs \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k\); \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\cdot,j}\) vs \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_j\); \(\mathbf{y}_t\) vs \(\mathbf{y}_{\cdot,j}\) in Appendix B; \(\psi\) vs \(\Psi\) with inconsistent parameters; distance function \(d\) defined as a random variable; expectation in (3.1) unclear; notation clash: \(S\) used for both soft thresholding operator and state vector (Reviewers 1, 3).
   - Details of the algorithms are missing. For example, Step 3 of Algorithm 3 ("100% largest \(d_j\)") is unclear; the meaning of "cluster set" \(\mathcal{C}_k\) is not explained; the initialization strategy from [11] (7 initial state sequences) appears ad hoc and lacks justification (Reviewers 1, 3).
   - Appendix A is left blank, and Proposition B.1 is merely a standard partitioning principle of K-means with missing proof (Reviewer 4).
   - The clustering stability criterion (Definition 3.2) seems to prioritise cluster reproducibility over parsimony; a discussion of why this aligns with the goals of the analysis is absent (Reviewer 3).

3. **Lack of real-world data application**: No real data example is provided, which lowers the practical importance and relevance of the proposed methodologies. There is no illustration of how the model performs on actual financial time series or how interpretability is achieved in practice (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).

4. **Simulation study is insufficient and poorly documented**:
   - All simulation details are referenced to another paper [9] rather than being self-contained; the reader cannot evaluate the setup, parameter choices, or motivation (Reviewers 2, 3).
   - The metric BAC is not defined, and the meaning of \(\mu\) (tables show a single integer while earlier \(\mu\) denotes cluster centres) is unclear (Reviewer 3).
   - Table captions are insufficient to understand results; much discussion consists of empirical observations without extracting general principles of algorithm performance (Reviewer 3).
   - No computational time comparison is provided (Reviewer 2).

5. **Incremental contribution and unconvincing results**:
   - The paper adds a standard regularisation penalisation term to the jump model objective in exactly the same way as to k-means, making the contribution fairly incremental and not ICLR standard (Reviewer 3).
   - The proposed model does not always outperform existing models (Standard Jump Model and Sparse Jump Model), and no recommendation is given on which penalty to choose or when to use the regularised model versus the sparse model in particular situations (Reviewer 2).
   - The paper does not demonstrate how the claimed better interpretability of feature selection is achieved; results only report BAC, not feature selection accuracy or interpretability metrics (Reviewer 2).
   - The assumption that the number of regimes \(K\) is known is restrictive, and no guidance is provided on how to choose \(K\) in practice (Reviewer 1; also implied by other reviewers' comments on missing ablation experiments).

6. **Missing details in equations and definitions**:
   - Eq. (1.6) lacks explanations on how each component, including penalty terms \(P(\mu)\), specifically enhances feature selection and regime accuracy (Reviewer 4).
   - It is unclear what the maximization in Eq. (1.2) is over, and the role of the state sequence in that equation is not explained (Reviewer 1).

7. **Algorithm 2 (Sparse Jump Model calibration) is not used in the development of the proposed method and occupies space that could be used for better exposition** (Reviewer 3).

8. **Lack of theoretical results**: The paper mentions theoretical results as future work; including them would have strengthened the incremental contribution (Reviewer 3).

Note: While one reviewer found the contribution clearly presented, the above weaknesses reflect a consensus that the paper suffers from major clarity and completeness issues. The minority positive assessment does not outweigh the pervasive concerns across all reviewers.
