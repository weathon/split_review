## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces GLEE, a framework and benchmark for evaluating LLMs in two-player sequential language-based economic games (bargaining, negotiation, persuasion). It standardizes parameterization of games and defines metrics (self-gain, efficiency, fairness). The authors collect 954K LLM vs. LLM game results and human data (one reviewer reports 3,405 game results, another reports 34k games from 3,405 players). A regression model is used to simulate human behavior for comparison. Reviewers score the paper 3, 5, 5, 6, indicating mixed opinions. Strengths include comprehensive parameterization, large-scale data collection, open-source release, and integration of human data. Weaknesses center on limited novelty relative to existing benchmarks [1–3] (reviewers 2,4), insufficiently deep analysis (reviewers 1,2), limited model selection (reviewers 2,3), unclear metric definitions (reviewers 4), readability issues (reviewer 1), and missing exploration of agent motivations (reviewer 4). Reviewer 3 (score 6) is more positive, finding the framework novel and the human dataset scale substantial; reviewer 4 (score 3) is more critical, questioning novelty and clarity.

### Strengths
1. **Comprehensive parameterized game design and large simulation scale.** The framework covers bargaining, negotiation, and persuasion with consistent parameterization, inspired by economic literature (reviewers 1,2,3). Over 954K LLM games and a substantial human dataset (3,405 game results from one count; 34k games from 3,405 players from another) were collected (reviewers 1,2,3).  
2. **Novel regression-based human modeling.** Using a regression model to simulate human participants and compare with LLM behavior is new and interesting (reviewer 1).  
3. **Open-source release and reproducibility.** Code and data are released on GitHub, facilitating further research (reviewer 2).  
4. **Useful classification of agent types.** The framework introduces classifications for aspects such as game horizon, information structure, and communication form (reviewer 4).  
5. **Extensive human-LLM interaction data.** The inclusion of human vs. LLM interactions provides a meaningful benchmark for comparing human and artificial agents (reviewer 2). Reviewer 3 particularly highlights the “solid foundation for analysis” from the human data (34k games from 3,405 players).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty relative to existing benchmarks.** Reviewers 2 and 4 note that similar frameworks already exist (e.g., [1] “Can Large Language Models Serve as Rational Players in Game Theory? A Systematic Analysis”, [2] “How Far Are We on the Decision-Making of LLMs?”, [3] “GTBench: Uncovering the Strategic Reasoning Limitations of LLMs via Game-Theoretic Evaluations”). The paper does not sufficiently differentiate GLEE or establish its unique advantages (reviewer 2). Reviewer 4 finds “no significant distinction between this framework and prior economic frameworks.” In contrast, reviewer 3 perceives the framework as addressing “lack of standardized methods” and therefore novel.  
2. **Superficial and incomplete analysis.**  
   - Regression models for predicting metrics yield only moderate adjusted R² values (e.g., 0.57 for bargained efficiency), suggesting the models do not fully capture interaction complexity (reviewer 2).  
   - The analysis of game parameters on evaluations is insufficient; it is hard to derive clear conclusions from the main text (reviewer 1).  
   - Figures (e.g., Figure 1) are hard to comprehend (reviewer 1).  
   - The paper lacks analysis of why human players perform worst as Bob in negotiation games (counterintuitive) (reviewer 1, Q1).  
   - No exploration of whether efficiency decreases under full information originate from LLMs’ limited long-context capability (reviewer 1, Q2).  
3. **Limited model selection.** The study uses only four LLMs (e.g., Qwen-2-7B, Llama-3-8B), which are not cutting-edge (e.g., GPT-4o), limiting generalizability (reviewers 2,3).  
4. **Unclear definitions of key metrics.** Metrics such as efficiency and fairness are not clearly defined in the framework (reviewer 4). Reviewer 3 also questions the validity of the self-gain, efficiency, and fairness definitions and whether interaction trajectories influence these scores.  
5. **Readability and presentation issues.** The paper is not easy to read, especially when introducing game scenarios and degrees of freedom; a graph to display scenario differences and a table for game parameters would help (reviewer 1).  
6. **Neglect of agent motivation and strategic bias.** The motivations of both players (e.g., fair vs. selfish) are not integrated into the framework. Examining outcomes like Alice (Fair) vs. Bob (Fair) or Alice (Selfish) vs. Bob (Fair) could provide valuable insights (reviewer 4).  
7. **Limited economic scope.** The three game families (bargaining, negotiation, persuasion) may not cover the full range of real-world economic interactions, and task setups (e.g., Alice/Bob) might bias model behavior (reviewer 3). Additional economic scenarios should be tested to broaden applicability (reviewer 3, Q1).  
8. **Human data validity concerns.** Human data is more inconsistent than LLM responses; the authors should further justify the use of a regression model to model human results (reviewer 1).
