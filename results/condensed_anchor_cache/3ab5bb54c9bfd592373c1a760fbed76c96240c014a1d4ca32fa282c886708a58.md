## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper argues that the traditional evaluation setup for record-specific Membership Inference Attacks (MIAs)—which uses dataset sampling as a source of randomness to create IN/OUT models—incorrectly captures privacy risk for outliers, because outlier status depends on the specific data sample. The authors propose a *model-seeded* evaluation that keeps the dataset fixed and varies only model randomness (initialization, training seeds). Experiments across 10 combinations of models, datasets, and attacks show that model-seeded evaluation detects high-risk samples missed by traditional methods (e.g., LiRA) and that the traditional setup underestimates the risk posed by a strong Differential Privacy adversary. The paper also argues that TPR at low FPR is unsuitable for per-record MIA and uses ROC AUC instead. Reviewers are divided: one reviewer (score 6) finds the idea novel and the evidence compelling; two reviewers (scores 3) find the comparison between setups invalid and the novelty limited; one reviewer (score 5) raises fundamental concerns about the threat model (attacker assumed to know training data) and lack of shadow-model diversity.

### Strengths
- **Novel idea**: Proposing model-seeded evaluation (varying model randomness) instead of dataset sampling to better capture the privacy risk of outliers in record-specific MIAs. (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4)
- **Clear overview and organization**: The paper provides a well-structured introduction to MIAs and clearly explains the motivation. (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 4)
- **New metrics demonstrate improvement**: The proposed RMSE and Miss Rate metrics show that model-seeded evaluation detects high-risk records missed by traditional setups. (Reviewer 1)
- **Comprehensive experiments**: Evaluation spans 10 combinations of models, datasets (including CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100), and attacks for both predictive and generative AI. (Reviewer 1)
- **Important and interesting topic**: Privacy auditing via MIAs is a timely and significant area. (Reviewer 2)
- **Original angle**: Introducing a new perspective on how to evaluate per-record privacy risks in MIAs. (Reviewer 4)

### Weaknesses
- **Threat model assumption is unrealistic (major concern)**: The proposal implicitly assumes the attacker has access to the target model’s training data (the fixed dataset for shadow models). In standard MIA, attackers do not have this access; shadow models are specifically used to simulate training data diversity. Keeping the dataset fixed and varying only seeds may produce shadow models that do not accurately reflect the target model’s distribution. If the attacker did have training data, the attack would be much simpler and shadow models would be unnecessary. This fundamental flaw makes the evaluation less relevant to real-world MIA scenarios. (Reviewer 4, echoed by Reviewer 2’s confusion about the privacy game)
- **Comparison between setups is not meaningful**: The traditional (dataset-sampled) and model-seeded setups measure different privacy games (average risk over datasets vs. per-record risk given a fixed dataset). Comparing RMSE, Miss Rate, or other metrics across these two distinct evaluation frameworks is invalid. (Reviewer 2)
- **Lack of novelty relative to prior work**: The proposed model-seeded setup is identical to Definition 3.4 in Ye et al. 2022 (the adversary distinguishes between models trained on fixed subsets that include vs. exclude the target point). The paper does not adequately discuss this prior work or clarify what is new. (Reviewer 2)
- **Use of ROC AUC for privacy risk evaluation is problematic**: Carlini et al. (2022a) argue that at low FPR (e.g., 0.1%) the TPR is the relevant metric for MIA; using AUC can obscure realistic attack success rates. The paper should report results using TPR at low FPR. (Reviewer 2)
- **Unclear privacy risk calculation**: In Algorithm 1, the step “calculate privacy risk using a chosen metric based on the prediction set” is vague. For each sample, is a threshold chosen to separate IN and OUT models, and then is AUC computed? This needs clarification. (Reviewer 1)
- **Argument against TPR at low FPR is insufficiently justified**: The paper claims TPR at low FPR is not suitable for per-record MIA but does not fully explain why. (Reviewer 1)
- **Outlier–risk correlation may not be universal**: The model-seeded method assumes that outlier status consistently correlates with higher privacy risk. However, some outliers may be easily predictable and thus less vulnerable to MIAs. The method may overestimate risk for such records, leading to overly conservative privacy evaluations with no mechanism to distinguish truly high-risk outliers. (Reviewer 3)
- **Computational overhead not analyzed**: The model-seeded approach requires retraining many models on a fixed dataset with different seeds. The paper does not compare the computational cost to traditional methods, making it hard to assess practical feasibility for real-world deployments. (Reviewer 3)
- **Missing experiments and analyses**:
    - Visual examples of the high-risk samples misclassified by traditional MIAs would help determine whether they appear as outliers to human perception. (Reviewer 1)
    - How does the gap between methods change with dataset complexity? Table 1 shows CIFAR-10 has the lowest miss rates; what would CIFAR-100 or more complex datasets yield? (Reviewer 1)
    - What would Figure 3 look like for CIFAR-10 or CIFAR-100? (Reviewer 1)
    - The paper should report Table 1 and Theorem 1 results using TPR at low FPR instead of AUC. (Reviewer 2)
- **Lack of discussion of recent MIA evaluation works**: The Related Work omits key recent studies on MIA evaluation metrics and the limitations of standard metrics. The statement starting at line 124 lacks concrete examples of why conventional metrics fall short. (Reviewer 3)
- **Need to clarify the privacy game**: The paper should explicitly define the adversary’s objective and the threat model for both the traditional and model-seeded setups, and justify why comparing metrics across the two is meaningful. (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4)
- **Suitability for a main conference**: Some reviewers (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4) feel the contribution is more appropriate for a datasets and benchmarks track or a workshop on evaluation methodology, rather than the main conference.
