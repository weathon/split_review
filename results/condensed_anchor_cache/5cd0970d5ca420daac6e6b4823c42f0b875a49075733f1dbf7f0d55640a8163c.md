## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper investigates targeted relearning attacks on LLMs that have undergone approximate unlearning. The authors show that with access to a small, often loosely related set of auxiliary data (e.g., public medical articles, general Wikipedia information about Harry Potter), an adversary can “jog” the memory of an unlearned model and recover supposedly forgotten information. They formalize the unlearning-relearning pipeline and test it on three common unlearning applications: removing hazardous knowledge (WMDP), mitigating memorization of copyrighted content (WHP), and suppressing specific undesirable words/phrases (TOFU). They also provide a simplified theoretical example (Section 6) to illustrate how correlated token pairs can enable recovery. Experiments span multiple models (Phi-1.5, Llama-2) and unlearning methods (gradient ascent, NPO, SSD, etc.), and demonstrate that LoRA unlearning is less robust than full-parameter fine-tuning. Reviewers generally agree the paper is well-written and covers important ground, but opinions on novelty differ: some find the contribution incremental relative to prior work (Qi et al. 2023, Lynch et al. 2024, Guo et al. 2024), while others value the breadth, clear factorization of relearn data types, and the mechanistic experiment in Section 6. Several weaknesses regarding presentation, metric clarity, and missing experiments are noted.

### Strengths

*   Demonstrates an important limitation: current LLM unlearning methods do not fully remove knowledge; relearning can recover supposedly forgotten information, suggesting that unlearning methods may be more akin to obfuscation than true removal, and that current evaluation metrics may be insufficient.
*   Comprehensive experimental evaluation across multiple unlearning applications (hazardous knowledge, copyrighted content, specific words/phrases), datasets (TOFU, WHP, WMDP), base models (Phi-1.5, Llama-2), unlearning methods (GA, NPO, SSD, etc.), and setups (full-parameter vs. LoRA).
*   Clear discussion and useful factorization of prior work limitations, separating relearning on the unlearning data itself vs. relearning on related but distinct data.
*   Provides both empirical results and theoretical intuition (Section 6, correlated token pairs) for why relearning can recover supposedly forgotten information.
*   Well-written, easy-to-follow, with clear schematics (Figures 1, 2) and motivation figures.
*   Provides useful evidence that LoRA unlearning is less effective against relearning than full-parameter fine-tuning.
*   The experiment in Section 6 (simplified setting with correlated token pairs) is especially interesting and a promising direction for better understanding when relearning is a danger/possibility.
*   The paper contributes to the literature by highlighting that unlearning may not be as easy as previously thought, and that the recovery of unlearned knowledge indicates the knowledge remains parameterised in the model.

### Weaknesses

*   **Novelty concerns.** Several reviewers note that the idea that relearning can recover unlearned information has been explored in prior works (e.g., Qi et al. 2023; Lynch et al. 2024; Guo et al. 2024 - “Robust Unlearning via Mechanistic Localization”). The authors differentiate their work primarily by a more rigorous separation of relearning data (excluding overlap with the original unlearned set), but this does not fully overcome the novelty limitation for some reviewers. The strength of the contribution was rated as relatively weak by Reviewer 1 (score 6) and Reviewer 3 (score 5), while Reviewer 4 (score 8) appreciated the breadth and depth. The contrast in assessment should be noted.
*   **Mechanistic explanation is trivial or underdeveloped.** The theoretical intuition in Section 6 is described as “obvious” or “trivial” by some reviewers. The experiments in Section 6 assume strong correlation between relearning and unlearning data, so the explanation (“when the unlearned set contains highly correlated pairs, relearning on one can recover the other”) does not provide further insight beyond the assumption. The claim that unlearning “does not remove associations between different parts of x” was questioned: minimising conditional probability *p*(x_i | x_{<i}) during unlearning should destroy all correlations within x. The authors are asked to clarify this point.
*   **Missing experiments and analyses.**
    *   Section 6 experiment should be expanded with more datasets, models, and n-gram correlations to strengthen the evidence for takeaway #3.
    *   No heuristics or quantitative rules are provided for predicting when relearning is easy/hard. For example, does model scale, ROUGE, embedding-based similarity, or number of unlearning steps predict relearning difficulty?
    *   No exploration of artificial correlations/decorrelations in a TOFU-like setting to quantitatively relate correlation and relearning success.
    *   Reviewer 4 recommends that all three metrics (from Section 3.3) should be plotted for every experiment (either main text or appendix) to confirm consistency across metrics. Currently different experiments use different metrics (e.g., Figure 5 reports NLL loss).
    *   No discussion of potential defenses that model providers (e.g., OpenAI) could deploy on finetuning APIs to mitigate the threat, such as keyword filtering.
*   **Presentation and formatting issues.**
    *   Figure 3: It is unclear which metric is being visualized as “Attack Success Rate”; the three metrics from Section 3.3 are introduced but not clearly linked. The colormap range should be expanded from [0, 0.8] to [0, 1.0]; unlearning steps = 0 should be included; axis titles for TOFU and WHP are missing. A lineplot format might be more informative than a heatmap.
    *   Figure 4: Missing axis titles for models; adjacent figures with the same y-axis use different y limits, which is misleading; duplicate legends.
    *   Table 1: Ambiguous caption – unclear whether it is a schematic or real experimental output. Column titles for TOFU and WHP are missing. Table 2 has the same ambiguity.
    *   Line 242-243: Need citations for gradient ascent as a common unlearning baseline.
    *   Line 251: Typo: “on the a set of text”.
    *   Line 424: Formatting error truncates important information: “Next, we construct the forget set D_u by collecting all the data that on a subset of the names.”
*   **Fuzzy definitions.** Section 3.1 introduces “uninformative/unrelated” text without a quantification method; this notion is likely to be contested in the community.
*   **Structural concerns.** The manuscript would be more exciting if results appeared earlier (currently Section 3-4 take long to reach results). Section 2 and 3 could be shortened or deferred to the appendix. Algorithm 1 is verbose and adds little information.
*   **Unaddressed reviewer questions.**
    *   Reviewer 4: On Line 421, how do you ensure the unlearned model has 0% success rate in generating “Anthony Mark”?
    *   Reviewer 4: In Figure 5, should the legend be “Anthony | x” and “Mark | x Anthony”?
    *   Reviewer 4: Why are different metrics reported for different experiments? Can the same 3-4 metrics be plotted for every experiment?
*   **Alternative framing.** Reviewer 4 strongly suggests that the story should highlight that current unlearning methods are merely “obfuscating” information rather than removing it, and recommends a rewritten title/abstract to communicate this more impactful point. (This was partially addressed in revision, but remains a structural concern.)
*   **Missing citations.** Reviewer 4 notes potential related works: https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.01814 and https://ai.stanford.edu/~kzliu/blog/unlearning.
