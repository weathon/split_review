## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates intersectional stereotypes in LLMs by (1) introducing the WinoIdentity benchmark (augmenting WinoBias with 25 demographic markers) and evaluating uncertainty parity on five causal LLMs for pronoun‑occupation coreference, and (2) using the warmth‑competence model to compare LLM‑generated stereotypes with human annotations, claiming alignment and suggesting LLMs could replace human subjects in social psychology research.

### Strengths
- **Intersectional focus**: The topic of intersectional biases in LLMs is important and relatively underexplored (Reviewers 1,2,4).  
- **WinoIdentity dataset**: A well‑thought‑out extension of WinoBias that facilitates future work (Reviewers 1,2).  
- **Diverse rephrasing**: The study guards against prompt‑robustness issues by using multiple rephrasings [1] (Reviewer 1).  
- **Uncertainty parity as a fairness metric**: Provides a fresh perspective measuring model confidence rather than only accuracy (Reviewer 2).  
- **Interdisciplinary application**: Applying the warmth‑competence model from social psychology to LLMs bridges AI and human perception research (Reviewers 1,2).  
- **Coverage**: Five LLMs and 50 intersectional identities are evaluated (Reviewer 1).  

**Disagreement**: One reviewer (Reviewer 3) found no strengths, citing poor writing, weak contributions, and disorganized presentation, and would reject the paper outright. Another reviewer (Reviewer 2) rated the paper 5 and found clear strengths.

### Weaknesses
- **Conceptual and definitional issues**:  
  - The paper mischaracterizes Blodgett et al. (2020) as solely about “LLMs learn biases”; it is about poor conceptualization of bias in NLP (Reviewer 1).  
  - Intersectionality is defined as interlocking mechanisms of social oppression, not merely differential discrimination; the paper’s framing is oversimplified (Reviewer 1).  
  - The claim that intersectional biases “have so far been overlooked” is overstated given prior works (e.g., Kirk et al., Lalor et al., and broader ML fairness literature; Reviewer 1,4).  
  - The definition of “uncertainty” is unclear and appears misused; randomness should come from prompt variations or model seeds, not from different samples (Reviewer 3). The interpretation that next‑token probability close to 0.5 indicates “uncertainly correct” assumes well‑calibrated probabilities, which is not justified (Reviewer 1).  

- **Benchmark construction and validity**:  
  - Augmented prompts in Table 2 are unrealistic and unnatural (e.g., “at her, the Black woman”, marking hegemonic identities that are normally unmarked). Terms “female pronouns” should be “feminine pronouns” (Reviewer 1).  
  - The benchmark is simply adding words to a pre‑existing benchmark (WinoBias) and does not constitute a substantive contribution (Reviewer 3).  
  - Criteria for selecting demographic intersections are not explained—whether guided by social science or dataset constraints (Reviewer 2).  
  - No benchmark statistics are provided (Reviewer 3).  

- **Fairness metric and measurement**:  
  - No concrete fairness quantification or metric is reported for the uncertainty analysis (Figure 1, Section 2.3; Reviewer 4).  
  - The main comparison (“designer” vs “black female designer”) is an unjustified indicator of intersectional bias; a more controlled comparison (e.g., with other identity terms) is needed. The observed drop could reflect general adjective effect or poor reasoning about intersectional identities (Reviewer 4).  
  - Table 3 shows decreased next‑word probability for both pro‑ and anti‑stereotypical sentences, suggesting a general coreference decline rather than a bias shift (Reviewer 4).  
  - The bias metric is unclear: is it |referent probability − 0.5|? If so, it should be reported directly instead of raw probabilities. Using |prob‑0.5| rather than the difference between occupations could fluctuate due to other tokens (Reviewer 4).  
  - The novelty of measuring unfairness as “uncertainty” is not established; prior works use log‑probabilities similarly (e.g., ACL 2023 long paper [8]; Reviewer 1).  
  - The choice to prioritize uncertainty parity over other metrics (e.g., calibration) is not justified, and comparison with established metrics is missing (Reviewer 2).  
  - The mechanism by which models “refuse” based on low confidence is not interpretable; users cannot understand when or why the model declines to answer (Reviewer 2).  

- **Human‑LLM alignment and replacement claim**:  
  - The paper reports “statistically significant alignment” but does not specify where this significance is measured (Reviewer 1). Statistical methods (effect sizes, confidence intervals) are missing (Reviewer 2).  
  - Figure 2 shows human_C correlates with both LLM_C and LLM_W, but human_W is not strongly correlated with either; these patterns are not explained (Reviewer 4).  
  - The claim that LLMs can replace human subjects in social psychology research is unsupported by a mere correlation. Criteria for substitution and validity issues are not addressed (Reviewers 1,3,4).  
  - Using LLMs for such research risks flattening representations, excluding under‑represented groups, and essentializing identities, as argued in prior work [9,10] (Reviewer 1).  
  - RQ2 (“Can LLMs help detect stereotypes that have not been studied before?”) is not effectively addressed; justifications beyond correlation are lacking (Reviewer 4).  

- **Paper structure and presentation**:  
  - The two parts (LLM benchmark and human study) feel disconnected and read as separate, unrelated studies (Reviewers 3,4).  
  - The paper is poorly written: Figure 1 is hard to read (e.g., cluttered with many words); tables list numbers without informative captions; the main body and appendix contain disorganized tables/figures (Reviewer 3).  
  - No clear transitions between the two research questions (Reviewer 3).  

- **Overstatement of contributions**:  
  - The methods for measuring stereotypes are largely derived from prior work (warmth‑competence model [11]; Reviewer 1).  
  - The claim of being “first to use uncertainty/statistics ideas to measure stereotypes” is false; many prior works use statistical approaches (Reviewer 3).  
  - Existing literature (e.g., Kirk et al., Lalor et al.) already benchmarks intersectional biases; this paper lacks clarity on novel insights beyond those works (Reviewer 4).  

- **Demographic selection and refusal interpretability**:  
  - Selection of identity intersections is not justified by social science literature or model behavior (Reviewer 2).  
  - No explanation of how the model decides to “refuse” responses based on low confidence, especially for marginalized identities (Reviewer 2).
