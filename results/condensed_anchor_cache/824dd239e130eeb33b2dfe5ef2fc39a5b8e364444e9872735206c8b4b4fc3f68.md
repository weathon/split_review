- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 3, 5, 3, 1

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes R3HF, a reward redistribution method for RLHF that allocates token-level rewards by treating reward prediction as a regression problem. Per-token reward is computed as the difference in the reward model’s score when that token is added to the current prefix. The method is tested on question-answering, a modified TLDR dataset (14,900 examples, not the original 117,000), and SafeRLHF, using LLaMA1. It is compared against PPO and DPO. Evaluation uses win-rates against the own-trained SFT model (using the same reward model) and GPT-4.

### Strengths
- The paper is easy to read and describes the method succinctly (Reviewer1).
- The idea of redistributing rewards at the token level in RLHF is compelling. Traditional RLHF methods assign a single, sequence-level reward and cannot capture individual token contributions, limiting optimization efficiency and nuanced language understanding (Reviewer3; also noted as “novel reward shaping” by Reviewer2, and “promising direction” by Reviewer4).
- The workflow of R3HF is reasonable, providing immediate, token-specific feedback to make language models more responsive to human preferences (Reviewer3).
- The experiments and ablation studies are comprehensive (Reviewer4). Note: This is in strong tension with other reviewers who find the experiments extremely weak.
- The overall direction of learning a dense reward function for RLHF in LMs is promising (Reviewer4).
- The paper considers a relevant topic: how to best align LLMs to human preferences (Reviewer5).
- Minority positive view: Reviewer3 (score 5) finds the experiment result good (Reviewer2 similarly states “experiment result is good”). The other four reviewers (scores 3, 3, 3, 1) are far more critical of the experimental setup.

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical flaw:** RLHF on single-turn generation is a contextual bandit, not a sequential MDP (there is no state transition probability; adding a token deterministically extends the sequence). Therefore a single reward per sequence is appropriate and the need for reward redistribution is invalidated (Reviewer1). The paper’s connection to DPO (Section 3.4) is moot because DPO also assumes a contextual bandit (Reviewer1’s question).
- **Practical flaw:** Learned reward models are explicitly not effective at providing reward for non-EOS tokens (Huang et al., 2024). The authors try to create a value function from the reward model, but their baseline method PPO already learns a value function. Moreover, recent works (e.g., RLOO, Ahmadian et al., 2023) argue that value functions are fundamentally not good in the LLM setting. The paper never compares to value functions, Monte Carlo estimates of value (like RLOO), or REINFORCE variants, and does not provide qualitative examples showing why the approach works (Reviewer1, also echoed by Reviewer5).
- **Poor experiment setup for TLDR:** The dataset uses only 14,900 examples instead of ~117,000 as in Stiennon et al. (2020). The authors should follow Huang et al. (2024) for open-source TLDR experiments. They compare against their own SFT baseline instead of existing human-written baselines (standard practice, see Huang et al., 2024). RLHF should be evaluated using KL vs. performance curves (Gao et al., 2022). Figure 5 shows clear overfitting of baselines but not the author’s method; simply reducing the learning rate of baselines would probably fix their performance. The use of an outdated model (LLaMA1) further weakens the results (Reviewer1).
- **Evaluation concerns:** Using the training reward model for evaluation is problematic because that model is explicitly trained with the method. GPT-4 win-rates are provided but are few. Results in Table 2a and 2b are inconsistent (DPO is worst in 2a and essentially equal to the top method in 2b), indicating the reward model and GPT-4 are not aligned (Reviewer1). It is unclear which reward model is used to calculate the “Average Score” in Table 1(a) and why that evaluation is reasonable given the reward model may be far from perfect (Reviewer4).
- **Heuristic reward design:** The reward design seems heuristic and probably not fit for complex reasoning tasks (Reviewer2). The method assumes each token’s contribution can be accurately calculated using a time-difference approach, which may not be feasible for sequence generation, especially for long sequences requiring context across entire sentences or paragraphs (Reviewer3).
- **Insufficient consideration of prior work:** The paper fails to sufficiently explore existing credit assignment and reward redistribution methods in RLHF (Reviewer3). Methods for learning a dense per-step reward without additional fine-grained label data have been clearly proposed (e.g., Feng et al., ICLR 2023; Yang et al., NeurIPS 2023). The central idea in lines 52–85 has been stated similarly in Yang et al. The paper lacks adequate citation, discussion, and comparison with these prior works (Reviewer4). Additionally, the proposed reward formulation essentially corresponds to potential-based reward shaping (Wiewiora, 2003), where the potential function is chosen as the reward model logit scores of intermediate tokens; novelty is limited to that choice (Reviewer5).
- **Lack of motivation for intermediate logits:** The authors do not clearly motivate why the logit scores of intermediate tokens under the reward model are a good potential function for reward shaping. State-of-the-art reward models are trained only on the last token (e.g., end-of-string token), and it is unmotivated why any other logit would yield a reasonable assessment of subsequence quality. There is a clear train-test mismatch (Reviewer5).
- **No principled case for better optimization:** The optimal solution to the regularized MDP with potential-based reward shaping is the same as the optimal solution with sequence-level rewards. Thus the approach can only hope for better training dynamics, not a better optimum, but the authors do not provide a convincing, principled case why their approach should actually provide better training dynamics (Reviewer5).
- **Insufficient baselines and hyperparameter analysis:** The experimental section is extremely weak, comparing only to PPO and DPO. It should compare to simple baselines (REINFORCE variants with various sequence- and token-level baselines) and state-of-the-art RL finetuning algorithms like the inner loop of WARP (Amini et al., 2024) and BOND (Sessa et al., 2024). It is well known that hyperparameter tuning is critical for RLHF (e.g., interactions between learning rate, regularization, number of steps); the authors should add experiments showing robustness across such choices (Reviewer5).
- **Writing and presentation issues:** The paper has inconsistent use of tenses in the experimental section and repetitive use of “Eq. equation” (Reviewer3). Equation (7) is confusing: starting from the second line, all \(\tilde r^{RM}\) should be \(\mathcal{R}_\phi\) (Reviewer4). Terms like “sequence-wide return” and “state-action sequence” (L96) are unclear. The meaning of \(p^*\) in L155 is not defined (Reviewer4). The source code is not anonymously available for review (Reviewer4).
- **Missing analysis and scalability questions:** The paper does not explain how the per-step reward function \(\mathcal{R}_\phi\) is trained (Reviewer4). There is no analysis of the reward distribution of the proposed method (Reviewer2). It is unclear if the method can scale to reasoning tasks (math, coding) (Reviewer2); the heuristic design likely fails in such settings (Reviewer2).
