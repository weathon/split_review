## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces EDF (Emphasizes Discriminative Features), a dataset distillation method that uses Grad-CAM activation maps to enhance high-activation discriminative regions in synthetic images and a Common Pattern Dropout (CPD) module to downplay low-loss supervision signals. The authors also construct the Complex Dataset Distillation (Comp-DD) benchmark from ImageNet-1K subsets. Experiments show EDF outperforms prior state-of-the-art on complex subsets and achieves near-lossless performance. Reviewer opinions diverge: one reviewer (score 3) finds the method heavily dependent on original-image initialization and lacking versatility; another (score 6) sees strong potential for advancing DD practicality; two reviewers (scores 5) note strengths in novelty/benchmark but raise concerns about hyperparameter sensitivity, dataset complexity, and assumptions about low-loss patterns.

### Strengths
- **Motivation and approach**: The use of Grad-CAM to target small discriminative regions in complex images is well-reasoned and innovative. The Common Pattern Dropout module for filtering low-loss signals is a creative solution to avoid non-discriminative features.
- **Extensive evaluation**: Experiments are thorough, settings are convincing, and analysis is detailed. Observations (low-loss vs high-loss comparison, t-SNE, Grad-CAM visualizations) help justify the method.
- **Benchmark contribution**: The Comp-DD benchmark, built from sixteen ImageNet-1K subsets (eight easy, eight hard), provides a valuable resource for testing dataset distillation on complex scenarios.
- **Strong results**: EDF achieves state-of-the-art accuracy on complex subsets, with substantial gains and near-lossless performance in several configurations.
- **Paper clarity**: The manuscript is well-organized and generally easy to follow.

### Weaknesses
- **Dependency on original-image initialization**: As shown in Figure 5, Grad-CAM’s discriminative features are highly contingent on the initialized image. With random noise initialization, Grad-CAM may lose significance and cause convergence issues, while baseline MTT remains viable. No experiments with random initialization are provided (R1).
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: Performance depends heavily on dropout ratio α, Grad-CAM update frequency K, and enhancement factor β, requiring dataset-specific fine-tuning. Different IPC configurations in Table 11 use distinct hyperparameters, complicating practical use given MTT/DATM already require many hyperparameters (R1, R2, R3).
- **Versatility and generalizability**:
  - EDF is demonstrated only on top of DATM; it is unclear if it integrates with other baselines (DC, DM, CAFE, DREAM) or whether methods other than Grad-CAM (e.g., other CAM variants) could be used (R1, R4).
  - Results on simpler datasets (CIFAR-10/100) are not reported; benefits there are unclear. Performance in a general environment (like DATM’s original setting) rather than only on Comp-DD is missing (R2, R3, R4).
- **Assumptions about low-loss patterns and “common patterns”**:
  - The claim that low-loss signals correspond to “common patterns” lacks detailed reasoning and justification (R4). In Equation (1), a low loss could arise from a small numerator or large denominator, which imply different meanings (R3).
  - The assumption that smaller activation regions are less discriminative is not fully justified. Additional qualitative evidence (e.g., superposed images with highlighted activation regions) is needed (R2).
- **Grad-CAM model bias and definition**:
  - Equation (3) defines Grad-CAM incorrectly: \(l\) should index feature maps, not convolutional layers, in the gradient-weighted sum. The correct formulation is from Selvaraju et al. (2017) (R2).
  - The network used for Grad-CAM is trained on the same real dataset and may capture biased or non-generalizable features. The paper does not explain how EDF mitigates this bias or adapts to it (R1, R2).
- **Comp-DD benchmark limitations**: ImageNet subsets are portrait-oriented with large backgrounds. The benchmark could be more challenging by including zoomed-out, cropped objects from uncurated datasets like COCO, Objects365, or SA-1B (R2). Also, the observation that small object sizes in complex scenes may be the reason for small activation regions (rather than discriminative features) is not discussed (R4).
- **Missing details and clarifications** (from R3):
  - The “Eval. w/ Knowledge Distillation” component lacks explanation.
  - In Algorithm 1, the specific relationship between low-loss supervision dropout (L_edf) and synthetic image gradient calculation (∇D_syn) is unclear.
  - Line 170 states that the smallest α × P parameters are dropped by loss, but Table 7 shows dropout applied to parameters in layers 2, 4, 5, and 6. The discrepancy needs clarification.
- **Wall clock time**: The proportion of training time spent on Grad-CAM computation is not reported. It is also unclear whether EDF can outperform DATM when both are run under the same wall clock time budget (R1).
- **Lossless claim and cross-architecture evaluation**:
  - The claim of being first to achieve lossless results on ImageNet subsets is questionable because DATM may also reach lossless at IPC 200/300; experimental comparison is needed (R1).
  - Cross-architecture generalization (e.g., ResNet50) to lossless is not shown. If lossless only holds on ConvNetD5, practical significance is limited given that larger networks achieve higher accuracy ceilings (R1).
- **Dataset dependence of observations**: The motivating observations (CIFAR10 vs IN1K-CIFAR10) may not generalize to other datasets without evidence (R4).

*Note: One reviewer (score 3) is notably more critical, especially regarding initialization, versatility, and the lossless claim; another (score 6) is more positive about the method’s potential. The above weaknesses capture all concerns.*
