## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes DIOTM, a neural method for learning the optimal transport (OT) map for the quadratic (Wasserstein-2) cost. It exploits displacement interpolation, deriving a dual formulation that involves time-dependent potentials and transport maps, and incorporates a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) regularizer derived from the optimality condition of the value function. The training uses a min-max (GAN-style) optimization with forward and backward transport maps. Experiments on 2D toy datasets and image-to-image translation (CelebA Male→Female at 64×64 and 128×128, Wild→Cat at 64×64) show improved stability and lower FID compared to existing OT-based models (OTM, NOT, DSBM, ASBM).

### Strengths
- The exploitation of displacement interpolation in neural OT is novel and opens a new perspective; one reviewer (score 8) was particularly positive about this and the derivation of the dual problem for displacement interpolation. (R1, R2)
- The HJB regularizer is theoretically justified from optimality conditions (not an ad‑hoc penalty) and is shown to improve training stability and to be less sensitive to the hyperparameter λ than R1 and OTM regularizers. (R1, R3, R4; Fig. 5, Table 3)
- Comprehensive theoretical derivations link the formulation to displacement interpolation and the HJB equation. (R3, R4)
- On 2D toy datasets (G→8G, G→25G, Moon→Spiral, G→Circles), DIOTM achieves better W² distance than OTM and other baselines. (R1, R3, R4; Table 1)
- On image-to-image translation, DIOTM outperforms OT-based models (OTM, NOT, DSBM, ASBM) in FID. (R2, R4; Table 2)
- Training dynamics are more stable than those of OTM. (R3, R4; Fig. 5)
- The paper is well‑written and provides sufficient background. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical gap in parameterization**: Restricting all t‑dependent transport maps to a linear interpolation via a single map may break the necessary c‑transform condition for each time, potentially undermining theoretical validity. The authors must clarify whether this is a constraint or a result of optimization. (R1)
- **Missing connection to barycenter literature**: The displacement interpolation formulation is essentially a Wasserstein-2 barycenter problem. The dual/semi‑dual forms (Theorem 3.1) have been derived in prior barycenter work ([1], [2]). This connection is not acknowledged, and the method’s relation to existing neural barycenter solvers is not discussed. (R1)
- **Limited to quadratic cost**: DIOTM relies on displacement interpolation and works only for the quadratic (Wasserstein-2) cost; generalization to non‑ℓ² costs is not straightforward. This limitation should be explicitly stated. (R1)
- **Inadequate high-dimensional OT accuracy evaluation**: The claim of improved accuracy is not supported in high dimensions. The 2D toy experiments are low‑dim, and image translation FID does not directly measure OT map quality. The authors should evaluate on high‑dimensional benchmarks such as the Wasserstein-2 benchmark ([3]) and compare to recent OT baselines ([4]). (R1, R2)
- **Missing comparisons to flow‑based transport methods**: No comparison is made to closely related flow‑based methods (Rectified Flow, Flow Matching; ICLR 2023) that also learn transport via velocity fields. These should be included for a comprehensive evaluation. (R2)
- **No visual comparisons with baselines**: Only DIOTM images are shown. Side‑by‑side qualitative comparisons with all baselines are needed to understand perceptual differences. Moreover, DSBM’s wild→cat results at 512×512 (from the DSBM paper) appear visually better than DIOTM’s 64×64 results in Fig. 2, yet DSBM has a worse FID – this discrepancy requires explanation. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- **Missing reconstruction experiments**: DIOTM trains two separate maps (forward/backward). Source→target→source reconstruction error (e.g., ℓ²) should be reported to test invertibility. (R4)
- **Unclear experimental setup for Table 2**: The baseline FID scores in Table 2 are not directly from the cited references (different resolutions, no identical experiments). It is unclear how these scores were obtained; the authors must clarify the experimental protocol (same training steps, architecture, evaluation set) and provide an FID‑vs‑training‑steps ablation. (R3)
- **Missing implementation details**: Architecture details for image‑to‑image networks and the number of images used for FID calculation are not provided. (R4)
- **Stability and training behavior not fully analyzed**: In Fig. 5, OTM’s loss spikes after 40K steps without explanation; if loss does not improve, why train for 60K instead of 40K? Multiple training runs should be shown to assess how often OTM diverges, and FID(epoch) curves would be more informative than loss curves. (R1, R3, R4)
- **Scalability to larger resolutions not demonstrated**: The largest resolution tested is 128×128. To convincingly show scalability, experiments at 256×256 or 512×512 (e.g., on datasets from Isola et al.) should be included. (R4; also R3’s comment on DSBM’s 512×512 results)
- **Theoretical rigor gaps**: The functional spaces for the optimal dual potentials are not specified, and it is unclear whether the supremum over continuous functions is attained. (R1)
- **Additional missing analyses and clarifications**:
  - Role of α in Eq. 8 (should be invariant). (R1)
  - Analysis of time‑sampling schemes (e.g., concentrated near 0/1 vs. 0.5). (R1)
  - Clarify whether the HJB regularizer is used alone or together with R1. (R1)
  - Training time and parameter count comparisons across methods. (R2)
  - Discussion of failure cases (e.g., Fig. 11 shows suboptimal results; R4) and practical constraints (e.g., ability to handle large distribution distances). (R2, R4)
  - Clarify integration in Eq. 6. (R2)
- **Minor issues**: Typographical errors (Line 49 double “the”, Line 243 “parametrization”), repeated citation, and unclear parameter notation for the two transport maps ($\overrightarrow{T}_\theta$ and $\overleftarrow{T}_\theta$ should use distinct parameter symbols). (R2, R4)
