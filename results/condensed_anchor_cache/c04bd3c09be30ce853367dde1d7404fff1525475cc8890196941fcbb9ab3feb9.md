## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a rational metareasoning approach for LLMs that fine-tunes models to selectively use intermediate reasoning steps only when necessary, thereby reducing inference costs while maintaining task performance. The method introduces a Value of Computation (VOC)-based reward function that penalizes unnecessarily long reasoning chains, optimized via Expert Iteration. Experiments across multiple benchmarks (science knowledge, commonsense reasoning, math problem solving, logical deduction, and MMLU for out-of-domain generalization) show 20–37% token savings compared to few-shot chain-of-thought and STaR baselines, on models ranging from 2.7B to 8B parameters. The paper is generally clear and addresses an important and timely problem, but reviewers raised concerns about insufficient baselines/ablations, incremental novelty, limited model/task scope, lack of robustness testing, unclear connection to the VOC framework, and missing comparisons to related work such as Quiet-STaR.

### Strengths
- The problem of balancing inference cost and performance for LLM reasoning is timely and practically relevant.
- The proposed method is simple, easy to reproduce, and could plausibly be adopted in practice.
- The VOC-based reward function is well-designed and thoughtfully applied.
- The approach is evaluated on a diverse set of benchmarks: science, commonsense, math, logical deduction, and out-of-domain MMLU.
- Empirical results show notable token reductions of 20–37% while preserving accuracy across multiple models (phi-2, etc.) and datasets.
- The method shows adaptive behavior: longer reasoning chains for harder questions, shorter for easier ones.
- The paper includes insightful qualitative results in the Appendix.
- The method outperforms STaR, which serves as a direct ablation of Rational Metareasoning.

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient baselines and ablations** – Several reviewers noted the lack of comparisons and ablations needed to understand the method’s contributions:
  - No comparison to Quiet-STaR (Zelikman et al., 2024), which already addresses the cost of reasoning. Reviewers requested a direct comparison to determine if the proposed approach provides orthogonal gains.
  - Missing ablation: directly fine-tuning the LLM to produce only the correct answer (without reasoning) to verify that the model retains genuine reasoning ability and does not simply rely on internal knowledge.
  - Missing ablation: generating reasoning chains, asking the LLM to summarize them, and fine-tuning on the resulting synthetic data – to test whether reinforcement learning is actually needed.
  - No evaluation on harder compositional reasoning benchmarks such as AQUA-RAT.
  - No prompting baseline that instructs the model to think as long as needed but respond concisely (with prompt variations); larger models may already implicitly track reasoning utility.
  - No ablation of the batching technique with increasing T (a design choice).
  - No comparison to alternative fine-tuning algorithms like PPO or iterated DPO (only Expert Iteration is used).
- **Incremental contribution** – The core idea of penalizing long reasoning chains and selecting the best chain for fine-tuning is an extension of STaR, and the novelty is limited. More justification or theoretical analysis of the reward function’s properties is needed.
- **Limited model and task scope** – Experiments focus on smaller models (phi-2, up to 8B); generalizability to larger models is unclear. The evaluation lacks realistic agent-oriented tasks (e.g., GAIA, ToolBench) where LLMs interact with tools and environments.
- **Missing robustness and scalability analysis** – No robustness testing under noisy inputs, adversarial examples, or varying task complexity. The scalability of the online reinforcement learning component to very large models and tasks is not analyzed; wall-clock time savings (including RL overhead) are not reported.
- **Unclear link between VOC theory and implementation** – The mapping from the theoretical VOC components (computations c, beliefs b, actions a) to the practical elements (input x, reasoning chain z, answer y) is not made explicit. Sections 2 and 3 are not well connected.
- **Algorithmic inconsistencies and unclear details** – Line 159’s description of approximating an optimal policy via rejection sampling is confusing; the algorithm selects only the single best reasoning chain rather than all chains above a threshold, deviating from standard Expert Iteration. The initialization $\pi_0$ and subsampling of $\mathcal{D}_n$ from $\mathcal{D}$ are unclear in Algorithm 1.
- **Figures and presentation** – Figures 1 and 2 are not very useful, especially the left subplots; information on the right may duplicate tables. A plot of performance vs. computational cost (tokens) to show the Pareto frontier would be clearer. For Figure 2, 5-shot and/or 0-shot CoT benchmarks for MMLU should be included. Writing improvements are needed in several places (e.g., line 97: Eq. (2) not “Eq. 3.1”).
- **Potential limitation for hard problems** – On harder reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, ProofWriter), reduction in reasoning length is much less impressive, suggesting the method may work well for easy tasks but struggle where CoT is actually needed.
- **Human-friendliness of short responses** – Shorter reasoning chains may not always be more interpretable for humans; a human study assessing qualitative aspects is missing.
- **Sensitivity of cost function** – The choice of logarithmic vs. linear cost in the reward function is not fully justified; details on how $\gamma$ is selected and how sensitive performance is to this parameter are lacking.
- **Questions that overlap with missing experiments**:
  - How necessary is the “rationalization” step (used because STaR uses it)?
  - What is the likelihood that the model fails to generate a correct answer, and are input tokens from failed cases counted?
  - How many Expert Iteration steps $n$ are performed, and what is the compute time vs. final performance relationship?
  - Is the method using a single reward function flexible enough across diverse tasks? Could alternative reward structures be more robust?
