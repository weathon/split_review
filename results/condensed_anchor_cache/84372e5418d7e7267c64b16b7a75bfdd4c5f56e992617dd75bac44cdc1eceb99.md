## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper uses causal analysis (activation patching, probing, circuit identification) to examine how small LLMs solve basic arithmetic (operands < 3 digits). It finds that a small subset of MLP neurons in later layers implement simple heuristics (e.g., operand range detection) and that the combination of these heuristics—termed a “bag of heuristics”—explains most of the model’s accuracy. Results are replicated across models (Llama3-8B, Pythia) and tracked during training. Reviewers generally found the topic timely, the experiments thorough, and the bag-of-heuristics finding a plausible explanation for the lack of length generalization. However, there were significant concerns about the narrow focus (only two-digit arithmetic), the neglect of the attention mechanism’s role, sampling limitations (only 100 prompts), overclaiming the explanation to general reasoning, and missing mechanistic detail about how heuristics actually compute answers. One reviewer (R5) strongly questioned whether the paper provides any explanation beyond identifying neurons, while others highlighted tokenizer dependence and potential variance in the causal analysis.

### Strengths
- The paper applies a comprehensive set of causal analysis techniques (activation patching, probing, circuit identification) to dissect LLM arithmetic behavior, offering a step-by-step examination from circuit identification to neuron-level heuristic classification. Reviewers praised the thoroughness and the clear motivation and explanation of hypotheses (R1, R3, R4).
- The identification of individual heuristic neurons (e.g., activating when an operand falls within a certain range) provides a novel and interpretable perspective on how LLMs compute arithmetic. The “bag of heuristics” concept elegantly explains the observed lack of length generalization (R1, R3, R4).
- Results are replicated across different model scales (Llama3-8B, Pythia). The analysis of heuristic development during training strengthens the causal claims (R1, R3, R4).
- The paper is well-written and well-structured, with good intuitions and visualizations that support the claims (R1, R3, R4). One reviewer (R2) noted the step-by-step examination offers a thorough perspective.

### Weaknesses
- **Overclaim of reasoning vs. arithmetic (R2, R3, R5)**: The title and abstract imply general math reasoning, but the study only covers basic arithmetic with operands up to two digits. The “bag of heuristics” may not generalize to multi-digit arithmetic or other reasoning tasks. R3 explicitly says the title is misleading and recommends a limitations section. R2 and R5 consider this an overclaim (simple one-step calculation is far from reasoning).
- **Lack of mechanistic explanation (R5, echoed by R1, R3)**: The paper does not explain how the identified heuristic neurons actually compute the answer. R5 argues it fails to provide any more detail than “activates certain neurons in later layers”; the computational process remains a black box. R1 notes the difference in logit contributions between correct and incorrect answers is small, suggesting the heuristics may not be the sole mechanism. R3’s knockout experiment query (e.g., whether removing an op1-even neuron specifically harms performance on even examples) highlights missing causal verification per heuristic type—and is not fully addressed by Fig. 7.
- **Neglect of attention mechanism (R5, indirectly R1)**: The paper focuses almost exclusively on MLP layers in the later half and on the last token (“=”), while ignoring how early-layer attention heads route operand and operator information to the final position. R5 states this understanding is crucial and missing. R1’s concern about the high variance of random counterfactual prompts in activation patching further hints at incomplete attribution.
- **Tokenizer dependence and model generalizability (R1, R2, R4)**: The analysis assumes numbers are tokenized as single tokens (true for Llama3 and Pythia up to certain limits). However, other tokenizers (e.g., Llama2) tokenize each digit individually, which could change the nature of heuristics. R1 calls this a significant limitation. R2 notes that all conclusions come from pre-trained checkpoints; analysis on fine-tuned LLMs is missing. R4 questions whether small models trained from scratch on arithmetic would show the same heuristics. R1 also points out that the training evolution experiment uses a model with a different tokenizer and much worse performance than Llama3-8B, making cross-model comparison difficult.
- **Sampling and statistical concerns (R5, R1)**: The paper uses only 100 prompts for most experiments, a very small sample relative to ~1M possible arithmetic combinations. This introduces high variance and raises questions about representativeness. R5 explicitly says this undermines the validity of the findings. R1 notes that activation patching with random counterfactual prompts may have high variance and lacks a sensitivity analysis (e.g., quantifying circuit variability across different sets of counterfactuals).
- **Experimental design and missing details (R3, R1)**: Several choices are not justified or ablated:
  - Eq. 2 measures faithfulness by averaging percentage change in logits; R3 questions why they did not instead check how much accuracy decreases after mean ablation, and whether this design variable was ablated.
  - The x‑axis in Fig. 7 is unclear.
  - The intersection threshold \(t=0.6\) (line 321) is not ablated; R3 asks if it was chosen based on manual inspection.
  - The measurement of heuristic involvement for incorrect prompts (lines 408–410) is not clearly explained.
  - R3 notes that the paper uses logic lens, which has been shown to be unreliable in recent work; alternative hidden state decoding methods (tuned lens, future lens, patchscope) could help classify unassigned heuristics.
  - R1 repeats that a more comprehensive analysis of errors (e.g., interference from other parts of the network) is needed.
- **Manual classification and potential bias (R2, R4)**: Classification of neurons into heuristic types is manual, introducing possible subjectivity. R2 asks if an automated or semi-supervised system could reduce bias. R4 adds that the classification may oversimplify complex neuron interactions.
- **Missing analysis on failures and training dynamics (R2, R3)**: R2 asks for more insights into failure cases (e.g., specific numerical ranges where the model tends to fail). R3 asks whether certain heuristics are reinforced at specific training stages; the paper tracks overall heuristic development but does not drill into per-heuristic evolution.
- **No comparison with alternative methods (R5)**: The heuristic matching method is not compared with other neuron importance identification techniques (e.g., gradient-based, attention attribution). R5 argues the method may not provide more useful information than activation patching and that neurons from any model would likely show similar results.
- **Focus exclusively on arithmetic (R2, R4)**: The paper does not explore whether the heuristic mechanism applies to other forms of reasoning (e.g., multi-step reasoning, arithmetic embedded in text). R2 and R4 consider this an important direction for future work.
