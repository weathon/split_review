## Merged Review

### Summary
This work introduces Head Relevance Vectors (HRVs) that align with visual concepts by analyzing cross-attention heads in text-to-image diffusion models. An HRV for a concept is a vector where each element scores the importance of the corresponding cross-attention head for that concept. The authors employ ordered weakening analysis to interpret HRVs and propose concept strengthening and adjusting methods for three applications: correcting mis‑interpretation of polysemous words, enhancing prompt‑to‑prompt image editing, and mitigating catastrophic neglect in multi‑concept generation. The paper builds on prior work (e.g., Hertz et al.’s P2P) but shifts focus to individual attention heads.

### Strengths
- Clear motivation that builds on the well‑studied problem of understanding cross‑attention in text‑to‑image models (R1, R2).
- The experiments are visually appealing and the ordered weakening analysis (e.g., Figure 3a) together with CLIP similarity metrics (Figure 3b) clearly demonstrates the effect of weakening different heads (R1, R3).
- Improvement over established baselines: SDEdit, P2P, and Attend and Excite (R1).
- Provides a new perspective by analyzing features at the level of individual cross‑attention heads (R3).
- Appendix offers extensive qualitative results that reduce doubts about cherry‑picking (R3).
- The proposed method is useful for three concrete applications: correcting polysemous words, boosting prompt‑to‑prompt editing, and reducing neglect in multi‑object generation (R1, R3).
- Includes discussions on extension to SDXL and the effect across different timesteps (R3).
- The idea of correlating visual concepts with diffusion model internals is interesting (R4).
- Comprehensive quantitative and qualitative comparisons with many other solutions; experimental settings are mainly clearly explained (R2). *Note: Reviewer 2 (score 8) was notably more positive than the other reviewers (scores 6), particularly regarding the clarity and comprehensiveness of comparisons.*

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and insight**: The core idea of manipulating cross‑attention maps to control generation is already established in prior work (e.g., P2P, Diffusion Self‑Guidance). The focus on individual heads and the disentanglement of heads by concept feels like a logical progression rather than a fundamentally new insight into the workings of T2I models (R1). While interesting, the paper does not provide new understanding compared to these earlier works (R1).
- **Unclear theoretical foundation for HRV updates**: The principle behind the HRV construction is insufficiently justified. Specifically, why each visual concept should be assigned the head with the largest value when updating HRVs (L224) is not explained. The paper lacks theoretical justification or intuition for why this procedure works, which is a critical weakness (R3).
- **Insufficient explanation of HRV construction**: The description of how HRVs are computed is ambiguous.
    * The concatenation of token embeddings (L205): it is unclear along which axis the concatenation is performed. If the result is \(N \times (d + H)\), the query \(Q \in \mathbb{R}^d\) does not match the cross‑attention operation (R3).
    * The notation \(K_1, \dots, K_N\) should be included in Figure 2 (R3). Proper equations and notations are needed throughout (R3).
    * The role of the <SOT> and many <EOT> tokens in the HRV update process is not explained. It is unclear whether these tokens are used as padding or carry semantic meaning (R4).
    * A textual description of the HRV construction should be provided instead of relying solely on Figures 2 and 4, to make the mathematical formulation and intuition easier to grasp (R4).
- **Practical limitations**:
    * The method requires fixing a set of concepts beforehand for every HRV construction. There is no study of how the HRV matrix changes when concepts are added, removed, or modified; the approach is not adaptable to dynamic scenarios (R2).
    * Manual settings, choices, and configuration are required for every concept during inference (Sec 5.1, Fig 5). This lack of automation limits scalability and usability (R2).
- **Evaluation gaps**:
    * Absence of failed cases: no analysis of limitations or examples where the method fails, which makes it difficult to assess robustness (R2).
    * Missing random weakening baseline in Figure 3; the observed effect could simply result from weakening any set of heads (R4).
    * No comparison with classifier guidance (e.g., using CLIP score to align with concepts) in Sec 5.1, which is a well‑established approach for guiding image generation (R4).
    * Human evaluation details are insufficient; Appendix C.2 needs a table with number of participants, question types, number of questions per participant, and quality‑control measures (R3).
    * The paper would benefit from more comprehensive evaluation on a larger set of generated images and human evaluation (R1).
- **Generalizability concerns**:
    * All main experiments, studies, and comparisons are based on SD v1. The section on SDXL is limited, and newer models (e.g., SDXL, SD3) might have eliminated many of the issues that the method attempts to solve. The applicability and effectiveness on more advanced architectures remain unclear (R2, R4).
- **Additional unanswered questions (overlapping with missing experiments/ablations)**:
    * How would ordered weakening and strengthening work on more complex images with entangled objects/concepts (e.g., “a plastic car melting”)? This could test whether HRVs handle adjective‑verb‑object entanglement better than baselines (R1).
    * Can the strength of a head be interpolated continuously (e.g., material=[-2,2])? This ablation is not explored (R3).
