## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates supervised non-autoregressive (NAR) GNNs for neural combinatorial optimization on TSP, MIS, and MVC. It finds a misalignment between minimizing training loss and solution quality (optimality gap), attributing this to NAR’s assumption of variable independence. To address this, the authors propose a supervised autoregressive (AR) framework that trains GNNs to complete partial solutions, thereby capturing conditional dependencies. Empirical results on six GNN architectures show that the AR framework does not exhibit the same misalignment, consistently improves performance over NAR methods, and generalizes better to larger instances. Reviewers generally agree the problem is relevant and the AR idea is interesting, but disagree sharply on the experimental rigor, baseline fairness, and contribution depth. One reviewer (score 3, confidence 5) finds the paper only demonstrates a phenomenon without explaining the rationale, weakens state-of-the-art baselines by removing key components (tree search, augmentation, sampling), and compares only to outdated AR methods. Another reviewer (score 5, confidence 4) raises concerns about the small training data size (10,000 TSP50 instances vs. 1,502,000 for DIFUSCO) and the exclusive use of greedy decoding. A third reviewer (score 8, confidence 2) finds the work a significant contribution but questions the generality of the misalignment across search algorithms and a potential mismatch between the GNN’s training objective (completing partial solutions) and greedy search’s one-variable-at-a-time process.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses a significant and rarely studied issue in supervised NCO: the misalignment between training loss and solution quality. (R3, R1)
- The proposed autoregressive GNN framework—training networks to complete partial solutions—is conceptually novel for supervised NCO and well-motivated. (R1, R2, R3)
- Empirical evaluation covers six GNN architectures on three distinct problems (TSP, MIS, MVC), providing comprehensive insights into NAR limitations. (R1)
- The experimental setup is clearly described and helps validate the misalignment problem. (R1, R3)
- The paper is well-written, with strong motivation and a clear methodological explanation. (R3)

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of theoretical rationale for the misalignment.** The paper shows a correlation (lower loss → no better gap) but does not explain why this occurs. (R1; R3 acknowledges the finding as a contribution but does not require a deeper explanation.)
- **Unfair weakening of baseline methods.**  
  - DIFUSCO originally achieves 0.00% gap on TSP50/100 with tree search; the authors remove tree search (gap rises to 0.79%) and then claim their AR method outperforms it (gap 0.65% on TSP50, 3.9% on TSP100). However, DIFUSCO on TSP10000 achieves a 2.58% gap with tree search, which is better than the 3.9% reported for TSP100. (R1)  
  - POMO originally reports 0.03% (TSP50) and 0.14% (TSP100) using augmentation and multiple sampling trajectories; the authors remove both (gap rises to 0.64%) and claim superiority over a four-year-old method. (R1)  
  - For AR baselines, the paper only compares to methods from 3–7 years ago, which are no longer state-of-the-art, and avoids comparing to more recent AR approaches. (R1)  
  - The authors restrict all baselines to greedy decoding and supervised GNN settings without justifying why this narrow setup is meaningful for the broader NCO literature. (R1; also R2 asks why only greedy decoding is used.)
- **Training data size is too small to draw robust conclusions.** The proposed model uses only 10,000 TSP50 instances and 5,000 instances for each of the other problems (e.g., TSP100, MIS, MVC). In contrast, DIFUSCO uses 1,502,000 TSP50 instances and EFFICIENTTSP uses 10,000. The effect of larger training datasets on the observed misalignment and on the relative performance of NAR vs. AR methods is not explored. (R2)
- **Generality of the misalignment across search algorithms is unverified.** The experiments demonstrating the misalignment only use greedy search (adding one variable at a time). It is unknown whether the same misalignment occurs with other search paradigms (e.g., threshold-based selection, beam search, simulated annealing). (R3)
- **Misalignment between GNN training and greedy search dynamics.** The GNN is trained to predict a probability distribution over all variables given a partial solution, but greedy search only uses the single most probable variable at each step. This discrepancy may limit the alignment between learned representations and the iterative decision-making process. (R3)  
  (A related point from R1: no analysis is provided on why an autoregressive approach was chosen over directly refining NAR methods.)
- **Unclear multimodal nature and local-optima avoidance.** The paper claims that the autoregressive approach better captures the multimodal nature of CO problems (multiple optimal solutions), but it does not provide a concrete example or show how the method avoids getting stuck in local optima. (R3)
- **Comparative effectiveness against modern AR methods is not established.** The paper does not demonstrate that the proposed AR GNN framework can match the performance of top-performing AR models (e.g., POMO with full augmentation/sampling), even on small/medium TSP instances. This limits the impact of the claimed improvements. (R1, also implied by R2’s questions)
- **Narrow contribution scope.** The work limits itself to supervised GNNs and greedy decoding without justifying why this specific setting yields a broad contribution to the NCO field. (R1; in contrast, R3 considers the contribution moderate (3) but still positive.)
