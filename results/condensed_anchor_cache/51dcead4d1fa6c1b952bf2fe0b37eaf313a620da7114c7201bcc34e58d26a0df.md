## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes M3L, a method that uses masked autoencoding (MAE) to learn joint visual‑tactile representations for robot manipulation policies trained with PPO in simulation. Evaluated on three simulated tasks (robotic insertion, door opening, dexterous in‑hand rotation), the authors show that multimodal self‑supervised pretraining can improve sample efficiency, policy performance, and generalization to physical‑parameter variations. The paper also reports that representations learned multimodally benefit vision‑only policies at test time.

### Strengths

- **Simple and intuitive idea:** Applying masked autoencoding (MAE) to fuse vision and touch is a natural, easy‑to‑understand approach that can be used with minimal modifications.
- **Clear presentation and reproducibility:** The paper is well‑written, the key ideas are clearly communicated, and the appendix provides detailed model and environment specifications, hyperparameters, and visualizations. Video results help understand the tasks.
- **Interesting cross‑modality benefit:** The result that multimodal self‑supervised learning improves vision‑only policy performance at test time is noteworthy.
- **Diverse task coverage:** The three simulated tasks span fine‑grained insertion, articulated object opening (door), and dexterous in‑hand rotation, demonstrating applicability across different manipulation types.
- **Innovation direction:** Incorporating multimodal representation learning (vision + touch) into robot policy learning marks a valuable research contribution.
- **Technical completeness:** Hyperparameters, architecture details, and reproduction information are provided.

### Weaknesses

1. **No real‑world evaluation (all reviewers):** The method is evaluated only in simulation. Real‑world tactile sensors produce noisy, non‑ideal signals; the paper does not test on a real robot or validate robustness to tactile noise. Reviewer 2 notes that simulation data is clean and asks what would need to change for real‑world deployment. Reviewer 3 states the tactile simulation lacks desired accuracy, making real‑world experiments a missing link.
2. **Missing external baselines (Reviewers 1, 2):** All comparisons are between variants of M3L. No comparison is made with existing baselines such as visuotactile transformers (e.g., Qi et al.), self‑supervised visual RL methods (e.g., Hansen et al.), or vision‑only representation learning approaches (e.g., [4,5] in the review). Without these, it is unclear whether gains come from the multimodal combination or from the masked autoencoding objective alone.
3. **Missing tactile‑only baseline (Reviewer 1):** The paper does not include a tactile‑only policy, which is needed to isolate the contribution of each modality.
4. **Only one task shows clear improvement over vision‑only (Reviewers 1, 3):** M3L outperforms the vision‑only baseline only in the in‑hand rotation task. In the insertion and door‑opening tasks the gains are marginal or absent, making it unclear whether those tasks benefit from tactile information. Reviewer 3 notes inconsistent results across tasks in Figure 6.
5. **Insufficient number of tasks for robust evaluation (Reviewers 2, 3):** Most simulation‑based RL papers evaluate on many environments. Three tasks are not enough to assess the method’s robustness and generalizability across diverse manipulation challenges. Reviewer 3 suggests at least 10 tasks with comprehensive statistical analysis.
6. **Weak generalization evidence (Reviewers 1, 2, 3):** Generalization experiments only vary physical parameters (mass, damping, friction) for a single object. No tests on objects with different shapes, sizes, visual appearances, or geometric variations are performed. Reviewer 2 states that the strong zero‑shift generalization claims in the introduction are not substantiated and that statistical significance is insufficient. Reviewer 1 adds that adding different noise levels to visual and tactile observations would be a stronger test.
7. **Missing proprioception (Reviewer 1):** Arm or hand proprioception information, which is easily available in both simulation and reality and provides rich manipulation cues, is not included in the multimodal pipeline.
8. **ViT computational intensity and overfitting concerns (Reviewer 3):** The Vision Transformer is computationally heavy and may overfit to the limited training distribution (few tasks, objects, background textures). The benefits of MAE should be demonstrated across a more diverse set of environments and datasets.
9. **Limited discussion of related work (Reviewer 2):** The paper ignores relevant robot‑learning papers with tactile data (e.g., [1,2]) and versatile tactile sensors (e.g., [3]), which are important for context.
10. **Reconstruction may overfit to training distribution (Reviewer 1, question):** Figure 10 shows near‑perfect reconstruction from partial observations, but it is unclear whether the model retains this capability under noise in visual or tactile inputs, or whether it overfits to the training distribution. This directly relates to claim of robustness.

**Minority/disagreement note:** Reviewer 3 (score 3) is more negative overall, emphasizing the need for many more tasks and statistical rigor and questioning the validity of the conclusions, while Reviewers 1 and 2 (both score 5) acknowledge the idea’s neatness but raise overlapping concerns about evaluation breadth and baselines.
