## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes CosPGD, a white-box adversarial attack for pixel-wise prediction tasks that uses cosine similarity between predictions and targets to weight the loss per pixel. Unlike SegPGD (which uses discrete weighting), CosPGD is applicable to both classification (semantic segmentation) and regression tasks (optical flow, disparity, image restoration). The proposal is a simple modification of SegPGD and leads to stronger attacks on semantic segmentation across multiple datasets. However, reviewer opinions split: one reviewer (8) finds it effective and generally superior; the other three (5 each) see limited novelty, insufficient comparisons, and unconvincing evidence for robustness claims. The core contribution is viewed as incremental rather than transformative.

### Strengths
1. **Generality across tasks**: CosPGD naturally extends from pixel-wise classification to regression tasks (optical flow, disparity, image restoration), whereas SegPGD is limited to classification. This is confirmed across multiple tasks and datasets.
2. **Simplicity and effectiveness**: It is a simple modification of SegPGD (replacing discrete correct/incorrect weights with continuous cosine similarity) yet yields stronger attacks on semantic segmentation, especially at standard iteration counts.
3. **Ablation experiments**: The paper provides extensive ablations on hyperparameters (perturbation bounds, step sizes, iteration steps) verifying effectiveness over PGD and SegPGD.
4. **Discuss differences to prior work**: The authors clearly explain the differences and advantages of PGD, SegPGD, and CosPGD in the motivation and methodology sections.

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty – strong similarity to SegPGD**: The core idea (pixel-wise loss scaling based on prediction accuracy) is shared with SegPGD. Using cosine similarity as a continuous weight is not a fundamental departure; both methods operate within the same iterative gradient-based framework. The novelty is incremental.
2. **Missing comparisons with recent methods**:
   - No comparison with the certified-radius-guided attack framework (Qu et al. [1]) for semantic segmentation, or with C&W [2] and MI-FGSM [3] that are known effective attacks.
   - For optical flow, only PGD is compared; it is unclear how SegPGD performs when adapted to regression, so the advantage over a modified SegPGD is unsubstantiated.
   - For image restoration, Fig. 7 shows only marginal improvement over SegPGD (especially at 20 iterations), raising questions about practical advantage.
3. **Lack of theoretical/empirical explanation why cosine similarity outperforms fixed weights**: The paper does not analyze why continuous cosine weighting is superior – e.g., whether it gives a more nuanced gradient, better captures semantic structure, or affects optimization stability. A justification is lacking.
4. **Inconsistent attack settings across tasks**: Non-targeted attacks are used for semantic segmentation and image restoration, but targeted attacks for optical flow. The rationale is not justified, and no results for targeted attacks on segmentation/restoration are provided, limiting generalizability.
5. **Insufficient support for robustness enhancement claims**: Section 4.3 (image restoration) shows degradation of NAFNet but does not demonstrate that CosPGD can improve model robustness through adversarial training. The claim “can efficiently enhance a new model’s robustness” needs direct evidence (e.g., improved performance after adversarial training with CosPGD). The denoising task results (Appendix D2) should be in the main body.
6. **Missing ablation on alternative distance metrics**: The choice of cosine similarity is not justified by comparisons with other distribution distances such as KL divergence or JS divergence. An ablation comparing different metrics is needed.
7. **Unfair/dubious comparison at low iterations**: The paper highlights that at 3 attack iterations CosPGD performs worse than PGD, but white-box attacks (and SegPGD especially) are not typically evaluated at such low iteration counts where a balancing factor adjustment is needed. This comparison appears to highlight a limitation of CosPGD rather than a fair advantage of SegPGD.
8. **Figures 14 and 15 are hard to read**: Numerical values in these figures are difficult to discern; tabular presentations would be more precise and useful.
9. **Missing definition of loss function \(L\)**: In equations (1), (5), and (6) the loss function \(L\) is not explicitly defined, which hinders reproducibility and clarity.
10. **Limited scope of robustness validation**: The paper focuses on attack effectiveness (degradation) but lacks adversarial training experiments that would validate the method’s utility for improving model robustness, which is repeatedly claimed.

**Note on reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (rating 8, confidence 5) strongly supports the paper as simple, effective, and general; the other three (ratings 5, confidence 4) view the contribution as incremental, the experiments as insufficient, and key claims unsubstantiated.
