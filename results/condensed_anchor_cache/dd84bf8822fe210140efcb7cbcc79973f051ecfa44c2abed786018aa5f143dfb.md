## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces the Composable Arithmetic Execution Framework (CAEF), which fine-tunes LLMs (specifically LLaMA 3.1-8B) to emulate Turing machines for step-by-step arithmetic. Using LoRA adapters for aligner and executor modules, the method converts operations into sequences of (state, command) pairs, achieving near‑100% accuracy on 50‑digit and 100‑digit tasks across seven operators (+, −, ×, ÷, ==, >, <) and outperforming GPT‑4o in some settings. Reviewers find the idea creative and the presentation clear, with strong results for long‑digit generalization. However, major concerns include: (1) the approach’s reliance on many inference passes (O(d²) steps vs. O(d) for a decoder‑only model), raising efficiency doubts; (2) unclear fairness of baselines (single‑query vs. step‑by‑step execution); (3) the premise of using LLMs for exact arithmetic over an external tool; (4) failures on repeating digit patterns (e.g., “999…”) that the authors attribute to inherent LLM limits but reviewers suspect stem from training data gaps; (5) abuse of “understanding” language; and (6) lack of clarity in explaining the framework’s concrete inputs/outputs and how adapters are selected. Reviewer scores range from 3 to 6, with the most negative (3) citing computational cost and baseline comparison issues, and the most positive (6) praising the ambition but questioning practical advantage over a direct Turing machine.

### Strengths
- **Idea and creativity** – CAEF is an interesting, refreshing approach that grounds arithmetic in Turing‑machine emulation, moving beyond memorization (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). The ambition to implement computational‑theoretic data structures via LLMs could have educational or software‑engineering applications if extended further (Reviewer 3).
- **Strong empirical results** – Near‑100% accuracy on 50‑digit and 100‑digit tasks across all seven operators (Reviewer 1). Substantial improvements over base LLMs for numbers with >10 digits (Reviewer 4). Performance surpasses GPT‑4o in some settings (Reviewer 1, Abstract).
- **Clear presentation** – Well‑written, with illustrative figures (e.g., Figure 1–4) and a logical flow from motivation to method (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). The modularity of aligner and executor (LoRA adapters) aids debugging (Reviewer 3).
- **Addresses a practical issue** – Unreliable arithmetic in LLMs is a critical real‑world problem (Reviewer 1). The paper tackles length generalization explicitly (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
- **Computational inefficiency** – Each problem requires repeated calls to `model.generate()` (one per transition), leading to O(d²) autoregression steps vs. O(d) for an end‑to‑end decoder‑only model (d = operand length). Storage overhead of intermediate prompts and adapter representations also hinders deployment (Reviewers 1, 2). A computational analysis is missing (Reviewer 2).
- **Unfair baseline comparisons** – CAEF solves problems step‑by‑step with many inference passes, while baseline LLMs (e.g., LLaMA‑3.1 instruct, GPT‑4o) are queried in a single pass without chain‑of‑thought prompting. A comparison where baselines also use the Turing‑machine execution, or are allowed CoT, is needed (Reviewers 2, 4).
- **Mismatch between premise and contribution** – If a Turing‑machine prototype already exists to generate training data, why not simply use that machine as a tool? The approach would be valuable only if the LLM generalizes to cases the machine was not designed for (Reviewer 3). Basic arithmetic is a relatively easy application, limiting impact (Reviewer 3). Moreover, the adapters are pre‑selected per operator; the model does not infer which operation to use, so the framework does not handle ambiguous problem descriptions (Reviewers 2, 4).
- **Robustness to digit patterns** – CAEF fails on repeating patterns such as “999…” or “456456…”. Reviewer 1 contests authors’ claim that this is an inherent LLM limitation, arguing it likely reflects absence of such patterns in fine‑training data. This raises concern about how consistently CAEF handles other unusual digit configurations (Reviewer 1).
- **Error propagation & modular complexity** – Multiple components (aligner, executor) must maintain high performance across many timesteps; a single failure can cascade, especially on longer sequences. Training and debugging aligner/executor pairs add complexity (Reviewer 3). A dedicated error‑analysis section would be welcome (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 4 struggled to understand the framework’s concrete inputs/outputs until late in the paper; figures are small and vague, and the explanation of “state” and “command” is too abstract (Reviewer 4).
- **Overclaims and language misuse** – Terms like “understanding” and “genuine understanding” in the abstract and text (e.g., L189) overstate the results and anthropomorphize LLMs (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing analysis of text‑interleaved problems** – No experiments or discussion of performance when arithmetic is embedded in natural language (Reviewer 2).
- **Unresolved questions from reviewers** – (i) What is the advantage of simulating the Turing machine with an LLM rather than using the machine directly? (ii) Could alignment and execution be merged into a single adapted LLM step to reduce failure points? (iii) Missing reference (EMNLP 2019) and citation format errors (e.g., Line 106) (Reviewers 3, 4).
