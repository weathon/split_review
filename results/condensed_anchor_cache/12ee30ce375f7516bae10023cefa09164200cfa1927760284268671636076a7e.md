## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a Verbalized Bayesian Persuasion (VBP) framework that extends classic Bayesian Persuasion (BP) to natural language settings by using LLMs (specifically Llama 3.1-8b) to instantiate the sender and receiver. It maps the BP game to a mediator-augmented game, transforms policy optimization into prompt optimization (using OPRO, FunSearch, and Prompt-Space Response Oracle), and claims convergence guarantees. Experiments are conducted in three scenarios (recommendation letters, courtroom, law enforcement) with increasing complexity (S1, S2, S3). Reviewers are mostly critical (scores 3,3,3) with one more positive reviewer (score 6) who finds the problem interesting and the integration of techniques well-organized.

### Strengths
- The problem of extending Bayesian Persuasion to natural language is interesting and timely; leveraging LLMs for persuasion in mixed-motive games is a novel and promising direction.
- The paper proposes a unified interface that integrates several advanced techniques (mediator-augmented game mapping, prompt optimization via OPRO/FunSearch/PSRO) to extend BP into more realistic, text-based scenarios.
- Mapping verbalized persuasion to the game-theoretic BP framework is novel; the real-world scenarios (recommendation letters, courtroom, law enforcement) are intuitive and exemplify the target setting.
- Optimizing prompts instead of directly doing RL on LLMs is a practical and less involved approach.
- The experiments are comprehensive with detailed ablations, examples in the appendix, and demonstration of strategy development over training (Figure 7 showing relevant categories selected more often).
- The paper is well-organized and effectively integrates multiple techniques (positive reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear contributions and confusion about core mapping**: The mapping of VBP to the mediator-augmented game of Zhang et al. 2024 is tenuous and confusing. In that work, a fictitious mediator maximizes an optimality objective while maintaining equilibrium among n players; here the sender acts as mediator but retains its own utility, and there are only two players. The paper does not clarify how the convergence guarantee follows from this mapping versus from PSRO.
- **Several methods are juxtaposed (OPRO, FunSearch, PSRO, mediator-augmented game) without clear necessity**: It is unclear which components are critical to VBP. The writing and Figure 3 suggest the convergence guarantee comes from the mediator-augmented game, but PSRO also provides convergence guarantees. A more succinct description of the necessary components is needed.
- **Benefits of verbalized BP are not clearly articulated**: The paper does not convincingly explain what is gained by using natural language beyond simple binary signals. In the classic BP setting, the optimal policy can be implemented with a yes/no recommendation. The experiments (S1 and S2) show similar results with and without a reward for clearer signals, suggesting the game collapses to a binary signal and the text itself may not matter.
- **The setting is too artificial to add much beyond existing toy game theory or direct LLM persuasion**: The simple BP games are not the right testing ground for studying LLM persuasion because neither player gains from using more than a binary signal. The paper does not demonstrate that the complexity of the VBP framework yields insights that cannot be obtained from simpler approaches such as prompting LLMs to lie/deceive/persuade directly.
- **Uncertainty about the role of the obedience constraint**: The paper uses the obedience constraint (the receiver follows the sender’s recommended action if it is optimal given the belief). If the receiver knows the sender’s policy (commitment assumption), the problem reduces to choosing a recommendation that is optimal for the receiver. The obedience constraint then seems to simplify the game so much that the LLM just implements a binary policy. How does the text of the recommendation letter matter under this constraint?
- **Commitment assumption unclear with prompt optimization**: The prompt does not specify exactly how and when to lie; how does this still guarantee the commitment assumption (that the receiver knows the sender’s policy)?
- **No full end-to-end transcripts in the main body**: The paper gives examples only in the appendix, but no full transcripts from the games. Prompts and stylized transcripts should be in the main body.
- **Iterated setting (S3) is underexplored**: The most interesting setting (multi-turn) receives little focus and insufficient analysis. Figure 12 is hard to follow; the hypothesis about the pattern of honesty (rising, falling, rising) could be explained by strategic cycles rather than aligned LLMs. More analysis and clearer figures are needed.
- **Figure 4 and Figure 7 issues**: 
  - In Figure 4, honest probability converges to 1.0 in all three settings. Is this expected? If the professor always recommends truthfully, the recruiter should always trust, then the professor could profitably deviate. Under what circumstances would a sender lie about a strong candidate? (Reviewers 2,4)
  - Figure 7: prompt categories and content are not exhaustively described (e.g., is “Tone” content always “Positive”?). The probabilities computation is unclear (average probability over the pool of policies?).
  - Figure 7 columns “Converged prob” and next column are redundant; the analysis is vague.
- **Method pipeline description is vague**: The iterative process of prompt optimization is not explained clearly. The overall pipeline is hard to follow.
- **Existing work already applied BP in NL settings**: Reference [1] applies Bayesian frameworks to enhance LLM performance in math/code generation, making the contribution limited.
- **Bespoke algorithms unlikely to be useful in practice**: The paper uses many specialized algorithms (OPRO, FunSearch, PSRO) unlikely to be adopted for training better LLMs; the takeaway for the community is unclear.
- **Terminology issues**: The paper uses “pretrained” when it means “aligned” (line 392); “inforset” typo; PSRO used for two different concepts; missing “or” line 312; grammar line 320.
- **Missing reproducibility**: No anonymized repository for code/data is provided.
- **Minority reviewer (score 6) acknowledges the problem is interesting and the paper is well-organized, but still notes insufficient detail and missing repo.**
