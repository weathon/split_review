## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies privacy vulnerabilities in large language models (LLMs) for cloud services. It finds that the token embedding space is highly sparse and that LLMs primarily operate in subspaces orthogonal to the embedding space, making privacy vulnerable to reconstruction attacks. The authors propose a distributed inference paradigm where users locally compute hidden states (with random scaling of hidden state magnitudes) and send them to the cloud, aiming to prevent server-side embedding inversion. Evaluations on multiple tasks show the method balances privacy, utility, and memory efficiency, and is compatible with low-bit quantization.

### Strengths
- **Well-organized and clearly motivated**: The paper is well-structured with clear explanations of the privacy problem and the analysis of LLM vulnerabilities.
- **Comprehensive experiments supporting embedding space findings**: Detailed experiments (Inclusion Ratio, cosine distance) convincingly demonstrate sparsity and direction preservation in shallow layers.
- **Innovative solution with straightforward intuition**: The distributed inference paradigm and random scaling of hidden states are intuitive and address a practical privacy concern.
- **Little impact on utility**: The proposed method preserves task performance (e.g., accuracy, Rouge scores) across summarization, translation, QA, and even math problems.
- **Effective against non-adaptive reconstruction attacks**: Empirical results indicate good defense against the evaluated optimization-based embedding inversion attacks.
- **Problem importance**: Privacy in LLM cloud services is a critical, underexplored area.
- **Interesting phenomena and thorough evaluation**: The paper reports interesting findings (sparsity, orthogonality) and evaluates on diverse tasks/datasets.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty of sparsity claim overstated**: Previous works (e.g., Zhang et al. ICML 2024, Liu et al. ICML 2023) already highlighted activation sparsity in LLMs; the paper's first contribution needs more precise positioning.
- **Proposition 1’s orthogonality claim may be a high-dimensional artifact**: Reviewers noted that in high-dimensional spaces, random vectors are nearly orthogonal – the empirical observations may simply reflect this, not that embeddings and residuals lie in distinct subspaces.
- **Theoretical inconsistency between Proposition 1 and defense effectiveness**: If residuals are nearly orthogonal to embeddings, then cosine distance (used by attackers) is insensitive to the random scaling of hidden states. Either the defense works via a different mechanism or Proposition 1 is incorrect; the paper does not resolve this contradiction.
- **Missing empirical comparison with differential privacy (DP) baselines**: No comparison with DP-based methods (e.g., Feyisetan et al. 2020, Mattern et al. 2022) to contextualize protection levels.
- **Insufficient attack evaluation**: Only reconstruction attacks (optimization-based watermark inversion) are considered. Additional attacks needed: attribute inference, transformer-based inference from privatized hidden states, and adaptive attacks trained on defense-modified embeddings. Evaluations against advanced strategies (e.g., Huang et al. 2021, Tong et al. 2024) are missing.
- **Missing baseline comparisons**: Key related works (e.g., Split-and-Denoise Mai et al. 2023, Bolt Pang et al. 2024) are not implemented, making it hard to assess relative effectiveness.
- **Limited protection scope**: Only the user context is protected; the instruction/prompt may also leak private information. Furthermore, if the LLM returns an accurate response, the response itself can reveal sensitive content (e.g., verbatim quotes). Suggested post-processing (e.g., Chen et al. 2023) is not explored.
- **Lack of formal privacy guarantees**: The method only offers empirical evaluations; as noted by Aerni et al. (CCS 2024), such defenses can be misleading without formal guarantees.
- **Computational demands and deployment overhead**: The scheme requires users to run part of the LLM locally (first i layers). High computational requirements may limit user adoption, and the paper does not quantify inference time or storage overhead on the user device. How to choose the number of local layers is not clarified.
- **Key design details unclear**: The mechanism for “shrinking hidden states” is not sufficiently explained (Reviewer 1). The rationale for using only Euclidean distance and cosine similarity (ignoring other measures) is not justified (Reviewer 3). The decision criteria for Rouge thresholds (e.g., “Rouge-1 < 0.5 sufficient for privacy compromise”) is poorly phrased (Reviewer 5).
- **Lack of essential background**: Readers unfamiliar with LLMs and reconstruction attacks may struggle to follow the paper (Reviewer 6).
- **Utility metric explanation**: Evaluation metrics are not consistently described across datasets, and it is unclear if they are comparable (Reviewer 4).
- **Relevancy of quantization compatibility**: While noted as a positive, the connection between quantization and the core privacy theme is tenuous (Reviewer 5).
- **Utility of cloud delegation**: If hidden states are computed locally, the benefit of cloud completion for short responses (e.g., classification) is questionable; local generation may be nearly as expensive (Reviewer 5).
- **Output protection and interaction with reconstruction**: The paper does not address whether the output is plaintext, whether the same protection applies to outputs, or whether outputs could help reconstruct inputs (Reviewer 6).
