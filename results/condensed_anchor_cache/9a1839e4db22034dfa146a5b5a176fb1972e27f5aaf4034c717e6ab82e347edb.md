## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a "wrong-over-wrong alignment" approach for LLMs: distinguishing varying degrees of incorrectness in the absence of correct ground-truth answers, then tuning models with preference optimization using those synthesized preferences. Methods include self-consistency, token probabilities, and LLM-as-a-judge. Experiments across 7 LLMs and 8 datasets show LLMs can distinguish wrongness up to 20.9% above random guessing, and alignment reduces incorrect outputs while improving calibration.

### Strengths
- **Novel and creative concept**: The idea of aligning LLMs by differentiating among wrong answers, without relying on correct answers, is introduced as a valuable approach for resource-constrained settings where ground truth is scarce (Reviewer 1, score 8; Reviewer 2; Reviewer 3; Reviewer 4). Reviewer 1 explicitly rates this as a high contribution (score 8, confidence 4) and emphasizes its potential for practical deployment.
- **Comprehensive and rigorous experimentation**: The paper tests multiple preference elicitation methods (self-consistency, token probabilities, LLM-as-a-judge) across diverse datasets and 7 different LLMs, with extensive analysis of task utility, model confidence, and calibration (Reviewer 1; Reviewer 2; Reviewer 4). Reviewer 2 notes “solid, methodologically rigorous results across diverse datasets.”
- **Important research questions raised**: The work highlights how incorrect answers can still provide value in training and evaluation, motivating further investigation (Reviewer 3).
- **Concrete findings and insights**: The paper reports quantitative gains (e.g., 20.9% above random, 4.5% more correct answers despite only aligning on wrong answers) and discusses model behavior patterns (Reviewer 1; Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Proxy function reliance and motivation contradiction**: The method uses proxy functions to compute correctness scores from ground truth, then derives wrong-over-wrong preferences. Reviewer 1 worries about biases when proxies are not well-represented and questions their reliability across tasks. Reviewer 4 states this process “contradicts the motivation … the resulting wrong-over-wrong preferences do not significantly differ from traditional right-over-wrong preferences.” Reviewer 3 adds that dataset curation inherently requires ground truth, which contradicts the paper’s claim of applicability when ground truth is absent.
- **Lack of explicit applications and experiments on motivated tasks**: The paper mentions theorem-proving and low-resource languages as potential use cases but performs no experiments on them. Reviewer 3 notes that knowledge-based tasks where performance was best inherently have correct answers, while the method performs poorly on SciBench (closer to theorem proving). The framing does not clarify which tasks are most suitable.
- **Insufficient baselines**: Baselines are only compared to the original (unaligned) models. Reviewer 4 criticizes the lack of comparison against state-of-the-art alignment methods. Reviewer 3 also notes insufficient comparison with models that are not aligned on wrong-over-wrong tasks.
- **Weak evaluation metrics**:  
  - Accuracy is used as the primary metric despite small option sets; Reviewer 3 recommends F1, precision, recall for more reliable assessment.  
  - ECE is criticized for binning sensitivity and lack of detailed calibration insight, especially relevant for a method focused on varying degrees of wrongness (Reviewer 3).  
  - Reviewer 4 notes that due to lack of calibration mitigation, accuracy metrics are influenced by label distribution.
- **Presentational and analytical issues**:  
  - Results are presented in bullet-point style without logical transitions, feeling like a collection of findings rather than a cohesive story (Reviewer 2).  
  - Tables (especially Table 1) are dense and hard to interpret; Reviewer 2 suggests moving detailed tables to appendix and using summary statistics or visualizations.  
  - There is a lack of clear motivation in abstract and introduction about how the method addresses practical limitations (Reviewer 2).
- **Method-specific concerns**:  
  - Logit-based methods assume high token probability “probably” implies correctness, but models can assign high logits to incorrect answers due to training biases (Reviewer 3, citing [1]).  
  - The heuristic that longer answers equal higher quality can misjudge responses; verbosity may obscure accuracy (Reviewer 3, citing [3] and [4]).  
  - LLM-as-a-judge for subjective wrongness without ground truth lacks empirical justification; examples used to align judges need specification (Reviewer 3).
- **High variance and near-random results in evaluations**: Out of 22 overall scores, only 1 exceeds 70% (20 points above random), while 12 are within 5 points of random guessing. Reviewer 3 asks for deeper analysis justifying that pairwise evaluations are not random, and for patterns explaining this variability.
- **Disagreement among reviewers**: Reviewer 1 (score 8) is notably more positive, emphasizing the approach’s novelty and potential, while Reviewer 3 (score 6, but with lower soundness/contribution scores of 2) is more critical, questioning motivation, metrics, and empirical support. Reviewer 4 also emphasizes the motivation contradiction. This disagreement suggests the work’s framing and evidence may not fully satisfy the more critical readers.
- **Additional questions and criticisms**:  
  - Why not average over evaluators? (Reviewer 2)  
  - The claim “knowledge-based tasks are easier while commonsense is most challenging” is overstated given modest performance gaps (Reviewer 2).  
  - What mechanisms explain the 4.5% improvement in correct answers despite only wrong answer alignment? (Reviewer 3)  
  - How does the method generalize to out-of-distribution tasks and subjective domains where “less wrong” is culturally variable? (Reviewer 1)  
  - How are examples selected for LLM-as-a-judge prompts? (Reviewer 3)  
  - Can the method be applied to low-resource languages with preliminary results? (Reviewer 3)
