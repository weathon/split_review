- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 1, 3, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper addresses auditing total data withdrawal, where a user requests removal of their data from both training and test sets for a machine learning task. The authors formalize the problem, propose that compliance is verifiable if and only if the model misclassifies the withdrawn point and all perturbed variations within a ball around it, and use an existing robustness verification algorithm (CROWN BaB) to check this condition. Experiments across datasets and architectures are presented. All reviewers acknowledge the importance of the problem, but the majority (scores 3, 3, 3) find major weaknesses in framing, assumptions, and practicality; one reviewer (score 1) is significantly more negative, asserting that the framing makes the approach inapplicable in most practical applications.

### Strengths

- The paper identifies and formalizes a very important problem: auditing and verifying compliance with data deletion/withdrawal, including provably refuting non-compliance. This is a major technical challenge in trustworthy ML.
- The approach of verifying through misclassification of instances within a closed ball is somewhat innovative and aims to enforce stringent guarantees against data misuse.
- The paper does a good job motivating the problem and introduces a structured approach to total data withdrawal (both training and test data), which could benefit discussions around data privacy and user rights.

### Weaknesses

- **Faulty core framing / unrealistic requirement**: The requirement that a model must misclassify the withdrawn point and all points in a ball around it is counterintuitive. If a model generalizes well to an unseen in-distribution point (e.g., an ordinary MNIST zero), it would be considered a violation, yet such generalization is expected and desirable. Several reviewers argue this does not map to practical use cases (R1, R3, R4). One reviewer (R2) notes that for points far from decision boundaries, removal does not change the model's output; the model still classifies correctly, but the paper’s definition would deem such a model not verifiable, which is incorrect. This makes the framing inapplicable in most applications.
- **Easily gamed / bypassed**: The definition can be trivially satisfied by changing the label of a point without actually removing it from training; membership inference might still detect the data. (R1) Additionally, a model could add a hard-coded if-statement to return a wrong label for the withdrawn region, which the paper would consider verification, yet the data was not actually removed. Thus the approach has high false positives and can be easily bypassed. (R2)
- **Limited relevance of total data deletion**: The core problem – verifying deletion from both training and test sets – raises practical concerns. During deployment, predictions are typically made on new data provided voluntarily; if a user continues to provide data, making predictions is likely legal. (R3) Effective techniques for verifying deletion from the training set already exist (e.g., membership inference frameworks achieve reliable results in real-world settings, see [Gaussian Membership Inference Privacy] (R3)). This casts doubt on the necessity of the proposed approach.
- **Unrealistic assumptions**: Assumption 3 (training loss of the model after removal goes to zero) is impractical for modern large models (e.g., LLMs) where achieving zero training error is not feasible. If unmet, the proof appears to fail, limiting applicability to a small subset of models. (R2, R3, R4) The paper also relies on an unspecified assumption about data points being at most distance epsilon from each other (Assumption 2); while reasonable for some datasets, it is not universally true.
- **Lack of mathematical rigor**: The paper provides only a proof sketch and no precise theorem statements (assumptions, conclusions, lemmas). The necessary and sufficient condition is claimed but not formally proven. (R1)
- **Algorithm is a black box**: CROWN BaB is not described; the reader cannot understand the algorithm’s high-level idea. No time or space complexity analysis is given. The choice of parameter δ is not justified. (R1) The verification method appears computationally prohibitive, requiring adversarial perturbation across all training data points, which would degrade model performance and scale poorly. (R3)
- **Experiments are unrealistic and poorly explained**: Baselines are not clearly described (what they aim to achieve, why they are reasonable). (R1) The experimental setup involves significantly modifying data points, which is easily detectable and does not reflect practical scenarios. (R2) The paper suggests evaluating on face recognition datasets but does not do so. (R2)
- **Minor technical issues**: Figure 1 is confusing. Equation 1 appears to use `max` where `argmax` is intended. (R1)
- **Unaddressed failure modes**: Some failures are reported (line 362), but the authors do not explain why they occur. Failures defeat the purpose of auditing. (R1) The paper says auditing deletion of test data is important but does not explain how it is actually verified. (R1)
- **Minority more negative view (Reviewer 2, score 1)**: This reviewer emphasizes that adversarial examples exist; to ensure that no point in the ball classifies correctly requires exhaustive search, which is infeasible. The approach’s assumptions and framing make it inapplicable in most applications. They rate soundness, presentation, and contribution all as 1. The other three reviewers (scores 3) share many criticisms but are less absolute in rejection.
