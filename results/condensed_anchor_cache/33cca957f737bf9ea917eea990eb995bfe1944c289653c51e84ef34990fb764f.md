## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes FEEDER, a demonstration pre-selection framework for in-context learning (ICL) and fine-tuning. It identifies a core subset (the FEEDER set) from the full training set that is both “sufficient” and “necessary” for the model’s predictions, then uses this subset for ICL or fine-tuning. A tree‑based merging algorithm approximates the expensive brute‑force search for this set. A bi‑level optimization method is also introduced for fine‑tuning. Experiments are conducted on 6 text classification datasets, 1 reasoning dataset (GSM8k), and 1 semantic‑parsing dataset, across 6 LLMs (335M to 7B parameters, including Llama 7B and Gemma‑2 2B; others are pre‑2020 models). Results claim that FEEDER achieves superior or comparable performance while using only half the training data for ICL, and boosts fine‑tuning performance. **Note: Reviewer 3 is significantly more critical (score 3) than the other reviewers (scores 6), particularly questioning the feasibility, benchmark relevance, and model scale.**

### Strengths
1. The paper is well written and easy to follow (Reviewer 1). The motivation to find a compact set of informative demonstrations is reasonable (Reviewer 2).
2. The idea of iterative optimization (Reviewer 1) and the extension from instance‑level to set‑level definitions of sufficiency and necessity are novel (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3). The tree‑based merging algorithm (using transitivity) is also novel (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).
3. The approach defines “sufficient” and “necessary” samples – sufficiency means a sample enhances prediction accuracy, necessity means a sample is required for correct predictions – and extends these to sets (Reviewer 2). The tree‑based merge builds from the bottom up, merging examples into nodes based on sufficient/necessary characteristics at each layer, and claims low complexity (Reviewer 2).
4. ICL evaluation results suggest FEEDER is an effective pre‑selector (or “compressor”) of demonstrations and can benefit from various demonstration selectors (Reviewer 2). Experiments cover multiple datasets and LLMs, showing effectiveness across settings (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Unclear goal & lack of efficiency evidence (Reviewer 2)**  
   - The paper does not clarify whether the primary goal is accuracy or efficiency (throughput). For ICL, no empirical evidence of efficiency gains is provided – e.g., no comparison of inference time, token count, or other metrics with vs. without FEEDER. “Efficiency” should be defined and measured.

2. **Computational cost and scalability of FEEDER discovery (Reviewers 2 and 4)**  
   - The FEEDER discovery algorithm requires calling the LLM at each stage to assess sufficiency/necessity relationships, yet the computational cost is not quantified (number of LLM calls, prompt size, model size, actual time/resources) (Reviewer 2).  
   - The algorithm’s complexity is stated as \(O(K \log_2 |D_{\text{TRAIN}}|)\) iterations, but each iteration pairs every data point with every other, leading to \(O(K |D_{\text{TRAIN}}| \log_2 |D_{\text{TRAIN}}|)\) (Reviewer 2). The tree‑based merging does not clearly avoid pairwise comparisons at each level; this needs clarification and a more detailed complexity analysis.  
   - A direct comparison of time cost and accuracy between FEEDER‑selected samples and randomly selected samples of the same size in ICL is needed (Reviewer 4).

3. **Ambiguity and lack of rigor in definitions (Reviewer 4, Reviewer 2)**  
   - The terms “sufficiency” and “necessity” are not given clear, formal definitions, nor is it explained how these properties are measured or determined in the ICL context (Reviewer 4). This undermines theoretical soundness.  
   - Poor demonstration examples are described only generally; concrete input‑output pairs that lead to poor performance would help contextualize the problem (Reviewer 4).  
   - Reviewer 2 asks: Can a sample be its own sufficient and necessary sample (Definitions 1 and 2)? Also, since ICL is limited to 5–10 examples, can the authors provide brute‑force results searching plug/unplug across the entire \(D_{\text{TRAIN}}\)?

4. **Feasibility and approximation concerns (Reviewer 3)**  
   - Even in the approximation version (Section 4.2), achieving precise accuracy using terms like \(1_{|W_{in}|}\) and \(1_{|W_{out}|}\) (Eq. 7, 8) seems challenging. Current benchmarks may not achieve 100% accuracy, questioning the method’s effectiveness and feasibility.  
   - As the number of samples per node grows, performing few‑shot tests becomes impractical. For instance, if the few‑shot limit is \(n=5\), a node containing 1000 samples cannot be tested in that setting.

5. **Outdated and narrow benchmarks (Reviewer 3)**  
   - Most benchmarks (except GSM8k) are sentiment/linguistic classification proposed before 2018, which are outdated and not representative of current community priorities.  
   - Besides Llama 7B and Gemma‑2 2B, the models tested are pre‑2020 and have limited parameter sizes (335M–7B). This reduces the impact and relevance of the conclusions for the present‑day community.

6. **Missing ablations and experimental analysis (Reviewers 1, 4)**  
   - Ablation studies are needed to demonstrate the individual importance of sufficiency and necessity (Reviewer 1).  
   - In tree construction, different design choices for merging nodes among adjacent layers require more experimental comparison (Reviewer 1).  
   - Table 1 shows that some datasets perform better with larger \(n\) (number of demonstrations) while others better with smaller \(n\). No analysis is provided to explain these discrepancies in terms of dataset characteristics (e.g., input space complexity, label distribution, noise) (Reviewer 4).  
   - The FEEDER set and direct sample selection from the training set yield similar performance trends as \(n\) changes, which is inconsistent with the “necessity” property. If FEEDER fulfills necessity, performance should improve (or at least plateau) with more examples, but this is not clearly reflected. Further investigation is required (Reviewer 4).

7. **Lack of larger‑scale model experiments (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3)**  
   - The metrics on GSM8k are low; models used are too small (up to 7B). It is uncertain whether the method scales to larger models, as few‑shot selection behavior may differ (Reviewer 1). The choice of models (many pre‑2020, small) further weakens the paper’s impact (Reviewer 3).
