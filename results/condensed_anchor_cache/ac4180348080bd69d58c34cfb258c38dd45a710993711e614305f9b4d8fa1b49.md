## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies the double descent phenomenon in supervised Out-of-Distribution (OOD) detection. It proposes an expected OOD risk metric, uses Random Matrix Theory to derive bounds for binary least-squares classifiers on Gaussian data, and empirically shows double descent curves for various OOD detection methods, architectures, and datasets. The paper also connects improvements in OOD detection to Neural Collapse (NC) metrics. Reviewers found the topic timely and the experiments extensive, but raised concerns about theoretical significance, the strength of assumptions, clarity of certain formulations, and limitations of the NC and ETF analyses. One reviewer (score 8) was notably more positive than the others (all 6) and increased their score after the rebuttal.

### Strengths
- **Novel perspective**: Extends the double descent concept, originally tied to generalization error, to OOD detection (Reviewers 1, 4).
- **Theoretical contribution**: Uses Random Matrix Theory (RMT) to derive bounds on OOD risk, providing a formal framework for understanding model complexity in OOD detection (Reviewer 1).
- **Comprehensive experiments**: Covers a variety of OOD detection methods (logit-based, feature-based), neural architectures (ConvNets, ResNets, VGG), and datasets (CIFAR-10/100, SVHN, etc.) with multiple model widths. The use of different filter sizes to vary complexity is appropriate (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- **Clear writing and connections to prior work**: The paper is well-structured, discusses related work thoroughly, and clearly presents experimental setups (Reviewer 2).
- **Valid results**: Both theoretical and empirical results appear sound and support the claims (Reviewer 4).
- **Minority reviewer**: One reviewer (score 8) was highly positive and increased their score after the rebuttal, indicating strong agreement with the paper’s contributions.

### Weaknesses
1. **Theoretical significance and assumptions**:
   - Theorem 1 closely resembles Theorem 1 of Belkin et al. (2020) with the addition of a nonlinearity \(\phi(\cdot)\); Theorem 2 is a straightforward extension. Without \(\phi(\cdot)\), the technical extension to OOD risk is unclear. Reviewers questioned the novelty and technical challenge (Reviewers 2, 3).
   - The theory relies on strong assumptions: data is Gaussian and isotropic (may not reflect real applications), the model is a one-hidden-layer network with a single neuron, and the nonlinear function is approximated linearly (lines 830‑835) – the validity of this approximation and the conditions under which it holds are not justified. This simplification may reduce the analysis to a purely linear case, limiting contribution (Reviewer 3).
   - The theorem imposes no explicit assumptions on the OOD distribution; it is unclear how the bounds behave under varying degrees of distributional shift between ID and OOD data (Reviewer 2).

2. **Connection between theory and practical OOD detection methods**:
   - The derived lower bound (Theorem) is not linked to any specific practical OOD detection method (e.g., MSP, ODIN, Mahalanobis). Reviewers asked for remarks on how the theoretical insights translate to empirical performance (Reviewers 1, 3 question 3, 4 weakness 1).
   - The motivation for the proposed expected OOD risk metric is insufficiently explained. Examples of practical usage or comparisons with existing OOD metrics (e.g., FPR95, AUROC) would clarify its unique contribution (Reviewer 4).

3. **Neural Collapse (NC) analysis concerns**:
   - The NC1 metric involves a trace of \(d\times d\) matrices (where \(d\) is the last-layer feature dimension). When model width is varied (intermediate layers), \(d\) changes; comparing NC1 values across different \(d\) may not be fair (Reviewer 2).
   - The claim in Sec. 5.2 that “lower NC metrics imply better performance in terms of both generalization and OOD detection” is insufficiently rigorous. While the paper demonstrates correlation, lower NC values do not universally guarantee better generalization or OOD detection (Reviewers 2, 4).
   - The ETF (Equiangular Tight Frame) connection assumes balanced classes. The paper does not state whether training datasets (CIFAR-10/100, etc.) are balanced. If unbalanced, ETF‑based insights may not hold, and behavior under overparameterization could differ (Reviewer 2).

4. **Experimental clarity and limitations**:
   - Label noise is introduced (Line 305) in experiments, which was described as artificial. Noiseless experiments would help disentangle the effect of model size on OOD detection alone (Reviewer 1).
   - Using different filter sizes to denote model complexity is appropriate but introduces limitations (e.g., changing the number of parameters while also altering feature dimensionality and receptive fields) that are not fully discussed (Reviewer 4).
   - The double descent phenomenon is studied for width; the role of network depth is not examined. Could double descent in OOD detection also depend on depth? The paper focuses solely on width (Reviewer 4).

5. **Formulation and reasoning issues**:
   - Equation (5) is unclear: \(z\) depends on \(x\), but the notation treats it as fixed in both terms (Reviewer 3).
   - Remark 4.1: If the proposed expression (5) yields a lower value, it should not simultaneously lead to conclusions (1) and (2). A more detailed explanation is needed to resolve this apparent contradiction (Reviewer 4).
   - Why Gaussian data is necessary for the proof—could the theory be extended to non‑Gaussian distributions? (Reviewer 3 question 2).
   - Remark 4.6 is not sufficiently explained; its connection to OOD detection methods remains unclear (Reviewer 3).
