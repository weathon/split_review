## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a novel objective called Adversarial Counterfactual Error (ACoE) for adversarial RL, framed as a POMDP induced by state-perturbing adversaries. ACoE naturally balances natural policy value and adversarial robustness. The authors propose a scalable model-free surrogate, Cumulative-ACoE (C-ACoE), realized in two algorithms (A2B and A3B) that estimate belief states. Experiments on MuJoCo, Atari, and Highway benchmarks with various attacks consistently show that C-ACoE methods outperform current state-of-the-art adversarial defense methods. Theory is provided to justify the objective and its generalization.

### Strengths
- **Novel, theoretically grounded objective.** ACoE provides a new perspective by treating adversarial perturbations as inducing a POMDP, and it naturally balances value optimization and robustness, unlike prior worst-case (maximin) or pure regularization approaches. (R1, R3, R4)
- **Comprehensive evaluation.** Experiments cover multiple RL environments (MuJoCo, Atari, Highway) and diverse attack types (e.g., myopic, long-horizon), demonstrating consistent superiority over strong baselines. (R1, R2, R3)
- **Scalable model-free algorithms.** C-ACoE is adapted to both PPO and DQN, with two belief estimation variants (A2B, A3B) that achieve strong empirical performance without requiring a known model. (R1, R2, R4)
- **Well-written and clearly motivated.** The paper sets up background well, compares with literature, and motivates the new objective and algorithms clearly. (R1, R2, R4)
- **Solid theoretical analysis.** Detailed derivations and justification for the surrogate objective and its effectiveness. (R3)

### Weaknesses
- **Reliance on attacker knowledge.** Both A2B and A3B require knowledge of the attacker (e.g., attack radius, attack preference). No justification is given for when such knowledge is realistic, nor how performance degrades if the assumed attack radius/preference mismatches the actual attack (compared to worst-case baselines). (R2)
- **Limited justification for belief construction choices.** The paper introduces A2B and A3B but does not compare against alternative plausible constructions for the surrogate belief. The lack of ablation or comparison weakens the motivation for these specific choices. (R3)
- **Underexplored scalability to high-dimensional continuous spaces.** Experiments focus on relatively low-dimensional environments (e.g., MuJoCo, Atari). The method’s practicality on more complex, high-dimensional continuous control tasks is not demonstrated; further experiments or explanations are needed beyond theoretical analysis. (R3)
- **Missing hyperparameter sensitivity analysis.** The impact of key hyperparameters—particularly the robustness-weight factor λ and the neighborhood size—is not analyzed. This raises concerns about the method’s sensitivity to tuning and its practical robustness. (R3)
- **Unclear learning of C-ACoE delta-network and role of b(s).** The paper states that δ is no longer dependent on b, but then uses b(s) in A2B and A3B. It is not explained how the C-ACoE objective is learned correctly when the RHS of Eq. (2) still involves b. The details in Algorithm 1 are insufficient; common knowledge (GAE, PPO-clipped) is included while these crucial parts are omitted. (R4)
- **Minor presentation issues.** Abstract is overly long; missing closing parenthesis in Eq. 2; line 362 should be π(s₁) instead of π(s₁′). (R1, R4)
- **Missing experiments on adaptive, long-horizon adversary.** The authors design algorithms for myopic adversaries, yet they perform well against long-horizon attacks. It remains unclear whether ACoE methods can be hacked by an attacker aware of the A3C heuristic (i.e., an adaptive attacker). (R1)
- **Trade-off between natural and adversarial performance is not analyzed.** No sensitivity analysis or discussion of the practical trade-off in clean vs. robust reward as λ varies. (R3)
- **Computational cost of KL divergence.** Both A2B and A3B require KL divergence between policies; this computational cost is not addressed. (R4)
- **Do we care about unperturbed performance?** The question of real-world applications where unperturbed states exist is raised; the paper does not list such applications. (R4)
