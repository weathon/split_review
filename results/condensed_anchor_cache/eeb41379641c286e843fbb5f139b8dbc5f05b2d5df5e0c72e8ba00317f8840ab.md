## Merged Review

### Summary
All four reviewers rate the paper a 3 (neutral) with confidence levels 3–4. They agree the idea of memory pruning for Bayesian optimization (BO) is simple and intuitive, and that the neurostimulation example is interesting. However, they raise substantial concerns about missing theoretical guarantees (no-regret property is lost), weak experimental evaluation (at most 200 data points, only one baseline), inaccurate complexity analysis (hidden dependence on domain size \(N\)), insufficient explanation of key pruning mechanisms (KL-divergence usage not spelled out), and unclear motivation (why use BO when the function is cheap to query). Reviewer 4 is the most critical, arguing that the algorithm is a heuristic that sacrifices the convergence guarantee of GP‑UCB and that sparse GPs or existing principled memory‑pruning methods [2] are stronger baselines that should be compared. Reviewer 1 notes the lack of theoretical backing and the extra cost of storing old standard deviations. Reviewer 2 finds the experiments too small to motivate dropping data. Reviewer 3 highlights unclear notation and insufficient illustration of the pruning decision.

### Strengths
- The core idea is simple, intuitive, and easy to implement (R2, R4).
- Explicitly upper‑bounding computation time is an important practical problem (R4).
- The neurostimulation example effectively illustrates a scenario where computation time is critical (R4).
- The paper is clearly written and the charts are visually appealing (R3).
- Memory pruning can be valuable in memory‑constrained scenarios (R3).
- The algorithm is tested on both synthetic benchmarks and a real dataset (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **No regret guarantee / heuristic nature.**  Reviewer 4 points out that the algorithm loses the no‑regret property of GP‑UCB because the training set size is fixed: \(\lim_{T\to\infty} T = m\) implies the cumulative regret never converges to zero. The paper should explicitly clarify that this is a heuristic (like TurBO), and the description in line 323 is misleading.
- **Inaccurate complexity analysis.**  Reviewer 4 argues that the memorization step for \(\sigma_t(\mathcal{X}), \sigma_{t-1}(\mathcal{X})\) requires scanning the entire discrete domain \(\mathcal{X}\) of size \(N\), yielding time complexity \(\mathcal{O}(N m^3)\) and memory \(\mathcal{O}(m^2 + 2 N m)\). This is comparable to sparse GPs, which may be faster when the acquisition‑function maximisation iterations \(M \ll N\). A direct comparison with sparse GPs is needed.
- **Weak experimental setup.**
  - All experiments use at most 200 data points, which modern computers handle trivially, so it is unclear whether dropping data is necessary (R2).
  - Only one baseline (Vanilla BO) is compared; many other BO methods, sparse GPs, and principled memory‑pruning methods (e.g., [2] with regret analysis, same \(\mathcal{O}(m^3)\) complexity and no‑regret property) should have been included (R2, R3, R4).
  - Reviewer 4 suggests comparing with other efficient heuristics (CMA‑ES, NES) or parallel/batch BO.
- **Unclear or missing theoretical backing.**  Reviewer 1 and R3 both note the absence of theoretical analysis (e.g., no justification for why the pruning strategy works, no regret or consistency analysis).
- **Insufficient explanation of key algorithm details.**
  - The randomized pruning strategy is described in only one line; a full subsection with all attempted strategies and insight into why random pruning performed well is needed (R1).
  - The KL‑divergence‑based pruning idea is mentioned but no equations are given for how it is computed between data points in a GP context (R3).
  - Notation is unclear: “\(m\)” in the abstract is treated as a “fixed value” without explanation; “\(u\)” in Algorithm 1 is not defined (R3).
  - How are the parameters \(m\) and \(q^*\) determined? (R3)
- **Additional technical concerns.**
  - When computing \(\min(\tilde{\sigma}(x), \sigma(x))\) the old standard deviation must be stored, adding memory cost if the old kernel matrix is kept, or computational cost if only data points are kept (R1).
  - Why not use \(\max(\tilde{\mu}(x), \mu(x))\)? Would that improve optimization? (R1)
  - In GP‑UCB, \(\kappa\) should increase with iterations; the paper uses a fixed \(\kappa\) without justification (R4).
  - Question: In Algorithm 1, how are all \(x \in \mathcal{X}\) enumerated? Is the domain discretized? (R2)
  - The experimental time for the Michalewicz 4D and Hartmann problems (Figure 6) may be insufficient; neither method appears fully converged (R3).
  - No discussion of high‑dimensional outputs or alternative acquisition functions (R3).
- **Motivation undermined.**  Reviewer 4 argues Bayesian optimization may not be the first choice for cheap‑to‑query functions, and the proposed method is not a global optimizer; this weakens the motivation. Reviewer 2 also questions the need for dropping points on small problems.
