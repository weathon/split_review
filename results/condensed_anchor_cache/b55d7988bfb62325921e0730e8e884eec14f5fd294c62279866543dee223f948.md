- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 5, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces CORE (Common randOm REconstruction), a compression technique for distributed optimization that projects gradient vectors onto a small number of shared random Gaussian vectors (a sketching-type scheme). The server and machines share the same random realizations, enabling reconstruction of an aggregated low-dimensional message. The approach is applied to convex linear models and generic non-convex optimization, yielding algorithms CORE-GD and CORE-AGD (accelerated with heavy-ball momentum). The authors claim that, when Hessian eigenvalues decay rapidly (i.e., the trace of the Hessian bound matrix **A** is small), CORE provably achieves lower communication complexity than existing methods.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written, the main claims are clear, and the proofs are easy to follow (Reviewers 2, 3).
- The comprehensive comparison in Table 1 summarizing order-wise communication complexities of various methods is appreciated (Reviewer 1).
- The proposed CORE technique is easy to implement and benefits from shared randomness (Reviewer 2).
- The benefit of CORE is evident when Hessians are highly skewed (small trace relative to \(dL\)), a common scenario in practice, suggesting promising utility (Reviewer 2).
- The paper provides extensive references to related work on communication compression (Reviewer 3).
- Numerical experiments are presented that compare the proposed methods with existing benchmarks (Reviewer 3; note that Reviewer 1 states that experimental results and practical comparisons are completely absent – see Weaknesses for this disagreement).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns**: The scheme essentially consists of projecting the gradient onto \(m\) Gaussian vectors, which has long been exploited in compressed sensing under a sparsity constraint. The similarity is not properly highlighted (Reviewer 1).
- **Communication cost definition**: It is unclear how the overall communication cost is computed; does it depend on \(m\) (the number of Gaussian realizations) because that is what is exchanged? (Reviewer 1)
- **Practical relevance of the linear convex case**: Linear models are not as practically relevant as non-linear models, which typically achieve better performance (Reviewer 1).
- **Assumptions**: The justification of Assumptions 4.5 and 4.6 in practice is not provided (Reviewer 1).
- **Lack of experimental validation / contradiction between reviewers**: Reviewer 1 states that no experimental results or practical comparisons are provided, and that the scheme is not practical because performance depends on specific Gaussian realizations and concentration of good vectors has not been shown; also the need to share the codebook is a disadvantage. Reviewer 3, however, notes that numerical experiments were conducted. This disagreement is unresolved; the empirical evaluation, if present, may be insufficient to demonstrate practicality.
- **Missing comparison**: Comparison with classical quantization schemes for distributed learning is absent (Reviewer 1).
- **Total communication cost independent of number of machines \(n\)**: The achieved \(\tilde O(r_{1/2}(f)/\mu^{1/2})\) does not improve with more machines, unlike DIANA/ADIANA. It would be worth exploring whether CORE could incorporate independent randomness per machine to benefit from larger \(n\) (Reviewer 2).
- **No stochastic gradient analysis**: The effect of stochastic gradients (variance \(\sigma^2\)) is not investigated. Does CORE maintain the optimal asymptotic rate \(O(L\sigma/\sqrt{nT})\)? Can it improve \(L\) to some \(r_\alpha\)? (Reviewer 2)
- **Acceleration only for quadratics**: CORE-AGD is proven to accelerate only for quadratic objectives. Is there an inherent reason? (Reviewer 2)
- **Inaccurate claim of outperforming ADIANA**: The iteration complexity of ADIANA (Li et al.) is actually \(\tilde O(\omega+(1+\omega/\sqrt{n})L^{1/2}/\mu^{1/2})\) after recent improvements [R2], which is minimax optimal. Using random-\(k\) operators, ADIANA’s total communication cost is \(\tilde O(d+(K+d/\sqrt{n})L^{1/2}/\mu^{1/2})\). Since \(r_{1/2}(f) \ge d\mu^{1/2}\), CORE is superior only when \(\max\{K,d/\sqrt{n}\}L^{1/2} \ge r_{1/2}(f)\). The paper should be more accurate in its comparisons and cite [R2] (Reviewer 2).  
  [R2] He, Huang, Yuan, "Unbiased Compression Saves Communication in Distributed Optimization: When and How Much", arXiv 2023.
- **Comparison with optimal preconditioning**: How does CORE’s communication cost compare to that of non-compressed gradient descent under optimal preconditioning, where the rate also relates to Hessians? (Reviewer 2)
- **Unsupported claims**: Several statements are made without evidence:
  - “When eigenvalues decrease rapidly, tr(**A**) is not large” – even if eigenvalues decay, the matrix bound **A** is worst-case, so rapid decay does not guarantee a small trace of **A** (Reviewer 3).
  - “tr(**A**) can be much smaller than \(dL\) in most scenarios” – no theoretical or empirical support is given (Reviewer 3).
  - “When data is normalized, tr(**A**) is dimension-free” – the provided bound \(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf A) \le d\alpha + L_0 R\) does not support this claim (Reviewer 3).
- **Theorems 4.2 and 5.2 are limited**: They give convergence rates for a single node (rate independent of \(n\)), making the comparison with distributed methods like DIANA misplaced. The practical significance of using such compression in a single-node setting is questionable (Reviewer 3).
- **Differential privacy result (Theorem 5.3)**: The theorem lacks a proper discussion and conventional utility guarantees; the assumed bound on \(\epsilon,\delta\) (with \(\delta = \sqrt{\epsilon}\)) is restrictive and leads to a very high privacy level per iteration, likely requiring large noise that hurts utility (Reviewer 3).
- **Inaccuracies in Table 1**:
  - DIANA originally uses TempGrad quantization, not Top-\(k\) as indicated (Reviewer 3).
  - ADIANA’s convergence guarantees are for unbiased compressors, but Top-\(k\) is biased; the table entry is thus misleading (Reviewer 3).  
  (Beznosikov et al., 2020, is cited for biased compression.)
- **Typos**: The manuscript contains a significant number of typos and grammatical errors (Reviewer 3 lists many examples in the abstract, introduction, and related work sections, not repeated here for brevity).
- **Open questions**:
  - What exactly does “limited bounded variance” mean, and how is it theoretically supported? (Reviewer 3)
  - What constitutes a “concrete method”? The Accelerated SEGA algorithm (Hanzely et al. 2018) is already a well-defined compression technique (Reviewer 3).
  - The derivation in equation (26) is unclear, specifically the transition to equation (b) (Reviewer 3).
