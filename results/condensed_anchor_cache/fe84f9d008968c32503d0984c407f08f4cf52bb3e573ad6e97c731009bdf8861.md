## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces NanoMoE, a family of structured matrices that replace dense linear layers with a block-diagonal low-rank form plus a mixing matrix M, motivated by connection to mixture-of-experts. Theoretical analysis shows NanoMoE can achieve higher rank and expressivity than low-rank factorization with few extra parameters. Experiments on synthetic matrix approximation (using a layer from OPT-13B) and AG News classification compare NanoMoE to low-rank baselines, reporting improved loss under matched parameter/FLOP counts. All four reviewers rate the paper 3, citing major experimental and conceptual weaknesses (limited tasks, missing baselines, no hardware efficiency evaluation, insufficient training details, overclaimed framing) that prevent the paper from demonstrating practical value.

### Strengths
- **Novel structured matrix family**: NanoMoE provides a new, theoretically more expressive alternative to low-rank matrices for replacing dense layers, with clear proof that it can achieve higher rank for the same parameter count. (R1)
- **Theoretical rigor**: The paper rigorously proves the expressivity advantages over low-rank factorization. (R1)
- **Experimental support for theory**: Initial experiments (synthetic and AG News) confirm that NanoMoE outperforms low-rank under controlled parameter/FLOP budgets. (R1)
- **Resource efficiency**: The method reduces parameters and FLOPs, making it potentially suitable for resource-constrained settings. (R3)
- **Maintained performance**: Despite reduction in resources, competitive results are achieved in the tested settings. (R3)
- **Relevance**: The idea is relevant to ongoing work on replacing dense matrices with structured alternatives; it is “a nice addition to the community.” (R4)
- **Well-written**: The paper is easy to follow. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Weak conceptual connection to sparse MoE**: The paper claims inspiration from sparse mixture-of-experts (referencing Mixtral, Switch Transformer), but NanoMoE does NOT use sparse routing, expertise specialization, or the training/memory benefits of sparse MoE. (R1, R2) The mixing matrix M simply averages partitioned inputs, which is “very different” from sparsely-gated MoE. (R2)
- **No comparison to dense layer baseline**: Experiments compare only low-rank; a critical missing baseline is the standard dense linear layer. (R2, R4) Without this, it is impossible to judge whether NanoMoE offers any improvement over the original architecture. (R4)
- **No comparison to standard MoE with dense experts**: The evaluation should include a conventional MoE layer (without low-rank structure) to assess if NanoMoE is a competitive replacement for true MoE. (R4)
- **No comparison to other expressive structured matrices**: Related structured matrices—Monarch matrices, Kronecker product, Tensor-Train decomposition, and methods from Oldfield et al. (multilinear MoE)—are not compared or even cited. (R1, R2) Monarch matrices (Dao et al. 2022) are particularly relevant as they also use block-diagonal structure with detailed efficiency analysis. (R2)
- **Very limited experimental scope**: Only two tasks: synthetic dense matrix approximation (using a single layer from OPT-13B with random Gaussian inputs) and AG News classification (small text dataset). Real-world tasks like image classification (CIFAR-10, ImageNet) or language modeling (e.g., nanoGPT) are missing. (R1, R3) A single transfer learning experiment or small-scale BERT pre-training would have been more convincing. (R2)
- **Missing critical training details for reproducibility**:  
    - Loss function for synthetic experiment not specified. (R2, R4)  
    - For AG News: vectorization details, vocabulary, OOV handling, number of epochs, optimizer, learning rate, batch size, regularization, weight initialization (especially for NanoMoE layers), granularity of K and r sweeps, activation function—all omitted. (R2)  
    - Hyperparameter tuning (learning rate, etc.) not described; prior work shows structured matrices need very different learning rates from dense layers. (R1)
- **No evaluation of actual runtime or hardware efficiency**: The paper defines FLOPs only at the numpy abstraction level and does not measure wall-clock time, memory bandwidth, or tensor core utilization. (R1, R2, R3) Intermediate tensors in NanoMoE are larger (size \(K r\) vs \(r\)), increasing activation memory and likely reducing hardware efficiency. (R1) Without real step-time comparison, claims of efficiency for pre-training are unsubstantiated. (R2)
- **Overclaiming and insufficient novelty**:  
    - The paper claims to “formally define the problem as parameter-efficient deep learning” but only defines the method, not the problem. (R2)  
    - The work does not scale beyond previous MoE-in-every-layer approaches (e.g., Oldfield et al., Potapczynski et al.) and uses a far smaller experimental setup. (R2)  
    - The title “Scaling Mixture of Experts to Individual Layers” overstates the contribution since NanoMoE is not a proper scaling of sparse MoE. (R2)
- **Overfitting concerns**: In Figure 5b, the low-error envelope suggests NanoMoE may overfit at higher FLOP budgets; this is not discussed. (R2) No analysis of whether NanoMoE is more prone to overfitting than low-rank is provided. (R2)
- **Narrow evaluation metrics**: Only loss is reported for AG News; classification accuracy is not given, making the evaluation less concrete. (R3)
- **Missing ablation on hyperparameters**: Sensitivity of NanoMoE to choices of K, r, and learning rate is not explored. (R1, R2)
- **No comparison to prior fine-grained MoE**: Approaches that also partition weights and use non-gated mixtures (such as in “Scaling Laws for Fine-Grained Mixture of Experts”) are not compared. (R3)
- **Figure clarity**: Figures 4 and 5 are hard to interpret due to transparent data points and high variance; effects of K and r are not disentangled. (R2)
