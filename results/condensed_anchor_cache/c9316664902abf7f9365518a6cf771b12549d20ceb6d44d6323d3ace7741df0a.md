## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces MoE++, a heterogeneous MoE framework that augments standard FFN experts with three zero-computation expert types: zero expert (discard), copy expert (skip), and constant expert (replace). These cheap experts allow simple tokens to bypass or simplify MoE processing, freeing more FFN experts for challenging tokens. The method also incorporates pathway-aware routing via gating residuals to stabilize expert selection. Experimental results show better performance while achieving 1.1–2.1× expert forward throughput compared to vanilla MoE models of the same size.

### Strengths
- Simple and effective addition to the standard MoE architecture, enabling heterogeneous computation across layers with minimal overhead.
- Improves both efficiency (throughput) and effectiveness (performance), making it impactful for real-world applications.
- Paper is well-written and insightful, with clear motivation, illustrations, and easy-to-read figures and tables.
- Thorough set of ablations is performed (acknowledged by all reviewers, though R3 notes they are only basic combinatorial ablations – see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Tuning sensitivity**: Hyper-parameters such as τ (controlling token allocation between zero-computation experts and FFN experts) complicate model tuning, as performance and burden distribution are sensitive to them (R1, high confidence).
- **Routing complexity and potential instability**: Pathway-aware routing with gating residuals adds complexity to expert selection. The interaction between gating residuals and the base routing mechanism requires careful calibration to avoid instability or suboptimal decisions. The added computational overhead from this interaction should be thoroughly analyzed (R1, high confidence).
- **Load imbalance concerns**: The dynamic routing mechanism can still cause load imbalances under diverse data distributions, leading to underutilized or overloaded experts. Some experts may be consistently bypassed while others are heavily utilized, wasting resources and limiting model capacity (R1, high confidence).
- **Limited empirical gains at small scale**: Performance improvements are not very significant and become noticeable only at large scale (~7B parameters) (R2, moderate confidence).
- **Missing wall-clock time measurements**: Table 1 claims zero-computation experts have zero complexity, but they still incur some real computation overhead. The paper lacks real-world wall-clock time demonstrations for these operators, especially when processing a batch of tokens (R3, high confidence).
- **Insufficient justification for expert set**: The choice of three zero-computation experts (zero, copy, constant) is not well motivated. Table 5 only shows basic combinatorial ablations; component‑level ablations (e.g., the contribution of each expert type and gating residuals) are missing, leaving unclear which components drive performance gains and whether some could be removed or modified for specific use cases (R1 questions; R3 medium‑low confidence).
- **Missing analysis of token‑expert assignment**: No examples are provided showing which tokens are allocated to zero, copy, and constant experts respectively, making it unclear how these experts specialize to different inputs (R3 question; high confidence).
- **Missing experimental details**: It is unclear whether baseline MoE models were also trained with residual gating, and how many experts are activated per layer in each model (R2 question; moderate confidence).
