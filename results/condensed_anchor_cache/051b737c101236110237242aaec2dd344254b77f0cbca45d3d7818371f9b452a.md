## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper identifies a “frequency collapse” problem in transformer-based time series forecasting, where high-frequency signals are lost in the top-layer embeddings, causing underfitting (e.g., pattern shifts, extreme-value deviations). The authors propose a Post‑Embedding ReMapping (PErM) strategy that inserts a fixed non‑linear remapping layer (e.g., random Fourier features, learnable pre‑trained LLM layers) between the backbone and prediction head to reinject high-frequency content. Experiments on several datasets show improvements over state‑of‑the‑art methods. Two reviewers consider the problem novel and the solution promising; the third reviewer finds the analysis shallow and the empirical evaluation incomplete.

### Strengths
- Introduces the concept of frequency collapse in time series embeddings, providing a fresh perspective on transformer underfitting.
- The proposed PErM layer is simple, plug‑and‑play, and adds little complexity.
- Thorough comparisons with multiple advanced methods demonstrate robustness on several datasets.
- Studying the inductive biases of modern architectures via spectral analysis of representations is a principled and important direction; the issue of high‑frequency loss in transformers is also being explored in other fields (e.g., NLP [1]) and deserves more attention in time series.

### Weaknesses
1. **Inadequate theoretical justification**  
   - The paper lacks a deep explanation of *why* transformers cause frequency collapse (e.g., role of attention mechanisms, multi‑layer gradient dynamics, oversmoothing). The provided analysis (Sections 3–4) is considered by one reviewer to be obvious and anecdotal, merely rehashing basic linear algebra under simplistic assumptions, without rigorous proof of high‑frequency absence.  
   - The reason *why* PErM functions (especially the non‑linear ones like LPT) reintroduce high‑frequency content is unclear. A frequency‑spectrum analysis before/after PErM and a discussion of how different functions affect the spectrum are missing. The connection between RFF and LPT is not explained.  
   - The relation to the low‑frequency principle in deep learning (which can improve generalization [2]) and methods that deliberately cut off high frequencies [3] is not discussed; the paper should address this apparent contradiction.

2. **Empirical evaluation limitations**  
   - Missing standard benchmarks (Electricity, Exchange) limits generalizability; one reviewer notes that a simple autoregressive linear model can outperform the reported results [4], and that removing transformer blocks in similar architectures yields comparable performance [5].  
   - Some compared results are inconsistent with prior work (e.g., PatchTST on Traffic/Electricity is lower than in the original paper [1]); the authors should explain or correct this for fair comparison.  
   - All results should be reported with standard deviations across different initializations.  
   - The analysis and solution are limited to patch‑based methods; applicability to other time‑series architectures is unclear.  
   - Existing work ties channel independence to smooth predictions [4]; the paper should discuss the correlation between frequency collapse and channel independence.

3. **Presentation and clarity**  
   - Several statements are asserted without justification: “the model’s forward process disrupts the internal temporal structure,” “sliding window sampling directly reflects temporal structure” (a well‑known fact, not a finding), “positional embedding disrupts autocorrelation” (why and how?), and the claim that “high‑frequency signals are absent” is not adequately supported.  
   - Some figures are not referenced or explained in the main text (axes, what the different curves represent, conclusions to be drawn).  
   - The related work section is incomplete; frequency‑response analysis is standard in signal processing and has been studied in recent structured sequence models [2,3]. The paper should better position itself within that literature.

4. **Limited scope of analysis**  
   - The paper deliberately removes positional encodings (lines 301‑304) without justification.  
   - The model’s performance varies with horizon length (better on short horizons and ETTm2 long horizons) but no theoretical explanation is given for this variability, nor for how PErM interacts with the prediction head.

**Reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (score 3) considers the analysis and evaluation too shallow for acceptance, while the other two (score 5) find the problem novel and the method promising. The criticisms about missing datasets, inconsistent baselines, incomplete theoretical grounding, and insufficient related‑work context are shared across all reviewers to varying degrees.
