## Merged Review

### Summary
VALL-E 2 extends VALL-E with **Repetition Aware Sampling** (penalizes token repetition during decoding) and **Grouped Code Modeling** (groups adjacent codec codes to shorten sequence length). The paper claims human parity on LibriSpeech test-clean and VCTK, with subjective scores (CMOS, SMOS) exceeding ground truth. Reviewer opinions are sharply divided: one reviewer (score 8) sees a breakthrough with strong experimental validation and practical impact; two reviewers (scores 3,3) consider the contributions incremental and the experimental setup flawed; a fourth (score 6) finds it a reasonable extension but lacking novelty for ICLR.

### Strengths
- **Clear writing and presentation** – paper is well-structured and easy to follow.
- **Human parity in zero-shot TTS** – VALL-E 2 achieves subjective scores above ground truth on LibriSpeech test-clean and VCTK (CMOS, SMOS), marking a new benchmark.
- **Effective practical improvements** – Repetition Aware Sampling mitigates word repetition/omission; Grouped Code Modeling improves inference speed and stability. Both are simple yet effective and can be adapted to other speech language models.
- **Strong experimental validation** – comprehensive evaluations on LibriSpeech and VCTK show improvements in robustness, naturalness, and speaker similarity.
- **Potential for assistive technology** – applications for individuals with speech impairments (e.g., aphasia, ALS).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty** – Repetition penalties have long been used in LLMs (CTRL, Keskar et al. 2019) and TTS (XTTS, Casanova et al. 2024), and iterative sampling is employed in VoiceBox and E2-TTS. Grouped code modeling is already known from MegaByte, RQ-Transformer, Block Transformer, UniAudio, GPST, and even Tacotron’s reduction factor. The paper does not compare with these approaches, and **MELLE claims to outperform VALL-E2**, further reducing contribution.
- **Weak baseline comparisons** – only compared to VALL-E, which underperforms VoiceBox, E2-TTS, DiTTo-TTS, and CosyVoice. No comparison with these stronger systems is provided.
- **Subjective evaluation dataset disparity** – NaturalSpeech3 (which also reports high performance) was trained on LibriLight; the paper compares results without controlling for training data, undermining fairness. Authors should replicate NaturalSpeech3 on the same dataset.
- **Missing ablation studies** – no ablation comparing Repetition Aware Sampling with traditional repetition penalties, no ablation of the Vocos decoder change, and the only ablations (prompt presence, dataset size) are self-evident. Subjective evaluation is best when group size=1, making it hard to distinguish from the baseline.
- **Major architectural change not properly examined** – switching from EnCodec’s original decoder to **Vocos** is a significant change; its impact on results is not isolated.
- **Unclear explanation for SIM improvement** – Repetition Aware Sampling improves WER, but its direct influence on speaker similarity (SIM) is not justified. Why do subjective evaluations improve significantly despite modest objective gains?
- **Missing evaluation on difficult cases** – the introduction claims robustness for complex/repetitive sentences, but no experiment tests these cases.
- **No code or model weights released** – significantly reduces reproducibility and contribution to the field.
- **Choice of tokenization not analyzed** – uses Byte‑Pair Encoding (BPE) rather than phonemes; the number of BPE tokens is not stated, and the impact on performance is not discussed. Including punctuation marks in BPE could affect prosody, but no analysis is provided.
- **No comparison with low‑bitrate codecs** – a natural alternative to grouped code modeling; authors do not compare with this approach.
- **Questions left unanswered** – why specifically choose Vocos? How would the model perform with phonemes or without punctuation?
