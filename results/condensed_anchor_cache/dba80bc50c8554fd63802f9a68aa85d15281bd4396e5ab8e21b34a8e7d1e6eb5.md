## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes WaveletGPT, a method that incorporates wavelet-inspired multi-scale structure into intermediate embeddings of GPT-style LLMs without adding extra parameters. The authors claim 40-60% faster pre-training and gains in performance across text, raw audio, and symbolic music. Reviewers diverge strongly: two (scores 6 and 6) find the idea original and promising, while two (scores 3 and 1) criticize insufficient execution, lack of rigorous comparisons, poor writing, and small-scale experiments that undermine claims of generality.

### Strengths

- **Original and simple idea.** The method is highly original, combining traditional signal processing concepts (wavelets) with LLM pre-training. It imposes multi-scale structure via simple average pooling and upsampling operations on half the embedding dimensions, requiring no extra parameters and minimal overhead (Reviewers 1,2,3,4).
- **Demonstrated faster convergence.** The approach achieves the same negative log-likelihood (NLL) in roughly half the training steps across text (text-8), raw audio (YouTube-Mix-8), and symbolic music (MAESTRO). Improvements are shown for both fixed Haar and learnable kernels (Reviewers 1,3,4).  
- **Multimodal scope.** The method is tested on three distinct modalities (text, raw audio waveform, symbolic music), and also on the Long Range Arena (LRA) benchmark, indicating potential for broad applicability (Reviewers 1,2,3,4).
- **Addresses an important problem.** Improving model performance through internal structural modifications rather than scaling parameters is a relevant research direction (Reviewer 3). The technique is “almost free” and does not require pre-trained teachers or major architectural changes (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses

- **Scaling and setup concerns.** Experiments are limited to small models (implied GPT‑2 scale ~120M parameters), short context windows (512 tokens), and small datasets: text‑8 (<100 MB), YouTube‑Mix‑8 (~5 hours of audio), MAESTRO (<10k samples). It is unclear whether benefits persist at 300M–1B parameters, longer sequences (e.g., 2048+), or more difficult tasks. The method sacrifices half the embedding space for pooled context – this may lose fine‑grained information and scale poorly (Reviewers 1,2,3,4).
- **Insufficient comparison to simpler alternatives.** No ablation compares the multi‑scale approach against a single‑scale averaging/convolution layer with kernel size equal to the largest window used. Without this, it is unclear if gains stem from multi‑scale structure or simply from adding convolutional layers. The claimed connection to wavelets is weak – the method reduces to hand‑crafted averaging and upsampling (a special case of convolution) and lacks core wavelet properties like orthogonality and perfect reconstruction (Reviewer 2).
- **Lack of ablation and design justification.** Many design choices are left unexamined: (a) why exactly half of the embedding dimensions are modified (what if more/less?); (b) why average‑pooling instead of biasing attention (e.g., as in WavSPA); (c) how residual connections treat the modified embeddings; (d) the parameter advantage from using two feed‑forward networks (FFNs) per layer versus one in the baseline – this may make the comparison unfair; (e) whether the max window size (equal to training context) hinders post‑training context extension (Reviewers 1,2,3).
- **Poor reproducibility and incomplete reporting.** The paper lacks explicit details on hardware, software versions, optimizer settings, batch sizes, learning rate schedules, and exact hyperparameters for each experiment. Key results are missing: Table 1 does not show learnable and unlearnable kernel results for all datasets; experimental data in Sections 4.3–4.5 are described qualitatively without a summarizing table. The “academic setup” is repeatedly invoked but never defined (Reviewers 1,2,3,4).
- **Limited evaluation.** No experiments on standard downstream NLP benchmarks (e.g., text summarization, question answering, machine translation) or on a standard speech task (e.g., LibriSpeech). The inclusion of LRA is appreciated, but more mainstream evaluations are needed. The choice of baselines (e.g., FNET, WavSPA) is not justified – the paper states non‑transformer/modified‑attention architectures are excluded, yet includes them (Reviewers 3,4).
- **Writing quality is poor.** The paper is hard to read: the introduction consists of dense, stitched‑together statements with repeated content (e.g., lines 53‑79 duplicated; lines 76‑79 duplicate an earlier passage). There are numerous grammatical errors, typos (e.g., “Large Language models”, line 114 types), and inconsistent font usage. Reviewers agree the writing alone is unfit for a conference publication and undermines the perceived rigor (Reviewers 1,2,3,4).
- **Dataset choices are questionable.** YouTube‑Mix‑8 (raw audio, 512 tokens = 32 ms of audio, practically meaningless without tokenization) and MAESTRO are small and not standard benchmarks for LLM pre‑training. The paper does not justify why these datasets were chosen over more widely used alternatives (e.g., LibriSpeech for audio, WikiText for text) (Reviewer 4).
- **Questions that amount to missing experiments or analyses:**  
  - Can the method be extrapolated to state‑space architectures? The claim is unvalidated (Reviewer 2).  
  - What is the effect of linearly varying α (0 to 1) as a proxy for the multi‑scale transform? The smallest kernel size is 2 (α=0.5), raising fairness concerns (Reviewer 2).  
  - How do the learned wavelet parameters compare to Haar wavelets? No visualization or frequency analysis is provided (Reviewer 3).  
  - The proportion of extra parameters and FLOPs introduced by learnable layers is stated absolutely (0.02M) but not relative to the base model. Clarification is needed (Reviewer 2).  
  - Notation inconsistencies (e.g., \(l\) in \(x_n^l(i)(k)\)) and errors (e.g., negative \(F\) when \(i \le E/2\)) need correction (Reviewer 2).
