## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Motif Structural Encoding (MoSE), a graph structural encoding based on homomorphism counts. MoSE is designed for Graph Transformers and is claimed to be strictly more expressive than the widely used Random Walk Structural Encoding (RWSE). The authors provide theoretical analysis relating MoSE to the Weisfeiler–Leman hierarchy, and evaluate on molecular property prediction (ZINC-12k, QM9) and a synthetic random graph dataset. Reviewers generally find the idea timely, the theoretical comparison insightful, and the empirical results competitive, but raise concerns about marginal gains over RWSE, computational cost, limited evaluation scope, motif selection difficulty, and novelty relative to prior homomorphism-based works (e.g., Jin et al. 2024, Deep Homomorphism Networks). Two reviewers assign 8; three assign 6, reflecting moderate enthusiasm tempered by practical and novelty concerns.

### Strengths
- MoSE’s homomorphism counts provide strictly greater expressive power than RWSE, as shown by theoretical proofs and examples where RWSE fails.
- The theoretical analysis is grounded in the WL hierarchy and clearly shows RWSE is a special case of MoSE; the comparison section is considered insightful.
- The encoding avoids the sign ambiguity of Laplacian positional encoding (LapPE).
- Experiments demonstrate that MoSE achieves state-of-the-art or improved results on ZINC and QM9 across multiple Graph Transformer architectures (e.g., GRIT+MoSE outperforms GRIT+RRWP).
- The synthetic random graph experiment is well-designed and tests two different base transformers.
- The paper is well-organized, clearly written, and easy to follow; one reviewer calls it “a beautiful read on the limitations of RWSE”.
- The research direction is timely, given recent interest in homomorphism-based methods for MPNNs.
- The theoretical arguments provide a plausible explanation for the observed performance gains.

### Weaknesses
- Performance improvements over RWSE are small; on several QM9 targets RWSE outperforms MoSE. Given the marginal gains, the practical need for a new encoding is unclear.
- Computational complexity is not adequately addressed. Preprocessing time compared to RWSE, RRWP, and LapPE is missing, and no optimization strategies (approximation, parallelization) are discussed. The cost likely scales poorly for large/dense graphs.
- Motif selection requires domain knowledge and there is no clear methodology; the impact of suboptimal selection is not explored. When domain knowledge is unavailable, guidance on which pattern graphs to choose is absent.
- Evaluation is limited to ZINC-12k and QM9. If the paper targets molecular property prediction, additional datasets (PCQM4M, Peptides-struct, Peptides-func, AQSOL) are needed. For a general-purpose claim, evaluation on other domains (e.g., CIFAR10, MNIST) is necessary.
- Theoretical novelty is limited: recent works (Jin et al. 2024) have already studied homomorphism-count expressivity. The paper does not clearly articulate how its theoretical contributions extend beyond prior work.
- The paper compares GRIT+MoSE to GRIT+RRWP empirically, but lacks a theoretical comparison (e.g., concrete graphs where MoSE distinguishes structures that RRWP cannot). No proof that MoSE captures information beyond the pairwise relationships in RRWP.
- The paper does not discuss when node-level structural encodings remain competitive given that state-of-the-art GNNs (e.g., GRIT) can incorporate edge features and encodings.
- The paper assumes significant background knowledge: details of RWSE, models like GSN and CIN, oblivious k-WL, GRIT deviations, and notation like “Spasm(C7∪C8)” are left unexplained. Proof sketches for the expressivity analysis would improve readability.
- Missing a baseline from computational chemistry for molecular datasets. The validity of QM9 benchmark experiments is questioned by chemists (practicalcheminformatics.blogspot.com/2023/08/we-need-better-benchmarks-for-machine.html).
- The core novelty is incremental: homomorphism counts have been used before with MPNNs; using them with Graph Transformers does not change the theoretical results, which hold for any downstream model. The strong focus on Graph Transformers seems forced.
- Several reviewers note that utility may be limited to addressing specific failure cases of prior encodings, given the minor change in experimental numbers.
