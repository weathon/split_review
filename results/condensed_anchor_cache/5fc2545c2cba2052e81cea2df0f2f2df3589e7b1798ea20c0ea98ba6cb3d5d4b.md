## Merged Review

### Summary  
The paper presents three modifications to the training pipeline for learning modular addition with encoder-only transformers: (1) increased diversity in the training data, (2) an angular embedding of integers onto a unit circle, and (3) a custom loss function designed to avoid convergence to trivial solutions. With these changes, the authors train models that handle up to \(N=256\) addends modulo \(q=3329\), a substantial increase over prior work (\(N\le6, q\le1000\)). They also show transfer to a few other modular-arithmetic functions (low-degree polynomials modulo \(q\)). Reviewers acknowledge that the techniques are empirically effective and the experiments are detailed, but they raise serious concerns about the problem’s motivation, the significance and novelty of the contributions, the consistency of the reported results, the relevance of some comparisons and analyses, and the lack of broader generality.

### Strengths  
- The experiments are well‑detailed, reproducible, and the methodology is clearly presented. (R1)  
- The proposed techniques improve performance and, as noted by one reviewer, escape the symmetry‑based lower bounds of Mohamadi et al. (2024) by using non‑uniform sampling and representations that are not permutation‑equivariant, aligning with results from Abbe et al. (2024). (R2)  
- Some analyses of the impact of different training decisions (e.g., data distribution, embedding) are interesting. (R2)  
- The paper investigates existing training methods in detail, identifies potential drawbacks, and proposes corresponding fixes. (R3)  
- Empirical evidence shows that each of the three changes helps. (R3)  
- The angular embedding and custom loss are tailored to the periodic structure of modular arithmetic, and the validation includes detailed ablation studies and visualizations (PCA plots). (R4)  
- The work could, potentially, inform ML‑powered cryptanalysis, given that modular arithmetic is foundational to cryptography. (R4; note disagreement from R2 below)

### Weaknesses  

1. **Motivation and relevance of the problem**  
   - Two reviewers (R1, R2) strongly question why it is interesting to train an ML model to perform modular arithmetic. R1 notes that if one already has angular embeddings, integers, or one‑hot encodings, solving the task is straightforward. R2 argues vehemently that the goal itself is misguided: a simple Python function (`lambda q, nums: sum(nums) % q`) achieves 100% accuracy for any \(N,q\), runs orders of magnitude faster, uses far less memory, and requires no 240 GPU‑hours. R2 states that prior work uses modular addition as a toy problem to study *learning phenomena* (e.g., grokking, curriculum design), not to obtain the best learned model.  
   - R3 echoes that the problem is limited (scaling up a specific problem tested only under a uniform distribution) and its impact for future research or applications is unclear.  
   - One reviewer (R4) is more positive about the cryptanalysis motivation, but R2 explicitly questions whether there exists any cryptanalytic application where a transformer that learns modular arithmetic would be preferable to calling highly‑optimized, accurate modular arithmetic routines.  
   - R1 also asks: in the cryptography use case, does partial (non‑100%) accuracy suffice? If not, how can one guarantee 100% evaluation accuracy?  

2. **Significance and novelty of the technical contributions**  
   - R3 finds the changes “natural and not surprising,” and notes that the evaluation on other modular arithmetic problems is preliminary—it is unclear whether the techniques can transfer to more general algebraic reasoning tasks.  
   - R1 points out that the techniques require the practitioner to know the problem’s structure (that it is modular addition) and are not general beyond modular arithmetic.  
   - R4 notes that the claim about converging to a “local minimum at the origin” is not entirely accurate: the tendency to predict \((0,0)\) is a consequence of MSE loss being minimized when predictions are close to the average target (which, due to symmetry, is near zero), not a traditional local minimum. This should be clarified.  
   - R4 also remarks that the data‑distribution and loss modifications add complexity; discussing possible simplifications or less resource‑intensive alternatives would be beneficial.  

3. **Inconsistency in reported results**  
   - R1 highlights a serious discrepancy: the bold row in Table 7 (\(N=20, q=257\)) shows different accuracy numbers from the first row of Table 8, yet both appear to describe the same setting. The same setting also appears with other (different) accuracy values in additional tables, causing confusion.  

4. **Questionable analyses and comparisons**  
   - R1 criticizes the PCA visualization in Section 5.4: the output dimension is already 2, so PCA is unnecessary. When MSE is low the outputs must lie on the unit circle (the target angular embeddings), and when MSE is high they will not. This does not reveal anything about the model’s internal workings.  
   - R4 argues that the comparison with interpretability‑focused works in Table 2 is not directly relevant, because those works do not aim to improve modular‑addition performance; the comparison is therefore less meaningful and potentially misleading.  

5. **Missing experiments, comparisons, and ablations**  
   - Several reviewers ask for additional investigations that would strengthen the work:  
     * R4 requests comparisons with other embedding techniques (e.g., abacus embedding at https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17399, dice embedding at https://aclanthology.org/2020.emnlp-main.384.pdf).  
     * R4 asks whether digit‑wise tokenization (representing numbers as sequences of digits) was tried.  
     * R3 suggests exploring active learning or adaptive sampling to generate training data.  
     * R3 notes that the evaluation uses a uniform test distribution, but in a cryptanalytic application the distribution may differ; the paper does not discuss how to adjust.  
     * R4 points out that while the model succeeds up to \(N=256, q=3329\), potential limitations as parameters increase further are not addressed.  
     * R1 mentions that Mohamadi et al.’s claim about the need for a fraction of data applies only to the “kernel regime” (where no feature learning occurs), not to trained networks—this nuance is missing.
