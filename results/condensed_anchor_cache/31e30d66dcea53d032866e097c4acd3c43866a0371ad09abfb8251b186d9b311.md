## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper provides PAC-Bayes generalization bounds for prompt engineering in vision-language models (e.g., CLIP). By treating prompts as a discrete parameter space and using a pretrained language model (LLaMA) to define a prior, with the posterior being a delta distribution on the prompt found by a greedy search, the authors obtain bounds that are tighter than typical deep learning PAC-Bayes bounds and uniform convergence bounds. The bounds are often within a few percentage points of the test error (e.g., on ImageNet) and remain non-vacuous in the few-shot regime (20 samples per class). The work offers theoretical support for why prompt engineering does not overfit despite optimizing on a training set. However, the analysis is limited to image classification, the bounds assume no data contamination in the pretrained encoder, and the practical utility of the bound for model selection is questioned. Reviewer opinions range from enthusiastic (scores 8) to moderately positive (score 6), with the lower score citing strong assumptions and limited practical takeaways.

### Strengths
- **Interesting and original perspective**: Combining PAC-Bayes theory with prompting is novel and intuitively appealing (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- **Well-written and easy to follow**: The theoretical development is clear and accessible (R1, R3, R4).
- **Tighter bounds than typical deep learning PAC-Bayes**: The obtained generalization bounds are remarkably tight (often within a few percentage points) compared to other PAC-Bayes bounds in the literature, including data-dependent ones (though for different hypothesis classes) (R1, R3, R4).
- **Non-vacuous bounds in the few-shot regime**: Demonstrating meaningful bounds with only 20 samples per class pushes towards strong generalization guarantees in low-data settings (R3).
- **Provides much-needed theoretical support**: The paper explains why prompt engineering works well despite training-set optimization, a largely empirical phenomenon (R4).
- **Intuitive use of a language model as a prior**: The idea of leveraging a pretrained LM (LLaMA) to define a prior over prompts is a key enabler for the tight bounds (R2, R3).
- **Empirical evidence on random labels**: Both training and test accuracy drop monotonically when training labels are randomized (Figure 6), suggesting prompts cannot overfit random labels (R3, though the reviewer notes this could also be due to search failure – see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Validity hinges on “no data contamination” assumption**: The entire derivation assumes the vision-language encoder (e.g., CLIP) was not trained on the test data (truly zero-shot). This is unverifiable given the opacity of CLIP’s training data. The authors acknowledge this, but the limitation is fundamental (R4). A possible remedy (pre-training an open-source model from scratch with full data control) would require enormous compute (R4).
- **Data-dependence of the prior vs. standard bounds**: The paper criticizes standard PAC-Bayes bounds as “vacuous unless conditioned on data,” but pretraining on massive data (like CLIP) is very similar to conditioning on data. The claim of being “not data-dependent” is inconsistent (R1).
- **Title and abstract oversell and are contradictory**: The title claims “understanding” prompt engineering, but the paper does not actually provide new insight (R1; the conclusion itself says the results are “not a new fundamental insight”). The abstract’s claim that the bound is “remarkably suitable for model selection” is not clearly supported empirically (R1, R3).
- **Bound not much better than naïve baselines in practice**: The difference between the uniform convergence (UC) bound and the PAC-Bayes bound is often only a factor of 2–3, meaning scaling the data 4–9 times could achieve similar generalization error. More experiments at larger scales would be needed to demonstrate practical significance (R2).
- **Bound is no more useful than training error for model selection**: Based on Figure 3, the PAC-Bayes bound does not appear to provide a better selection criterion than simply picking the prompt with lowest training error. There is no clear example where the bound leads to a different and better choice (R3).
- **Only evaluated on image classification**: Prompting is equally or more common in NLP (e.g., text classification, hard prompt tuning), yet the paper tests only vision-language tasks. Extension to NLP is a natural next step (R2, R3).
- **Greedy search is suboptimal**: The greedy prompt search is far simpler than methods like those in Wen et al. (2023). Obtaining bounds for a suboptimal classifier limits the interest—would the bounds hold for better prompts? (R3).
- **Linear probe significantly outperforms the greedy prompt search** (Table 3), casting doubt on whether prompt engineering (even with tight bounds) is the method of choice (R1).
- **Random-label experiment may reflect search failure, not a property of prompts**: The fact that accuracy drops with random labels (Figure 6) could simply mean the greedy search cannot find a good prompt under random labels, not that prompts are inherently unable to overfit (R3).
- **The analysis does not suggest a practical algorithm**: The bound does not improve over just minimizing training error, and no practical algorithm (e.g., SRM) is shown to be better (R3).
- **No ablation on the prompt used for the language-model prior**: Would prompting LLaMA differently (e.g., with “Suggest a good CLIP prompt for classifying cats vs dogs”) yield a better prior and tighter bounds? This is unexplored (R3).
- **Transferring the generalization problem to the language model**: Using a pretrained LM (LLaMA) as a prior might just shift the generalization question to that model—does this simply assume away the problem? (R2).
- **Questions that overlap with criticisms**:
  - How does data-dependent PAC-Bayes (prior learned on pretraining data) differ from the proposed approach? (R1)
  - Can similar ideas be applied to hard prompt tuning in NLP? (R2)
  - What criteria should be used to judge if a new generalization bound is significantly better? (R2)
  - Could a better prior prompt (e.g., via GPT-4) improve the bounds? (R3)
