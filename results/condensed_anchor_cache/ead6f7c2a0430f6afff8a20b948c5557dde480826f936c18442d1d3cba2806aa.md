## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes using Shapley Taylor Interaction Indices (STII) to analyze whether value functions in masked (MLM) and autoregressive (ALM) language models, speech models, and image classifiers reflect known data structures: multi-word expressions, positional/syntactic distance, vowel-consonant vs. consonant-consonant transitions, and pixel interactions near object boundaries. STII values generally align with these patterns; autoregressive LMs differ from masked LMs, suggesting the latter learn more hierarchical structure.

### Strengths
- **Novel application of STII to idiomatic expressions in LMs** — reveals how models capture non-compositional patterns in multiword expressions and how these differ from regular syntax (Reviewer 2, who is more positive than the majority).
- **Multi-domain coverage** (language, speech, image) expands impact across NLP, speech, and computer vision communities (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- **Focus on modern Transformer-based autoregressive and masked LMs**, unlike prior feature-attribution studies (Reviewer 3).
- **Interpretability of representations and model architectures** is a valuable research topic; STII’s effectiveness across positional distance, syntactic distance, MWEs, phoneme classes, and object boundaries is interesting (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Unclear motivation and contribution (majority concern)** – The paper does not clearly state the research hypothesis or why STII is the method of choice. Data patterns appear handpicked; it is unclear what difference in STII is significant or whether STII is a reliable tool for comparing models. The findings are intuitive and overlap with prior probing works on MLMs, CLMs, and speech representations (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). The application confirms known structures without discovering new dependencies or providing quantitative comparisons with alternative methods (Reviewer 1). One reviewer (Reviewer 3) asks whether STII is considered better than prior methods, if there is intuition for its choice, and whether the decision was influenced by Transformer architectures.
2. **Lack of comparison with existing interpretability methods** – No baseline comparisons with other techniques such as mutual information, CCA, or other interpretability approaches. For speech models, it would be useful to compare STII to other speech representations known to correspond to phoneme discrimination (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). The paper would benefit from using SOTA models and reporting statistical significance (Reviewer 1).
3. **Computational complexity and practicality** – STII is extremely computationally intensive; the paper only briefly mentions an approximation method (Monte Carlo Permutation Sampling) without describing it in the main text (Section 2 merely cites). Further insights into practicality for large-scale datasets are needed (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). One reviewer (Reviewer 1) suggests that conditional mutual information or CCA could be used instead and asks why a new technique is required.
4. **Insufficient empirical validation for language modeling** – Each sample sentence is truncated to 20 tokens, and Figure 1 shows distance only up to 6. To study positional influence, the experiment should cover short to long distances (up to max sequence length 512) (Reviewer 4).
5. **Paper density and readability** – Abbreviations (MLM, ALM, STII) are introduced early but not always defined at first use, making the paper hard to follow. The entire third application is relegated to the appendix; condensing the paper to present main results up front would improve clarity (Reviewers 1, 2).
6. **Minor positive reviewer notes** – One reviewer (Reviewer 2) who rates the paper higher (score 6) still raises a question about the varying scales of STII values: MLM experiments range 0.4–0.8, ALM range 0.2–0.4, phoneme experiments a narrower 0.165–0.210. This reviewer asks whether the narrower range in phoneme experiments might be influenced by Monte Carlo sampling, and requests insight into the observed scale differences (Reviewer 2). This question is listed here as it overlaps with a potential methodological weakness.
