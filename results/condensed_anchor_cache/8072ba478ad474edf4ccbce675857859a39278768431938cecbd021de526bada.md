## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a novel machine unlearning method framed as a Stackelberg game between a defender (unlearning model) and an attacker (membership inference attack, MIA). The defender aims to remove specific training data’s influence while the attacker tries to detect those traces. The framework uses implicit differentiation for a gradient-based solution that is integrable into end-to-end pipelines. Experiments on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 with ResNet-18 demonstrate a trade-off between model utility and unlearning effectiveness.

### Strengths
- **Original game-theoretic framing**: Modeling machine unlearning as a Stackelberg game between a defender and an MIA attacker is a notably original idea. It introduces a systematic, strategic approach that integrates attack advancements into the design of unlearning algorithms.
- **Gradient-based solution via implicit differentiation**: Using implicit differentiation and differentiable optimization to solve the game enables efficient, end-to-end optimization of the unlearning process. This is a novel methodological contribution.
- **Validation on two datasets with balanced trade-off**: Experimental results on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 show the framework achieves a balanced trade-off between model utility and unlearning effectiveness, supporting the practical promise of the approach.
- **Fresh perspective on machine unlearning**: While the idea of integrating attacks into a defensive framework is not entirely new, this work offers a refreshing adversarial perspective that is both intriguing and relevant (all reviewers note the importance of the problem).
- **One reviewer (R1, rating 6) is particularly positive** about the originality of the Stackelberg framing and the gradient-based solution; the other two reviewers (R2, R3, rating 5) also praise the novelty but raise more concrete concerns about analysis and scope.

### Weaknesses
- **No theoretical analysis of the game**: The paper lacks any theoretical guarantees or discussions on convergence, complexity, optimality, or which solution concept the algorithm converges to. Neither theoretical proof nor convergence curves (utility evolution, loss/accuracy over time) are provided. (R1, R2, R3)
- **Limited experimental conditions**: All experiments use only a randomly sampled forget set (10% of training data) and a single architecture (ResNet-18) on CIFAR-10/100. Broader conditions are missing, e.g., forget set sizes of 20%, class-specific forgetting, poisoned sample forgetting, and other network architectures. This is a significant limitation since class-wise unlearning and poisoned-data removal are common real-world scenarios (references [2,3,5] cited by R3). (R1, R2, R3)
- **Questionable real-world relevance of random forget set**: The assumption that the forget set is randomly sampled is not justified for regulation-driven unlearning (e.g., GDPR). If random, the problem reduces to understanding the effect of training set size on model performance, which trivializes the scenario. (R1)
- **Unclear metric selection and real-world benefit**: The paper does not clearly justify how the chosen metrics benefit real-world applications. The primary goal of machine unlearning is to erase data influence while maintaining high model performance; metrics should reflect that regardless of retraining performance. (R1)
- **Metrics too tied to integrated MIA**: KS and MIA metrics are closely related to the MIA already integrated into the unlearning algorithm. To provide a more holistic evaluation, the paper should include additional diverse metrics such as Weight Distance [1], Activation Distance [2,3], Relearn Time [4], and Epistemic Uncertainty [5]. (R3)
- **Missing baseline: unscrubbed model performance**: The paper does not report the performance of the unscrubbed model on test, retain, and forget sets, which is necessary to establish a clear baseline for unlearning effectiveness. (R3)
- **Missing details on existing methods (FF, IU)**: The behavior of methods like FF and IU under increased noise levels is not investigated; the paper should explain why they fail and how they perform with different noise magnitudes. (R3)
- **No comparison with contemporary methods**: The literature review overlooks recent works (e.g., [5–8]), and the experiments do not include any contemporary method for benchmarking (e.g., pruning-based techniques [1]). (R3)
- **No consideration of multiple attackers or attack types**: The framework only considers a single attacker with one MIA method. Multiple attackers or multiple attack strategies could pose stronger or more stealthy threats, and this is not addressed. (R2)
- **Computational overhead and efficiency not analyzed**: The paper does not report the number of epochs used for retrained models or the additional computational cost introduced by the MIA, making it hard to assess practical applicability. (R3)
- **Missing ablation/convergence analysis for gradient-based method**: It is unclear how the utilities of both players evolve during training and whether the algorithm converges to a specific solution concept (e.g., Stackelberg equilibrium). No loss/accuracy curves for the proposed method versus retrained model are given. (R1, R3)
- **Notation and equation clarity issues** (R3 only):
    - In Eq.(5), the precise formulation for \(f\) is missing; the stationarity conditions should account for \(\nabla V(\tilde{D}_{\theta_u}^{tr}; \theta_a')\) explicitly.
    - In Eq.(4), it is unclear whether \(D_{\theta_u}\) should be \(D_{\theta_u}^{val}\) or \(\tilde{D}_{\theta_u}^{tr}\).
    - In Section 5, it is unclear whether \(n\) denotes the size of the attack model parameters.
    - In Section 3, using \((x_j^f, y_j^f)\) instead of \((x_f^j, y_f^j)\) would be clearer for index notation.
- **Minor: FT achieves the best test accuracy in tables**, which warrants discussion. (R3)
