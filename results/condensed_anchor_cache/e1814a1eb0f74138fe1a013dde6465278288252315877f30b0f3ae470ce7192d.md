## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper presents X-ALMA, a multilingual machine translation model focused on delivering high-quality translations across 50 languages (low- to high-resource) by prioritizing quality over language count. It uses plug-and-play LoRA adapter modules grouped by language families to prevent language conflicts during training. The training recipe includes three pre-training and two post-training stages, with the last stage introducing Adaptive-Rejection Preference Optimization (ARPO) to mitigate over-rejection when preferred and dispreferred hypotheses have high lexical overlap. X-ALMA surpasses open-source multilingual LLMs (Aya-101, Aya-23) on FLORES-200 and WMT’23 per COMET-22, and the authors release models and a multilingual preference dataset. Reviewer 2 notes that using language-specific adapters for multilingual MT is not new and that ARPO does not address the core granularity issue of preference loss, while Reviewers 1 and 3 find the approach novel and valuable.

### Strengths

- Well-written, clear, and easy to follow; motivation (mitigating quality loss when scaling languages) is solid, timely, and important.
- Experimental settings are sound and robust; extensive evaluations demonstrate consistent superior performance across language subgroups compared to baselines.
- ARPO is a useful preference optimization method that addresses shortcomings of previous approaches applied to MT (over-rejection of near-tie hypotheses); it is a creative combination of existing ideas.
- The plug-and-play module architecture effectively reduces language conflicts during training.
- The multilingual preference dataset to be released is a valuable resource for the community.
- The work has potential for significant impact, particularly in delivering high-quality translations for mid- and low-resource languages.

### Weaknesses

- It is unclear whether the method works for non-English translation directions (e.g., source and target both non-English) – can one load more than one language module and translate across language families? The paper should clarify this capability.
- All evaluation relies on reference-based automated metrics (COMET-22); no human evaluation is conducted to validate whether the automated findings hold in practice.
- One reviewer (Reviewer 2) points out that using language-specific adapters for multilingual MT is not new; the paper does not establish sufficient connection to prior pre-LLM modular and adaptable approaches for multilingual NMT. This is important even if the technical setups differ.
- The same reviewer (R2) argues that ARPO does not address the core issue: the granularity at which the preference loss is defined. Instead of diluting the signal from the dispreferred response with a penalty when preferred and dispreferred are close, a more meaningful learning signal would help the model focus on differences at a finer granularity, especially where preferences hinge on subtle translation nuances. ARPO risks being a temporary fix. (Reviewers 1 and 3 view ARPO more positively.)
- No comparison with state-of-the-art encoder-decoder NMT models that are trained on specific language pairs. The paper only compares with LLM-based MT models. Performance on Chinese-English and English-Chinese appears less effective than WMT-reported SOTA results, while it outperforms WMT in other pairs; the reason for this discrepancy is not discussed.
- Clarification needed: Why is parallel text referred to as a "pseudo-monolingual dataset" (L249-250)?
- Clarification needed: What does "accurate classification" mean in L213-215?
- Clarification needed: In Pre-Training Stage 2 (fine-tuning with 10B tokens), are an equal number of tokens taken from each language in the group, or proportionally?
