## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes combining distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) with physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) for indoor sound source localization. The PINN embeds the acoustic wave equation and impedance boundary conditions. Two strategies are presented: simultaneous training on all data and incremental time-stepping. All experiments are conducted on synthetic 2D/3D data, despite the abstract and introduction claiming use of real DAS measurements. The fiber network installation described in Section 2 (Fig. 1) is never used in the experiments. Reviewers found the motivation promising but raised major concerns about lack of real-world validation, no quantitative metrics or baselines, weak novelty relative to prior PINN-based sound field reconstruction (Olivieri et al., 2024), poor presentation clarity, and insufficient scope (single static source only). Two reviewers scored 5 (positive) and two scored 3 (critical), disagreeing on the strength of the contribution and the adequacy of the evaluation.

### Strengths
- Contributions are clearly specified and the motivation for combining DAS and PINNs is well established.
- The physics-guided neural network (embedding the wave equation and impedance boundary conditions) is a strong and promising approach in the emerging field of DAS.
- The two localization strategies (full-data simultaneous and incremental time-stepping) are sensible and relevant for real-time deployment.
- The method does not require labeled source positions for training, making it potentially widely applicable.
- Simulations in 2D and 3D demonstrate feasibility, and the approach leverages existing optical fiber infrastructure for privacy-preserving surveillance.
- Efficiency and simplicity are highlighted as strengths (no need for large labeled datasets).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of real-world validation**: Despite repeated claims in the abstract and introduction of using “real indoor DAS measurements,” all experiments are synthetic. The fiber network installation described in Section 2 (Fig. 1) is never used. This is a major discrepancy that severely limits practical relevance.
- **No quantitative evaluation**: No localization error metric (e.g., Euclidean distance) is reported. Only qualitative visualizations (e.g., Fig. 4c, Fig. 7) are provided; these show multiple possible source locations, making claims of “stable localization” and “accurate inference” unsupported. Baseline comparisons (beamforming, MLP without PINN, other deep learning methods such as those surveyed by Grumiaux et al.) are absent.
- **Limited novelty and weak differentiation from prior work**: PINNs have already been applied to sound field reconstruction by Olivieri et al. (2024). The claimed contribution (adding impedance boundary conditions) is seen as a small incremental step. The combination of DAS and PINNs is considered a straightforward application rather than a methodological advance. Lower-score reviewers question whether the key contribution is in methodology or the specific application.
- **Poor presentation and clarity**:
  - PINN architecture (Fig. 2) is not clearly explained: inputs, outputs, learned parameters, and how spatial coordinates (x,y,z) are handled are ambiguous. It is unclear whether coordinates are a fixed grid, random samples, or initial guesses for the source position.
  - Loss function symbols (x_pde, x_bc, x_ic, x_data, x_0 in Eq. 7 and Eq. 11) are not properly introduced; the relationship between terms and optimization over x_0 is unclear.
  - Figure descriptions (Figs. 3, 4, 5, 6, 7) are too brief – symbols, axes, and what is displayed (green squares, red stars, multiple final locations) are not explained. Acronyms (FEM, BC, IC) are used without prior definition.
  - A missing paragraph after line 182 breaks the narrative flow.
  - Essential sections are missing: background on PINNs, expanded related work on both DAS and PINNs, and an ethics statement (especially critical given the emphasis on surveillance without compromising privacy).
  - Only 13 references are included; many relevant DAS works (e.g., Cedilnik et al. 2019, Jousset et al. 2022, Lior et al. 2021) and PINN works (e.g., Krishnapriyan et al. 2021, Cuomo et al. 2022, Raissi et al. 2019) are omitted.
- **Narrow experimental scope**: Only a single static sound source is tested. There are no experiments with moving sources or multiple sources, nor is the theoretical applicability to such scenarios discussed. This significantly limits practical relevance.
- **Training strategy comparison unconvincing**: The paper claims Strategy 1 (full-data) significantly outperforms Strategy 2 (incremental), but Fig. 4 shows similar performance. Lines 270–271 appear to have the strategy indices swapped (should be Strategy 2 outperforming Strategy 1). No numerical evidence supports either claim, and the trade-offs between strategies are not analyzed.
- **Practicality of sensing layouts**: The Halton distribution and Volume configuration require fibers covering the entire space, which is impractical (e.g., 1m × 1m × 1m rooms would be clogged). Real fiber placements are constrained by infrastructure. The paper does not address optimal array design or explain how fiber placement can be controlled.
- **Missing ablations and additional experiments**:
  - No ablation study comparing with and without impedance boundary conditions.
  - No evaluation of the effect of different fiber geometries, fiber lengths, or noise levels.
  - No comparison to optimization without boundary conditions (as requested by Reviewer 2).
- **Methodological details omitted**:
  - How the weights in Eq. (7) are chosen.
  - How impedance and boundary conditions are determined for the synthetic environments.
  - Training setup: epochs, optimizer, learning rate, batch size not reported.
  - Simulation parameters (geometry, source characteristics) insufficiently described; reproducibility is hindered.
  - Meaning of “1250 distinct monitoring channels” (line 132) and the “interrogator” in Fig. 1 not explained.
- **Correspondence between real fiber deployment and simulation assumptions is weak**: The paper uses Halton distributions and random arrays, but accurate fiber locations are often unknown in practice. The only connection to DAS is the underlying physics model; experiments with piecewise-linear fiber arrays more typical of real DAS setups are missing.
- **Reviewer disagreement**: Two reviewers (scores 5,5) found the approach innovative and well-motivated, while two (scores 3,3) considered the novelty low, the evaluation insufficient, and the presentation unclear. The lower-score reviewers particularly emphasized the lack of real data, missing quantitative metrics, and weak differentiation from prior work.
