## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Verbalized Graph Representation Learning (VGRL), a training-free framework that uses a frozen LLM as enhancer, predictor, optimizer, and summarizer to perform node classification on text-attributed graphs. Optimization is done entirely in text space via prompt engineering. Two reviewers (R2, R4) see the goal of full interpretability in graph learning as a promising direction and note the open-source code. Two reviewers (R1, R3) give the lowest scores, with R1 finding no notable strength and R3 stating the method lacks any strong positive aspects. All reviewers identify serious weaknesses (see below).

### Strengths

- **Goal:** The aim of a fully interpretable graph representation learning method is a promising research direction (R2). The paper explores an interesting question of how to achieve explainability of traditional models in the text space (R4).
- **Approach:** Introduces a new way to utilize LLMs for interpretable graph learning (R3). Proposes a viable solution by performing optimization in the text space (R4).
- **Open-source code provided** (R2).
- **Contrast:** R1 explicitly states “I don’t recognize a notable strength of this paper.”

### Weaknesses

1. **Insufficient and obsolete experiments**
   - Only validated on the single, small, obsolete Cora dataset. Missing additional TAGs such as Citeseer and ogbn-arxiv (R1, R3, R4).
   - Only compared with a vanilla LLM; no comparison to any existing related work or SOTA baselines (R2, R3). Ablation studies are present but without SOTA context (R2).
   - No experiment evaluates the explainability of the model; only prediction accuracy is reported. A case study is not rigorous enough (R4).

2. **Plagiarism of theoretical analysis**
   - Section 6 (THEORETICAL ANALYSIS) and Appendix A are nearly identical to Section 4.4 and Appendix A of He et al. (2023, “Harnessing Explanations”), with only variable names changed, and lack proper citation (R2, R3). One reviewer (R1) also notes the paper merely combines that work with verbalized prompt classification.
   - The copied theoretical part does not explain the empirical results (R4).

3. **Misunderstanding or lack of substantiation of “explainability”**
   - The authors assume that expressing prediction logic in natural language automatically equates to explainability, but natural language can generate irrelevant or misleading explanations that do not reflect the model’s true reasoning. A rigorous definition of explainability (e.g., Carvalho et al. 2019) is missing (R4).
   - No related work on explainability is discussed (e.g., Zhang et al. 2024, “LLMExplainer”); the prompt optimization philosophy is highly similar to existing work (e.g., Yuksekgonul et al. 2024, “TextGrad”) (R4).
   - Interpretability and efficiency are claimed as primary contributions, but no empirical or theoretical analysis substantiates these claims (R2, R4).

4. **Poor presentation and clarity**
   - Writing is vague with ambiguous phrases: “stepping stone” (line 31), “deeper insight” (line 44), “training dynamics” (line 109) are introduced but never elaborated (R4).
   - Specific technical details are unclear or erroneous: line 141 “And iterates over the input mini-batch B one-pass input”; line 144 the definition of one-hop neighbors is missing (R2).
   - Methodology is very hard to follow; it should be summarized into algorithms (R4).
   - Lines 85–100 discuss Graph LLMs without motivating the connection to explainability (R4).
   - The term “embedding” in line 268 is not defined; how it is implemented is unclear (R4).

5. **Not tailored for graph tasks**
   - The proposed work does not address challenges specific to graph-structured data; the authors do not mention “graph” in the two challenges they propose (R1).

6. **Lack of novelty / incremental contribution**
   - The framework merely combines existing work ([1] Verbalized Machine Learning and [2] Harnessing Explanations) and shows improvement only on Cora (R1). The theoretical part is a simple replication of He et al. (R4).
