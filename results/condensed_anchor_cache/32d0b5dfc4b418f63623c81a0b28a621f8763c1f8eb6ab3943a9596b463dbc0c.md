## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a self-supervised GNN-based framework for learnable mesh coarsening to accelerate PDE simulations. It uses a stability loss and differentiable implicit solver, aiming for generalizability across different setups. Reviewers find the idea interesting but raise significant concerns: limited novelty (differentiable implicit methods and implicit GNNs already exist), weak experimental evaluation (missing comparisons with existing mesh coarsening works, reduced-order modeling baselines, and classical methods like AMR), insufficient performance advantages (worse accuracy in one test case, no speedup or memory benefit, no prediction time reported), limited generalization (performance drops for different mesh resolutions and source terms), and overlooked interpolation error for field solutions. One reviewer (R1, score 3) is substantially more critical than the others (scores 5), emphasizing low novelty and weak evaluation.

### Strengths
- Clear and easy-to-follow exposition of the method.
- Focus on a relatively new problem in the community: automated mesh coarsening for physics simulations.
- Automatic optimization of the grid, reducing human effort compared to manual methods.
- Designed loss term to soft-enforce numerical stability on the coarsened grid.
- Results are comparable with recent global-optimization-based coarsening methods.
- Interesting trial to extend on-the-fly optimization to a learnable GNN, potentially saving time in future deployments without re-optimization per grid.
- Self-supervised GNN coarsening framework is a significant advancement over traditional grid-specific optimization.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty:** The differentiable implicit solver claimed as a contribution is already supported natively (e.g., PyTorch `torch.linalg.solve`), and implicit GNNs have been proposed (e.g., Gu et al. NeurIPS 2020). (R1, more negative)
- **Weak evaluation and missing baselines:**
  - Missing comparisons with existing mesh coarsening methods (e.g., Pfaff et al. ICLR 2021, Alet et al. ICML 2019). (R1)
  - No comparison of the quality of the generated clusters/clustering against baseline coarsening techniques. (R4)
  - No evaluation against classical numerical analysis methods (e.g., adaptive mesh refinement) or speed-accuracy tradeoff with varying uniform resolutions. (R1)
  - No comparison with additional reduced-order modeling baselines such as UPT and Transolver. (R4)
  - The method is tested only on very simple PDE cases (simplified wave propagation and subsurface flow); more complex scenarios would strengthen the evaluation. (R2)
- **Insufficient performance advantages:**
  - The proposed method has worse accuracy than the baseline in 1 out of 2 test cases, without justification for why this trade-off is acceptable in practice. (R3)
  - Training is slower and more memory-intensive than the baseline, yet results are comparable or only slightly better. (R2)
  - The reported speedup (e.g., from 22s to 5s) is not substantial, and the training overhead questions the overall efficiency gain. (R3)
  - There is no evaluation of prediction (inference) time, which is a key metric for mesh coarsening. (R1)
- **Limited generalization capabilities:**
  - Performance deteriorates significantly when applied to meshes of different resolutions, contradicting the paper’s central claim of improved generalizability. (R3)
  - Performance also degrades when tested with additional source terms, showing the learned coarsening strategy is not robust to changes in the PDE problem. (R3)
- **Ignored interpolation error:** The method only ensures node-wise accuracy. For field solution applications, interpolation from the coarsened grid (especially super-coarsened) would introduce significant additional errors that are not considered. (R2)
- **Missing implementation and clarity details:** (Multiple reviewers inquire; these overlap with criticisms/missing ablations)
  - How does the GNN decide which nodes to keep? Is it Top-K? (R3)
  - After obtaining coarsened nodes, how is the new mesh generated? The method only mentions triangular meshes; what about 3D or non-simplex elements? (R3)
  - How many timesteps are used for gradient backpropagation (single-step or multi-step)? If multi-step, how many? (R3)
  - The claimed improvement from an implicit method is not supported by experimental results; where are they? (R3)
  - The requirement of differentiability is easy for explicit schemes but challenging for general PDE solvers (e.g., WENO for nonlinear advection). How would the method extend? (R3)
  - In Sec 3.3, \(S_{ij}\) is said to be learnable by an NN. What NN architecture outputs \(S_{ij}\)? Does it depend on the graph topology (via a GNN)? Otherwise it cannot generalize to other mesh structures. (R3)
  - The first appearance of “stability loss” in the main text lacks an explanation, hindering readability. (R3)
  - How does the method handle cases where sensor locations do not align with grid points? Are barycentric interpolants used? (R3)
  - Eq. 9 (size regularization) does not seem connected to stability criteria (e.g., CFL); its role and connection to Eq. 8 need clarification. (R3)
  - What are the input quantities of the GCN for each task? (R4)
  - Can the method adapt to non-fixed mesh topology systems (e.g., Lagrangian systems like the Deforming Plate model in MeshGraphNets)? (R4)
  - Were alternative remeshing strategies (e.g., Delaunay triangulation) considered? How would they compare? (R4)
  - The colorbar in Figure 8 of Appendix A is not explained. (R4)
- **General question (overlapping with weakness):** Since a GNN is already employed, why not directly generate the solution with a larger GNN instead of coarsening the mesh? (R2)
