## Merged Review

### Summary  
The three reviewers gave scores 3, 1, 1. One reviewer (R1) was notably more positive, praising the novel neuroscience-inspired idea, clear writing, and valuable human dataset, while still identifying major weaknesses. The other two reviewers (R2, R3) rated the paper very low, finding the contribution minimal, the experimental evaluation severely lacking, and the novelty insufficient. All reviewers agree on several core deficiencies: missing error bars and statistical tests, single‑dataset evaluation (HAHA500), no comparison to state‑of‑the‑art models, no ablation studies, and unclear loss definitions. R3 further emphasizes dataset confounds (background as a confound, dataset too easy) and unfair training comparisons. The contrast between R1’s moderate rating and the two low ratings is preserved throughout.

### Strengths  
- The paper introduces a novel neuroscientific inductive bias—domain‑specific streams for body and scene processing—into deep action recognition, and shows that this architecture improves performance over a baseline ResNet50 and yields accuracy patterns that better match human performance (R1).  
- The manuscript is well‑written and clearly communicates the motivation, experimental design, and findings (R1).  
- The collected human behavioral dataset (N=28 on the HAHA500 dataset) is a valuable resource that could be incorporated into action recognition benchmarks for human‑machine comparisons (R1).  
- The overarching goal of building networks that emulate human action recognition is practically important, and comparing network performance to human performance is of scientific interest (R3).

### Weaknesses  
- **Lack of experimental rigor**: Results are presented without error bars or any statistical analysis; the claim in Fig. 1 that baseline models perform similarly on ORIG, body‑only, and background‑only is not supported by the displayed data (eyeballing suggests ORIG >> BG >> Body) (R3). The manuscript contains only one main figure with minimal supporting information, falling below standard publication requirements (R2, R3).  
- **Limited scope and generalizability**: Experiments are restricted to a single dataset (HAHA500) and a simple action recognition task, raising doubts about generalization to more complex scenarios (R1). No comparisons to state‑of‑the‑art action recognition models are provided, and the paper does not discuss how the proposed inductive bias could be integrated with SOTA architectures (R1).  
- **No ablation of stream contributions**: The paper does not isolate the individual contributions of the body and background streams, making it impossible to assess the true benefit of the dual‑stream design (R1). Reviewer 2 additionally asks which component of the loss contributes most under body‑only or background‑only conditions.  
- **Insufficient novelty**: Using separate backbones for different information streams is not new; prior work (Tesfaldet et al., 2017, *Two‑Stream Convolutional Networks for Dynamic Texture Synthesis*) already applies separate networks for appearance and dynamics with a combined loss (R2). The loss function details (L_body, L_background, L_combined) are not defined, so the only potential novelty—the combined loss—remains unclear (R2). The overall result that a body‑background separated backbone produces human‑like accuracy patterns is not surprising given prior literature (R2).  
- **Dataset and confound issues**: Humans achieve >0.7 accuracy on background‑only stimuli, indicating the dataset is too easy and that background is a major confounding factor; this undermines interpretability of both human and model results (R3). The quality of the body/background separation (e.g., Yolo v8) is not evaluated (R3). There is no control for basic visual variables such as contrast, size, or other confounds (R3).  
- **Unfair training comparison**: The proposed architecture is trained on body‑only and background‑only stimuli, whereas the baseline is trained on full stimuli; therefore, the better performance on those conditions may simply reflect the training data, not the architecture itself (R3).  
- **Missing temporal analysis**: Action recognition inherently involves dynamics and time, yet the paper only considers single frames and does not study motion (R2, R3).  
- **Unexplained design choices**: It is not justified why the network should process background information at all if the goal is to focus on the action (R2). The loss components (L_body, L_background, L_combined) are not defined or analyzed (R2).
