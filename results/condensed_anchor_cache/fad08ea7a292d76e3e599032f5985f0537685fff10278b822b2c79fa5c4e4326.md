## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes model-agnostic meta-learners for estimating heterogeneous treatment effects over time, covering history, regression, propensity, doubly robust, and a novel inverse-variance weighted doubly robust adjustment. It provides a theoretical analysis of asymptotic error rates and confirms insights with one synthetic dataset and a MIMIC-III real-world experiment using a Transformer base learner. Reviewers are split: two rate the paper 8, praising the practical relevance, clear presentation, and the value of the meta-learner framework; two rate it 6, citing weak experimental validation, insufficient motivation, and a lack of comparisons to existing methods. All agree the problem is important and the theory is a solid contribution, but disagreement persists on the breadth and conclusiveness of the empirical work.

### Strengths
- The problem of time-varying heterogeneous treatment effect estimation is of high practical relevance, especially for personalized medicine using observational data.
- The paper provides a **comprehensive organization** of existing learners and extends them to a unified framework of model-agnostic meta‑learners (HA, RA, PI‑RA, DR, IVW‑DR). The framework is novel and will likely have wide applicability, given the popularity of meta‑learners in static settings.
- Presentation of technical results is excellent. Non‑expert readers benefit from helpful interpretations, such as:
  - Explanation for why equation (3) fails to adjust for post‑treatment confounders.
  - Clarification of the constant variance assumption in Theorem 2.
  - Insight into the weighting mechanism of the IVW‑DR learner (lines 413‑419) and its connection to static binary‑treatment settings.
  - Intuition for which synthetic dataset each method should perform best on.
- The theoretical analysis is good and comprehensive: asymptotic bounds are provided for each meta‑learner, and the paper gives practical guidance on when each method is preferable.
- The empirical evaluation (though limited in scope) clearly shows benefits of different approaches, with results corresponding to the hypotheses derived from theory.
- The paper includes a **comprehensive related works** section.

### Weaknesses
- **Experimental validation is insufficient.** Only one synthetic dataset and one real‑world dataset (MIMIC‑III) are used, and only one instantiation of the base learner (Transformer) is tested. No comparisons to existing state‑of‑the‑art methods for dynamic treatment effect estimation (e.g., CRN, CT, or IPW approaches like Papadogeorgou et al., 2022) are provided. Reviewers 1 and 3 note that ICLR traditionally values comprehensive experiments; the current work “feels unfinished” in this regard.
- **The claim of “model‑agnostic” is not empirically supported.** The paper states that the meta‑learners can work with any ML model, but only one architecture is tested. Performance likely varies across networks (e.g., Transformer vs. Mamba vs. RNNs). Reviewers 1 and 3 request evidence with different base learners to substantiate the claim.
- **Insufficient discussion of results and link to theory.** The experiment discussion is brief. Notably, PI‑HA is asymptotically biased, yet in the real‑world dataset it sometimes performs best (and often outperforms unbiased alternatives). The paper does not explain this discrepancy.
- **Missing conclusion, limitations, and future work.** The paper ends abruptly at Section 7 without a concluding paragraph. This omits an important reflection on limitations and directions for future research.
- **Motivation for the meta‑learner approach is not fully convincing.** Simply stating that meta‑learners are state‑of‑the‑art in the static setting is not sufficient. The paper should explain *how* the time‑varying setting benefits from model‑agnostic meta‑learners and clarify the challenge of time‑varying confounding (especially future covariates not accounted for by methods like CRN) in more detail.
- **Novelty concerns.** Reviewer 1 notes that IPW estimators for spatio‑temporal data have been proposed by Papadogeorgou et al. (2022, JRSS-B). The contribution of the current paper relative to that prior work is not clearly delineated.
- **Clarity of technical details needs improvement.**
  - Figure 1 caption lacks explanation of edges (e.g., why \(X(t-2)\) directly influences \(X(t+1)\)).
  - Some concepts are not clearly defined (e.g., whether “response surfaces” equal “response functions”).
  - The pseudo‑outcome in Eq. 8 (PI‑RA learner) needs explanation: why the time index of history \(H\) is \(t+1\) instead of \(t\) when \(A_t = a_t\) or \(b_t\).
  - Implementation details of the propensity score are unclear: the action sequence \(A_{:l}\) is high‑dimensional; how is its probability conditioned on history \(H_l\) modeled?
  - Notation is confusing: \(\mu\) and \(\delta\) are introduced alongside \(\tau\) and \(\mu\) with unclear reasoning (Reviewer 4).
  - For the RA‑learner, the static version uses only the final term of Eq. 8; the time‑varying extension is much more complex and intuition is lacking.
  - The paper does not clarify whether separate models are fit for each nuisance function, or if a multi‑task framework is used. (Reviewer 4)
  - Sample splitting is not mentioned in the experiments. (Reviewer 4)
  - The definition of a regression estimator “admitting a particular rate” in Assumption 3 should be stated precisely. Concrete examples for common base learners (linear regression, random forests, neural networks) would help readers understand Theorem 1.
- **Missing practical implications of rates.** The paper should discuss how the asymptotic rates translate into sample size requirements for a desired accuracy.
- **Real‑world experiment details.** The RMSE is reported on predicted normalized blood pressure; authors should specify the normalization and provide an absolute evaluation (e.g., what constitutes a “good” RMSE in clinical terms).
- **Minor issues:** Line 290: “major different” should be “difference”. The sentence “Here, we section outline…” contains a grammatical error. The paper lacks a discussion of computational efficiency.
