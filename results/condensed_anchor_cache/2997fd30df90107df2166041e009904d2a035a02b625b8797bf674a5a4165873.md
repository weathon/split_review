## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces an episodic control-based adversarial policy training approach for two-player competitive games. Historical evaluations of game states are stored in an episodic memory and used to modify rewards, encouraging the adversarial agent to prioritize states leading to winning outcomes. The method is evaluated on MuJoCo two-player simulation environments. The authors claim superior attack performance and defense difficulty compared to existing adversarial policy training baselines.

### Strengths
- Using historical experience to predict rewards and augment training is a sensible idea and appears effective within MuJoCo games (R1, R2, R3).
- The proposed method yields better empirical results than baseline adversarial policy training techniques on the selected environments (R1, R3).
- The paper is generally well-written and easy to follow, with the methodology well-organized (R3). The group-based episodic feedback technique is interesting (R3).
- Additional experiments in the appendix demonstrate generalizability to other baselines (R3).
- The findings have potential implications for improving robustness of agents in two-player competitive games (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and differentiation**: Using history in robust RL is not new (R1 cites "Robust Deep Reinforcement Learning against Adversarial Perturbations on State Observations"). The idea of leveraging good past trajectories is similar to Prioritized Experience Replay, Hindsight Experience Replay, etc., and these should be cited and differentiated (R3). One reviewer (R3) considers the application to adversarial policy training in two-player games novel in context, but the other two reviewers find the contribution limited.
- **Design justification and clarifying details**: The choice of episodic control over training a reward function from history features or using a transformer/CNN instead of LSTM lacks justification (R1). It is unclear whether multiple or a single pattern is selected per episode (R2). In Equation 3, the "otherwise" scenario needs explanation (R3). Section 4.3.1 does not clarify the difference between using historical scores vs. 3-step patterns (R3).
- **Contradictory statement in related work**: The claim that adversarial attacks on observations are "unrealistic since the real-world environment cannot be manipulated" is misrepresented; adversarial perturbations are realistic, and the paper's own approach indirectly manipulates the environment (R3).
- **Lack of theoretical guarantee**: The method is purely empirical and resembles reward manipulation; a bounds analysis is needed (R2).
- **Limited evaluation scope**: Experiments are confined to MuJoCo. It is unclear if the method would succeed in more complex environments like StarCraft II (R2). Additionally, the victims attacked are not robust; attacking robust agents or agents that also use history as input would be more meaningful (R1).
- **Computational complexity**: Updating episodic memory every episode increases complexity considerably. A wall-clock time comparison is missing, and it is unknown if less frequent updates would work (R3).
- **Assumption about moving average rewards**: The method uses a moving average of recent rewards; if training destabilizes and the moving average decreases, it is unclear whether the feedback remains helpful or if the formulation assumes rewards always increase (R3).
- **Anecdotal evidence**: Conclusions that the agent learns multiple winning strategies and both adversarial and non-adversarial strategies are based on a single anecdotal observation; more examples are needed (R3).
- **Clarity and completeness**: Terms like "non-loss rate" need definition (R3). Sections 4.3.1 and 4.3.2 are too brief to understand the comparisons (R3). There is a grammatical mistake: "(In practical, we use sliding window)" (R3). The claim that existing adversarial policy works are on-policy (implied from "only includes evaluations from a single game") needs clarification (R3).
