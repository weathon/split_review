## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes VLP, a vision-language preference learning framework for embodied manipulation. It defines three types of language-conditioned preferences—Intra-Task Preference (ITP), Inter-Language Preference (ILP), and Inter-Video Preference (IVP)—and constructs a dataset MTVLP (4.8K videos) with implicit preference orders without human annotation. A cross-modal transformer fuses video and language tokens to predict preferences, which are then used for reward learning or direct policy optimization. Experiments on Meta-World show superior performance and generalization to unseen tasks and language instructions compared to several baselines.

### Strengths
- The three-fold categorization of language-conditioned preferences (ITP, ILP, IVP) is novel and thoughtfully structured. ITP captures traditional preferences, ILP acts as a regularizer, and IVP enhances instruction-following. (R1, R2, R4)
- The framework is supported by both theoretical analysis (approximation of negative regret) and extensive empirical evaluation that addresses four key questions. (R1, R3, R4)
- The cross-modal architecture effectively fuses video and language via learnable parameters to compute preferences. (R2)
- Strong experimental results: VLP consistently outperforms existing VLM-based RLHF methods and shows good generalization to unseen tasks and language instructions. (R1, R2, R3)
- The paper is well-structured, clearly written, and the graphical illustrations enhance readability. (R1, R3, R4)
- Empirical evidence shows that inclusion of ILP and IVP contributes to improved performance, and the 4.8K videos are both necessary and sufficient to achieve over 97% ITP accuracy. (R2)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited evaluation environment**: Only Meta-World is used, and only five test tasks out of 50 are evaluated, which is not challenging compared to the 45 training tasks. Related works often test on multiple environments. No experiments on more complex domains (e.g., MANISKILL2, MyoSuite). (R1, R3)
- **Dataset construction limitations**: The trajectory sampling pipeline is simple and diversity is unclear; the dataset size (4.8K videos) is modest. (R1) Details on video pair construction for ITP, ILP, and IVP regarding optimality levels (expert, medium, random) are not fully explained, and the definition of “medium-optimality” across all 50 tasks is not provided. (R2 – question overlapping with missing analysis)
- **Missing comparisons**: Key baselines such as RL-VLM-F and CriticGPT are not compared. (R1) RoboCLIP (Sontakke et al., 2023) is not included; since VLP uses video input, comparing against a zero-shot video-based method would help isolate the benefit of the cross-modal architecture. (R2) No comparison with human preference labels, which would validate the quality of generated preferences against human intent. (R3) The performance of scripted policies (upper bound) is not reported. (R3 – minor)
- **Theoretical justification is weak**: The claim that the preference model approximates the true negative regret is not logically justified. Equations (10) and (11) have similar shapes but the model is trained on pseudo-labels derived from optimality and video-language alignment, not from the true reward function. The cross-entropy optimization objective does not guarantee approximation of regret. (R2) Additionally, the theoretical analysis assumes access to all possible segments, which weakens its practical implications. (R3)
- **ILP and IVP definitions may limit generalizability**: The preference labels in Table 1 assign a negative signal even when videos or language instructions from different tasks are semantically related (e.g., manipulating a drawer vs. a cabinet). This overlooks shared subgoals and may restrict the model to a carefully selected task distribution, contradicting claims of broad generalizability. (R2)
- **Train-test task split concerns**: The authors do not justify why a particular split of Meta-World tasks was chosen for training/testing. Using the pre-defined ML45 split (or an explicit random split) would improve reproducibility and reduce the risk of selecting a split that favors the proposed method. (R4)
- **Architectural novelty is overstated**: The use of cross-modal attention, while effective, is not a novel architectural contribution by itself. The specific adaptation for the problem should be more precisely defined rather than claiming general novelty. (R4) Figures also have room for improvement: Fig. 2 is standard and conveys limited novelty; Fig. 3 attentions are not clearly visible. (R1)
- **Missing analysis**: (R3 – questions overlapping with missing experiments) Computational cost comparison with approaches like R3M or VIP is not provided. The sensitivity of the model to the quality and diversity of language instructions (e.g., GPT-4V vs. a weaker model) is not tested. (R2) Writing suggestions: Table 1 would benefit from notation clarification (e.g., \(v_i^j\) and \(l^k\)), and the term “language” is used ambiguously in places (e.g., “unseen language” might be misinterpreted as a different spoken language rather than new English instructions).
