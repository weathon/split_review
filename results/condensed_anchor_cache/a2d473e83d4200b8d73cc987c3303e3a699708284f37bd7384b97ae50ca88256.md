## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes CEPBO, a high-dimensional Bayesian optimization (BO) method that uses a Condensing-Expansion Projection technique. At each iteration, a new random projection matrix (Gaussian or hashing) is sampled to project the original space \(\mathbb{R}^D\) into a low-dimensional space \(\mathbb{R}^d\), a GP is built in \(\mathbb{R}^d\), and the acquisition function maximizer is projected back to \(\mathbb{R}^D\) via the transpose of the matrix. Two algorithms result: CEP-REMBO (Gaussian projection) and CEP-HeSBO (hashing projection). Experiments on synthetic and real-world problems show that both algorithms outperform existing embedding-based methods, such as REMBO, HeSBO, and ALEBO.

### Strengths

*   The writing is clear and easy to understand (R1, R3).
*   The method cleverly circumvents the strong “effective dimension” assumption required by many embedding-based BO approaches, which is exciting and could be a significant contribution (R4).
*   Unlike REMBO, HeSBO, and ALEBO (which use a fixed projection matrix for the entire optimization), and even BAxUS/Bounce (which sample only one matrix per subspace), CEPBO generates a new projection matrix every iteration (R1).
*   The paper provides some theoretical analysis and mathematical derivation to support the feasibility of the condensing-expansion projection (R1, R4).
*   The experimental results indicate that the proposed method is competitive and frequently superior on high-dimensional BO problems (R2, R3).
*   The real-world experiments are sufficient and described in detail in the appendix (R4).
*   The research field is significant and the overall structure is relatively clear (R3).

### Weaknesses

*   **Lack of novelty and insufficient explanation of benefits:** The use of low-dimensional random projections is not new, and the paper does not adequately explain why the proposed scheme offers advantages over existing methods (R1, R2). The condensing-expansion technique has been successfully applied in other fields (e.g., federated learning), and the paper reads as a simple migration without articulating unique challenges or innovations specific to Bayesian optimization (R3).
*   **Questionable reverse projection (pre-image):** The mapping back to the original space uses \(A^\top\), which is arbitrary. In general, finding the pre-image is an ill-posed problem. The authors do not justify why they did not use the pseudoinverse of \(A\), and the property that \(\mathbb{E}[A^\top A] = I\) (Eq. (1)) is insufficient to guarantee that the deviation from the true pre-image will be minimal (R1, R2).
*   **Insufficient theory and unresolved hyperparameter selection:**
    *   The analysis (Formulas 2 and 3) only proves feasibility but does not provide a guarantee on the probability of successfully finding the optimal solution within the subspace. This is a key issue (R4).
    *   The selection of the low-dimensional space dimension \(d\) directly affects performance (it determines proximity between \(x\) and \(\tilde{x}\)), yet how to set \(d\) is not discussed (R4, Q3).
    *   The motivation for the scaling factor in the boundary handling of Section 3.4 is unclear. For the two convex projections, a scaling factor is applied but its origin (appears to be \(\sqrt{D}\)) is not justified theoretically and is treated as a fixed parameter without hyperparameter analysis (R1, R4, Q4).
*   **Missing comparisons with recent methods:** The baselines are limited to REMBO (2013), HeSBO (2019), and ALEBO (2020). The paper fails to compare against many recent embedding-based methods (e.g., CoBO, BAxUS, MAVE-BO, DSO) and other non-embedding high-dimensional BO methods (e.g., Vanilla BO, MCTS-VS, TurBO) that are known to be strong (R1, R4). Including BAxUS is specifically required (R1).
*   **Experimental setup is questionable and not thorough enough:**
    *   Only 50 iterations are used in the benchmark function experiments. In Figure 2, ALEBO does not converge on the Holder Table function, yet the results are truncated. This raises concerns about fairness and whether experimental choices were designed to favor CEPBO (R4, Q2).
    *   The real-world test problems have dimensions no greater than 100 (12, 14, 36, 60). This does not convincingly demonstrate the method’s suitability for truly high-dimensional tasks (\(D > 200\)) (R4, Q5).
    *   Experiments with complex feasible domains (e.g., non-symmetric, irregular) are missing, and the proofs assume \(x \in \mathbb{R}^D\) without discussing constraints (R4, Q6, Q7).
    *   When a function possesses an intrinsic effective dimension \(d_e\) and the chosen \(d > d_e\), other methods may outperform CEPBO; such experiments are missing (R4, Q8).
*   **Missing complexity analysis:** The paper motivates CEPBO by stating that high-dimensional BO “can be expensive and infeasible,” but it does not provide a specific complexity analysis or runtime comparisons of the proposed method against standard high-dimensional BO (R3).
*   **Poor manuscript preparation and presentation details:**
    *   Numerous typos: e.g., “as as” in line 144; a bolded subscript \(t\) in Algorithm 1 (line 9); text in Figure 3 is too small and unreadable (R4).
    *   The paper lacks an organization section to improve readability (R3).
*   **Minority reviewer’s more positive view:** While Reviewer 3 (score 5) finds the presentation good and the results promising, the same reviewer also criticizes the lack of novelty and missing complexity analysis. The other three reviewers (score 3) share the major concerns described above.
