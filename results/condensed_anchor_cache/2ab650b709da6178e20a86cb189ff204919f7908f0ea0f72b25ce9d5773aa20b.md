## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies length and compositional generalization in seq-to-seq models (deep sets, simplified one-layer transformers, SSMs, RNNs) under realizability, a strongly limited capacity, and the assumption that the hypothesis minimizes expected risk (infinite data). It proves that under these conditions the learned representations are linearly related to the ground-truth representations, enabling generalization. Negative results show that relaxing the capacity restrictions allows non-generalizing solutions. Experiments on toy examples verify the theory.

### Strengths
- **First provable guarantees** for length and compositional generalization across multiple architectures (deep sets, transformers, SSMs, RNNs); the theoretical framework is novel and a pioneering contribution to the ML community. [R1, R4]
- **Clear definitions** of length generalization and compositional generalization, with well-structured exposition and effective figures. [R2, R1]
- **Generality**: Results are extended to various scenarios, including $C^1$-diffeomorphisms and discrete tokens, and the paper examines a range of models. [R2, R3]
- **Inclusion of both positive and negative results**; negative results convincingly show that when capacity restrictions are weakened, generalization fails. [R3, R4]
- One reviewer (score 6) particularly highlighted the novelty of defining and analyzing length generalization and the demonstration that without capacity restrictions generalization is impossible; this reviewer found the assumptions reasonable and the study of several architectures valuable. [R4]
- The rationale for focusing on simple limited-capacity models is explained convincingly. [R2]
- Proofs appear correct and solid. [R1]

### Weaknesses
- **Overly simplified models** that depart substantially from practice:
  - The decoder-only transformer is modeled as $w(\sum_{j=1}^i \frac{1}{i} \psi(x_i, x_j))$ with a single-layer bijective perceptron; this cannot capture the softmax operation and applies only to single-layer transformers. [R1]
  - The paper does not study standard attention; this should be made clearer in the abstract/intro. [R3]
  - The transformer result assumes positional encodings where tokens far enough apart do not interact, which does not hold for practical positional encodings; this limitation needs better justification or extension. [R4]
  - It is unclear why such simplified models bear any relation to practice; negative results show that even slight expansions of the tight capacity restrictions break generalization, suggesting the simplified architectures have no chance of applying to real settings. [R3]
- **Strong, unrealistic assumptions about the learning setup**:
  - The analysis assumes the hypothesis minimizes **expected risk exactly** (equation 1), which is much stronger than typical PAC assumptions and rare in practice (non-convex hypothesis class, iterative optimization like gradient descent rarely yields a global minimizer). This assumption needs further justification. [R2, R4, R1]
  - There is **no consideration of statistical issues**; the ERM approach effectively assumes infinite data, combined with full-support coverage over continuous variables, to pin down the learned function. It is not clear whether the arguments extend to finite datasets, even large ones. [R3, R1]
  - Theorems are built under expected risk minimization, deviating from the practical scenario of finite samples. [R1]
- **Coverage and support assumptions are very strong**:
  - For transformers, the assumption requires full support over the joint distribution of each pair of tokens, which is even stronger than marginal support. Relaxing these assumptions is unclear. [R3]
  - The discrete tokens extension uses “regular closedness of the support” (Theorems 6,9,11,12) even though the support of discrete tokens is not regular closed, making the “equality almost everywhere → equality everywhere” arguments potentially unnecessary. [R2]
- **Results are not surprising given the assumptions**: with a bijective single-layer perceptron, the equation $A\sum \psi(x_j) = B\sum \phi(x_j)$ follows directly, making the core conclusion expected and diminishing novelty and impact. [R1]
- **Overly optimistic perspective**: In practice length/compositional generalization is very difficult, yet the paper frames the problem as “length generalization happens and we want to explain it”; the negative results may be the more realistic takeaway. [R3]
- **Experiments are weak and unconvincing**:
  - Conducted only on simplified models; real-world experiments are needed. [R1]
  - Plots show trends with very small y-axis errors, making the claim that length generalization always occurs unclear; baselines that do not length generalize would help. [R3]
  - The experimental section lacks a clear objective and does not provide strong empirical support for the theoretical claims; it mainly verifies the theory with no deeper analysis. [R4]
- **Presentation and clarity issues**:
  - Definitions lack precision: e.g., Definition 1 does not clarify what “zero generalization error” means or whether “trained on sequences up to length $T$” means zero error on $P(T)$. [R4]
  - Theorems 3 and 4 reference Assumptions 12 and 15 that are deferred to the appendix; these should be stated in the main text, or informal statements should be provided. [R2, R4]
  - Proofs contain repetitive copy-paste and potential errors: page 28 line 1483 incorrectly references “equation 31 of equation 30” (should be “equation 67 of equation 66”); Appendix C.3.1 says “Assumption 2” when it likely means “Assumption 15”; equation 66 has $\psi$ and $\phi$ swapped; equation 108 would be more readable with derivative notation; the formula on page 37 line 1987 should have line breaks and explanation. [R2]
  - Reasoning from equation 30 to 33 is unclear; it may be possible to derive equation 33 directly by setting $x_1 = x_2$ in equation 30. [R2]
- **Several unanswered questions that overlap with criticisms**:
  - Can the optimization process be incorporated into the theoretical framework? [R1]
  - What is the main difference from the proof in Wiedemer et al. [1]? [R1]
  - For deep sets, why can’t $A\psi(x) = B\phi(x)$ be derived directly from $\omega(\psi(x)) = \rho(\phi(x))$ for $t=1$? [R2]
  - How realistic is Assumption 18? Provide concrete examples. [R2]
  - Can the results be extended to deep, multi-layer models? [R4]
  - Can the negative results be extended to general classes with bounded VC dimension instead of assuming no constraints at all? [R4]
