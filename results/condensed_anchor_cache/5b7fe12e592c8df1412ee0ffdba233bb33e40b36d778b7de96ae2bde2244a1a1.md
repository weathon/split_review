## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes FedQ-Advantage, a model-free federated Q-learning algorithm for tabular episodic MDPs. It uses reference-advantage decomposition for variance reduction, event-triggered communication with heterogeneous conditions, and policy switching (including optional forced synchronization). The authors prove that FedQ-Advantage achieves near-optimal regret (matching the information bound up to a logarithmic factor) and logarithmic communication cost. Multiple reviewers described it as the first model-free federated RL algorithm to attain both properties simultaneously, and noted near-linear regret speedup compared to single-agent learning. Synthetic experiments are provided.

### Strengths
- **Theoretically rigorous**: Proofs are thorough, well-structured, and the mathematical treatment is solid (reviewers 1,2,3,6). The analysis covers event-triggered communication, reference-advantage decomposition, and heterogeneous triggering conditions.
- **Novel combination of techniques**: The paper introduces heterogeneous event-triggered communication and forced synchronization in a federated RL setting, showing creativity in reducing communication while maintaining performance (reviewers 1,2,3,6). The reference-advantage decomposition for variance reduction is effectively integrated into the federated framework.
- **SOTA guarantees**: FedQ-Advantage is the first model-free federated algorithm to achieve both almost optimal regret (approaching the information bound) and logarithmic communication cost (reviewers 1,3,5,6). Communication cost matches the best policy-switching cost in single-agent Q-learning (reviewer 6).
- **Empirical support**: Synthetic experiments match the theoretical regret predictions and demonstrate benefits over baselines (reviewers 4,5,6). This strengthens the credibility of the theoretical results.
- **Significance for the community**: The trade-off between regret and communication efficiency is a core challenge in federated RL, and the paper makes a valuable contribution (reviewers 1,3,6). The algorithm is designed for distributed RL tasks.

### Weaknesses
- **Poor readability and notation density**: Multiple reviewers (1,4,5,6) found the paper extremely hard to read due to excessive notation, lack of intuition, and unclear motivations for design choices. Specific issues:
  - The “Updates of estimated value functions and policies” section is nearly 90% equations with no narrative (reviewer 1).
  - Equation 4 is referenced on page 5 before it appears on page 7, making it difficult to follow (reviewer 6).
  - The stage/round notation, especially the meaning of \(k_h^t\) and Figure 1 (including \(k_{12}\) and \(k_{22}\)), is confusing and not clearly explained (reviewers 2,5). The logic of how stage \(t\) is composed of rounds \(k_h^t, k_h^t+1, \dots, k_h^{t+1}-1\) needs clarification (reviewer 5).
  - Long in-line equations in the main text (e.g., Item 4, page 7) lack accompanying explanations (reviewer 6). The algorithm description is difficult to follow overall.
- **Disagreement on novelty and communication cost**:
  - A minority reviewer (rating 3) considered the integration of existing techniques (variance reduction [4,5], unaligned stage design [5]) into federated Q-learning to be incremental, despite the majority praising it as novel and SOTA (reviewers 1,3,5,6). This reviewer also argued that the communication cost \(O(SAH^2)\) or \(O(MSAH^2)\) is not particularly low compared to other federated Q-learning algorithms that achieve \(O(H)\) communication rounds (citing [2,3]), contrasting with the majority view that logarithmic communication is a major strength. The paper should better contextualize its communication guarantees against these baselines (reviewer 4).
- **Insufficient analysis of event-triggered communication and forced synchronization**:
  - The event-triggered communication strategy lacks analysis under highly variable agent performance (reviewer 2). The interaction between forced synchronization and the exploration–exploitation tradeoff is not explained; conditions where forced synchronization is beneficial or detrimental need rigorous analysis (reviewer 2). The interplay between the three mechanisms (communication triggering, policy switching, forced synchronization) is not clearly explored (reviewer 2).
- **Privacy concerns**: The algorithm requires agents to send many estimates to the server besides value estimates, which may expose too much information about local datasets (reviewer 4).
- **Missing comparisons and limited related work**:
  - The related work does not adequately separate value-based and policy-gradient federated methods, and the claim that “most existing model-free federated algorithms do not actively update exploration policies” is imprecise. Federal algorithms with function approximation (e.g., [1,2,3]) incorporate active exploration strategies and deserve a fuller comparison (reviewer 5).
  - Lack of comparison to model-based FedRL methods in terms of convergence speed and communication complexity (reviewer 3).
  - The paper does not explore the general trade-off between regret and communication complexity beyond the proposed algorithm (reviewer 3).
- **Limited scope**: Results are restricted to tabular MDPs with discrete state-action spaces; no function approximation. Questions remain about extending the key components (event-triggered communication, policy switching, heterogeneous conditions, reference-advantage decomposition) to linear or nonlinear function approximation settings (reviewers 3,5).
- **Misleading claim of “non-martingale concentration”**: The paper states it handles non-martingale concentration, but the actual approach approximates a non-martingale sequence with a martingale via a designed heterogeneous trigger condition that forces communication. The concentration argument is then standard martingale convergence. This is a straightforward technique, not a true non-martingale concentration inequality; renaming is suggested to avoid confusion (reviewer 6).
- **Unexplained empirical gap**: In Figure 3, Concurrent UCB-Advantage (theoretically equivalent to FedQ-Advantage) shows larger regret than FedQ-Advantage, but the reason is not discussed (reviewer 5). The corresponding communication rounds for this comparison are also missing (reviewer 5).
