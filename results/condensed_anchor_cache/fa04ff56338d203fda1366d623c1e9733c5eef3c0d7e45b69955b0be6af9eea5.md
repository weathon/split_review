## Merged Review

### Summary
Three reviewers evaluate LOGRL, a goal-conditioned offline RL method for lead optimization that uses reaction rules from USPTO-MIT as actions and beam search for generation. Scores are 5 (Reviewer 1), 3, 3 (Reviewers 2 & 3). All note clear writing and the novelty of using complete target molecules as goals to avoid similarity metrics in training. However, the majority (Reviewers 2 & 3) find the experimental comparison weak, the baselines inadequate, and the practical relevance to lead optimization unconvincing. Reviewer 1 is more positive on the method’s framing but agrees that the experiments are preliminary.

### Strengths
- Novelty: The core idea of using goal-conditioned RL with complete molecules as goals to remove dependency on similarity metrics during training is considered novel by all reviewers (R1: “main novelty”; R3: “to the best of my knowledge … novel”).
- Clarity: The paper is well-written and clearly presents the method and background (R1: “presented in a very clear way”; R2: “well-written and presents clearly”; R3: “method section is well explained with minor exceptions”).
- Use of reaction rules: Offline RL with reaction rules from USPTO-MIT ensures synthesizability of generated molecules, an important practical consideration (R1, R3).
- Offline RL choice: R1 notes that offline RL avoids sparse rewards in navigating chemical space.
- Comprehensive related work: Acknowledged by R2.
- Improvement over baselines: The method outperforms the two RL baselines on Tanimoto similarity and QED (R3: “seems to improve upon their baseline on all experiments”).

### Weaknesses
- Insufficient experimental evaluation:
  - Only two RL baselines (S model, Q+S model) are compared, both online‑RL methods; no comparison with state‑of‑the‑art generative optimization methods or standardized benchmarks (R1, R3). R3 suggests looking at benchmarks such as [1] and scaffold-hopping methods [3].
  - Metrics are limited to Tanimoto similarity and QED; no evaluation of multi‑property optimization, binding affinity retention, or other drug‑relevant properties (R1, R3). R1: “The evaluation metrics used … do not show if the proposed method can optimize any molecular properties or at least retain high binding affinity.”
  - The authors claim “the search strategy separates property optimization from training” but do not present property optimization results (R1).
  - Experiments are run only once, without confidence intervals; results for online RL baselines could be sensitive to random initialization (R1).
- Unfair or unclear comparison:
  - LOGRL is an offline RL method trained on expert data, while baselines are online RL (PPO) and may not use the same expert dataset; this gives LOGRL an unfair advantage (R2). R2 asks whether baselines also use expert data.
  - R2 suggests that with abundant expert data, imitation learning might outperform offline RL; the choice of offline RL over supervised learning is not justified.
  - R1 notes the absence of comparison to the conceptually similar Molpher model [1].
- Practical relevance for lead optimization:
  - The training objective is to make molecules more similar to known drugs, which may be undesirable because novel structures are needed to avoid patented space. Real lead optimization aims to improve properties without harming binding affinity (R1: “I am unsure if I understand how this model could be used in practice”).
  - R3 states that lead optimization is far more complex than optimizing only QED or similarity, and the paper does not demonstrate a technique for that general task.
  - R3: The claim “the results shown induce confidence in our model to be able to generate lead candidates that satisfy multiple properties” (Sec. 6) is too strong for the experiments performed.
- Methodological concerns:
  - Policy gradient is on‑policy; using it in an offline RL setup is questionable without justification (R2).
  - No mechanism to ensure output diversity; potential mode collapse (R2: “there doesn't appear to be a regularizer that can control all possible outputs directed toward the target molecule”).
  - The second claimed contribution (“search strategy”) appears to be simply generating many molecules and sorting by score, which is not novel (R3).
  - R3: The description of how reaction rules are mined and the reaction dataset are insufficient; more detail is needed.
  - R3: The last paragraph of Sec. 4.6 (“we found that under the condition G(a_t leads to g)…”) is hard to understand.
- Minor: Typo in Sec. 4.5, line 3: “batchwe” (R2).

### Reviewer Disagreement
- Reviewer 1 is the only positive evaluator (score 5, confidence 4), finding the method novel, clear, and motivated. Reviewer 1 rates soundness 2 and contribution 2, acknowledging preliminary experiments.
- Reviewers 2 and 3 (both score 3) are more critical: R2 rates soundness 1, contribution 1; R3 rates soundness 1, contribution 2. They consider the experiments weak, the baselines insufficient, and the practical applicability for lead optimization not demonstrated.
- R1 questions significance but does not focus on the offline/online RL comparison or diversity issue; R2 and R3 raise those as core weaknesses.
