## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper demonstrates that digital twins (ANNs trained to predict single-neuron responses in mouse visual cortex) fail to capture population-level response geometry—specifically the eigenspectrum differentiability and hierarchical discriminability observed experimentally. Testing dataset choice, reliability selection, network architecture, covariance-based loss, and data augmentation all failed to produce differentiable representations. Only dropout regularization at sufficiently high rates produced differentiable representations and improved hierarchical discriminability, albeit at the cost of reduced single-neuron prediction accuracy. The study highlights the need for population-level evaluation and suggests regularization as a path forward, though quantitative gaps remain. Reviewer assessments ranged from 5 to 8, with strong agreement on the question’s importance but notable differences in perceived clarity, generality, and actionability.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles an important gap in computational neuroscience by focusing on population-level neural response geometry rather than only single-neuron predictions.
- It is reasonably clear and well-written, accessible to both neuroscience and machine learning audiences (though one reviewer found clarity lacking).
- The study meticulously replicates key experimental findings and tests a broad set of training procedures, including dataset variation, architecture changes, reliability selection, and training objectives.
- It is extremely important to point out that current state-of-the-art models fail to capture population-level characteristics and to identify what aspects of training can correct this.
- The paper provides a fairly straightforward way to mitigate some shortcomings: dropout regularization, which is useful for developing more biologically realistic models.
- The research sets the stage for developing more accurate digital twins by emphasizing principles of robustness and generalization.
- A more positive reviewer (rating 8) praised the exposure of model shortcomings and the simple mitigation via dropout, calling it very useful.

### Weaknesses
- The results are limited by the number of models and datasets available for testing; this limits actionability.
- Only one regularization technique (dropout) was tested; it is unclear if the observed effects are specific to dropout or if other regularization methods (e.g., weight decay, batch normalization) would yield similar results, limiting the generality of conclusions about regularization. This missing comparison also extends to whether results hold for task-trained models.
- Despite improvements with dropout, models still show quantitative differences from experimental data, especially in discriminability measures and visual area hierarchy, warranting deeper analysis.
- There is a trade-off where enhancing population-level representation reduces single-neuron prediction accuracy, raising practical concerns. The paper does not test a combined objective that optimizes both simultaneously.
- The focus on a shared-core architecture limits insights; exploring more varied architectures (e.g., area-specific modules, varied receptive field sizes) could better reflect the visual cortex’s hierarchical nature. A more critical reviewer (rating 5) emphasized these remaining gaps.
- The study relies on computational simulations; direct physiological validation would substantiate the findings.
- The paper is not sufficiently self-contained. The model architecture (described only in the Appendix) was hard to follow—a diagram would help. Background theory on differentiability (Stringer et al., 2019) lacks intuitive explanation, e.g., why the cutoff is 1 + 2/d.
- Training only on correlations between neurons (covariance loss) loses single-neuron predictivity; a combined objective was not attempted.
- The claim that digital twins fail to capture hierarchical relationships is overstated; only one aspect (discriminability) is shown to be missing, not all relevant aspects of hierarchy.
- Several conceptual and experimental questions remain unanswered, overlapping with missing experiments, ablations, or explanations:
  - Does the dropout result hold for task-trained models?
  - Can multi-objective optimization balance single-neuron accuracy and population-level representation?
  - Would adding biological features like spiking noise, synaptic dynamics, or neural variability improve population properties?
  - Would area-specific modules or varied receptive field sizes capture hierarchical processing better?
  - How generalizable are the models to different visual stimuli given non-differentiable representations for gratings?
  - Would spiking neural network models help capture population response geometry and address trial-to-trial variability?
  - How is it possible for a digital twin to predict single-neuron responses accurately yet fail on population structure? (This points to a missing explanation.)
  - Why does the model lack increasing receptive field sizes (standard in CNNs)?
  - What is the intuition behind the formula for CC_max?
