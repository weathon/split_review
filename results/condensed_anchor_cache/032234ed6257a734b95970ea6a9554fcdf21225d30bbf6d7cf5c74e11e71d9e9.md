## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies correlation clustering in the streaming setting (edge-labeled complete graph) and proposes the first learning-augmented algorithms for the problem. The algorithm uses a predictor that provides pairwise dissimilarity estimates \(d_{uv}\) and achieves a \(\min\{2.06\beta,\,3\}+\varepsilon\) approximation in \(O(n\log n)\) space under a \(\beta\)-level predictor quality measure. It adapts ideas from Cambus et al. (SODA’24) and Chakrabarty and Makarychev (NeurIPS’23) for the streaming phase and from CMSY (STOC’15) for post-processing. The algorithm is simpler than the recent \(1.847\)-approximation streaming algorithm of Cohen-Addad et al. (STOC’24), but loses in approximation quality unless predictions are very good (\(\beta<1.5\)). Experiments on small synthetic and real-world datasets (thousands of vertices) show the algorithm outperforms pivot-based baselines and is comparable to agreement-decomposition-based algorithms when the predictor is accurate.

### Strengths
- **Problem and direction are important and natural.** Correlation clustering is a fundamental problem. The learning-augmented approach is a promising and practically motivated direction that had not been explored for streaming correlation clustering before; several reviewers are surprised it was not studied earlier (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- **Clean, simple algorithm.** The algorithm is much simpler than the state-of-the-art streaming algorithm by Cohen-Addad et al. (STOC’24), which is challenging to implement and restricted to insertion-only streams. The simplicity makes it potentially practical (R1, R2, R3).
- **Rigorous theoretical analysis with advanced tools.** Despite the simplicity of the algorithm, the theoretical analysis is sound and uses nontrivial techniques. The paper provides a clear approximation guarantee that degrades gracefully: when predictions are good, the approximation is better than the well-known \(3\)-approximation of Pivot; when predictions are poor, the algorithm never performs worse than \(3\) (R1, R2, R3, R5).
- **Empirical validation.** Experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate that the oracle improves performance over non-learning baselines significantly, and the results are competitive with more complex algorithms (R2, R4). The experiments support the theoretical findings (R1, R5).
- **Generally well-written and organized.** Most reviewers find the paper well-written and the presentation clear (R2, R5). The theoretical results are presented in a structured manner.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and reliance on prior work.** The core ideas are largely adapted from existing approaches (Cambus et al. SODA’24, Chakrabarty and Makarychev NeurIPS’23 for the streaming phase; CMSY STOC’15 for post-processing). The baseline \(3\)-approximation comes for free from prior work. The main theoretical improvement is marginal and depends entirely on prediction quality. Some reviewers see some novelty in combining these ideas with predictions and in the post-processing adaptation, but overall novelty is limited (R1, R4; contrasted by R2, R3, R5 who credit originality).
- **The \(\beta\)-level predictor definition is artificial and lacks connection to practice.** The notion does not reflect natural data properties or standard prediction models. The result essentially says “if predictions are good then the algorithm is good,” without concrete examples of what realistic similarity functions or prediction algorithms would satisfy the definition. The relation between the edge labels and the dissimilarity values \(d_{uv}\) is not thoroughly discussed; concrete examples are needed (R3, R4).
- **Practical concerns about the learning-augmented framework in streaming.** The streaming model targets space efficiency, but training a predictor (e.g., a GNN) typically requires storing almost all edges, which defeats the purpose. The paper does not explain how to implement the oracle efficiently in a streaming context, nor does it address the overhead of training time, memory, or satisfying triangle inequality constraints. The predictors used in experiments (noisy predictor assuming known optimal clustering, spectral clustering requiring the full adjacency matrix and eigenvector computation) are impractical for streaming settings. Alternative realistic predictors or a discussion of these issues is missing (R1, R4, R5). One reviewer asks whether the algorithm can support other predictors like binary classification (R5, question).
- **The improved approximation factor is weak in practice.** To outperform the simple \(3\)-approximation of Pivot, the prediction quality must satisfy \(\beta < 1.5\), i.e., very high accuracy. The experiments confirm that the improvement appears only when \(\beta\) is low. The simplification of the algorithm comes at the cost of losing the \(1.847\) approximation of Cohen-Addad et al.; this trade-off is not sufficiently justified, and a rigorous comparison with that algorithm is missing (R1, R4).
- **Streaming model handling is weak.** The algorithm avoids subtle streaming issues (e.g., edge arrival order) by using sparsification. It is not clear how to recover the original clustering from the sparsified clustering. The algorithm assumes \(n\) is known in advance, which may add a logarithmic factor to complexity. These points should be addressed (R2, R3). One reviewer asks explicitly how to go back from sparse clustering to the original clustering (R2, question).
- **Experiments are insufficient.** The datasets are small (thousands of vertices), where streaming algorithms may not be needed. No running time comparisons are provided, and the overhead introduced by the predictors is not measured. The offline and insertion-only algorithms are presented as baselines, but they are less general than the dynamic setting, so the comparison is weak (R5).
- **Presentation weaknesses.** The optimization objective is never formally defined (R3). Figures have text that is too small (R5). The paper would benefit from a table summarizing theoretical results and comparisons with existing work (R5). The key insights of the post-processing analysis are not clearly articulated; they only become clear in the appendix (R4). Presenting high-level ideas before detailed proofs and moving related work to a separate section would improve readability (R1).
- **Questions overlapping with criticisms (included above):**
  - Clarify the technical improvements over Cohen-Addad et al. (STOC’24) (R1) – overlaps with novelty trade-off.
  - Provide concrete examples of \(d_{uv}\) and the optimal clustering (R3) – overlaps with missing connection to practice.
  - How to efficiently implement the oracle (R4) – overlaps with practical concerns.
  - Discuss the relation to the BCMT (SODA’23) \((1+\varepsilon)\)-approximation (impractical time) and its implications for the practical claims of the paper (R4) – not covered elsewhere; could be added as a missing discussion point. (We include this as it is a specific comparison request.)
  - Can the algorithm support other predictors like binary classification (R5) – overlaps with prediction model discussion.
