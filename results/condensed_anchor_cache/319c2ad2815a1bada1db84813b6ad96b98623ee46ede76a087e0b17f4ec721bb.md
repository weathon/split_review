## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper revisits backdoor-based evaluation of saliency-based representation visualization (SRV) methods, revealing implementation limitations and unreliable nature due to trigger generalization of existing watermarks. It proposes a generalization-limited backdoor watermark (GLBW) formulated as a min-max problem to find worst-case potential triggers and minimize their effects. The method is evaluated on CIFAR-10 and GTSRB, comparing against baselines including BWTP. The work aims to provide a more faithful XAI evaluation.

### Strengths
- Addresses the challenging and important problem of automatically evaluating explanation methods without human annotation.
- Provides a critical analysis of limitations of existing backdoor-based XAI evaluations, particularly the unreliable nature caused by trigger generalization and implementation issues. These findings are non-trivial and useful for practical evaluation.
- Cleverly links backdoor attacks and model interpretability; although the perspective of using watermarks for evaluation was introduced in prior work, the further non-trivial exploration is meaningful and valuable.
- The proposed GLBW method is novel and reasonable, with a clear design philosophy (min-max optimization).
- Experiments are solid and comprehensive: include multiple trigger inversion techniques to assess generalization, detailed comparisons on CIFAR-10 and GTSRB, and extensive appendix with datasets, models, training, and evaluation settings.
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow; code will be released.

### Weaknesses
- Several reviewers questioned whether trigger generalization is necessary for XAI evaluation. One reviewer (score 5) argued that if adding a watermark changes the prediction, the saliency should highlight the watermark; potential triggers with different visualization but zero cross-entropy loss may not be problematic for the intended use. The paper should clarify more strongly why these potential triggers matter for evaluating saliency maps.
- Multiple reviewers raised the discrepancy between simple backdoor triggers (small patches, low-level patterns) and real objects (complex, high-level semantics). A saliency method that highlights a simple trigger well might not indicate good performance on real-world features. The authors need to discuss this discrepancy and its implications.
- The explainability metric is based on backdoored patterns; it is unclear why good performance on such patterns serves as a reliable reference for complex features in real images.
- The paper lacks comparisons with state-of-the-art SRV evaluation methods (non-backdoor approaches). Only a vanilla backdoor baseline is used, making it difficult to assess the true contribution relative to established techniques.
- Empirical evidence for superiority over traditional backdoor methods is insufficient. For instance: (a) No direct quantitative IoU comparison between the minimum bounding box approach and the proposed method to support claims of inaccuracy. (b) No clear empirical study showing the negative impact of inconsistencies when taking absolute values of gradients across SRV methods; the justification for this change needs stronger empirical support.
- Several details require elaboration: (1) Differences between GLBW and BWTP in terms of formulas are not clearly distinguished. (2) The selection of M pixels (top saliency pixels) may artificially inflate IoU because the saliency map is often larger than the small trigger, making the union small and IoU high—this is not fundamentally better. (3) The method treats universal adversarial perturbations and triggers as equivalent, but their characteristics differ; more explanation is needed on why this is acceptable.
- The explanation of BWTP and GLBW relies heavily on the appendix, making the main text hard to follow at first sight.
- Minor corrections: In Eq (1), L receives y in {0,...,K-1} for benign/backdoor loss but y in R^K for penalty loss; in Eq (2), |b'| is not defined.
- Questions that overlap with criticisms should be addressed: How do potential triggers affect watermark-based saliency evaluation? How to evaluate which saliency evaluation metric is better? Why is trigger generalization important for XAI evaluation (provide more discussion in appendix)? Provide more ablation studies isolating each proposed change (e.g., effect of adaptive optimization, min-max formulation, absolute gradient normalization).
