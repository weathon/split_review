## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers describe DRDO as a method that combines explicit reward modeling with preference learning, training an oracle model to provide reward signals that are distilled into a policy via a loss combining a reward difference term and a contrastive log-unlikelihood (focal) term. The goal is to handle non-deterministic or noisy human preferences, which challenge DPO’s Bradley-Terry formulation. Experiments on Ultrafeedback and TL;DR (with CNN Daily out-of-distribution evaluation) show improved win rates over DPO and e-DPO. However, reviewers note that the approach is largely an extension of Fisch et al. (2024) with an added unlikelihood term, and opinions on novelty and theoretical soundness vary substantially: one reviewer (R1, score 6) finds the work a relevant, simple extension; another (R5, score 3, soundness 1) considers the theoretical analysis flawed and the motivation insufficient.

### Strengths
- **Relevance and simplicity**: The paper addresses a timely problem (noisy preferences in DPO) and proposes a clean extension to existing reward distillation methods (R1).
- **Empirical breadth**: Experiments are extensive across multiple datasets (Ultrafeedback, TL;DR), model sizes (e.g., Mistral 7B, Llama-2 7B), and settings (deterministic vs. non-deterministic preferences, OOD with CNN Daily). The inclusion of temperature variation and GPT-4-based win-rate evaluations supports the robustness claims (R1, R3, R5).
- **Mechanistic insight**: The explanation of how the contrastive log-unlikelihood term adjusts gradients toward the winning response (Eq. 6) clarifies the algorithm’s behavior (R1).
- **Reference-free advantage**: DRDO avoids a reference model, which may reduce computational overhead (R2).
- **Theoretical grounding**: The paper provides lemmas and proofs to motivate the approach, and the core intuition—that binary 0-1 labels fail to capture preference uncertainty—is sound (R3, R4).
- **Significant performance gaps**: Empirical results show clear improvements over DPO and e-DPO, especially on ambiguous preference subsets (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear variable definitions and notation**: The definition of key terms is missing or ambiguous.  
  - It is not stated how the policy’s reward estimates \(\hat{r}\) (or \(\hat{r}_1, \hat{r}_2\) in the loss) are parameterized—whether they come from the policy model or the oracle (R1, R2).  
  - The variable \(\alpha\) appears in both Eq. 5 and Eq. 6 without clarification of whether it is the same hyperparameter (R1).  
  - The loss term \(\log(y_w)\) in Eq. 5 is ambiguous: is it \(\log(y_w \mid x)\) or \(\log(y_w, x)\)? This could affect the oracle training when \(y_w\) and \(y_l\) are close (R1).  
  - No pseudo-code or algorithmic flowchart is provided, making the overall procedure hard to follow (R4).  
- **Insufficient attribution and overclaimed novelty**:  
  - The reward distillation loss is directly taken from Fisch et al. (2024); the paper does not clearly differentiate its own loss landscape or contributions (R1, R2, R3).  
  - The main novelty—the contrastive unlikelihood term—is only briefly explained in the main text and lacks detailed comparison to the standard DPO gradient (R1).  
  - Lemma 1 restates known results (from references [1] and [2]), and Lemma 2’s proof in Appendix A.2 appears to contain mathematical errors (e.g., a stated equation that should approach 1 is not correctly derived); these issues are critical because they motivate the entire method (R5).  
  - One reviewer (R5, score 3) finds the motivation for tackling “non-deterministic” preferences (where \(p(y \succ y’) \approx 1/2\)) unconvincing, noting that pluralistic preference diversity—rather than mere noise—is a more compelling justification.
- **Missing ablations to isolate contributions**:  
  - It is impossible to determine whether gains come from improved reward distillation or the unlikelihood term. A simple ablation (e.g., replacing the first term in Eq. 6 with a standard DPO objective) is needed (R1).  
  - Ablation studies on the focal (contrastive log-unlikelihood) component alone, and on the reward-difference term alone, are absent (R2, R5).  
  - No comparison to IPO, another reference-free alternative to DPO, is provided (R5).
- **OOD experiment and data coverage concerns**:  
  - The OOD setting (CNN Daily articles in the TL;DR dataset) is unclearly motivated: does it test prompt shift, response shift, or both? A more systematic evaluation (e.g., a plot of performance gap vs. OOD amount) is expected (R3).  
  - The “oracle” is trained from limited preference data; its coverage may be insufficient to truly act as an oracle, especially under low-data-coverage assumptions (Assumption 1). How knowledge distillation overcomes limited coverage is not explained (R4).  
  - Table 1 shows nearly identical performance gaps across the “clear” (hc/he) and “fuzzy” (lc/le) preference subsets, suggesting that either the partition is uninformative or the gains stem from something other than handling ambiguity (R4).
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity and complexity**:  
  - The focal loss introduces two additional hyperparameters (\(\alpha\) and \(\gamma\)), and tuning them—especially for OOD settings—is challenging and risks overfitting to a particular evaluation distribution (R1, R3).  
  - A sensitivity analysis is missing (R1).
- **Computational overhead**: While DRDO avoids a reference model, the cost of training and maintaining an oracle model is not compared to DPO or e-DPO; practical efficiency claims are unsubstantiated (R2).  
- **Presentation deficiencies**: Terminology (“reward difference”, “contrastive log-unlikelihood”) is used without clear intuition or theoretical connection to preference ambiguity (R2). The paper’s narrative suddenly introduces oracle knowledge distillation without explaining how it directly addresses the stated limitation of DPO (the underfitting/overfitting due to 0-1 labels) (R4). Reviewers note poor presentation scores (2/4 by R2 and R4).
