## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Tabular Foundation Models (TabFMs) by fine-tuning a pretrained LLM (LLaMA-2-7B) using Generative Tabular Learning (GTL) on a collection of 115 public tabular datasets. The method represents table rows as text with column and task descriptions, and uses next-token prediction plus a numerical reconstruction loss. The model is evaluated in zero-shot, few-shot in-context learning, and fine-tuning settings. Reviewers note the promising direction but raise substantial concerns about evaluation scope, methodology, and claims.

### Strengths
- **Goal and impact**: The ambition to create a tabular foundation model is noteworthy; tabular data is widely used and improvements are important (R1, R4).
- **Dataset contribution**: The collected set of 115 datasets is a valuable resource for future research (R2, R4).
- **Simplicity and clarity**: The method is simple (good sense); the paper is mostly clear and easy to follow (R2, R3, R4). Reviewer 2 particularly appreciated the transparent analysis in Appendix A.4.
- **Novelty**: The idea of fine-tuning LLMs like LLaMA for general-purpose tabular tasks via generative modeling is original and interesting (R1, R3).
- **Promising few-shot results**: Table 1 shows that LLaMA-GTL clearly outperforms vanilla LLaMA and is competitive with GPT-4 in the few-shot/zero-shot regimes (R2, R4). Reviewer 4 found these results “quite promising” and “exciting.”
- **Numerical reconstruction loss**: The added numerical loss appears to help adapt language models to numerical data (R1).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited evaluation scope and simplistic benchmarks** (R1, R2, R3, R4)
   - Table 2 uses only 9 tasks, 0 regression tasks, maximum dataset size ~50K, 2 trivial (100% accuracy) and 3 very simple tasks where logistic regression performs well. The proposed LLaMA-GTL vs. vanilla LLaMA shows only 2 wins, 6 ties, 1 loss, and is the only best solution on 2 tasks (R2). The small gap between logistic regression and XGBoost suggests the datasets are too simple to be representative of real-world complexity (R4).
   - These limitations make the paper’s positioning as a “foundation model” with “profound understanding and universal capabilities” premature (R2). The scope should be narrowed to few-shot classification, and the title/abstract/claims should reflect that (R2, R3).
   - Additional experiments on newer or more challenging datasets are needed to rule out memorization by the LLM, especially since many benchmark datasets existed before LLaMA’s cutoff (R1).

2. **Lack of comparisons to non-LLM tabular foundation models** (R1, R2)
   - Baselines such as UniTabE and XTab (which also pretrain on multiple tables without LLMs) are discussed in related work but not compared in few-shot experiments (R1). This comparison is essential to understand the added value of the LLM backbone.

3. **Prompt and shot sensitivity not measured** (R1, R2)
   - Table 1 results vary considerably across shot counts (e.g., GPT-4 on Diabetes), and LLMs are known to be sensitive to prompt formatting. No standard deviation over shots or prompt variations is reported, making it hard to draw robust conclusions (R1). The effect of the rich prompt template vs. simpler formats (e.g., “List” format from TabLLM) is not ablated; this matters because prompts strongly affect performance (R2).

4. **Unclear contributions of individual GTL components** (R2, R3)
   - The method introduces multiple changes from prior work (TabLLM): new backbone (LLaMA), new prompt template, language modeling loss, numerical loss. It is not clear which components are critical. Is a 7B model necessary, or would a smaller LLM suffice? The impact of the numerical loss and the prompt template should be isolated (R2).
   - The claim that “in-context learning doesn’t help” is presented as a finding, but this is actually a limitation – foundation models typically benefit from ICL, and the lack of benefit needs deeper investigation (R3).

5. **Requirement for semantic feature and task descriptions** (R2, R4)
   - To apply TabFM to new tasks, users need GPT-4 to generate descriptions and then manually correct them – a significant burden compared to traditional tabular models that work with raw features (R2). This is a limitation, not an advantage, because many real-world tasks have anonymous or non-semantic feature names (R4). The model may also be brittle to feature scaling (e.g., heights in cm vs. inches) (R4).
   - No experiments are provided to evaluate robustness to noisy, irrelevant, or non-informative features – a common tabular scenario (R4).

6. **Overblown claims and missing limitations** (R2, R4)
   - Claims about surpassing GPT-4 should be qualified with a justification that GPT-4 is a strong baseline for this scope; otherwise, the accent is misleading (R2).
   - The “Limitations” paragraph lists future work rather than actual limitations of the method and analysis. It should be reorganized to first state limitations (R2).
   - In Table 2, baselines LR, LightGBM, and XGBoost are fitted without hyperparameter tuning; tuning via CV often improves them, so the reported performance may be underestimated (R4).

7. **Table clarity and interpretability** (R3)
   - Tables 1 and 2 are massive and hard to parse; smaller tables or figures would help. The indices in Section 3 are confusing (R3). Specific examples from Table 1: on Bank, LLaMA-GTL reaches AUC 85 with 0 contexts but ICL doesn’t help; on C. Hous. and Car it underperforms baselines; on Diabetes, Heart, Income, Jungle it shows no advantage over zero-context baselines (R3). The narrative about when TabFM succeeds is unclear.

8. **Minority positive reviewer notes** (R4)
   - Reviewer 4, while overall positive (score 5), shares many of the above concerns regarding feature meaning sensitivity, noisy features, dataset simplicity, and hyperparameter tuning. That reviewer still found the few-shot results exciting but emphasized these unanswered questions.

*Note: Several weaknesses overlap with questions raised by reviewers; those questions have been incorporated as missing experiments or analyses (e.g., prompts, memorization, feature meaning).*
