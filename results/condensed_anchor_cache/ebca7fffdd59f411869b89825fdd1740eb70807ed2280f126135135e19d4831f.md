## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper presents NV-Embed, a decoder-only LLM-based embedding model that achieved #1 on the MTEB benchmark (score 69.32 on 56 tasks, 59.36 on 15 retrieval tasks). Key techniques: a latent attention layer for pooled embeddings (replacing mean pooling or last-token); removal of causal attention mask during contrastive training; two-stage contrastive instruction tuning (first on retrieval datasets with in-batch and hard negatives, then blending non-retrieval tasks); and extensive data curation including hard-negative mining, synthetic data generation, and adding more retrieval datasets for the improved v2 model. The paper documents the system with detailed ablation studies (Table 2, Table 4) and out-of-domain testing beyond MTEB.

### Strengths
- Very strong empirical results: #1 on MTEB benchmark at submission with high scores across all task types. This is a key achievement.
- Extensive ablation studies (Table 2: attention type, Table 4: single‑stage vs. two‑stage, hard negative mining, public retrieval set AD, synthetic data) justify each design choice.
- Detailed description of data curation (hard‑negative mining, synthetic data generation, multi‑class labels) improves transparency and reproducibility.
- Clear presentation and documentation of a top‑performing system that provides valuable guidance to the community.
- Out‑of‑domain testing on a leaderboard outside MTEB demonstrates generalizability.
- Architectural innovation: latent attention layer for pooling, though its impact is modest, it is a clean contribution.
- The combination of techniques produces record‑breaking results, confirming the effectiveness of the overall pipeline.

### Weaknesses
- Technical depth is limited; no single highly novel algorithmic piece. The work is primarily an effective engineering combination of existing techniques (including use of decoder‑only models for retrieval, which is already seen in GritLM).
- Latent attention layer shows only small improvement (<0.5 points on MTEB) over mean pooling with bidirectional attention (Table 2), raising concerns about added complexity for minimal gain.
- Two‑stage training lacks mechanistic explanation or intuition for why it works; the paper would benefit from more discussion on how the retrieval‑focused first stage affects generalization. A reviewer also asked what happens if the order is reversed.
- The overall training process is complex (multi‑stage, hard‑negative mining, synthetic data, many hyperparameters). Implementation details for hard‑negative mining and data synthesis are not fully specified, raising concerns about reproducibility and robustness. One reviewer (score 6) strongly emphasized this difficulty.
- The v2 → v2 improvements are under‑discussed; a reviewer requested more ablations and description of what changed between v1 and v2 (especially positive‑aware hard‑negative mining, synthetic data, multi‑class labels).
- Questions remain about optimizer usage (e.g., whether the optimizer is restarted between stages) and the impact of reversing the two‑stage order.
- Core methodological techniques (hard‑negative mining, multi‑stage training, instruction tuning) are well known; the paper’s primary contribution is a very effective application and analysis rather than fundamental novelty.
