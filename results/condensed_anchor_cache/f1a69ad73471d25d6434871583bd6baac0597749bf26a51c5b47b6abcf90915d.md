## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a computationally-efficient divisive normalization (DN) layer inspired by visual cortex, implemented as group-wise normalization with parameters initialized from a multivariate Gaussian. Tests on AlexNet, VGG-16, and a two-layer CNN on CIFAR and ImageNet show small accuracy gains (~2–3%) and improved robustness on ImageNet-C (AlexNet only). The authors report that DN can replace ReLU in small networks and leads to self-organization of orientation and color-opponent cells. Reviewers broadly agree the paper is well-written and addresses an interesting topic, but the contribution is severely limited: missing comparisons to other normalization methods, lack of scaling to modern architectures, insufficient justification of compute overhead, and neither strong enough for deep learning practice nor sufficiently novel for neuroscience modeling. One reviewer (R3, score 6) is more positive, highlighting the simplicity and the thought-provoking ReLU-free result, while another (R4, score 1) finds the paper makes no meaningful contribution. All reviewers note missing baselines and limited evaluation scope.

### Strengths
- Paper is well-written and easy to follow (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Addresses an important and biologically-motivated computation (divisive normalization) (R1, R4).
- The proposed DN implementation is simpler and more efficient than Miller et al.’s overlapping-window approach (R1, R3).
- Relative to Miller et al., the model shows significant improvements in the limited experiments shown (R1).
- Robustness results on ImageNet-C are interesting and appreciated (R1).
- The finding that networks using DN + BatchNorm can eliminate ReLU is thought-provoking (R3).
- The discussion comparing DN with other normalization forms (batch norm, group norm, layer norm) is interesting and suggests feature competition in DN and standardization in batch norm serve different computational purposes (R3).
- The emergence of orientation tuning and color-opponent cell grouping is a nice result (R3; though see weaknesses on novelty).
- (Minority) R3 gives the highest score (6) and notes relatively thorough evaluation on small to AlexNet/VGG networks.

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty and insufficient comparative baselines:**
   - Missing comparisons to standard normalization techniques: Local Response Normalization (LRN) from original AlexNet, BatchNorm, LayerNorm, GroupNorm, and Ren et al. (2016) who showed large gains from simple modifications of BN/LN (R1, R2). It is unclear whether DN outperforms these simpler methods (R1, R2).
   - The only DN comparison is Miller et al.; no comparison to other DN implementations in deep networks (e.g., Burg et al., Ren et al.) (R1, R4). The claim of being the first end-to-end trainable model with DN is false; these works already did that (R4).
   - The discovery that first-layer filters separate into edge detectors and color-opponent units is not new: the original AlexNet (trained on two GPUs with independent normalizers) already produced that separation (R2). Color opponency can also be obtained via linear ICA on natural scenes (Lee et al. 2000, Eichhorn et al. 2009) (R4).
   - Normative aspects of divisive normalization have been extensively studied (Schwarz & Simoncelli, Sinz & Bethge, Lyu et al.); the paper does not add significant neuroscience insight beyond what is known (R4).
2. **Insufficient evaluation scope and architecture generality:**
   - Only tested on AlexNet, VGG-16, and a two-layer CNN; not on more modern or state-of-the-art architectures (ResNet50, EfficientNet, ViT, CorNet) (R1, R2, R3, R4). The best ImageNet top-1 accuracy is 62.3%, which is far below current SoTA (e.g., OmniVec ViT 92.4%) (R4). Gains of ~2–3% are small relative to the 30% gap to SoTA (R4). It is unclear if DN scales or helps on architectures with sufficient capacity (R2).
   - The biological plausibility argument for using AlexNet/VGG is weak; architectures like CorNet are more aligned with visual cortex and should be tested if the goal is neuroscience modeling (R1). Also, deeper networks achieve better BrainScore and may better model visual cortex (R1).
   - No transfer learning evaluations (e.g., linear classification on DTD, Cars, Birds, or other OOD datasets) to test if DN learns generic features or overfits to ImageNet statistics (R1).
   - ImageNet-C robustness results are only provided for AlexNet; need to confirm they generalize to VGG-16 (R1). The results are not averaged across corruption severity levels; the standard ImageNet-C benchmark averages over all severities (R1).
3. **Compute overhead not justified and potential confounds:**
   - DN adds significant computational cost: ~25% slower per epoch for AlexNet and ~50% slower for VGG-16, yet accuracy gains are only ~2% (R1, R2). It is unclear if the same or better gains could be achieved by a compute-matched baseline (e.g., training longer, making the network wider/deeper) (R1, R2). The performance increase may simply be due to increased model expressiveness, not DN specifically (R2).
   - R1 asks: Can similar gains be obtained by a compute-matched baseline increased in parameters or training epochs?
4. **Specific technical issues and weaknesses:**
   - Asymptotic complexity analysis in Section 3.3 appears incorrect: when λ = N/8, (N−8λ+1)*8λ = N, which is not O(N²). The derivation needs clarification (R3).
   - Figure 2 interpretation is unclear: the authors claim that local opponency does not emerge in (a) but does with DN, yet many filters in (a) are also color-opponent and the individual filters look similar across subpanels (R1).
   - Figure 3 activation map scaling is not normalized; the differences between baseline and DN seem imperceptible and may be due to contrast differences (R2).
   - Question: The DN formulation implicitly includes a squaring nonlinearity (ϕ(x)=x²), which is a non-negative, non-saturating activation. Is the finding that ReLU is unnecessary simply due to this implicit nonlinearity? A fairer comparison would replace the squaring with ReLU, softplus, exp, etc. (R3). (This is a missing ablation/experiment.)
   - Question: Does group norm produce similar self-organization of tuning cell types? (R3). (Missing comparison.)
   - Question: Could DN serve as a drop-in replacement for group norm in modern networks? (R3). (Implies missing experiment.)
   - Some statements in the paper are too strong or incorrect: e.g., "The numerator of their normalization formula is still first order, so it cannot remove the second order dependency." This is misleading; a linear numerator combined with a nonlinear denominator can remove certain second-order dependencies (variance correlations) (R4). See Sinz & Bethge, Sinz et al., Lyu.
5. **Overall message and contribution unclear:**
   - The paper does not clearly deliver as either a deep learning mechanism (due to limited architectures, small gains, no SoTA impact) or a neuroscience contribution (no evaluation of neural data prediction, many previous normative models exist) (R1, R4). R1 suggests focusing more on building a better model of visual cortex with DN to achieve a significant neuroscience contribution. R4 finds that the paper addresses no clear gap in knowledge and makes no significant contribution.
