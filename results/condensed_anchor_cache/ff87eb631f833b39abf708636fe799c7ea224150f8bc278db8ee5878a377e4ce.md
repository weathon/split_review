## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses Inverse Constraint Inference (ICI) and proposes two algorithms, BEAR and PCSE, for learning feasible constraints from expert demonstrations under unknown transitions. It provides theoretical sample complexity guarantees and evaluates on Gridworld and Point Maze tasks. The reviewers acknowledge the novelty of theoretical analysis for ICRL in unknown settings but raise significant concerns about clarity of definitions, presentation, empirical validation, assumptions, and comparison with prior work. There is notable disagreement: Reviewer 4 (score 3, confidence 5) finds the paper’s core definition (3.1) incomprehensible due to typos and missing explanations, making all subsequent results unverifiable, while others (scores 6,6,5, confidence mostly 3) are more positive but still critical of presentation and empirical support.

### Strengths
- The paper presents novel sample complexity guarantees for ICRL in the setting where the transition model is unknown, a gap not addressed in prior work (Reviewer 1).
- Section 4 does a good job of describing the lemmas in understandable terms, and the steps in the proofs are mostly well explained (Reviewer 1).
- The theoretical analysis is concrete and well-detailed, and the empirical results for the provided environments (Gridworld, Point Maze) are generally positive (Reviewer 2).
- The problem of strategic exploration in ICRL is interesting and may have practical applications; the paper provides two algorithmic solutions (BEAR, PCSE) with corresponding sample complexity results (Reviewer 3).
- Numerical validation is included, which is not common in purely theoretical RL papers (Reviewer 3).
- The problem setting of designing provably-efficient algorithms for ICRL is inherently interesting (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Definition and clarity issues (Reviewer 4, severe; also noted by Reviewers 1 and 3).** The paper is based on Definition 3.1, which due to typos and absence of explanation is impossible to understand in a formal manner. The structure of cost functions and how they relate to observed expert demonstrations is unclear. Without a precise mathematical definition, it is impossible to verify subsequent results, including Lemma 4.3, and all theorems based on it. Reviewer 4 lists multiple specific typos: line 103 “space” unspecified; line 107 extra dot; line 137 reward $r$ should not be in the MDP tuple; line 138 redundant phrase; line 139 cost not defined as bounded; line 139 symbol $\Pi^*$ never defined; lines 141,143 bad definition missing dependence on cost $c$. Reviewer 4 states “too many typos” implying the authors have not carefully read the paper. Reviewer 3 notes the equation at l. 143 likely has a typo and Definition 3.1 lacks context. Reviewer 1 points out that in Equation 9, the supremum is over distributions over state space rather than state-action space, and the definition of $\Pi^r$ (rewards) appears to have the value function difference in the wrong order (should be $V^{r,\hat{\pi}^*} - V^{r,\pi} \ge \mathcal{R}_k$), which is a critical point needing clarification. Reviewer 3 also notes that Eq. 2 is missing definitions of $\zeta$ and $E$, and the interaction setting should be introduced earlier. Overall presentation is not sharp; Section 5 (especially 5.1 and 5.2) would benefit from non-technical elaboration similar to Section 4 (Reviewer 1, 3). The description of PCSE is hard to process (Reviewer 3).

- **Empirical evaluation concerns (Reviewer 1, 3, also mentioned by Reviewer 2).** The figures in the Empirical Evaluation (Sec. 6) are visually difficult to parse: UCB results are almost entirely hidden in Figure 3’s top and middle rows; the y-axis titles for rows 1 and 2 are the same, making it unclear what each row represents; it is unclear if the shaded regions represent standard deviation, standard error, or confidence intervals. The comparison to baselines is unclear: neither BEAR nor PCSE appears to beat the Random algorithm in terms of WGloU score. Reviewer 3 adds that most curves are not separated with statistical significance, so the numerical validation is not conclusive. Reviewer 2 notes that the algorithm’s scaling to continuous state and action spaces is not addressed; the current test case (Point Maze) is simple with discrete actions, and the discussion of how theoretical assumptions translate to practice (e.g., discretization, finite vs infinite horizon) is lacking (Reviewer 1).

- **Assumptions and connection to practice (Reviewer 1, 2, 3).** The paper assumes finite state/action spaces and infinite horizon, but the Point Maze experiment is a continuous environment with a finite horizon. There is a brief mention in Appendix D.3 about density models, but overall discussion of how the state/action spaces are discretized and the implications of using finite horizon when theoretical results assume infinite horizon is missing. The deterministic policy assumption is reasonable for MDPs but less so in CMDP; the authors should discuss whether determinism is necessary and prove it formally (Reviewer 3). The application setting that allows unconstrained access to the MDP online while the expert acts under constraints is not clearly motivated; examples of such applications would help (Reviewer 3). The PAC requirement is questionable because under approximation errors the learned policy may violate true constraints; some sub-optimality may be acceptable in unconstrained RL but constraint violations are less acceptable (Reviewer 3). Lines 175-181 contain informal/incorrect considerations about optimal trajectories (Reviewer 3).

- **Related works and novelty (Reviewer 1, 3).** The paper lacks a broader discussion of related works, especially comparisons to: Chou et al., “Learning constraints from demonstrations,” 2020; Kim and Oh, “Efficient off-policy safe reinforcement learning using trust region conditional value at risk,” 2022; Moskovitz et al., “Reload: Reinforcement learning with optimistic ascent-descent for last-iterate convergence in constrained mdps,” 2023; Lindner et al., “Learning safety constraints from demonstrations with unknown rewards,” 2024; Kim et al., “Learning Shared Safety Constraints from Multi-task Demonstrations,” 2024 (Reviewer 1). Also missing comparisons with prior works on IRL, reward-free exploration, and ICRL with a generative model (Reviewer 3). BEAR looks like a standard bonus-based exploration approach; the main novelty seems to be adapting bonuses to the ICRL setting, but the paper does not describe uncommon technical challenges arising from the specificity of the setting and how they are addressed (Reviewer 3). Additional related works on misspecification in IRL (https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/26766, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.06854) and sample-efficient IRL (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.15392, https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.17355, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.03812) could also be mentioned (Reviewer 3).

- **Theoretical justification and hidden dependencies (Reviewer 1, 3).** In Theorem 5.6, the sample complexity of PCSE is the minimum of BEAR’s complexity and a term dependent on the minimum cost advantage function. The justification via Corollary C.6 that PCSE optimizes a tighter bound is not clear: the left-hand sides of the two cases in Corollary C.6 involve different quantities (one a maximum over policies, the other a maximum over state-action space) and it is not obvious one is tighter (Reviewer 1). The sample complexity results include terms with hidden dependencies on $S, A, \gamma$ that are not made explicit (Reviewer 3).

- **Computational tractability and algorithm details (Reviewer 3).** The described approaches may not be computationally tractable; this is not addressed. Additionally, the $C_k$ bonus in BEAR seems to include the advantage, but the estimation of the reward is not mentioned anywhere.

- **Motivation for optimization formulation (Reviewer 1).** The paper would benefit from further discussion of why the estimation should be included in the optimization problem rather than solving naively and handling estimation errors in analysis. The optimality criterion and the role of the second constraint (accuracy) in preventing an unnecessarily large feasible set are not clearly motivated.

- **Reviewer disagreement (all).** There is substantial disagreement in overall assessment: Reviewer 4 (score 3, confidence 5) considers the paper’s core definition incomprehensible and the results unverifiable, while the other reviewers (scores 6,6,5, confidence 3) are more positive but still identify significant weaknesses in presentation, empirical validation, and motivation. This contrast suggests that the paper requires major improvements in clarity and rigor.
