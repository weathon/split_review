## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies the complexity of deterministic verifiers for nonsmooth nonconvex optimization when interacting with an omnipotent prover. The authors introduce weak and strong verification of Goldstein stationary points and use a connection with Carathéodory’s theorem to give an efficient deterministic strong verifier given access to an exact first-order oracle. They then prove exponential lower bounds for deterministic zeroth-order verification of uniform Goldstein points, and for nonuniform Goldstein points when queries are restricted to a lattice of width 10 ε δ. The lower bounds imply that randomization is necessary for efficient zeroth-order finding of uniform Goldstein points, contrasting with smooth optimization. The perspective of verification is novel, but the technical execution and motivation received mixed reactions (scores 3‑6).

### Strengths
- The question of how to *verify* Goldstein stationarity is very nice and has not been studied before; it is natural because the definition lacks an obvious witness (R1, R3).
- The approach is novel and offers a valuable theoretical perspective, fundamentally different from “testing” notions (R4, R5).
- The paper is well-written, with clear intuition and proofs (R2).
- Showing that randomization is necessary for uniform Goldstein points via zeroth-order is an interesting contrast with smooth optimization (R2).
- The lower bounds are theoretically solid and the proofs are elegant (R2).
- The positive result (Theorem 4) efficiently reduces verification to a Carathéodory decomposition, highlighting a structural insight (R3).
- Some reviewers draw an analogy to complexity classes (NP) and note that Lemma 2 naturally relates verification hardness to finding hardness (R4).
- The missing reference (Yun et al., 2019) on testing local optimality for ReLU networks is acknowledged (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Validity and rigor of the deterministic first-order verifier (Theorem 4).**  
  – The algorithm assumes a gradient is returned at the query point, but the function is not assumed differentiable everywhere. If the oracle is changed to return a subgradient, it is not obvious that an output of \(b=0\) still guarantees \(\delta\)-Goldstein stationarity: the absence of a subgradient from the convex hull of currently sampled subdifferentials does not preclude its existence at other points in the \(\delta\)-neighborhood; verifying that would seem to require checking many sets and may be computationally infeasible (R1).  
  – A concrete counterexample shows the algorithm may require differentiability at points \(\{y_i\}\) that are not differentiable (e.g., \(f(x)=\max\{-1,\min\{x,1\}\}\) at \(x=0\) with \(\varepsilon=1/2,\delta=1\)) (R4).  
  – Even if Clarke subdifferentials are used, the convex program in the algorithm cannot be guaranteed to be efficiently solvable; even for convex functions this may be computationally intractable (R4).  
  – Fact 2 (the key convexity argument) is stated for polytopes, but a Clarke subdifferential need not be a polytope (e.g., \(\partial(\|x\|)(0)\)) (R4).  
  – The prover must be not only computationally unbounded but also “omniscient” to extract the required Carathéodory decomposition from the function, which is not made rigorous; this vagueness may affect the whole theoretical framework (R4).

- **Correctness of the lower bound proofs (Corollaries 1 and 2).**  
  – In the proof of Corollary 1, the prover is assumed to provide the same query sequence \(\{x^{(i)}\}\) for two different functions \(f_0\) and \(f_1\) that are consistent on those points. But a legitimate prover would communicate with each function individually and could give different information; why would the prover force an identical sequence? This casts doubt on the proof (R4).  
  – The same issue arises in Corollary 2 (R4).

- **Motivation and scope.**  
  – The primary motivation for studying verification is questioned: an efficient strong verifier cannot be an efficient tester (Yun et al., 2019), and the paper does not convincingly establish why these lower bounds on verification are more informative than existing ones for finding (R4).  
  – The paper only considers deterministic verifiers. A randomized protocol would be more natural and widely applicable; the interactive multi-round scheme becomes artificial in the deterministic setting because a one-round protocol already achieves the same communication (R5). The lack of discussion of randomized verification limits the paper’s generality (R5).  
  – Given prior work (Jordan et al.), it is unclear what new high-level takeaway the results provide. The main progress—an exponential lower bound for deterministic zeroth-order—holds only under restrictive assumptions (no randomness, only function evaluations) and does not settle whether the linear-in-dimension bound for deterministic first+zeroth order algorithms is tight (R3).  
  – The positive verification result is a straightforward application of Carathéodory’s theorem that additionally requires arbitrary precision first-order queries. A more interesting open question is whether deterministic verification can be achieved with finite accuracy (R3).  
  – The lattice width in Corollary 2 is of order \(\varepsilon\delta\), which is too large for practical relevance (R4).  
  – The proof technique (e.g., for Theorem 7) is intuitive but relatively straightforward (R5).  
  – The assumption of unbounded computational power for the prover is never used in the proofs and could be removed; its presence confuses the setup (R5).

- **Unclear or insufficient explanations.**  
  – The explanation of the geometric construction behind Theorem 7 is too high-level; the “geometric question” and “advantage of the symmetry of the lattice” are not elaborated. A more detailed explanation, possibly with a visual aid, would help (R2).  
  – No discussion of the analogous smooth case for lattice queries is provided; clarifying differences in complexity between smooth and nonsmooth settings would be useful (R2).

- **Technical issues in definitions.**  
  – In Definition 7 (weak verification), the “if” conditions for completeness and soundness are always true, so the verifier would always return \(b=1\), making the definition appear inconsistent (R4).  
  – The multi-round interaction scheme is not well-motivated for a deterministic verifier; it can be compressed to a single round, which makes the setup seem artificial (R5).

- **Missing future work discussion.** The paper has enough space to discuss future directions, which would help readers appreciate the contribution and potential impact (R2).
