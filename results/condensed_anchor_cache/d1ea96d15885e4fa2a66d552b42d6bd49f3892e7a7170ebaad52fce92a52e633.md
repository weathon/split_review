## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a differentiable sensor layout optimization framework for end-to-end learning of task-specific pixel layouts. It introduces two pixel parametrizations (rectangular and curvilinear) and a drop-in sensor simulation module that approximates sensor sampling from high-resolution images. Experiments on semantic segmentation (Cityscapes) and classification/ multi-label classification (CelebA, MNIST) show performance improvements with learned layouts. Reviewer opinions split: two reviewers (score 8) highlight novelty, clarity, and manufacturability; two reviewers (score 5) raise concerns about limited experimentation, missing comparisons, and generalization.

### Strengths
- **Novel contribution**: First work to integrate sensor layout optimization into end-to-end differentiable training; fills a gap in the imaging pipeline (optics and ISP have been optimized, but not pixel layout). (R1, R2, R4)
- **Fully differentiable in a physically plausible manner**: The layout parametrization is differentiable, enabling joint training with network parameters. (R1)
- **Flexible framework**: Adaptable to different camera-based tasks (classification, semantic segmentation). (R1)
- **Manufacturability demonstration**: The work shows a realization of the learned layout, indicating practical feasibility. (R2)
- **Well-defined deformation class**: The layout is required to be bijective and bi-Lipschitz, ensuring differentiability and enabling end-to-end training. (R3)
- **Well presented and motivated**: Clear explanation of concepts at an appropriate level of detail; the energy/resource-efficiency direction is important and attractive. (R4)
- **Initial experimental confirmation**: Benefits of learned layouts are observed across multiple tasks and network architectures (Deeplabv3+, PSPNet, SegNetXt). (R2, R4)

### Weaknesses
1. **Training details and reproducibility**: The reported performance of Deeplabv3+ on the original images is lower than in the original paper. It is unclear whether the improvement comes from the sensor layout change or from extra fine-tuning (more epochs). Authors should clarify if results follow the official GitHub repo parameters and training procedure. (R1)
2. **Simulation vs. real-world validation**: It is unclear whether the hardware sensor described in Sec. 4 was evaluated in simulation or with real captures. No details are provided on how the manufactured layout was tested with real data, making it difficult to verify the hardware design’s validity. (R1, R2)
3. **Limited visualizations**: Current sensor images are mostly from low-resolution MNIST, where sampled outputs are too vague. More visualizations for high-resolution images (e.g., Cityscapes with learned layouts) are needed to help readers understand the visual impact of the learned layout. (R1)
4. **Missing quantitative computational cost analysis**: The introduction claims speed advantages (reduced network size and training time), but no quantitative results on training/inference time are provided. A comparison of computational overhead with and without the proposed method is required. (R1, R3)
5. **Unclear choice of input resolution**: All experiments use a lower resolution instead of the original image size. The rationale is not given. Experiments at the original resolution (with computational cost analysis) are needed to assess practical scalability and whether the benefits persist at higher resolutions. (R1, R3, R4)
6. **Generalization to diverse applications and scenes**: Experiments are limited to semantic segmentation on Cityscapes and multi-label classification on facial attributes. It is unclear how the method would generalize to other tasks (e.g., object detection) or diverse scenes (indoor/outdoor, anomalous scenes). The parametrization with only two parameters (θ₁, θ₂) may restrict adaptability to complex scene variations. Additional experiments are needed. (R3)
7. **Insufficient comparison with non-uniform baselines**: The only baseline with a non-uniform layout is uniform sampling (Zhao et al. 2017). No comparison with existing non-uniform sampling methods (e.g., Marin et al. 2019) is made, making it hard to assess the true advantage of the proposed approach. (R3)
8. **Unsubstantiated claim about small-object focus**: The paper argues that the rectangular layout places more pixels toward the left and right edges due to a higher density of small objects on sidewalks. This claim lacks quantitative support (e.g., accuracy per dense area or per class for small objects near the horizon). Such analysis is needed to verify the intended effect. (R3)
9. **Unclear explanation for rectilinear vs. curvilinear performance**: The rectilinear layout outperforms the curvilinear layout in all experiments, attributed to limited adaptability of curvilinear layouts in image corners. A more detailed explanation is required. (R3)
10. **Color Filter Array (CFA) ignored**: The optimization does not account for the CFA, which could negatively affect color-dependent tasks. (R2)
11. **Optics complexity not addressed**: Non-homogeneous pixel layouts increase the difficulty of optics design (pixel pitch optimization). The paper notes that task-specific learned optics could mitigate this, but the current work does not address it. (R2)
12. **Figure 1 caption insufficient**: The meaning of red and green arrows in Fig. 1 is not explained. The caption should be more descriptive of the proposed pipeline. (R1, R4)
13. **Training pipeline ambiguity**: It is unclear whether the camera parameters (sensor layout) are learned per sample or once from the dataset and then fixed for evaluation/inference. A high-level description of the overall training pipeline is needed. (R1)
