## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SegDT, a multimodal transformer that jointly learns semantic segmentation and action prediction for the goal-reaching stage of object navigation (assuming the target is already visible and within ~7–10 m). Training proceeds in phases: pre-training a segmentation module on HM3DSem, offline behavior cloning on shortest‑path trajectories, online fine‑tuning via PPO with knowledge transfer from an RNN‑based policy that uses ground‑truth segmentation. Experiments in Habitat Sim report a 40.2% success rate on HM3D.

Reviewers are sharply divided. One reviewer (score 6, confidence 3) praises the novelty of combining segmentation and navigation in a single transformer and the clever two‑phase training that addresses transformer limitations. Another (score 5, confidence 3) sees value in the visual‑recognition perspective but finds the problem setting overly simplified. Three reviewers (scores 3, 3, 1) raise major concerns: the task reduction to a visible‑goal scenario sidesteps the core exploration challenge; comparisons to full‑navigation methods are unfair; absolute performance is low (40.2% vs. 64% for PEANUT); and the method is compared against neither other transformer planners nor simple geometric baselines. The most negative reviewer (score 1) argues the research question is not valuable.

### Strengths
1. **Novel architecture** – Combining segmentation and navigation in a single transformer that aggregates information from multiple frames for both action prediction and object segmentation is novel and promising (R1, R2, R3).  
2. **Training strategy** – The two‑phase procedure (offline BC + online fine‑tuning with RNN knowledge transfer) intelligently addresses transformers’ poor sample efficiency and credit assignment in RL settings (R1, R2).  
3. **Competitive results** – SegDT achieves competitive results over several prior object‑navigation methods when evaluated in the restricted goal‑reaching setting (R1).  
4. **Ablations and qualitative results** – Clear ablation studies (R1) and good qualitative evidence that temporal information improves segmentation (R1).  
5. **Clear figures** – Figures 2 and 3 effectively illustrate how SegDT works (R4).  
6. **Readability** – The paper is well written and easy to follow (R4, R5).  
7. **Well‑defined task and setting** – The experimental problem is well defined, and the Habitat‑based evaluation is straightforward (R5).  
8. **Fairness baseline** – The inclusion of a baseline with more steps helps reduce bias when comparing to methods trained on both exploration and navigation (R5).

### Weaknesses
1. **Problem simplification** – The paper addresses only the *reaching* stage, assuming the target is already visible and within 7–10 m. This sidesteps the main challenge of object navigation (exploration), making the contribution limited and the task closer to simple goal‑reaching (R2, R3, R5). A naive solution (single‑view depth + shape completion + RRT* planner) would likely achieve a much higher success rate than the reported 40% (R5).  
2. **Unfair comparisons**  
   - Compared methods (DDPPO, OnavRIM, PIRLNav) are designed for the full navigation task including exploration; evaluating them on a goal‑reaching setting without retraining or adjusting the initialisation biases results in favour of SegDT (R2, R3, R5).  
   - It is unclear whether all methods are initialised at random locations with the target visible in the same distance range (R3).  
   - Reward functions differ: SegDT uses geodesic distance to goal, whereas PIRLNav originally uses sparse success/failure rewards (R3).  
   - Table 2 favours SegDT because it exploits multiple previous frames, an obvious advantage over single‑frame baselines (R2).  
3. **Low absolute performance** – SegDT achieves 40.2% on HM3D, while recent works such as PEANUT (64%) and VLFM (52.5%, training‑free) perform substantially better, calling into question the method’s effectiveness (R4).  
4. **Limited evaluation**  
   - Experiments are only on HM3D; related ObjectNav work typically evaluates on multiple datasets (AI2THOR, RoboTHOR, ProcTHOR, Gibson, MP3D) (R4).  
   - The model is trained on 28 object categories but evaluated on only 6 in Table 1 and 4 in Table 2 (R5).  
   - Trajectories are limited to 64 steps, while some baselines use 500 steps; scaling to longer trajectories is unexplored (R1).  
5. **Missing comparisons**  
   - No comparison to other transformer‑based action planners (e.g., RT‑1, Perceiver‑Actor, PaLM‑E) (R5).  
   - No comparison to 2D‑to‑3D lifting methods such as MaskClustering for semantic segmentation (R1).  
   - No comparison to a simple geometric shortest‑path baseline in Table 1 (R5).  
   - The related work section omits important modular‑based ObjectNav studies (R4).  
6. **Insufficient analysis and unclear contributions**  
   - Limited analysis of *why* the method works; more insight into what the transformer learns is needed (R1).  
   - The novelty and design rationale are not clearly separated from implementation details; additional ablations are required to justify each design choice (R3).  
   - In Table 1, RL with RNN and GT segmentation outperforms SegDT with GT segmentation; this is not explained (R3).  
   - The ablation study (Section 4.2) does not isolate the quality of the training signal; it merely shows what kind of data the model prefers (R5).  
7. **Technical weaknesses**  
   - Computational complexity during inference is higher than baselines, but the paper provides no concrete numerical evidence (R1, R2).  
   - The method relies on a pre‑trained Mask2Former; sensitivity to the quality of this initial segmentation is not explored (R1, R2).  
   - When the segmentation model is frozen, it is unclear what is actually trained – only linear projections and learnable tokens? (R5).  
   - Input resolution 160 × 120 padded to 160 × 160 is suboptimal; it may not be a fair representation for other methods (R5).  
8. **Presentation and clarity issues**  
   - Figure 1 is duplicated and lacks an adequate explanation in text or caption (R2, R4).  
   - Distance to goal is stated as 7 m in L150‑151 and 10 m in L322‑323; this inconsistency is confusing (R2, R5).  
   - The statement at L441‑442 could be clearer (R2).  
   - Calling HM3D rendered at 160×120 “photorealistic” is an overstatement (R5).  
   - The conceptual difference between “Offline” and “Online” training phases is unclear; both appear to be offline distillation (R5).  
   - Minimising cumulative distance is not a meaningful goal; the target should be minimum steps and highest success/crash rate (R5).  
9. **Questions that overlap with criticisms / missing experiments / clarifications**  
   - How does performance scale with trajectories longer than 64 steps? (R1)  
   - What criteria would the authors propose for selecting the most valuable frames for the segmentation loss? (R1)  
   - How could the approach be extended to the full navigation problem including exploration? (R2)  
   - What is the impact of sequence length (why 64?) on performance and computational cost? (R2)  
   - Why was a decoder‑only GPT architecture chosen over encoder‑only or encoder‑decoder alternatives? (R4)  
   - Can SegDT be adapted to modular‑based methods, which treat reaching as a point‑to‑point subtask with ~99% success? (R4)  
   - Why is a shortest‑path baseline not included in Table 1? (R5)  
   - In Table 2, are the mIoU values evaluated on the same set of trajectories? If not, the comparison is biased because mIoU is sensitive to object size; evaluation should be on trajectories that are successful regardless of the segmentation model (R5).  
   - After the segmentation model is frozen, what parameters are actually updated? (R5)  
   - Is the padding from 160×120 to 160×160 fair for other methods? (R5)
