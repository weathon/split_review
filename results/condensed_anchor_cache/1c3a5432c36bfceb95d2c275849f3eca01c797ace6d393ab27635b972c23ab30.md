## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper challenges the assumption that individual knowledge is localized in LLM parameters, demonstrating via fidelity and reliability evaluations that existing methods (parameter layers, chains) lack faithfulness. Instead, it proposes that capabilities (shared attributes across datasets) can be localized, and introduces a Commonality Neuron Localization (CNL) method that identifies “commonality neurons” using a gradient attribution score with threshold σ=6. On GSM8K, CNL achieves a neuron overlap rate of 96.42% for same-task examples. Causal enhancement/erasure experiments show that manipulating these neurons (0.15% of parameters) significantly affects model performance, supporting the claim that they embody capability. The paper uses diverse datasets (math, program, language) and includes a decoupling experiment to separate individual knowledge from commonalities.

### Strengths
- Clearly motivates the problem by evaluating three existing knowledge localization methods (ROME, KN, KC) and demonstrating their lack of fidelity and reliability; this finding is insightful and compelling for the community.
- Addresses an important, topical question: how LLM parameters relate to performance.
- Proposes a new perspective (capability localization) and the CNL method, which is structured and inspired by grounded techniques like integrated gradients from computer vision.
- The decoupling dataset and experiments are informative.
- Uses a wide variety of datasets (math, program, language) to evaluate CNL.
- Shows causal evidence that fine-tuning commonality neurons improves performance both within and across datasets, and erasing them degrades performance, demonstrating their causal role.
- Successful identification of neurons with high overlap (96.42% on GSM8K) and that these neurons constitute only 0.15% of parameters yet significantly affect performance.

### Weaknesses
- **Conceptual clarity and justification**: The term “commonality” is not clearly defined, nor its relation to knowledge localization adequately explained (reviewer 1). The conceptual differences and limitations of replacing individual knowledge location with much coarser “commonality neurons” are insufficiently discussed; potential applications differ radically (reviewer 3). The mapping between commonality neurons and capability is conjectural (line 525-526), inconsistent with the main claim that “the located neurons embody the collection of capabilities” (reviewer 3). The paper does not clarify which specific capabilities the neurons represent or which quantitative results in Section 6 support them (reviewer 3). The distinction between “existing technology cannot localize individual model parameters” and “individual knowledge cannot achieve parameter localization” in general is unclear (reviewers 1, 3). The authors sometimes use unscientific terminology (e.g., “believes”, “ideologically”) (reviewer 3).
- **Arbitrary threshold and lack of sensitivity analysis**: The attribution score threshold σ=6 is used without justification or exploration of alternative values; it is critical to which neurons are considered significant. No sensitivity analysis is provided to show how results change with different σ; σ=6 seems particularly high and may exclude many relevant neurons, biasing the view of localization (reviewer 2). Similarly, in Section 5.1 line 352, S=19 is selected without clear justification or hyperparameter sweep (reviewer 2). The authors should provide detailed steps to ensure results are not cherry-picked (reviewer 2).
- **Narrow scope of decoupling experiment**: The decoupling experiment focuses primarily on mathematical tasks, limiting generalizability. Expanding to other domains (e.g., emotion recognition, language understanding) is crucial to support broader claims about data commonalities and parameter localization (reviewer 2).
- **Lack of statistical significance and control conditions**: Performance improvements from enhancing located neurons are reported without statistical evidence (confidence intervals, p-values) to rule out random fluctuations (reviewer 2). For random-neuron conditions in enhancement/erasure experiments (Tables 2 and 3), it is unclear whether multiple random samples were evaluated; without this, reliability of comparisons is uncertain (reviewer 2). More control conditions are needed: multiple sets of randomly selected neurons averaged over trials, different thresholds for neuron activation, varying neuron group sizes, to demonstrate that effects are specific to commonality neurons rather than general network fluctuations (reviewer 2).
- **Lack of common evaluation metric**: The study evaluates different knowledge localization methods (ROME, KN, KC, and CNL) using distinct metrics tailored to each method’s assumptions. A shared, objective metric for comparing accuracy, consistency, granularity, and impact on performance is missing, making direct comparison impossible (reviewer 2). This is the biggest weakness (reviewer 2). The authors should benchmark CNL against established methods in a reproducible way (reviewer 2).
- **Missing comparisons and baselines**: CNL is not evaluated on the same dataset used to evaluate previous methods (Table 1) (reviewer 4). The baseline performance of the model without fine-tuning is not included in Table 2 for comparison, unlike Table 3 (reviewer 4). The conclusion of Table 2 is ambiguous (reviewer 1).
- **Scalability concerns**: CNL’s neuron-level analysis could become computationally prohibitive as models grow to hundreds of billions of parameters. Larger models might require different thresholds/hyperparameter sweeps, increasing tuning efforts. CNL relies on extensive data from various domains, and data needs will grow with model size. These issues need discussion or new tests (reviewer 2).
- **Practical applicability unclear**: It is not clearly explained how identifying capability neurons improves model interpretability in practical applications, or whether CNL offers actionable changes for real-world LLM use (reviewer 2).
- **Presentation clarity and rigor**: Figure 2 is blurry and missing x-axis label (reviewers 1, 4). Figure 1, Figure 3 red boxes, and Figure 4 colors are under-explained; captions need to be extended and more precise (reviewers 2, 3). The role of Section 4.2 and its connection to the rest of the paper is unclear (reviewers 1, 3). In line 182, \(e_i\) appears undefined (reviewer 1). Missing reference in line 216 (reviewer 1). In formula (8), line 329, parameter sets \(P_s\) are not clearly defined (reviewer 3). There are several typos and incomplete sentences (reviewer 3). The authors should clarify methodology: e.g., how attribution score compares to existing choices in literature (reviewer 3), and verify that GPT4o-generated samples for fidelity/reliability tests preserve semantics—perhaps use GPTJ itself for generation (reviewer 4).
- **Minor reviewer contrast**: One reviewer (score 5) is notably more critical, emphasizing insufficient conceptual discussion, lack of rigor in attribution definition, and weak support for the main claim; the majority (scores 6) are more positive about the insight and method but share concerns about evaluation, thresholds, and presentation. This disagreement should be considered.
