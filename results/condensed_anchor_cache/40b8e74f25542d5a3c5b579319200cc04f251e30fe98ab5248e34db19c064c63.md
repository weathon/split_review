## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper investigates scaling laws for downstream task performance in machine translation, focusing on how pretraining data size and distribution alignment affect translation quality after finetuning. It proposes a log-law for translation metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, COMET) when pretraining and finetuning distributions are well-aligned, and finds that with misalignment, translation metrics can fluctuate while downstream cross-entropy monotonically improves. Experiments use T5 models pretrained on subsets of Multilingual C4 and finetuned on various WMT translation tasks.

### Strengths

- The paper seeks empirical answers to important questions about scaling laws for downstream transfer, providing practical guidance on when to seek different pretraining data vs. more data, and how much finetuning data is sufficient.
- The work focuses specifically on machine translation, an important NLP task not previously the main focus of transfer scaling law studies, and goes into deeper empirical depth with clearer takeaways.
- The paper concretely shows that downstream cross-entropy can be misleading: it monotonically improves with pretrain size even when translation metrics like BLEU do not, a useful result for future researchers.
- The experiments are extensive, fully controlled, and systematically isolate different variables’ effects. The proposed log-law for translation quality metrics is well-motivated and comprehensively validated across multiple metrics and scenarios.
- The paper is exceptionally well-grounded in prior literature and makes important observations that challenge common assumptions about cross-entropy and translation quality.
- The writing is generally clear and the structure effective (though one reviewer found it disjointed; see Weaknesses). The paper provides abundant charts and empirical evidence to support conclusions.
- The work is highly valuable from the perspectives of innovation and importance, offering a new method for evaluating pretraining data value.

### Weaknesses

**Definition and formalization of “alignment” between pretraining and downstream distributions.** Multiple reviewers (R1, R2, R3, R4) criticize the lack of a precise, formal definition of “alignment.” The term is central to the paper’s claims but is only defined operationally in hindsight (e.g., “when the scaling law fails to fit,” lines 227–228). R1 notes that it is unclear whether alignment refers to language overlap, domain similarity, or both; examples like Icelandic (low-resource, different domain) vs. German (high-resource, same domain) illustrate the ambiguity. R2 and R3 request a more formal, quantifiable definition. Without one, the paper’s main distinction between “well-aligned” and “misaligned” cases remains vague and cannot be applied to new scenarios without post-hoc analysis.

**Limited scope to encoder-decoder architectures and high-resource settings.** R1 strongly criticizes the exclusive focus on encoder-decoder models (e.g., T5) despite the field’s move to decoder-only architectures. R1 points to findings that contradict those from recent decoder-only MT work (e.g., [1] showing that large pretraining data is always beneficial even with large finetuning data). R1 argues that the claim “no need to pretrain when finetuning data is large enough” (lines 355–359) may be an artifact of in-domain test sets (WMT) and would not hold on out-of-domain data; the authors should test generalization to other domains. R1 also notes that studying only high-resource languages (billions of tokens for pretraining, millions of parallel sentences) is less compelling, as low-resource or cross-lingual settings would be more practical. R3 and R5 ask whether the findings carry over to decoder-only LLMs (R3’s question; R5 requests connection to recent LLM-based MT work like ALMA, TowerLLM, and English-centric LLMs finetuned on parallel data). R5 further notes the experiments are limited to pairwise language combinations; no results for realistic multilingual pretraining with severe data size misalignments or language similarity effects.

**Outdated or insufficient evaluation metrics.** R1 argues that the heavy reliance on BLEU scores is problematic because BLEU does not align well with human judgments, and ROUGE is primarily a summarization metric, not standard for translation. R1 suggests using BLEURT or other modern metrics. While the paper does include COMET, R1’s point stands that BLEU is used prominently. R4 questions whether the log-law generalizes to NLP tasks with precision/recall metrics, and whether cross-entropy can always be used to observe the scaling law.

**Presentation and writing issues (with disagreement among reviewers).** R1 finds the paper disjointed and redundant, with repeated statements (e.g., “when the distribution is well-aligned, both metrics and CE align with the scaling laws”) and frequent figure references that are pages away from the relevant text. R2 agrees that the ordering is confusing: the paper starts with results before discussing experimental details, making it hard to interpret early claims. R2 also notes that “Remark 2” is unclear, and Section 3.4 (“A Guide for …”) is confusing (especially part 2). In contrast, R3, R4, and R5 find the writing clear, well-structured, and of good quality. The disagreement is preserved: while most reviewers praise clarity, one reviewer (R1) and to some extent R2 find the presentation problematic.

**Missing references and details.** R5 points out a repeated citation (“Tran et al., 2019” three times, lines 142–143). R5 also requests that fine-tuning dataset sizes be included in the line mentioning them (lines 264–265). R4 suggests including a reference to the inference scaling law from OpenAI’s o1 model to place the work in a broader framework.

**Questions overlapping with criticisms.** The following reviewer questions are treated as weaknesses because they point to missing experiments, comparisons, or formalization:
- R1: How does the model perform on out-of-domain test sets?
- R1: How would the findings change for low-resource languages or cross-lingual transfer?
- R2, R3, R4: Provide a formal definition of “alignment.”
- R3: Do findings carry over to decoder-only models?
- R4: Can the log-law be applied to other NLP tasks with different metrics (precision/recall)?
- R5: How do findings translate to multilingual pretraining with many languages and severe data size misalignments? Any findings on language similarity?
