## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Causal Concept Graph Models (Causal CGMs), which extend Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs) by allowing causal dependencies among the interpretable concepts. The model is designed to be causally transparent by design, supporting human-in-the-loop corrections and counterfactual reasoning. Experiments on CelebA and CIFAR-10 show that Causal CGMs achieve performance close to opaque black‑box models while improving interpretability. Reviewers appreciated the motivation and the breadth of experiments, but raised significant concerns about causal validity, novelty, experimental scope, and missing details.

### Strengths
- Well written and clearly structured (R1, R3, R5; but R4 found some sections hard to follow).
- Addresses a key limitation of prior CBMs by dropping the unrealistic assumption of causally independent concepts (R1, R2, R4).
- The approach enables causal transparency by design, allowing human‑in‑the‑loop corrections and interventional/counterfactual analysis (R1, R4). R5, however, argues that human‑in‑the‑loop corrections are at odds with the stated interpretability motivation.
- Experiments are extensive and show that Causal CGMs perform similarly to black‑box models and improve over standard CBM/CEM baselines (R1, R2, R3, R5). The model also recovers logic rules and supports good interventional performance (R2).
- The method description is detailed and well‑structured (R3). Plots are praised as great (R5).
- The perspective of using learned causal representations for interpretability (rather than domain generalization) is interesting (R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Causal opacity definition**: “Causal opacity” is used repeatedly but remains vaguely defined; it is unclear how it differs from a general lack of interpretability (R1). This overlaps with a question about XAI vs. interpretability (R5).
- **Causal validity and identifiability**: Using predictive power to identify causal parents may recover spurious associations rather than true causal structure (R2). Theorem 3.2 is stated without proof and lacks explicit assumptions (no interventions or sufficient variability are assumed) (R2). The claim that \(p(v'_i \mid pa_{V'}(v_i), u_i) = p(v_i \mid u_i)\) is questioned: if the parent set is non‑empty, this equality cannot hold (R2). No identifiability guarantees are provided (R2, R5). A minority reviewer (R5) notes that the method is nearly identical to Causal VAE [1] (only name and motivation differ) and that high‑dimensional embeddings are not novel; moreover, the approach lacks the identifiability results of other causal representation learning methods.
- **Missing comparisons and related work**: The paper does not compare with other causal deep‑learning methods, such as Neural Causal Models (NCM) or causal representation learning approaches (R2). A reviewer notes that CCGM+CD (recovering the graph from ground‑truth labels) may be very similar to NCM, and the large accuracy drop compared to CCGM suggests the learned causal graph may not be correct (R2). No discussion of identifiability of (latent) concepts is provided (R2).
- **Abduction and counterfactual inference**: The mechanism for abduction (inferring exogenous variables) is not specified; it is unclear whether the inference is deterministic or probabilistic and how uncertainty is handled (R1).
- **Acyclicity guarantee**: How the model guarantees the causal graph is acyclic is not explained (R1’s question; a methodological gap).
- **Accuracy–interpretability trade‑off**: The model appears to sacrifice accuracy for interpretability, but the paper does not justify whether the trade‑off is optimal and does not explore different configurations (R1).
- **Experimental scope**: Only simpler benchmarks (CelebA, CIFAR‑10) are used; semantically richer datasets (AwA2, CUB) are not included (R4). It is unclear whether the full CIFAR‑10 dataset was used or a subset (R4).
- **Joint training details**: It is unclear whether concept encoders are trained jointly with the CGM, which CBM formulation (independent, joint, or sequential bottleneck) is used, and how the bottleneck architecture might influence the quality of the learned causal graph (R4).
- **Human‑in‑the‑loop evaluation**: No experiments or user studies support the claimed utility of human interventions; the soundness and value of the learned causal graphs are not evaluated (R4). One reviewer (R5) suggests that human‑in‑the‑loop corrections are contradictory to the interpretability motivation and should be moved to the appendix.
- **Unexplained experimental observations**: The drop in performance for certain numbers of intervened concepts in Figures 4 and 5 is not explained (R4). The experiment described in the last paragraph of the experimental section (lines 463‑476) is unclear: how variables \(i, j\) are selected, whether results are averaged over all pairs, what ground‑truth structure is used, and why an RCCE of 0 is considered desirable (R3).
- **Table/Figure issues**: Table 3 has two columns titled “CELEBA” (R3) and column 4 is likely CIFAR‑10 but not clearly labeled (R4). Table 1 is hard to read (font too small) (R2). Figure 3 description is inconsistent with the picture (R1).
- **Assumption of pre‑specified concepts**: The paper builds on vanilla CBMs that assume human‑specified concepts are available; this limitation is well‑known and has been relaxed in prior work; the choice is not justified (R4).
- **Minor technical points**: The claim that SCMs are *the* standard framework for causal modeling is too strong; other frameworks (e.g., Rubin causal models) exist (R5, suggested rephrasing). The description of conditional probabilities in SCMs (line 111) appears to conflict with the deterministic reduced‑form definition (R5). The term “interventions” should specify “hard interventions” (R5). The distinction between “concept” and “high‑level feature” and between concept‑based modeling and representation learning is unclear (R5). Theorem 3.2 might follow directly from d‑separation; the proof could be simplified (R5). The training objective (line 257) seems to maximize the log‑likelihood of unobserved variables; a variational lower bound would be appropriate (R5).
