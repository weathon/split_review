## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates data scaling laws for imitation learning in robotic manipulation, focusing on zero-shot generalization to unseen objects and environments. Through extensive real-world experiments with over 40,000 demonstrations and 15,000 rollouts across many environments and objects, the authors find that generalization performance follows an approximate power-law with respect to the number of training environments and objects. Crucially, diversity in environments and objects matters far more than the total number of demonstrations; beyond about 50 demonstrations per environment-object combination, additional data yields minimal gain. The authors validate these findings by collecting data for two new tasks using an efficient strategy and achieving ~90% success rates. The work uses Diffusion Policy and a handheld gripper (UMI) for data collection, and evaluates with a human-scored rubric.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses a timely and important question—data scaling for zero-shot generalization in imitation learning—that has only recently become feasible, and provides a principled analysis of the underlying principles.
- A rigorous empirical protocol is followed: large-scale data collection (40k+ demos, 15k+ real-robot rollouts), training of many models, and evaluation across diverse environments/objects with careful minimization of evaluation bias.
- The proposed scaling law (power-law relationship with number of environments/objects) is empirically validated and holds for two new tasks trained post-hoc.
- Practical insights are distilled into an efficient data collection strategy, which is critical given the cost of robot data collection.
- The visual presentation of objects and scenes is excellent, and the appendix provides helpful tips for practitioners.
- The paper identifies that diversity of environments/objects is more important than sheer demonstration count, and that adding more demonstrations beyond ~50 per combination yields diminishing returns.
- The authors plan to open-source code, data, and models, facilitating future work.
- The discussion of evaluation criteria and the design of a scoring rubric is a useful contribution; while potential for subjective bias exists, the authors adequately argue its necessity over simpler metrics.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of detail on initial conditions and variation in environment setup.** It is unclear how the initial robot pose, object poses (e.g., relative position of cup and bottle in pouring), and environment configurations are varied across trials. Without this information, it is difficult to assess whether policies learn robust manipulation strategies or merely open-loop trajectories. Reviewer 1 specifically asks: “What level of variation is done in the evaluation environment setup over multiple runs in the same environment on the same object?” This missing description undermines confidence in the claimed generalization.
- **Limited task difficulty variation and narrow validation.** The primary analysis focuses on two relatively simple tasks (pouring water, rearranging a computer mouse). The two additional tasks (towel folding, unplugging a charger) used to validate the scaling law appear significantly simpler/shorter-horizon, making it unclear whether the scaling laws generalize to tasks with longer planning horizons, precise alignment, or obstacle avoidance. Reviewer 1 notes this limits the generalizability of the findings.
- **Objects are all from the same category (e.g., water bottles) and are very similar.** This may explain why increasing the number of objects per environment does not improve performance (Figure 6). The claim that the policy can operate on “nearly any object, in any environment” (line 47/48) is overstated given the restriction to tabletop environments and same-category objects. Reviewer 2 explicitly points this out.
- **Unexplained performance drops in Figure 6.** Increasing the number of objects per environment sometimes decreases task performance (e.g., right plot, M=4). Reviewer 2 and Reviewer 1 both question this counterintuitive result, and Reviewer 1 also asks why the normalized score drops at the highest demonstration count. No explanation is provided.
- **Missing standard deviations and definition of correlation coefficient.** Plots lack error bars, making it hard to assess empirical significance. The correlation coefficient *r* (line 380) is not defined. Reviewer 2 requests these additions.
- **Insufficient detail on data collection and potential bias.** The paper does not specify the number of demonstrators (with IDs), the data collection protocol, or statistics such as number of failed demonstrations, action variance, or task horizon per demonstrator. The hardware (UMI) may introduce bias from a skilled teleoperator (e.g., optimal speed, precise height). This leaves uncertainty about whether the observed scaling laws are genuine or influenced by data quality variation across objects/environments. Reviewer 3 emphasizes this concern.
- **Only one algorithm (Diffusion Policy) and a single seed are used.** This limits the ability to draw comprehensive conclusions about scaling laws across algorithmic choices.
- **Generalization is only studied in the single-task setting; multi-task learning, which is highly relevant to modern robot learning, is not considered.** Reviewer 4 raises this.
- **The dataset scale is limited.** The number of unique objects and environments, while diverse, may be insufficient to guarantee that the power-law relationship holds at larger scales. Benchmarking against other robotic manipulation datasets is lacking (Reviewer 4).
- **Flawed claim that environment and object generalization “encompass all factors a policy may encounter during real-world deployment.”** Reviewer 4 identifies this statement as incorrect; other factors (lighting, background clutter, robot wear, etc.) are not considered.
- **Out-of-domain generalization is not quantified.** No distribution distance metric (e.g., feature-space distance) is used to measure how far the test distribution is from training. While appendix visuals help, a rigorous metric is missing (Reviewer 4).
- **Caption 7 inaccuracy:** The phrase “maximum number of demonstrations” should read “maximum number of environment-object combinations” (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing explanation for α and β in Figure 5** (Reviewer 3).
- **Whether each heatmap cell in Figure 6 uses the same number of demonstrations is not clearly explained** (Reviewer 3).
- **No in-domain evaluation is included,** which would clarify the performance gap between zero-shot and in-domain data (Reviewer 3).
- **The scoring rubric, while justified, still introduces potential subjective bias** (Reviewer 4).
