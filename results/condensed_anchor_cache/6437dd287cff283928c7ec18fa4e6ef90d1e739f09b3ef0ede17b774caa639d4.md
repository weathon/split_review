## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces IDEATOR, a black-box jailbreak attack on Vision-Language Models (VLMs) that uses a VLM as an adversarial agent to generate jailbreak text prompts and a diffusion model (Stable Diffusion) to create corresponding jailbreak images. The method is training-free, instruction-based, and iteratively improves prompts via a breadth-depth exploration strategy. Experiments on MiniGPT‑4, LLaVA, and InstructBLIP report Attack Success Rates of 94%, 82%, and 88%, respectively, demonstrating effectiveness and transferability. Reviewers acknowledge the work's novelty in leveraging a VLM for red‑teaming, but raise concerns about limited model diversity, insufficient theoretical grounding, lack of automated evaluation validation, and the absence of tests on closed‑source commercial models. Two reviewers rated the paper 5, while two rated it 3, reflecting disagreement on the contribution’s significance and rigor.

### Strengths
- Novel approach that uses a VLM itself as a jailbreak agent, extending red‑teaming from LLMs to multimodal systems (R1, R2, R3).
- Method is training‑free and easy to implement (R4).
- Well‑written and clearly articulated methodology (R1, R3, R4).
- Impressive attack effectiveness and strong transferability across different VLM architectures, with concrete ASR results (94% on MiniGPT‑4, 82% on LLaVA, 88% on InstructBLIP) (R1, R3, R4).
- Generates more diverse jailbreak images compared to prior adversarial or query‑relevant image approaches (R1).
- Provides valuable insights into VLM vulnerabilities and security implications (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Overclaimed novelty / insufficient innovation.** The claim of “previously unrecognized vulnerabilities” is exaggerated because multi‑round iterative jailbreaking has already been validated in LLM settings (R1). Some reviewers view the work as merely prompt engineering with no technical or theoretical innovation (R4), and note strong similarity to prior work (Ref1: arXiv 2407.15050) without a detailed differentiation (R2).
- **Limited victim model selection.** Only MiniGPT‑4, LLaVA, and InstructBLIP are tested; this narrow set may not represent the broader VLM landscape. The choice of models is not justified, and experiments on more diverse architectures (e.g., closed‑source commercial models like GPT‑4o) are missing (R1, R2, R4).
- **Weak theoretical justification.** The conjecture that a VLM can serve as an effective red‑team model lacks a solid theoretical framework. The paper does not explain why a VLM generates effective jailbreak prompts, nor does it address potential biases in either the attacker or victim VLMs (R2). The Empirical Understanding section describes observed behaviors without explaining why they occur, making transfer to other models difficult (R4).
- **Inadequate evaluation methodology.** The reliance on manual review of harmful outputs is not validated; comparisons with automated evaluation methods (keyword matching, LLM‑based evaluators) are needed (R3). Many detailed experimental results are not presented, limiting the paper’s contribution (R4). The breadth‑depth exploration strategy is not new, and its impact on diversity (as opposed to simply increasing ASR) is not demonstrated (R4).
- **Lack of cost and detectability analysis.** The method uses multiple rounds of queries, but no comparison of query count or time cost with other methods is provided. In a black‑box setting, high query volume can make the attack more detectable (R1).
- **Absence of defense discussion.** The paper does not evaluate the method against existing jailbreak defenses for LLMs/VLMs nor offers insights for designing countermeasures (R1).
- **Potential bias from identical attacker/victim model.** Using the same Vicuna‑13B version of MiniGPT‑4 for both attacker and victim roles raises concerns about result generalizability. Direct transfer attacks between different architectures (e.g., MiniGPT‑4 attacker → LLaVA victim) are not explicitly demonstrated (R3).
- **Unanswered implementation questions.** Several key details are missing: how relevant in‑context examples are defined (and whether they differ per SD / attacker / victim model) (R4); how the attacker VLM handles refusal to output malicious prompts (besides using MiniGPT‑4) (R4); whether text/image prompts contain harmful tokens that could be filtered by the victim VLM or SD safety measures (R4); whether text and generated images are semantically related and how that is controlled (R4); and how randomness of Stable Diffusion is addressed (e.g., can all images from a successful prompt reliably perform the attack?) (R4).
- **Minor typographical error:** “llva” should be “llava” (line 25) (R3).
- **Code and data not released**, limiting reproducibility (R2).
