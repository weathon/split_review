## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a novel method to calibrate linguistic expressions of certainty (e.g., “Maybe,” “Likely”) by representing each phrase as a distribution over the probability simplex rather than a single score. Calibration is framed as an optimal transport problem. Experiments on radiologist reports and LLM outputs show that the method is comparable to simpler baselines like Platt scaling and histogram binning, with the main advantage being a direct phrase-to-phrase mapping. Reviewers agree the idea is interesting and mathematically sound, but disagree on its practical contribution: one reviewer (score 8) finds it robust and impactful, while three others (scores 5,6,6) note marginal improvements, limited scope, and complexity issues.

### Strengths
- Considering confidence as a probabilistic simplex and framing calibration as optimal transport is a novel and interesting formulation (R1, R3).
- The proposal to associate uncertainty phrases with distributions instead of single scores is creative and opens new modeling possibilities (R2, R3).
- Very clear presentation of the method and insightful comparison against existing calibration work (R2).
- The miscalibration estimator using confidence distributions is more robust to increasing the number of bins (R3).
- The method does not require an explicit mapping from phrase to score, allowing direct calibration of certainty phrases (R4).
- Intuitively models how populations use uncertainty expressions, with potential for population-level calibration (R4).
- Strong mathematical modeling, including a composition of source distributions, an optimal transport map, and an indexing function (R3, R4).
- Potential real-world use cases in medicine (calibrating radiologists) and LLM uncertainty expressions (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Marginal or no improvement over baselines**: Table 1 shows the proposed method performs similarly to simpler post-hoc calibration methods (Platt scaling, histogram binning) that do not require confidence distributions, questioning the usefulness of the added complexity (R1, R2, R4). One reviewer (R4) notes the method seems to remove outlier expressions and calibrates to the middle area.
- **Limited scope**: The method currently only works for fixed uncertainty phrases in isolation, not free-form generation with flexible uncertainty quantifiers (R2). It also focuses on binary classification, which may limit its impact (R3).
- **Motivation and justification for distributions is weak**: The link to uncertainty phrases appears superficial—they serve as names for distributions without strong justification (R2). Individual users often interpret phrases in consistent orders, making scalar values or simple binning potentially sufficient; at a population level, it is unclear why phrases should align with complex empirical distributions rather than a straightforward rubric (R2).
- **Complexity hinders adoption**: The mathematical modeling is dense and difficult to understand for non-experts (R4). The relative complexity to non-statisticians could limit adoption (R3). The candidate confidence distributions could be very large, making optimization slow (R3).
- **Missing deeper analysis of phrase semantics**: The paper lacks a deeper analysis and clarifying examples about the semantics and use of uncertainty phrases (R4). The introduction promises it can help radiologists report more consistently, but no evidence is provided (R4).
- **Unclear ground truth density determination**: It is unclear how the ground truth uncertainty distribution is reliably determined; Table 3 and Figure 5 show that the choice of density function impacts evaluation results, yet no guidance is given on how to collect these functions (R2).
- **Interpretability questions**: The proposed post-hoc calibration method makes the calibration map less intuitive (Figure 3), challenging claims of enhanced human interpretability (R2). The captions for Figure 4 and Table 1 highlight interpretability but the method appears to reduce it (R2).
- **Problematic attribution of uncertainty labels in radiologist experiment**: In the “Calibrating Radiologists” experiment (Section 4.2), confidence labels are extracted using Llama 3 (line 320–321) rather than from the radiologists’ own report-level confidence labels—this attribution seems problematic and needs clarification (R2).
- **Dubious LLM generation of distribution parameters**: It is questionable whether LLMs can accurately generate a sequence like `Beta(2, 3)` that reflects their internal belief; the in-context learning setup uses only 1 example (Appendix unclear) (R1). (Low reviewer confidence 2 on this point.)
- **Minor notation issue**: L265: $\triangle_{K-1}$ should be $\Delta^{K-1}$ (R1).
- **Formatting issue**: Formula 6 is outside the margin (R2).
- **Potential human mental model conflict** (R3 thought question): If humans already mentally correct for miscalibration, applying post-hoc calibration could lead to worse outcomes if their mental models no longer apply while the calibrated expressions are still imperfect—this is a human-factors concern.
