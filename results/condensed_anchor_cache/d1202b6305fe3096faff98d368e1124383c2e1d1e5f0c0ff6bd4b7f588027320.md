## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates Subset Adversarial Training (SAT), where adversarial examples (AEs) are constructed on only a subset of training data. Using class-wise (CSAT) and example-wise (ESAT) splits, it shows that robustness transfers to classes never attacked, hard examples (measured by entropy) provide better transfer (though the trend diminishes on more complex datasets), and that perturbing only 50% of data recovers most baseline AT performance, even on ImageNet. Experiments cover CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet-200, and transfer to SVHN, Oxford-Flowers-102, Caltech-256. The paper proposes a custom-balanced loss weighting and entropy-based metrics for subset selection.

### Strengths
- Addresses the relevant problem of robustness generalization to unseen classes, examples, and tasks.
- Well-written and easy to follow; experimental protocol is clearly described.
- Comprehensive empirical study with both CSAT and ESAT settings, entropy metrics, $L_2$ and $L_\infty$ attacks, and multiple datasets.
- Valuable insight: adversarial robustness can transfer to classes that were never adversarially attacked during training.
- Generating AEs on only 50% of data (or even 30% for downstream tasks) recovers most baseline AT performance, suggesting potential computational savings.
- Experiments demonstrate a clear correlation between class/sample entropy and robust accuracy on the subset.
- Robustness transfers to other datasets, indicating potential use in pretraining (minority positive reviewer 2, score 8, highlights this practical benefit).
- The paper proposes a method to estimate robustness transferability, useful for testing ML models when new classes/examples appear.

### Weaknesses
- Lacks a theoretical explanation for the observed transferability from subset A to B. It is unclear why certain classes (e.g., ‘cat’) transfer robustness better than others (e.g., ‘car’), or why a subset is sufficient. The paper does not provide mechanistic understanding (e.g., feature space analysis, proximity to decision boundary, breadth of activated feature maps).
- The paper does not clearly justify the practical benefit of SAT over full AT. Both approaches use the same labeled dataset and the same number of training examples; only AE generation time is reduced, yet the largest cost is model training. It is not shown that SAT achieves better robust accuracy than full AT, so the computational benefit is questionable (reviewer 1, score 6).
- It is unclear how to determine the optimal subset size for SAT in practice; guidelines are missing.
- The observed robustness transfer may be due to general model capacity rather than a property specific to adversarial training. Figure 4 shows clean accuracy follows a similar trend to robust accuracy, suggesting potential confounding with generalizability. The paper does not disentangle these effects.
- Only PGD-AT is evaluated. More advanced methods such as TRADES and AWP should be tested under the same settings to assess practical relevance.
- The downstream task transferability is similar to contrastive adversarial training, but the paper does not discuss the similarities, differences, or novelty in data selection compared to that line of work.
- The paper mentions that the trend of harder examples providing better robustness transfer diminishes with dataset complexity, but does not explain why (e.g., due to input space dimensionality or feature diversity). A detailed analysis on complex datasets is needed.
- The paper uses stronger data augmentation than usual (e.g., random crop and flip on CIFAR-10). The impact of these augmentations is not ablated.
- There is an error in the text: on page 6, lines 6 and 11, results are described for the class “car” but the numerical values correspond to the line “plane” in Figure 2.
- Figures are not readable when printed.
- Several questions remain that overlap with missing analysis: (i) What is the exact adversarial training process? Are all examples perturbed or only a subset? If only a subset, how is the proportion determined? (ii) What is the objective of transferring robustness across classes/tasks? If models are retrained on new tasks, why not simply adversarial train them at that point? (iii) Why does robustness transfer notably increase for smaller $\epsilon$ and decrease for larger $\epsilon$? (iv) Why does entropy correlate with transfer? (v) Could the authors provide ablation studies on the data augmentation methods?
