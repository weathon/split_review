## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes OASIS, a toolkit with four methods to measure and understand stereotypes in T2I models: Stereotype Score (M1) comparing attribute prevalence to societal priors (often 50% due to unknown prior), WALS (M2) for spectral variance along stereotypical attributes, StOP (U1) to discover internal associations in T2I models, and SPI (U2) to trace emergence of stereotypical attributes during generation. Applied to FLUX.1, SDv3, and others, OASIS finds strong stereotypes persist, worse for nationalities with lower internet footprints. Reviewers agree the problem is important and the approach is interesting, but concerns center around insufficient validation of metrics, reliance on biased CLIP, limited scope to nationality, and the use of real-world distributions as ground truth. One reviewer (R2) rated the paper highly (10) praising the novel contribution and clear methodology, while others (R1,R4,R5: 6; R3:8) noted significant weaknesses in clarity, validation, and broader applicability.

### Strengths
- Addresses a significant and socially important problem of measuring stereotypes in T2I models (R1,R3,R4,R5).
- Proposes a diverse set of metrics (Stereotype Score, WALS, StOP, SPI) that give a more complete picture (R1,R4,R5).
- Incorporates real-world census data to define expected rates of attributes (R4).
- Provides analysis of when stereotypes emerge (early generation process) (R1,R4).
- Majority of reviewers found the paper well-written, clear, easy to follow, with good visual presentation and terminology linking to examples (R3,R4,R5; contrast: R1 found it hard to follow).
- Analysis of nationalities with lower internet footprints highlights inclusion issues (R3).
- StOP and SPI offer insights into origins of stereotypes, beyond mere detection (R3,R5).
- Thorough testing across three popular models (R1,R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Validation of metrics**: Lack of validation that the metrics produce accurate results; CLIP’s well-documented biases may skew the stereotype score, but no analysis of how these inaccuracies propagate (R1,R4). Need for a smaller annotated dataset to validate (R4). No human evaluation to validate the measures (R1,R4).
- **WALS and equation confusion**: It is unclear which method for measuring δ<sub>A</sub> is used in the paper; justification and comparative analysis are missing (R4). Equation (6) in S3.2 does not make sense (R1). The text in S3.2 is confusingly described (R1).
- **Scope limited to nationality**: Predominantly focuses on broad nationalities; unclear how OASIS extends to other stereotypes like gender, race, or intersectional combinations (nationality × gender × profession) (R3,R4). Needs deeper discussion of limitations and types of stereotypes OASIS can or cannot handle (R3).
- **Real-world distribution as ground truth**: Using census data as expected rate may perpetuate societal stereotypes (e.g., if fewer women doctors, generating equal numbers is not necessarily a stereotype) (R4,R5). The definition of stereotypes and the fallback to 50% prior when unknown limit the contribution over prior work (R1). Distinction from bias detection is not fully explained; methodology seems similar to existing bias detection (R5).
- **LLM attribute generation**: The method of prompting LLMs to get stereotypical attributes is already common (R5 citing [1]).
- **Presentation hardness**: Paper is quite hard to follow for some metrics; descriptions overly complex and formulas obfuscate insights (R1). Core idea is simple but heavy notation (R1,R5).
- **Lack of benchmarking validation**: SPI analysis shown only on a handful of examples; need for larger-scale systematic analysis (e.g., top 100 contexts, average step, variance across attributes) (R4). Observations on trade-off between stereotype score and variance (Figure 5) not well-supported by correlation (R4).
- **No mitigations** proposed (R1).
- **Unclear figures**: Figure 3 points not clearly labeled (R4). Abstract wording “incorrectly categorizes biases as stereotypes” may be the opposite (R2). Use of alternative visual variation measures (e.g., HoG) not explored (R2).
- **Concerns about nationality prompting**: In racially diverse countries (e.g., USA) or culturally similar neighbors (e.g., North/South Korea), nationality-based prompting may rely on stereotypes to indicate citizenship (R3).
