## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates whether LLMs can reorganize their internal representations according to novel semantics provided in-context, using a synthetic graph tracing task where nodes are referenced by pretrained tokens (e.g., "apple", "bird") and connectivity is defined by a graph (grid, ring). The authors find that as context scales, representations abruptly reorganize to reflect the graph structure, but that strong semantic priors (e.g., days of the week) can prevent this reorganization from dominating the first principal components. They analogize to energy minimization and percolation theory. All reviewers found the problem interesting and the experimental design elegant, but concerns were raised about model generality, reliance on PCA for evidence, theoretical mismatches, and clarity of connections to percolation.

### Strengths
- The problem is interesting and important (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). The paper investigates an underexplored phenomenon – flexible re-organization of LLM representations during in-context learning.
- The experimental design is original and elegant: a synthetic graph tracing task with well-defined structures (grid, ring) allows controlled observation of representation changes (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). The task is simple and easy to reproduce, enabling future analysis (Reviewer 1).
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow; figures clearly convey the central findings (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). The flow mirrors a natural investigation.
- The authors ablate alternative hypotheses (Reviewer 1).
- The paper connects empirical observations to a theoretical foundation (energy minimization) (Reviewer 4). The findings provide insight into LLM abilities to zero-shot generalize to novel connective structures (Reviewer 2).
- The authors go to reasonable lengths to verify their claims (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses
- **Model generality is severely limited.** The study uses only Llama 3 8B (Reviewers 1, 4). Reviewer 1 stresses the need to verify findings on models of different sizes (e.g., 1B, 70B, 405B), different families (Gemma, Qwen, Mistral, OPT), and different types (instruct vs. non-instruct). Reviewer 4 similarly notes that extending to at least one more model class would be easy and necessary. Without this, results may reflect artifacts of this specific model/size.
- **Heavy reliance on PCA and lack of quantitative evidence for graph structure learning.** Multiple reviewers flagged this as a major weakness. Reviewer 2 notes the authors admit PCA can be misleading. Reviewer 3 argues that the connection between PCA plots (e.g., Figures 1(c), 2(c), 3(c)) and graph structure is not rigorously established; manually added vertices are needed to interpret the plots, raising concern that structures may be artifacts of visualization. Reviewer 3 calls for quantitative metrics that directly measure alignment between representations and graph structure (e.g., relating PCA eigenvectors to the adjacency matrix or Laplacian) rather than relying on qualitative visual patterns. The paper’s quantitative results in Section 4 help but require more discussion (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 4 also asks whether coordinates z2 and z3 align with the principal axes found by PCA.
- **Theoretical analysis mismatch: Equation (4) does not match the data generation process.** Reviewer 3 points out that Equation (4) computes the probability of seeing each node with random sampling with replacement, but the actual procedure starts with a random first token and then follows a random walk. This mismatch undermines the theoretical analysis. The authors should either modify the equation to reflect the random walk or provide a clear justification.
- **Percolation theory connection is unclear.** Reviewer 3 finds the link to percolation theory not clearly established; it is unclear how the concepts of connectivity and critical thresholds relate to the in-context learning task. The argument lacks a mechanistic link between observed transitions (Figure 8(b)) and percolation. Similarly, Reviewer 1 suggests the Dirichlet energy minimization analogy is contrived because the trivial solution (all nodes same embedding) would minimize energy, and the paper does not fully explore that or alternative frameworks (e.g., distance-preserving embeddings).
- **The task is artificial, and generalizability to natural language is unclear.** Reviewers 4 and 1 question how these findings extend to realistic language tasks. Reviewer 2 questions whether the finding is particularly surprising, given that LLMs can translate unseen languages entirely in-context, which constitutes a much more complex graph structure.
- **Mechanism for the abrupt representation shift is unexplored.** Reviewer 1 asks whether there is a connection to induction heads or transformer circuits that could cause the change. This missing mechanistic analysis weakens the interpretability of the results.
- **Missing experiments and ablations.** Reviewer 1 asks whether the model does not “understand” early examples before the shift, and whether repeating early exemplars would help. The same reviewer asks what happens when the number of concepts is increased – is there a point where the model can no longer shift structure? Reviewer 2 asks whether the authors plan to extend to more complicated graph structures (e.g., those harder to represent). Reviewer 3 notes that the window size N_w=200 (used in Section 3.1) exceeds the minimal context length for the shift (100), and asks which layer Figure 1(c) corresponds to.
- **Minor weakness:** Reviewers 1 and 4 note that the Dirichlet energy/percolation framing could be better connected to empirical observations; Reviewer 1 flags awkward phrasing at L269 (“Given that we empirically do not observe this to be the case, we can safely assume…”).
