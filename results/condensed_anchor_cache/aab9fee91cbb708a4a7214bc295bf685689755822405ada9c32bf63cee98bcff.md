## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies how to incorporate categorical features (price bucket and brand category) into GNN-based user-item recommendation. It compares three approaches: (1) one-hot encoding as node features, (2) category-value nodes in a tripartite graph, and (3) hyperedges connecting items sharing the same attribute. The authors propose a hyperedge-based model that applies GCN for node aggregation and UniSAGE for hyperedge aggregation, trained with Bayesian Personalized Ranking (BPR) loss. Experiments on Amazon Grocery, Amazon Tools, and Yelp datasets show that the hyperedge method outperforms the other two variants and several baseline recommenders. The paper focuses exclusively on user-item collaborative filtering, not session-based recommendation.

### Strengths
- The paper studies an important task: integrating item categorical features into GNN collaborative filtering.
- The hyperedge-based approach is simple and its adoption in graph-based recommendation is relatively recent.
- The authors clearly describe the different ways of handling categorical features and outline differences from existing work.
- Experiments are conducted on three real-world datasets (Amazon Grocery, Amazon Tools, Yelp), providing a broad evaluation setting.
- The hyperedge method achieves the best results across all three datasets in Table 1.
- The paper is well written and easy to follow.

### Weaknesses
- Limited novelty: The idea of connecting nodes sharing the same attribute via hyperedges has been explored previously (e.g., Wu et al. 2021, Knowledge-Based Systems). The paper’s application to collaborative filtering is straightforward, lacking new technical insights. The justification for extending session-based hyperedge methods to user-item systems is not sufficiently argued. (All reviewers; one reviewer [score 6] noted that after the rebuttal these concerns were adequately addressed, while the other three [score 3] maintained the novelty is too limited.)
- The method considers only two specific categorical features (price bucket and brand category), limiting its generalizability. The paper does not discuss how to handle multiple types of attributes or their combinations, nor the effect of different numbers of distinct values per feature.
- No code was released at review time, hindering reproducibility.
- Experimental validation lacks rigor: no repeated runs or statistical tests (e.g., t-tests) are reported. Evaluation metrics are given only at high cut-offs (50, 100); lower cut-offs (10, 20) are not reported, and the authors did not confirm whether the observed trends hold there. Additionally, baselines were used with default parameters instead of being tuned for the specific use case, which may lead to unfair comparisons.
- The reviewer noted that only two baseline methods for incorporating categorical features were compared (one-hot encoding and category-value nodes); given that the paper actually includes three comparisons, this perception indicates either insufficient coverage or unclear presentation.
- Missing definitions and minor errors: In Equation (1), \(\tilde{d}_j, \tilde{d}_i\) and \(N(v)\) are not defined; \(z_u\) and \(z_i\) on page 5 are not mathematically defined. Typos include “use-item-attribute graph” (p.3), a sentence needing rewrite (“Another way…”, p.4), and “each datasets” (p.7). Figure 2 is low resolution and not vectorized.
- The time and storage complexity of constructing hyperedges, especially when many categorical features are present, is not analyzed.
- The paper does not investigate how the number of distinct values per categorical feature affects performance.
- (Note of reviewer disagreement: One reviewer (score 6, confidence 4) considered the weaknesses adequately addressed after the author rebuttal. The other three reviewers (score 3, confidence 4) continued to view the novelty and experimental setup as insufficient.)
