## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes TICK, a fully automated evaluation protocol that uses LLMs to generate instruction-specific checklists (YES/NO questions) for evaluating instruction-following, and STICK for self-refinement and Best-of-N selection. Experiments claim improved alignment with human preferences (46.4%→52.2%) and generation quality gains (+7.8% on LiveBench reasoning, +6.3% on WildBench). While reviewers acknowledge contributions in scalability, interpretability, and thorough experimental analysis, concerns center on limited novelty, insufficient evaluation metrics, restricted experimental scope, missing methodology details, and lack of efficiency discussion. Scores range from 5 to 6 (confidences 4-5), with reviewers 2 and 4 being more positive and reviewers 1 and 3 more critical.

### Strengths
- TICK enhances transparency and interpretability by decomposing instruction evaluation into a series of specific YES/NO questions, enabling fine-grained identification of strengths and weaknesses in model outputs (Reviewer 3).
- The method removes the major constraint of manually constructing checklists that existed in prior works (e.g., InfoBench), significantly improving scalability of automated instruction-following benchmarks (Reviewer 4).
- Comprehensive experimental analysis with both automated and human consistency evaluations across multiple benchmarks (Reviewers 1, 3).
- Interesting and novel finding that the generated checklists can help LLMs refine their initial responses and improve generation quality (Reviewers 2, 4).
- Thorough validation of LLM-generated checklists against human-generated checklists, providing useful insights for practitioners using LLM-as-judge (Reviewer 2).
- The paper is well-written, well-organized, and easy to follow (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- The topic of LLMs as judges is highly relevant and findings offer significant insights for the industry (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns**: The core idea of using decomposed checklists for instruction evaluation, validation, and refinement is not new—prior works (FollowBench, InfoBench, Self-Contrast) already explored similar decomposition techniques. This paper's main contribution (enabling LLMs to generate their own checklists) is a straightforward prompting approach, and the fundamental differences and substantive contributions need clearer definition (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). Additionally, the approach requires LLMs to follow complex instructions to generate the checklist, placing even higher demands on model capability than the instruction-following task itself (Reviewer 2).
- **Insufficient evaluation metrics for checklist quality**: Checklist similarity is measured using BLEU and ROUGE, which are lexical-matching metrics unreliable for knowledge-intensive tasks. These should be replaced with semantic similarity metrics (e.g., BERTScore) as used in related rubric evaluation work. Furthermore, reporting the percentage of recalled human-crafted checklist items and the percentage of precise LLM-generated items would be more informative. Missing manual annotation results for checklist quality evaluation (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- **Limited experimental scope and baselines**: The study examines only three benchmarks (Internal, InfoBench, WildBench). Comparisons of TICK to other evaluation methods are limited to direct scoring and an ablated Check-then-Score version, excluding comparisons with fine-tuned models or established frameworks. Self-refinement baselines are insufficient—only vanilla self-refinement is compared, requiring additional strong baselines for a comprehensive understanding of STICK's effectiveness (Reviewers 1, 2).
- **Missing methodological details**: Insufficient explanation of the construction details and statistics of the Internal dataset (Reviewer 1). No discussion of human annotation details, including demographics of annotators, training procedures, and internal agreement among annotators (Reviewer 4).
- **Alignment and validity concerns**: The preference labeling approach of annotators does not fully align with TICK—human annotation considers response quality while TICK only considers instruction-following ability (Reviewer 1). Low inter-annotator agreement for direct scoring (0.194) raises concerns, as the study only demonstrates TICK's effectiveness through pairwise correlation with human annotations; if inter-annotator agreement for pairwise scoring is similarly low, it may undermine validity (Reviewer 1).
- **Computational expense**: The existing design is computationally expensive during inference due to large token requirements and multiple generations during self-refinement stages. Distillation or reducing this expense would be a valuable direction (Reviewer 2). There is also a lack of in-depth discussion of evaluation efficiency (Reviewer 3).
- **Minor issues and unaddressed questions**: Figure 3(a) caption appears out of sequence or unclear (Reviewer 1). Self-refinement using STICK results in a minor decline in the last iteration, requiring further explanation (Reviewer 1). Unaddressed questions remain about: effectiveness for complex logical relationships (selective, chain, or combined relationships as in ComplexBench); applicability to broader task types like mathematics and programming where instruction-following is less central; and performance of weaker/smaller LLMs in decomposing checklists and accurately scoring (Reviewer 3).
