## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes APOHF, a prompt optimization method that uses only human preference feedback (pairwise comparisons) rather than numeric scores. APOHF iteratively selects prompt pairs inspired by dueling bandits, trains an MLP performance predictor on the feedback, and chooses prompts balancing exploitation and exploration. Experiments cover user instruction optimization (rhymes, word sorting, sentiment from BigBench), text-to-image generation, and response optimization. The authors claim efficient prompt selection with few feedback instances. Reviewers are split: some (scores 5,6,6) praise novelty and efficacy; others (scores 3,3) question practicality, generality, and experimental rigor.

### Strengths
- The problem is important and the motivation is clear: many real-world tasks lack reliable numeric scores for prompts, making human preference feedback a natural alternative (Reviewers 1,2,3,4,5).
- The algorithm design is novel in combining dueling bandits with an MLP utility predictor, and it is theoretically grounded in the appendix (Reviewers 1,2,5).
- APOHF outperforms baselines (e.g., random selection, fixed prompt) across multiple tasks, and the wide range of experiments (text-to-text, text-to-image, response optimization) further validates its effectiveness (Reviewers 1,2,5).
- The paper is well-written, clear, and easy to follow (Reviewers 1,3,4,5).

### Weaknesses
1. **Practicality concerns (Reviewer 3, strongly negative, confidence 4)**  
   - The core assumption that users will provide preference feedback on their own prompts is questionable given LLMs’ improving zero-shot/few-shot instruction-following capabilities. The paper does not clarify when manual human feedback is uniquely valuable.  
   - Automated prompt optimization (e.g., self-reflection, reasoning errors, self-refining) may be more useful; no comparison is offered.  
   - Training a reward model on human preferences once and then generalizing during inference could be more efficient than repeatedly querying humans per task. The advantages of APOHF over a pre-trained reward model are not discussed.  
   - The experiments are limited to a few tasks (rhymes, word sorting, sentiment, text-to-image); missing tasks like math, coding, and role-play further reduce the perceived practical scope.

2. **Comparison with state-of-the-art prompt optimization methods (Reviewer 2, confidence 5; Reviewer 5, confidence 5)**  
   - The paper excludes comparisons with SOTA methods (e.g., TextGrad, APE, OPRO, APO) because they require numeric scores. However, surrogate metrics (e.g., embedding similarity between LLM output and ground truth) could enable such comparisons on tasks with available ground truth (e.g., rhymes, word sorting in Table 6). A detailed analysis of at least APO and OPRO is missing.  
   - For the sentiment task (Table 6), the initial instruction score is reported as 0, which seems implausible for a simple “determine sentiment” prompt; this ambiguity needs clarification.

3. **Prompt domain generation and static set (Reviewer 2, confidence 5; Reviewer 4, confidence 5)**  
   - The process of generating the discrete prompt domain via ChatGPT in-context learning lacks detail. No experiments analyze how different generation methods affect APOHF’s performance.  
   - The candidate prompt set is generated once and remains static throughout optimization, limiting the discovery of prompts outside the initial set. A more adaptive approach (e.g., generating new prompts based on the learned scoring function) would be stronger, and the current weak synergy between the two components is a limitation (Reviewer 4).

4. **Dependency on the MLP performance predictor (Reviewer 2, confidence 5)**  
   - The choice of embedding model and the MLP architecture introduces significant variability. The paper does not analyze how these choices affect final performance, which weakens confidence in robustness.

5. **Dependency on the generator LLM and lack of generalization experiments (Reviewer 4, confidence 5)**  
   - The scoring function is learned from responses generated by a specific LLM; prompt quality is a function of that LLM. No experiments test whether the learned scoring function transfers to a different generator LLM or to different tasks (e.g., QA vs. summarization). This limits generality.

6. **Assumptions about human feedback – noise, consistency, and stability (Reviewer 1, confidence 4)**  
   - The BTL model assumes consistent, transitive preferences; real-world human feedback may be noisy and context-dependent. The paper does not examine robustness to feedback inconsistencies.  
   - The method assumes user preferences are stable across iterations, but in dynamic tasks they may shift. No experiments test handling of preference drifts.  
   - The method relies on an initial task description (examples) that may be biased or incomplete; there is no mechanism to iteratively expand the prompt domain based on feedback.

7. **Lack of theoretical justification for certain design choices (Reviewer 4, confidence 5)**  
   - Choosing the highest-scored prompt from history as one of the two prompts in each iteration is not theoretically justified beyond empirical observation.  
   - The claim that using a text encoder avoids the need for a whitebox LLM is not clearly advantageous (whitebox access is no worse than an open-source encoder).

8. **Limited practical significance and resource costs (Reviewer 5, confidence 5)**  
   - For tasks like text-to-image, optimizing single samples (e.g., “garden” or “street”) has limited practical value; meta-prompt optimization would be more impactful.  
   - The paper does not report total API calls or token consumption, making it hard to assess cost.  
   - In Figure 3, APOHF’s score curves lack fluctuations (scores seem to stay constant), which is unexpected and needs explanation.  
   - The test set size is not reported.

9. **Prior work diminishes novelty (Reviewer 5, confidence 5)**  
   - A prior paper from Harvard/MIT (PROMST, arXiv:2402.08702) also integrates human feedback for prompt optimization in multi-step tasks. The contribution of APOHF is less clear without a comparison or discussion of differences.

10. **Minor presentation issue (Reviewer 5, confidence 5)**  
    - The use of square, circular, and triangular symbols in figures makes the curves hard to read; a revised style is recommended.
