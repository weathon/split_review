## Merged Review

### Summary
SEAL (Semantic-Augmented Imitation Learning via Language Model) is a hierarchical imitation learning framework that uses LLMs to generate and label sub-goal representations from language instructions without prior task hierarchy knowledge. It employs a dual-encoder structure combining supervised LLM-guided sub-goal learning with unsupervised Vector Quantization (VQ) for sub-goal representations, plus a transition-augmented low-level planner. Experiments on two discrete grid-world domains (KeyDoor and Grid-World) show improved task success over existing HIL and LLM-based planning methods, especially with small expert datasets. R2 and R4 (scores 5, 6) find the approach innovative and effective, while R1 and R3 (scores 3, 3) view the contribution as limited and the experimental evaluation insufficient.

### Strengths
- **Important problem**: Learning semantically meaningful sub-goals from unsegmented demonstrations without dense expert labels is a well-motivated challenge (R1, R3).
- **Automatic sub-goal definition and labeling**: LLMs are used to autonomously define sub-goal spaces and label demonstration trajectories, eliminating the need for predefined task hierarchies or extensive manual labeling (R1, R2, R4).
- **Innovative use of LLMs beyond plan generation**: The labeling mechanism directly supports policy training, demonstrating a novel way to harness LLMs for hierarchical task decomposition (R2).
- **Dual-encoder structure**: Combining supervised LLM-guided sub-goal learning with unsupervised Vector Quantization is an interesting design, though its benefit is debated (R1, R4). R4 specifically notes that this strengthens sub-goal representation robustness.
- **Comprehensive experiments**: The paper compares SEAL to many baselines (R1, R3).
- **Transition-augmented low-level planner**: Adapts well to intermediate sub-goal transitions, improving performance on long-horizon tasks (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Simple, limited experimental domains**: All experiments are conducted only on two discrete grid-world environments with few objects (n=3) and simple state/action spaces. This severely limits the evidence for SEAL's effectiveness on complex, continuous, or partially observable tasks (R1, R2, R3, R4). R1 notes the environment is represented as text, constraining applicability to any domain not easily textified.
- **No ablation studies for key components**: The paper claims VQ and the transition-augmented low-level planner as key contributions but provides no ablation experiments to isolate their effects. For example: (a) does VQ help compared to a model without quantization? (b) does the transition weighting benefit the low-level policy beyond a shared-weight baseline? (c) how do supervised and unsupervised losses interact, and what are concrete failure cases of using either alone? (R1, R2, R3)
- **Heavy reliance on LLM quality and prompt engineering**: The method depends on the LLM's ability to generate accurate sub-goal hierarchies and map states to sub-goals. The prompts are highly specific to the tested environments, raising concerns about transferability and generalizability to new tasks (R1, R2, R4). R2 questions how a user should decide what to include in prompts.
- **Limited contribution of the low-level planner**: The only influence on the low-level agent is assigning higher weights to states with sub-goal transitions. This is considered a minor addition (R1, R3). R3 further asks: how are the intermediate states (transition points) identified to apply these weights?
- **Lack of comparisons to relevant methods**: (a) Data-efficient RL/IL methods such as Glide and EfficientZero are not compared, despite SEAL's claimed data-efficiency advantage (R2). (b) Other task decomposition and skill library generation approaches (e.g., Voyager) are not included (R1, R3). (c) Methods that learn rational sub-goals from demonstrations and instructions, or ground language plans through counterfactual perturbations, are more relevant baselines than some current ones, especially for the discrete environments used (R3).
- **State-subgoal matching scalability**: The method of using LLMs to classify states into sub-goals based on natural language descriptions may fail in more complex environments with many similar objects (e.g., multiple identical boxes in Sokoban) or continuous states/actions (e.g., robotics). Existing work (e.g., LOTUS, Skill-based Model-based RL) has already explored the difficulty of such matching (R1, R2).
- **Partially observable environments not addressed**: The method assumes sub-goals can be decomposed offline, but in partially observable settings, initially-generated sub-goals may become invalid and need adaptation (R1).
- **Missing justification for LLM necessity**: The paper does not justify why LLMs are required over simpler alternatives, such as abstract task descriptions from humans (R3), or why the dual-encoder is better than directly using an open-source LLM (e.g., Llama) to represent sub-goals (R1). No quantitative or qualitative comparison is provided (R3).
- **Codebook size not discussed**: R2 asks how the codebook size for the VQ module was chosen and how it affects model performance. No ablation or sensitivity analysis is given.
- **Unclear motivation for each baseline**: R3 questions what specific claim each baseline comparison supports—the mapping between baselines and claims is not clearly explained.
- **Algorithm complexity vs. domain simplicity**: Algorithm 1 includes multiple loss terms, but the tested domains are simple discrete grid-worlds. It is unclear why such sophistication is needed when simpler methods handle more complex domains (R3).
