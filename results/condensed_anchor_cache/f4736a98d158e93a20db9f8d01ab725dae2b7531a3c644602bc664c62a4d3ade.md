## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes QuantFormer, a transformer-based architecture with vector quantization for forecasting single-neuron calcium imaging responses in mouse visual cortex. The model is pre-trained via masked autoencoding and then fine-tuned for activity classification and forecasting. Reviewers find the idea interesting and the paper well-written, with some ablation experiments and interpretability steps, but raise significant concerns about the evaluation protocol, missing baselines, insufficient justification for the quantization approach, and potential data leakage. All four reviewers assign a score of 3, indicating weak accept/borderline, but the reviews collectively highlight fundamental methodological and validation issues.

### Strengths
- **Novel architecture**: The vector quantization approach is interesting and shows some improvement in forecasting; the idea of reframing prediction as classification via a codebook is original (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- **Interpretability efforts**: The analysis of the neural codebook and t-SNE of neuron tokens (Appendix A-10) provides a first step toward interpretability, which is important for biological applications (R4).
- **Qualitative comparisons**: The cross-former vs. QuantFormer visualizations (A6-A7) are informative (R4).
- **Ablation experiments**: Some ablation studies are included (e.g., Table 3 comparing quantization to autoencoder), helping to disentangle component importance (R2, R1).
- **Code provided**: The authors provide the source code, aiding reproducibility (R4).
- **Positive scaling results**: The model demonstrates performance across different stimuli and subjects, though reviewers question the strength of this evidence (R1, R2).

### Weaknesses
1. **Insufficient justification for vector quantization** – The paper does not adequately explain why vector quantization is superior to other approaches for neural activity forecasting. This motivation, especially in Section 3.3, is insufficient compared to alternatives like direct regression or masked modeling without quantization (R1, R3, R2). An ablation comparing quantization to a simple linear layer after the transformer encoder (as in BERT) is requested (R1). The small fixed codebook (32 entries) limits the model's ability to represent novel activity patterns not seen during pre-training, making it strictly not image-computable (R3).

2. **Missing baselines and incomplete comparisons** – The evaluation lacks comparisons to several relevant state-of-the-art methods:
   - Neural-specific forecasting models: Neural Data Transformers (NDT1 – Ye & Pandarinath 2021), MTM / "universal translator" (Zhang et al. 2024), and Zhu et al. 2022 (for calcium data) are neither cited nor compared (R1, R3, R4).
   - Simple but strong baselines: PSTH (peri-stimulus time histogram) computed from training-set repeats is a critical missing null model; linear encoding models that predict responses from stimulus alone are also absent (R2, R3).
   - The paper should also compare to models pretrained on SENSORIUM 2022 data (R3).
   - The existing time-series transformers compared (e.g., Crossformer) are not strong baselines, as evidenced by their very poor performance in Figure 3 (R1, R2). Without knowing if these implementations replicate original results on their own benchmarks, the comparison is weak (R2).

3. **Evaluation on visually evoked responses is problematic** – The primary evaluation uses visually evoked responses where neural firing is dominated by the visual stimulus:
   - The null hypothesis (PSTH) would likely account for most of the explainable variance; the paper must quantify how much variance the PSTH captures (e.g., correlation upper bound using repeated stimuli) and show that the forecasting model exceeds it. The 0.33 correlation in Table 2 is suspiciously low for a model that should beat PSTH (R2, R4).
   - The model essentially "squeezes" stimulus-driven patterns into a combination of neuron ID and stimulus tokens; without evidence against this interpretation, the forecasting value is unclear (R2).
   - If forecasting is the goal, the authors should evaluate on datasets without such strong external drive (e.g., spontaneous activity or datasets with unique stimuli) (R2, R3).

4. **Potential data leakage and confounding in training/test protocol** – The pre-training phase may include data from the same neurons and images that appear in the test set. Since the Allen dataset includes repeated stimuli, the model could learn mean responses to specific stimuli, contaminating generalization measurements (R3). The authors state they do not use neuron IDs during pre-training, but this does not prevent the model from learning stimulus-conditioned averages across neurons (R3). A cleaner test would involve completely held-out neurons and images (R3).

5. **Limited downstream tasks and scaling analysis** – Only two downstream tasks (activity classification and forecasting) are evaluated, insufficient to demonstrate the model's versatility as a "foundation model." More complex tasks such as decoding behavior, predicting responses to novel stimuli, or real-time optogenetic applications are missing (R1, R3). No scaling analysis with increasing data size or model complexity is provided to support foundation model claims (R1).

6. **Unclear evaluation metrics and normalization scheme** – The correlation computation is not clearly defined (over what dimensions, how averaged). The normalization scheme (using an "accumulated gradient") is poorly explained; a clear definition and motivation for why it improves performance are needed (R3, R4). The effect of normalization on different models’ performance should be analyzed to ensure fairness; visual comparison of normalized vs. unnormalized responses would help (R3).

7. **Biological validity concerns** – It is unclear what the upper bound for correlation is given noise in single-trial neural data; repeated stimuli should be used to estimate the noise ceiling (R4). The model should be tested on reproducing known linear-nonlinear properties of neurons (R4). The use of raw fluorescence traces rather than deconvolved or spiking data is at odds with the stated motivation of sparsity; fluorescence is less sparse, so SENSORIUM or spiking data would be a better match (R3).

8. **Incomplete or incorrect literature review** – Several citations are inaccurate or missing:
   - The SENSORIUM dataset from Wang et al. 2023 is incorrectly cited; that paper does not use the SENSORIUM 2022/2023 competition data (R4).
   - Lines 176-177 claim all encoding/decoding methods rely on spiking data, but works like Wang et al., Sinz et al., Antoniades et al., Turishcheva et al. use calcium traces (R4).
   - The statement that most prior approaches rely on trial-averaged data and behavioral variables is incorrect for at least three of the cited papers (R4).
   - Missing important works: Zhang et al. 2024 (neuron forecasting), Zhu et al. 2022, Ye & Pandarinath 2021, and POYO (Azabou et al. 2024) for neuron-specific tokens (R1, R3, R4).

9. **Writing and clarity issues** – Several sections are confusing:
   - The dataset description (Section 4.1) should explicitly state the number of unique neurons (about 8500 if non-repeating across sessions, 3000 if repeating) rather than 230,000 traces, which gives a misleading impression of data diversity (R4).
   - The generalization experiment is not clearly explained: how were neuron tokens trained/tested when transferring to a new mouse? Were those neurons seen in pre-training? Other models’ generalization ability is not reported (R4).
   - The role of the stimulus token is ambiguous: is it per unique image or per stimulus category (natural images, gratings, etc.)? The statement that stimulus information can be considered encoding is confusing (R1, R4).
   - Equation (2): why is loss computed on both masked and unmasked portions? Rationale for balancing components is needed (R1).
   - The classification into "active" vs. "non-active" at 10% more activity seems arbitrary, and baseline activity computation is undefined given back-to-back image presentation in Allen (R3).

10. **Concerns about scientific claims** – Claiming a "foundation model for visual cortex" is overstated: the model is not image-computable, is trained on a finite set of stimuli, and is evaluated only on the same dataset (R3). The "real-time optogenetic intervention" motivation is not tested (R3). The claim that "brain signals can be encoded with 32 indices" (lines 392-394) needs more interpretation and hypothesis (R1). The t-SNE visualization in Figure A-10 does not clearly separate low- and high-activated neurons, contradicting the text (R4).
