## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Causal Differential Networks (CDN) to identify direct intervention targets in perturbation experiments (e.g., single-cell Perturb-seq). The method first separately infers causal graphs from observational and interventional data using a pre-trained amortized causal discovery model (Wu et al., 2024). It then trains an axial-attention-based classifier (Ho et al., 2020) on the paired graph adjacency matrices to predict which variables were targeted. Evaluations are performed on seven single-cell transcriptomics datasets (five Perturb-seq, two chemical perturbation studies) and on synthetic data.

### Strengths
- The problem of identifying direct perturbation targets from high-dimensional biological data is relevant and important.
- The paper is well-structured and clearly written.
- Extensive evaluation is presented on both real-world and synthetic datasets, with non-trivial performance improvements over the considered baselines.
- The use of supervised causal discovery to impose inductive biases in a biological setting is interesting, even though asymptotic guarantees are lacking.
- The approach offers a scalable way to identify perturbed variables, which is meaningful for biology.

### Weaknesses
1. **Novelty and methodological contribution are questioned.**  
   Multiple reviewers (R2, R3, and to a lesser extent R1) find the contribution marginal. CDN is essentially a direct combination of two existing algorithms (Wu et al., 2024 for amortized causal discovery; Ho et al., 2020 for axial attention). Modifications to the axial attention (operating over paired adjacency matrices, pooling over incoming edges) are straightforward adaptations. R2 further argues that learning two graphs separately and comparing them is “overkill” for the task. R3 states that extracting edge embeddings from a pre‑trained model and training a one‑layer classifier is a simple supervised learning task without biology‑specific motivation.

2. **Causal framing is misleading and assumptions are absent.**  
   R3 argues that the method is not inherently causal: there is no mechanistic model, intervention modeling, or causal graph; using a pre‑trained causal discovery model does not make the classifier causal. The formalization of “targets” may not be appropriate for biological systems where conditional independencies can change in ways not restricted to directly perturbed variables, especially if the true process is not a structural causal model (SCM). The paper claims “it is common to assume” an SCM (l. 50), but R3 disagrees that this assumption is widely made in computational biology, and acyclicity is generally not considered a good assumption. R2 requires a formal list of identifiability assumptions (e.g., faithfulness) to be stated. R4 notes that the claim that only “informative differences in causal graphs” matter may break down in non‑linear scenarios.

3. **Missing basic baselines and comparisons to existing biology‑specific tools.**  
   R1 requests a trivial baseline that simply selects the *n* genes with the largest log‑fold change, which would help quantify the added value of causal modeling. R3 notes the paper does not compare to any prior specialist methods used to predict intervention targets in biology applications, so the practical usefulness to the biology community is hard to assess.

4. **Linear/MLP baselines are flawed, making comparisons invalid.**  
   R3 points out that the LINEAR and MLP baselines “take as input the mean expression of all perturbation targets, plus the top 2000 highly‑variable genes.” Since the perturbation targets are what need to be predicted, it is impossible to use their mean expression as input. This makes the baseline design nonsensical; a sensible baseline would use covariance/correlation information or the same pre‑trained edge embeddings used by CDN.

5. **Lack of discussion on off‑target effects.**  
   R1 raises that the evaluation does not explicitly address scenarios where an intervention directly affects multiple genes (including off‑target effects). The robustness of the method in such cases is unclear.

6. **Insufficient details on supervised learning setup and data generation.**  
   R2 asks for explicit description of the data generation process (distributions, causal relationships, noise) used to train the supervised model, as this is critical for validity. R2 also questions how the two graphs are learned separately and whether constraints prevent them from varying too much; handling of discrepancies between graphs that are not attributable to the intervention is not clarified. R4 asks whether the causal featurizer is fixed or fine‑tuned across datasets (using the same model for every dataset is argued to be unreasonable) and why separate models for inverse covariance and correlation are trained when both derive from the covariance matrix. R3 and R4 note uncertainty about the quality of ground‑truth labels for drug perturbation datasets; filtering to perturbations with over 10 differentially expressed genes (l. 294) ensures strong effects but does not improve label accuracy.

7. **Motivation for decoupling graph learning is unconvincing.**  
   R2 argues that identifying intervention targets is, in principle, easy: a variable is intervened iff its conditional distribution changes given any other set, which can be tested by learning the Markov blanket of a domain‑index variable. This can be done efficiently with greedy algorithms, making the claim that existing methods “scale poorly” and “must search combinatorial spaces” puzzling. R3 also finds the discussion of forward versus backward prediction confusing as a motivation.

8. **Theoretical context claims are vacuous.**  
   R3 says that Section 3.4’s claim that the function class (axial attention) is “well‑specified” is trivial and provides no insight; it does not address whether the method would work in the large‑sample limit or give any guarantees.

9. **Out‑of‑distribution generalization is not addressed.**  
   R4 notes that supervised learning offers no guarantee when no labels are available; the experiments on “unseen cell lines” appear to be in‑distribution (same variable space) and do not test OOD scenarios. R2 also asks about the generalizability of the supervised model across different domains.

10. **Minor clarity issues.**  
    R4 asks what “DE” refers to in Figure 3; R3 finds the description of the correlation metric (l. 335) unclear; R2 requests additional literature review on early work in causal change detection and supervised causal discovery (e.g., Hoover 1990, Tian & Pearl 2001, Huang et al. 2020, Jaber et al. 2020; Li et al. 2020, Dai et al. 2023). These affect the paper’s presentation quality.
