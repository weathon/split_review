## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses the problem of predicting the order in which edges were formed in a graph without time information, an important task in network science. The main contribution is a new training framework that uses a diffusion model (TopoEvoDiff) to generate augmented temporal networks, thereby mitigating data scarcity. The underlying prediction model is a three-layer vanilla neural network that receives embeddings of two edges and outputs a probability of which edge appeared earlier, formulated as a pairwise ordering problem. Experiments on synthetic and four real networks claim a 13.7% improvement in accuracy over baselines. However, reviewers raise serious concerns about problem formulation, missing experimental details, metric interpretability, and support for claims.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important and under‑studied problem in network science (R1, R4).
- The overall idea of using a diffusion model for data augmentation to overcome limited temporally labeled data is logical and relevant (R2, R3).
- The method shows a notable improvement in prediction accuracy (R2) and is as good as or better than state‑of‑the‑art baselines (R3).
- The framework eliminates the need to retrain for each network type, offering potential efficiency and generalization (R2).
- Preliminaries and Subsection 3.1 contain clear technical details (R4).
- The inclusion of baseline descriptions in the appendix is a good space‑saving approach (R4).
- The paper is relatively easy to understand (R1, R3), although clarity was judged poor by another reviewer (R4). The idea of generating many network examples for history reconstruction is well‑presented to a non‑expert (R3).
- The approach is moderately novel in applying diffusion models to a new task (R1), though another reviewer saw low originality in the diffusion‑for‑augmentation component itself (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Problem formulation mismatch.** The paper claims to predict edge generation times, but the model only predicts the pairwise order of edge appearance, not actual times. This is a fundamentally different task (R1, R4). For example, Peixoto & Rosvall (2017) model temporal networks as sequences plus waiting‑time distributions, while Junuthula et al. (2019) use temporal point processes for continuous times. The authors do not bridge this gap (R1).
- **Severe lack of experimental details, making the paper non‑replicable.**
  - The edge sampling procedure is not described (R1, R3, R4): e.g., is 50% of edges selected randomly, via random walk, or how? (R1).
  - The training/evaluation process is barely explained (R4). Key steps are left to a vague phrase: “In this experiment, conducted under the CPNN framework, we generated augmented temporal networks using …”. This is insufficient; e.g., how are the 100 timestamps from one network generated? Are outputs derived from two different timestamp networks? Do you create |E_i| – |E_{i-1}| data points per timestamp? (R4).
  - The generation process for the network in experiments is unclear: does it start empty, with 50% of edges, or otherwise? How is the next edge selected – by comparing all possible edges? (R4).
  - Details of the “trainingNet” and “targetNet” are barely mentioned (R4).
  - Only four real‑world networks are used, limiting demonstration of adaptability across domains (R2, R4). More diverse networks (social, PPI) are needed (R2).
  - Basic network statistics beyond node counts of synthetic networks are omitted (R4).
- **Evaluation metric is not interpretable.**
  - Table 1’s relative ranking metric lacks context: what is a good value? (R1, R3). The accuracy measure (edge generation order accuracy) is not clearly defined; it is unclear how it is computed for real networks (R3, R4). For example, do you take an entire network and two edges that are consecutive, or one in the network and one not? (R4).
  - No statistical tests are reported, so the claimed improvement may not be significant (R3).
- **Claims not supported by evidence.**
  - Figure 4: generated networks have clustering coefficients in the 0.8–1.0 range, which is almost never seen in real networks. The original networks’ clustering coefficients are not visible in the figure, making the claim that “clustering coefficient distributions closely match” unverifiable. In fact, the lower clustering from the AAAI baseline is more representative of real networks (R1).
  - Subsection 4.6 reports that the model can generate almost the same network as the original, which suggests severe overfitting rather than meaningful augmentation (R4).
- **Missing comparisons.** The paper lacks comparison with other generative or data‑augmentation techniques, such as GANs, which could highlight unique advantages of diffusion models in this context (R2). The effect of augmentation magnitude (number of augmented networks) does not improve performance (Subsection 4.4); it is unclear whether the model was tested without any augmentation (i.e., a baseline) (R4).
- **Clarity and presentation issues.**
  - The paper is not self‑contained; crucial details are omitted (R4). While one reviewer found the presentation clear (R3), others note significant sloppiness: proofreading errors (lines 238–239, 257–258), Table 1 caption missing explanation of parentheses, and the table shrunken beyond readability (R1). Figure 2’s output label “Edge time prediction” is misleading because the model only outputs which of two edges came earlier (R4). Section 3.4 on the graph diffusion model reads as a generic description rather than focusing on the paper’s specific contribution (R4).
- **Low originality.** The use of diffusion models to enhance training via data augmentation has been applied to many other problems. The only potentially novel aspect – application to edge‑timestamp inference – is undermined by the fact that the model does not actually predict timestamps (R4). R1 also notes the contribution is only moderate.
- **Real‑world relevance unclear.** Even in the best case, the predicted order is often wrong; the practical advantage of reconstructing this order is not explained (R3).
