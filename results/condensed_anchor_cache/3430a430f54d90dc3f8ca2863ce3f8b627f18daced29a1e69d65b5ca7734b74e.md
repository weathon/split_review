## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a polarity-aware semantic retrieval task and two metrics (Polarity Score and Semantic Similarity Score). The approach fine-tunes sentence embedding models (four models) on two datasets (SST‑2 for sentiment, News Headlines for sarcasm detection) using contrastive losses (triplet, etc.) with generated examples that pair similar meaning but opposite polarity.

### Strengths
- [R1] Proposed a new formulation of the text retrieval task.
- [R1] Extensive experiments on two datasets with consistent results; confirmed previous findings that sentence‑embedding models perform well in zero‑shot classification and unsupervised retrieval.
- [R2] Sound and rigorous experimentation, with reproducible results hosted from an anonymous repository.
- [R2] Polarity scores for all loss configurations, semantic similarity scores for all loss configurations, and aggregated scores across all configurations are reported meticulously.
- [R2, R3] Crystal clear presentation on the system, training configuration, data generation, and tasks (R2). Paper is self‑contained with enough tables and figures to illustrate text descriptions (R3).
- [R3] Implementation details allow readers to potentially reproduce results; identifies a few promising future works.
- [R4] Investigates polarity capturing and semantic capturing of fine‑tuned sentence embedding methods; conducts experiments on two datasets with four base models.

### Weaknesses
- [R1, R2, R4] **Significance and motivation unclear.** The paper does not explain why polarity‑aware retrieval is important for downstream applications or for investigating model capacity (R1). Conclusion and generalizability are lacking (R2). Motivation is unclear and experimental findings are trivial (R4).
- [R1] **Same‑dataset setup limits generalizability.** Training and retrieval are on the same dataset; only shown that similarity is retained within that dataset. Unclear how this is better than standard benchmarks that include zero‑shot classification and unsupervised retrieval.
- [R1] **Missing baseline.** The paper introduces a new example generation approach but does not compare against a baseline using original example generation.
- [R1] **Related work gap.** The problem is relevant to the “forgetting effect” and its solutions (e.g., Chen et al., 2020; Luo et al., 2023); this should be discussed.
- [R2] **Lack of justification for design choices.** No explanation for why SST‑2 and Sarcastic Headlines were chosen, nor why the three loss functions were selected. No discussion on extensibility of the work.
- [R2] **Weighted‑sum combination questionable.** The two metrics are combined as \( C_\beta(s) := \beta \cdot P_M(s) + (1-\beta) \cdot S_{M\_M}(s) \). It is not justified that they can be combined in a weighted sum because the “units” of the two metrics may differ.
- [R3] **Lacks technical contribution.** No originally proposed model architecture, new loss function, unique training strategy, or modification to model architecture. The paper reads as a summary that fine‑tuning is important, which is well‑established.
- [R3] **No standard deviation reported.** Experiments show only mean values; standard deviations are missing.
- [R4] **Poorly written and ill‑illustrated.** Motivation and problem definition are unclear (R4). Many technical details are not well illustrated, e.g., formal definition of polarity, definition of semantic score, meaning of \( w_i \) in Sections 3.2.1 and 3.2.2, the reference model for semantic calculation in Sections 3.3 and 4, and how \( \beta \) is set.
- [R1] **Specific experimental questions.** Why only 3 epochs for Figure 4? Did the “max” models remain consistent? Why not test fine‑tuned models on STS using a standard benchmark?
- [R4] **Semantic score definition confusing.** It seems that fine‑tuning always decreases the semantic score because the model diverges from original parameters. Clarify the reference model: in Section 4 is it the base model without further fine‑tuning? Is it the same in Section 3.3?

**Note on reviewer disagreement:** Two reviewers (R1, R2, both score 5) found the presentation and experimentation strong and rigorous. Two others (R3, R4, both score 3) considered the contributions weak, motivation unclear, and writing poor. This split is reflected above.
