## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper adapts Causal Mediation Analysis (CMA) to trace how knowledge about distinct visual attributes (objects, style, viewpoint) is stored in the UNet and text-encoder of Stable Diffusion v1.4 with CLIP-ViT-L/336px. It finds that knowledge is distributed across many UNet components, with sets often distinct per attribute, but the CLIP text-encoder contains only one causal state — the first self-attention layer corresponding to the last subject token of the attribute. Based on this, the authors propose DIFF-QUICKFIX (DiffFix), a closed-form editing method that directly adjusts the output projection of that single layer to remove or update concepts, achieving ~1000× speedup over fine-tuning baselines with comparable CLIP-score performance.

### Strengths
- The adaptation of Causal Mediation Analysis to text-to-image diffusion models provides a principled, novel basis for knowledge tracing and yields meaningful conclusions about knowledge localization.
- The paper reveals a striking difference from language-only models: knowledge is distributed across UNet layers rather than isolated, while the text-encoder has only one causal state (first self-attention layer, last subject token) — a unique insight into multi-modal knowledge storage.
- The knowledge localization analysis directly enables a highly efficient editing method (DIFF-QUICKFIX) that optimizes a single weight matrix in closed form, achieving up to 1000× speedup over fine-tuning-based methods with comparable editing performance, as validated qualitatively and via CLIP-score on the same prompts as prior works.
- The paper is well-written overall, with clear motivation, abundant qualitative and quantitative results in the appendix, and appropriate background context.
- The use of corruption-based importance (adding Gaussian noise, measuring CLIP-score drop) is effective, and the experimental design comparing against recent editing methods is sound.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited scope and generalization uncertainty:** All experiments use only Stable Diffusion v1.4 (with CLIP-ViT-L/336px). It is unclear whether the findings (e.g., only one causal state in the text-encoder) transfer to other SD versions, other CLIP backbones, or other diffusion model architectures (e.g., Imagen). The paper may overclaim generality (“knowledge in text-to-image models”) based on a single checkpoint.
- **CMA novelty is limited:** The core interpretation framework (Causal Mediation Analysis) is borrowed from prior work, offering limited methodological novelty beyond the application to diffusion models.
- **Heavy reliance on CLIP-score for causal state identification:** CLIP-score may be sensitive to only prominent attributes (e.g., colors, large objects) and may not discriminate finer visual attributes (e.g., viewpoint, texture). The paper does not investigate whether the identified causal states would change under different metrics or whether CLIP-score is sensitive enough for general visual knowledge.
- **Fig. 2 raises a contradiction for the editing approach:** The figure shows strong overlap across attributes — most UNet components are causal for all tested attributes, with only a few attribute-unique layers. This implies that very few components independently control distinct attributes, calling into question how effective a single-layer edit can be for concept removal/update.
- **Fig. 3 seems highly unlikely and needs corroboration:** The result that only one layer and one token in the text-encoder encodes relevant knowledge (for all attributes tested) appears implausible. The authors should provide additional validation (e.g., ablation, alternative corruption methods) to confirm this result.
- **Lack of quantitative comparisons to baseline editing/ablation methods:** While qualitative results are compelling, the paper lacks a thorough quantitative comparison against existing concept ablation techniques beyond CLIP-score. Without such comparisons (e.g., FID?, user study?), it is difficult to assess relative performance.
- **Unclear definition of “visual attribute”:** The paper uses “attribute” loosely (object, style, viewpoint, etc.), which can confuse readers, especially when earlier works define attributes as specific property types (color, texture, etc.).
- **Threshold selection for CLIP-score is confusing:** The subsection on selecting a threshold for CLIP-score lacks sufficient context and motivation. It is not immediately clear why thresholding is needed or how it connects to identifying causal layers.
- **Editing method limitations:** DIFF-QUICKFIX’s “removal” results (e.g., “Snoopy” → dog) often appear as attribute editing (replacing the concept) rather than true object removal. The paper does not discuss whether the method can be extended to other editing tasks (e.g., object insertion, attribute-specific editing) or what its failure modes are.
- **Figure captions lack clarity:** For instance, Fig. 6 shows “photo of a apple in a beach” and “photo of a apple in a city” without explaining which image corresponds to which location, making it hard to interpret the edit.
- **References missing venue information:** Most references lack publication venues, which is improper for a research paper.
- **One reviewer questioned whether the CLIP-score approximation is valid:** The paper approximates |CLIP(x_restored, c) - CLIP(x_corr, c)| by CLIP(x_restored, c) alone, but this ignores the sign and magnitude of the absolute difference around the baseline, potentially biasing the measured effect.
- **Lack of results on trademarked object removal:** The paper claims to remove trademarked objects (top of page 3), but no such results are shown (possible removal during manuscript preparation per Reviewer 1).

*Note:* Reviewer 2 (score 8) and Reviewer 4 (score 8) are more positive, emphasizing the insight from causal analysis and the practical efficiency of the editing method. Reviewer 1 (score 5) and Reviewer 3 (score 5) are more critical, focusing on generalization concerns, limited novelty, and reliance on CLIP-score.
