## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes an unsupervised anomaly detection framework (UniCAD) that jointly learns a latent representation, clusters data via a mixture of t-distributions, and detects anomalies using an anomaly-aware likelihood maximization. Anomaly scores are derived from the mixture model and are further enhanced by a “vector sum” inspired by Newton’s law of gravitation. Experiments on 30 tabular datasets (with 17 baselines reported, although one reviewer notes a discrepancy of “15” in some places) show that UniCAD achieves a higher average rank than competing methods.

### Strengths

1. **Unified framework** – The idea of integrating representation learning, clustering, and anomaly detection into a single likelihood expression is considered interesting and powerful. Several reviewers note that simultaneously addressing these three components has strong potential.
2. **Physics-inspired design** – The vector‑sum anomaly score, motivated by gravitational analogy, is a simple and effective heuristic that improves performance. The concept is noted as thought‑provoking and novel despite its heuristic nature.
3. **Experimental strength** – The paper includes a large evaluation (30 datasets, 10–17 baselines) and provides useful ablation studies that isolate the contribution of each component. The ablation experiments are appreciated.
4. **Presentation** – The paper is generally well‑written and easy to follow. The mathematical notations are clear, and the appendix offers a comprehensive study.

### Weaknesses

1. **Novelty concerns** – Multiple reviewers question the novelty:
   - The t‑distribution based clustering is essentially a minor modification of Gaussian mixture models (GMMs) and is well‑established (Yamanishi et al., KDD 2000; Pesevski et al., 2018). The gravity‑based, likelihood‑based, and neighbor‑averaging techniques are also well‑known.
   - The combination of a mixture model with a clustering objective is similar to DAGMM; the paper does not adequately justify why this specific combination is more advantageous than alternatives (e.g., Deep SVDD with mixture models, or different self‑supervised methods with clustering objective).
   - Robust model fitting by ignoring potential outliers has a long history (e.g., RANSAC); the proposed framework is an instantiation of this idea with limited groundbreaking novelty.

2. **Theoretical foundation** – Several reviewers find the theory insufficient:
   - Equation 1 does not correspond to a coherent statistical model. For example, it implies that for a point labeled “outlier”, the conditional probability \(p(x\mid\text{outlier})\) is 1, and the model treats the top \(l\) points by score as deterministic outliers with no probability of being inliers.
   - Algorithm 1 has no convergence guarantee; oscillation patterns (e.g., repeatedly changing which points are considered outliers) are theoretically possible.
   - The vector‑sum score is a heuristic with no formal rigorous justification; probability distributions do not have a direction, and the gravitational analogy is a stretch.

3. **Unfair comparison** – Only 2 of the 10 compared algorithms involve representation learning. Since UniCAD uses a learned representation, the comparison is considered unfair—the performance gains may be attributed to representation learning rather than the core anomaly detection method. The paper should compare against methods that also incorporate representation learning to isolate the contribution.

4. **Missing baselines** – Recent strong deep‑learning methods (e.g., from ADBench, NeurIPS 2022) and more contemporary graph‑based AD methods (2023–2024) are omitted, making it difficult to assess state‑of‑the‑art performance.

5. **Vector‑sum concerns** – Several questions arise:
   - An instance lying between two prototypes could receive a **zero anomaly score** if the two directional vectors cancel. Is that desirable?
   - In a dense central cluster, a point in the centre could receive a **very high anomaly score** because forces from surrounding prototypes neutralize. This is counter‑intuitive (should be the least abnormal point). The paper provides only a synthetic example (Appendix Fig. 4); a deep dive on real datasets is needed.
   - Figure 2 compares outlier scores based on different model estimates (different prototypes), which is not statistically valid.
   - The method’s sensitivity to the number of mixture components is not explored; performance may degrade when components are not well‑separated or the mixture model is a poor fit.

6. **Computational complexity** – No discussion of the overhead of vector sum, especially in high‑dimensional settings or with many mixture components.

7. **Evaluation concerns** – 
   - Hyperparameter analysis shows that performance depends on parameter choices, raising the risk of overfitting to the 30‑dataset mega‑dataset even when settings are held constant.
   - Critical difference diagrams (Appendix Fig. 5) indicate that the winning method is not statistically significantly different from the second‑ or third‑ranked methods.
   - **Isolation Forest** performs surprisingly well on average; the paper should discuss this observation.
   - The framework is tested only on tabular data; its effectiveness for **image anomaly detection** remains unclear.

8. **Presentation issues** – Several inconsistencies and unclear passages:
   - Citation formatting (brackets missing) and inconsistent baseline counts (17 vs. 15).
   - Figure 1 has crossed arrows that are not explained.
   - Acronym “MM” is not defined.
   - Symbol “J” in Section 3.3.1 (M‑step) is not defined.
   - In Section 4.4, “omitting the likelihood maximization loss” is vague.
   - Color coding in Table 1 is confusing (green appears to indicate a winner but is used differently).
   - “Items 1, 2, 3 …” in Introduction are referenced before Figure 1 is cited.
   - The inclusion of GAD methods in the appendix is not justified; if they are important, more recent GAD methods should be included.

9. **Missing details** – The process for selecting the values of hyperparameters \(l\) and \(K\) in Table 1 is not described, and the method for handling the potential shortcut solution with the autoencoder (Sec. 3.3.2) is insufficiently justified.
