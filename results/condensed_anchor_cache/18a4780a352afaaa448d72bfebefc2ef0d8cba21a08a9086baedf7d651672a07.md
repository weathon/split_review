## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a framework to minimize MPC usage during secure LLM inference by introducing architectural changes during fine-tuning. The setting assumes a public open-source model is fine-tuned on private data; the client can preprocess most layers in the clear, leaving only final private layers to be executed under MPC. The three core techniques are: (1) freezing initial layers before fine-tuning, (2) using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to reduce matrix dimensions in MPC, and (3) merging attention heads to further lower the cost of MPC operations. The authors report substantial efficiency gains (3.6–11.3× runtime, 2.4–6.9× communication across MPC settings) while typically preserving over 90% of task performance. Reviewers had diverging views on the motivation and novelty: one reviewer questioned whether the problem setting is clearly distinct from prior work, another found the innovation limited to network structure rather than protocol improvements, and a third praised the application of layer freezing and LoRA to secure inference.

### Strengths
- The ideas are sound, and the authors provide an implementation for reproducibility.
- Head‑merging is novel and works well for accuracy while boosting MPC efficiency noticeably.
- Experiments are comprehensive, well described, and enable good reproducibility.
- The three techniques (Layer Freezing, LoRA, Head Merging) demonstrably reduce the cost of MPC in secure inference.
- The paper innovatively introduces layer freezing and LoRA from plaintext inference into secure inference, specifically reducing the bottlenecked matmul dimensions in MPC.
- Threat models are well described, along with explanations of why each component can be optimized while considering potential attacks, and why certain parts (e.g., mixture of weights) inherently cannot benefit from such optimizations.
- The paper is overall well written.

### Weaknesses
- **Problem motivation and setting unclear.** The premise (fine‑tuning a public open‑source model on private data) differs from the standard secure‑inference setting (model owner wants both model and query private). The reviewers questioned what realistic use‑case this solves: who owns the fine‑tuning data, how many parties are involved in the fine‑tuning MPC, and who is the client at inference time (e.g., does this become a 3‑party protocol)? These details are left unspecified, making it difficult to assess the relevance. Consequently, the baselines cited study a fundamentally different problem.
- **Limited innovation.** One reviewer noted that the proposed method does not improve the MPC protocol itself; the techniques (head‑merging, layer freezing, LoRA) only restructure the fine‑tuned network. Another reviewer considered the application of layer freezing and LoRA to secure inference innovative, but overall the contribution is focused on architectural partitioning rather than new MPC primitives.
- **Lack of criterion for frozen fraction \(f\) (Section 5.1).** No clear rationale or threshold is given for choosing how many layers to freeze across different LLMs. One reviewer noted that it is unclear what impact different values of \(f\) have on both performance (accuracy/efficiency trade‑off) and security. Another reviewer asked whether there is a reference basis for selecting the number of frozen layers and what happens if \(f\) is too large or too small.
- **Insufficient accuracy comparison with other MPC+LLM methods.** The experimental results do not compare the accuracy of this framework against other approaches that combine MPC and LLM for secure inference. More relevant comparisons are needed.
- **Unfair hardware comparison.** Only the 2PC‑Dealer protocol runs on GPU, while other compared MPC approaches run on CPU. Performance gains may be due to hardware acceleration rather than the proposed architectural changes. An apple‑to‑apple comparison (e.g., 2PC‑Dealer also on CPU) is necessary to isolate the impact of the optimizations.
- **Security‑proof issues.** The claimed proof in the appendix is very high‑level and lacks necessary detail. The functionality is defined over real values, but all cited protocols operate over fixed‑point values, making the instantiation inconsistent. Moreover, the protocols rely on probabilistic truncation for fixed‑point arithmetic, which is not provably secure (as pointed out by Li et al., USENIX Security 2023).
- **Citation and reference issues.** The paper cites [73] as the state‑of‑the‑art for 2PC+Dealer, while earlier referring to SIGMA as such (SIGMA appeared at PoPETs 2024). Several preprint references should be verified for conference publication status.
- **Missing clarification on head‑pruning vs. head‑merging.** The paper does not clearly explain the difference between the proposed head‑merging and existing head‑pruning techniques.
- **Lack of extension discussion.** One reviewer asked whether the framework could be extended to an n‑PC or distributed setting to handle enterprise‑level request volumes (millions of requests in a short time). This is not addressed.
