## Merged Review

### Summary
All four reviewers acknowledge the novelty and motivation of orbit-equivariance as a relaxation of standard permutation equivariance for GNNs, and appreciate the theoretical hierarchy, the max-orbit concept, and the construction of Orbit-Indiv-GNN and m-Orbit-Transform-GNN. Two reviewers (R1, R3) rate the paper highly (8), praising the writing, framework, and theoretical grounding. Two reviewers (R2, R4) rate it lower (6), raising significant concerns about the clarity of the exposition, the training procedure for m-Orbit-Transform-GNN, the dependence on node ordering, and the strength of experimental validation. All agree that the proposed datasets are new and useful, but disagreement exists on whether the experimental results are sufficient to fully support the claims.

### Strengths
1. The paper introduces a well-motivated hierarchy (Equivariance ↔ Orbit-Equivariance with max-orbit=1 ↔ max-orbit up to |orbit|) that unifies various GNN architectures under one umbrella. (R1, R3)

2. Theoretical analysis clearly connects orbit-equivariance to the 1-WL test for identifying orbits (Propositions 3, 4) and provides expressiveness results for different model classes. (R1, R2, R3)

3. Concrete constructions of orbit-equivariant GNNs are given: Orbit-Indiv-GNN (using node individualization) and m-Orbit-Transform-GNN (using a post-hoc transformation), with Theorem 2 providing a universal guarantee. (R2, R3)

4. The concept of max-orbit cleanly characterizes the extent to which equivariance is broken while preserving structural inductive biases. (R1, R2)

5. Two new datasets are created—Alchemy-Max-Orbit (synthetic, ground-truth orbit-equivariant labels) and Bioisostere (real-world drug design)—that explicitly require orbit-equivariant predictions. (R1, R2, R4)

6. Numerical experiments show that Orbit-Indiv-GNN (especially Orbit-Indiv-GCN) significantly outperforms standard GCN and RNI-GCN on tasks with orbit-equivariant targets. (R2, R4)

7. The paper is well-motivated with the drug-discovery example (Figure 1) and is generally well-written (R1, R3), though one reviewer disagrees on clarity (see Weaknesses). (R1, R3)

8. Hyperparameters, dataset details, and code are provided, supporting reproducibility. (R2)

### Weaknesses
1. **Node ordering dependence in the definition of node-labeling functions** – The function \(f\) implicitly assumes an ordered node set \(V=\{1,\dots,N\}\), which introduces an arbitrary ordering that is not intrinsic to the graph structure. This makes it unclear whether \(f\) is well-defined solely from the graph, unless it is automatically equivariant. (R2)

2. **Training procedure for m-Orbit-Transform-GNN is counterintuitive** – Cross-entropy loss is applied to the output before the transformation, meaning the model is trained to predict correct labels *before* the orbit-sorting step. The intended use of the orbit-sorting cross-entropy loss *after* the transformation is not followed, which undermines the purpose of the transformation and may explain poor empirical performance. (R2)

3. **m-Orbit-Transform-GNN is confusing, hard to justify, and underperforms** – The construction (Figure 5) is difficult to follow; it uses intuitively unexplained operations (e.g., rewriting output values). Many possible orbit-equivariant GNNs with a given max-orbit exist, and it is unclear why this particular construction is chosen. Moreover, 2-Orbit-Transform-GNN performs worse than Orbit-Indiv-GNNs on the Bioisostere dataset, suggesting the construction requires further exploration. (R2, R3)

4. **Non-differentiability of the proposed orbit-equivariant processes** – Both Orbit-Indiv-GNN and m-Orbit-Transform-GNN rely on non-differentiable steps (e.g., sorting based on orbit assignment). The paper does not discuss whether a learnable, differentiable equivariance-to-orbit-equivariance layer is possible, and whether such a layer could improve performance. (R3)

5. **Clarity and motivation issues in presentation** – While two reviewers (R1, R3) find the paper well-written, another (R4) finds the writing flow hard to follow and requests better conceptual illustration of max-orbit and more motivating examples. The description of Orbit-Transform-GNN is particularly confusing (R2). Minor presentation issues include inconsistent indexing (0-index vs. 1-index in Figure 5, R2), repeated abbreviations (R2), and notation suggestions (R2). (R2, R4)

6. **Ambiguity in claims about model classes being “not orbit-equivariant”** – It is unclear whether the statement means that *all* functions in that class fail to be orbit-equivariant, or only *some*. Clarification is needed for GCNs, RNI-GCN, Unique-ID-GNNs, etc. (R2)

7. **Clarification needed on “accuracy 0” for GCNs on Alchemy-Max-Orbit** – The claim that GCNs would achieve 0 accuracy on these datasets likely refers to *graph* accuracy. Node accuracy could be non-zero, and the distinction between node, graph, and orbit accuracy should be made explicit. (R2)

8. **Quality of the Bioisostere dataset** – The dataset is generated using RdKit (semi-empirical methods) rather than accurate DFT calculations (e.g., QM9, OC20), which may limit its realism for molecular property prediction. (R4)

9. **Efficiency of Orbit-1-WL for large-scale graphs** – Determining orbits via the WL test may be computationally expensive for large graphs; the authors should discuss scalability. (R3)

10. **Missing references to related theoretical work** – The paper does not cite or discuss theoretical works that unify positional encoding GNNs with equivariant GNNs, or node-labeling approaches for more powerful GNNs. (R1)

11. **Terminology concern** – The term “orbit-equivariance” could be misleading, as the framework assigns a multiset to each orbit rather than learning equivariant representations of orbit elements under a permutation action. (R1)

12. **Novelty assessment** – While the paper provides a useful framework, it builds on existing literature (positional encodings, node labeling) and is not a completely unexpected advance. (R1)

13. **Figure 1 clarification** – It should be made explicit that the three Fluorine atoms are in the same equivalence class only if positional coordinates are not part of the node features. (R1)

14. **Lack of strong real-world experimental results** – Beyond the proposed synthetic and semi-empirical datasets, there is no validation on established real-world benchmarks (e.g., ZINC, QM9). (R1)
