## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a differentially private (DP) statistical inference method that integrates the Bag of Little Bootstraps (BLB) with a noise-addition mechanism. It estimates parameters on data subsets, adds Gaussian noise for DP, and combines estimates. Asymptotic properties are analyzed using nonlinear expectation theory and the central limit theorem to derive test statistics and hypothesis tests. The method is validated on a toy experiment estimating the mean of a univariate Gaussian.

### Strengths
- Tackles the interesting and important problem of releasing DP statistics from big data while enabling valid statistical inference (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- Makes an effort to ensure the statistical properties of the proposed algorithm are well understood, which is valuable for applied researchers (Reviewer 1).
- Introduces a novel integration of DP with the BLB method, providing a general and scalable approach that could be adapted to various estimation methods (Reviewer 2).
- Uses nonlinear expectation theory and the central limit theorem to analyze the asymptotic distribution of statistics and address noise variance variability and estimation uncertainty; this application of nonlinear expectation theory to DP inference is new (Reviewers 2, 3).
- The mathematical notation is easy to follow, and the algorithm and inference methods are clearly described (Reviewer 4). *(Note: Reviewer 3 strongly disagrees on clarity – see Weaknesses.)*

### Weaknesses
- **Overstated contributions and missing related work.** The claim of “pioneering differential privacy in static massive data” is inaccurate; DP statistical inference and the use of BLB in DP algorithms have received considerable prior attention (Reviewer 1). Specifically, Covington et al. (2021, arXiv) and Evans et al. (2023, American Political Science Review) both use BLB within the sample-and-aggregate framework to solve the same problem, and the paper does not explain why a different approach is taken nor compare with these methods experimentally (Reviewers 1, 3). The paper is also missing many other relevant citations on DP statistical inference (Reviewer 1).
- **Limited and insufficient simulation study.** The experiments are limited to estimating the mean of a univariate Gaussian (Reviewer 1, 3, 4). No coverage or validity of variance estimates is shown (Reviewer 1). There are no comparisons to existing DP inference algorithms, nor any justification of the method’s usefulness for more sophisticated estimands or for truly large-scale data (Reviewers 1, 3). The paper claims to address “big data” but does not substantiate this with experiments showing that existing approaches do not work (Reviewer 1).
- **Unclear motivation and questionable utility of the proposed method.** The paper argues that the naive method (adding independent noise to each bag and averaging) suffers from dependence across bootstrap estimates and heteroskedastic noise. However, the proposed algorithm does not remove the dependence (it is inherent to bootstrap sampling), and it only reduces heteroskedasticity from B different noise variances to two – a minor improvement (Reviewer 4). Moreover, the paper itself notes (below Theorem 1) that the noise variances in the proposed method are guaranteed to be larger than in the naive method, so the proposed method likely has worse statistical accuracy. No theoretical or numerical improvement is demonstrated, making the motivation for the more sophisticated method unclear (Reviewer 4). Why not simply use the naive method, which is easier to analyze and has better utility? (Reviewer 4).
- **Poor presentation and incomplete exposition.** The paper is very difficult to understand, especially the sections relying on nonlinear expectation theory (Sections 2.3 and 3). The theory is not properly explained, and notation is introduced without definition: e.g., \(F\) in Section 2.1; \(n_k\), \(\mathcal{F}_k\), \(\Sigma\) in Section 2.3; \(S_0\) and \(\lambda_0\) needed for \(\xi_1\); \(\overline{\lambda}\) and \(\underline{\lambda}\) in Section 3 (Reviewer 3). Algorithm 1 is incomplete: it lacks mapping from computation steps to formulas, making it impossible to implement from the algorithm alone (Reviewer 1). The introduction should be rewritten to convey key ideas and contrast with existing solutions (Reviewer 1). The font size in all figures is too small, and Figures 4 and 5 referenced in Section 4 are missing (Reviewer 3). *(Note: Reviewer 4 found the notation easy to follow; this indicates a significant divergence of opinion.)*
- **Flawed sensitivity handling and privacy parameters.** The sensitivity of \(\hat{\theta}_k\) in the experiment is incorrect: data are sampled from a Gaussian, so they can be any real numbers, making sensitivity infinite. The paper does not mention clipping to bound sensitivity. Definition 1 similarly allows infinite sensitivity (Reviewer 3). The paper uses \(\delta = 0.01\), which is very large; it is recommended to use \(\delta \ll 1/n\) because \(\delta = 1/n\) already allows clearly non-private mechanisms. Also, \(\epsilon\) is typically varied to investigate privacy strength, not \(\delta\) (Reviewer 3). The term “Gaussian differential privacy” is used incorrectly; it should be “Gaussian mechanism” to avoid confusion with a different definition of DP (Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of analysis on utility loss and computational complexity.** The paper does not assess how much information is lost due to the privacy mechanism, i.e., there is no evaluation of the privacy–utility trade-off (Reviewers 2, 4). The computational complexity of the proposed mechanism is not analyzed, which limits understanding of its scalability to large datasets (Reviewer 2).
- **No clear connection to real-world problems or large-scale data.** The paper does not specify what real-world problems would benefit from combining DP and BLB in static massive data, nor does it demonstrate the practical necessity of the proposed approach (Reviewer 2). The connection to large-scale datasets is advertised but not substantiated (Reviewer 3). Questions about sample sizes and possible bias from clipping (if sensitivity cannot be bounded a priori) remain unanswered (Reviewer 1). What exactly is the confidence band on the y-axis in Figure 3? (Reviewer 3).
