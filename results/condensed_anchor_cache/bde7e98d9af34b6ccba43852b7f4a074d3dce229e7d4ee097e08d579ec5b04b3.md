## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces HOMER, a training-free scheme that extends the context limit of LLMs (up to 64K tokens) using a divide-and-conquer approach: long inputs are split into chunks, each chunk is token-pruned via attention importance, and adjacent chunks are hierarchically merged at progressive transformer layers. An optimized computational order reduces memory to logarithmic scaling. Evaluated on Llama-2 (7B/13B) on perplexity (PG-19), passkey retrieval, and QA (Multi-document QA), HOMER shows strong perplexity at 64K (the only method with reasonable PPL), impressive passkey retrieval at 32K (22.4% → 80.4%), and memory reduction up to 70%. It is complementary to RoPE-based methods like NTK and YaRN, with best QA performance when combined with NTK. However, concerns about reasoning degradation, complexity, fair comparison, and unclear use case limit confidence.

### Strengths
- Novel, training-free approach using context compression/merging, orthogonal to position-interpolation methods (PI, NTK, YaRN) and combinable with them.
- Surpasses all baselines on passkey retrieval at 32K tokens (22.4% → 80.4%); extends to 8× pretrained context length.
- Only method with reasonable perplexity on 64K-long documents; memory reduction up to 70% via KV cache compression.
- Extensive experiments with Llama-2 (7B/13B) and comparison with recent baselines (YaRN, NTK, PI, etc.).
- Empirical feasibility of token pruning demonstrated on multiple tasks.

### Weaknesses
- **Reasoning and QA performance concerns:** HOMER alone underperforms NTK on QA (Table 3), and heavy compression/merging may damage complex reasoning chains (e.g., logical inference, multi-hop reasoning). Unclear whether the technique remains useful for tasks beyond retrieval and simple QA. (Reviewers 1, 2)
- **Marginal gains at shorter context lengths:** For Llama-2-13B, HOMER shows almost no improvement on perplexity or passkey retrieval for contexts below 32K, raising doubts about its utility in common ranges. (Reviewer 2)
- **Fundamental use-case questioned:** Reviewers 3 points out that if HOMER is not for streaming decoding, simply using the last 2K tokens directly yields lower perplexity (<6) than HOMER (>8 at 64K, Figure 1) – casting doubt on the necessity of the method. HOMER also requires recalculating KV pairs for previous context, incompatible with streaming. This contrast is a major concern.
- **High method complexity:** The pipeline involves chunk merging, token pruning, propagative embedding refinement, and computational order reduction – multiple stages with significant engineering overhead and parameter tuning (e.g., top-K selection for pruning). This limits adoption and reproducibility. (Reviewer 2)
- **Missing comparisons and fairness:** 
  - No HOMER+YaRN results on QA (only HOMER+NTK shown); YaRN seems to perform poorly on QA – why? (Reviewer 4)
  - Comparison with YaRN/NTK on QA may be unfair because those baselines clip context documents, whereas HOMER uses the full compressed context. (Reviewer 4)
- **Clarity issues:**
  - Propagative refinement (Sec. 3.2) is confusing: how are refined embeddings gathered at lower layers, and why is it needed if the final layer representation is already computed? (Reviewers 2, 4)
  - Efficiency improvement (Sec. 3.3) is too laconic; the binary-tree computation seems straightforward, not innovative. (Reviewer 4)
  - Token pruning method lacks explanation of top-K selection and the speed-accuracy tradeoff. Unclear how uncalibrated vs. calibrated pruning criteria differ (ablation 4.4). (Reviewers 2, 4)
- **Questions that overlap with criticisms:**
  - What if only the final-layer attention importance is used for pruning across all layers? How does that compare? (Reviewer 1)
  - Inference time is not discussed; given extensive manipulations, significant overhead is expected. (Reviewer 2)
