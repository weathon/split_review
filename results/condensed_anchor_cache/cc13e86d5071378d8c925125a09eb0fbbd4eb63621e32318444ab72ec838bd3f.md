## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents Generative Value Learning (GVL), a method that uses vision-language models (VLMs) to estimate task progress (value) from video frames. GVL frames value estimation as a temporal ordering problem: it shuffles video frames and asks the VLM to order them by progress, then derives per-frame values from that ordering. Without task-specific training, GVL performs zero-shot and few-shot value prediction across 300+ real-world tasks and multiple robot embodiments. The method is applied to dataset filtering, success detection, and advantage-weighted regression for policy learning. Experimentally, GVL outperforms the LIV baseline in most settings and shows small improvements in downstream policy learning.

### Strengths
- The approach is simple yet effective, leveraging VLMs' world knowledge in a novel way by casting value estimation as temporal ordering over shuffled frames. This framing is compelling and well-motivated by the desiderata for a universal value estimator.
- Shuffling video frames is found to mitigate the temporal bias inherent in video sequences, leading to better value estimates compared to non-shuffled baselines.
- A new evaluation metric, Value-Order Correlation (VOC), is introduced to measure rank correlation between predicted values and ground-truth frame order in expert videos. This metric is thoughtfully designed and well-suited to the problem.
- Extensive experiments across a large suite of real-world robot datasets (300+ tasks, diverse embodiments including bimanual manipulation) demonstrate the generality of GVL. Results show clear superiority over the LIV baseline for text-based goals (Figure 2) and decent performance on complex videos (Figure 4).
- GVL enables several downstream applications without any training: dataset quality estimation, success detection, and weighted imitation learning. It is shown to improve policy learning via data filtering and advantage-weighted regression.
- Good ablation studies verify the importance of each component (shuffling, in-context examples, autoregressive prediction) and robustness to various factors.
- The paper is well-structured and easy to follow, with helpful figures that convey the high-level idea.

### Weaknesses
- The experimental comparisons are limited. Only one baseline (LIV) is used in many cases; comparisons on visuomotor control tasks are notably missing, and LIV is not compared on the ALOHA dataset. More recent representation learning baselines should be included for downstream policy learning. *One reviewer (score 6) was particularly concerned about insufficient comparisons, while the other three (score 8) found the evaluation extensive but still noted this limitation.*
- The effect of each proposed component (shuffling, autoregressive value prediction vs. traditional returns) is not ablated. A systematic study separating these contributions is needed to understand their individual efficacies.
- There is a potential circularity in the VOC evaluation: the OXE dataset is assumed to contain sufficient-quality expert trajectories to validate GVL, yet later the same metric is used to filter low-quality data from OXE. This inconsistency weakens the evaluation.
- More analysis is needed on GVL's ability to handle non-monotonic progress or repetitive motions. It remains unclear whether GVL truly evaluates frame quality or merely learns to output a linear ordering from shuffled inputs.
- The real-world downstream improvement over a diffusion policy baseline is small, limiting the practical impact. The method is technically simple, which some reviewers considered a weakness in terms of originality, though it is well-motivated and effective.
- A primary concern is that GVL requires the complete video sequence to compute values for each frame, making it unsuitable for online or real-time decision-making where actions must be taken from a single observation. This restricts applications to offline settings, unlike some comparable methods that operate incrementally. The applicability to online RL training is also questioned.
- The block diagram omits the role of the task description; without it, value estimation is ill-defined. The paper should clarify how the language instruction is integrated.
- The reasoning in the Dataset Quality Estimation section is incomplete. A direct comparison—training policies with and without removing low-quality subsets—would strengthen the experiment.
- Several typos and minor errors are present (e.g., missing comma in line 163, missing space after "2)" in line 178, "meaningfully" should be "meaningful" in line 197, "ataset" in line 393). Also, some arrows in Figure 1 are bidirectional and should be unidirectional for consistency. The term "ALOHA-13" is not defined.
