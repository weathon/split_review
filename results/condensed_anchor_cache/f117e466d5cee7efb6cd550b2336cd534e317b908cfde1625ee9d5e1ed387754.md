## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes DiffIntersort, a differentiable approximation of the Intersort score (Chevalley et al., 2024) for discovering the causal order of variables from interventional data. Intersort evaluates a score over permutations; the authors reformulate it using a potential function and apply differentiable sorting (Sinkhorn operator) to enable gradient-based optimization. They also introduce CausalDisco, a method that optionally adds DiffIntersort as a regularization term to a standard DAG learning loss. Experiments compare DiffIntersort against DCDI and GIES on linear SEMs, random Fourier features, neural network-generated data, and simulated gene regulatory networks. Reviewers note that the work addresses a valuable problem (scalable causal discovery for end-to-end learning) but raise substantial concerns about motivation, theoretical justification, novelty relative to existing differentiable permutation methods, missing efficiency analysis, and experimental reproducibility.

### Strengths

- The idea of regularizing causal discovery using a differentiable score for the topological ordering is interesting and potentially impactful, as ordering errors cause many downstream mistakes.
- DiffIntersort empirically yields scores close to the original non-differentiable Intersort, which is reassuring for the relaxation.
- The development of scalable approaches for causal discovery is valuable for applications like genomics; the proposed algorithm outperforms baselines on gene-regulatory and random Fourier features scenarios and is on par on linear SEMs and neural network-simulated data.
- The differentiable operator (using Sinkhorn) is novel in this context and may benefit other permutation-based causal discovery methods.
- One reviewer finds that DiffIntersort scaling to graphs with up to 2000 variables (claimed) could make a good contribution to the causal discovery community, especially given the possibility to integrate causal regularization into deep learning pipelines.

### Weaknesses

1. **Novelty and Motivation**
   - Several reviewers view the contribution as limited: DiffIntersort is essentially a straightforward combination of Intersort (Chevalley et al., 2024) with the differentiable sorting techniques from Annadani et al. (2023) and others. The paper focuses on DiffIntersort rather than the more novel regularization idea, which propagates to experiments lacking ablations on regularization strength.
   - The motivation for learning the causal ordering from interventional data is questioned: given interventional data, the entire DAG equivalence class can often be identified, and recovering the ordering alone may not be a necessary problem (once the CPDAG is known, the exact DAG given an ordering is trivial). The paper does not clearly state whether the goal is causal ordering or full structure discovery, and how many interventions are needed for order identifiability.

2. **Theoretical Justification and Assumptions**
   - The assumptions are not fully articulated. It is unclear whether the method (and the underlying score and distance measure) is fully nonparametric or requires further restrictions on functional form or noise distributions. Only Interventional Faithfulness is mentioned; hidden assumptions in the distance measure are not discussed.
   - No justification is given for the identifiability of the causal order itself. How many single-variable interventions are required? Do the authors implicitly assume the whole graph is identifiable from the CPDAG, and if so, is ordering learning redundant?
   - There is no discussion of non-convexity issues that plague differentiable causal discovery, as recently highlighted by Ng et al. (2024).

3. **Overlooked Related Work**
   - The related work section is incomplete. The decomposition in Eq. 3 strongly resembles parameterizations used by NO-CURL (Yu et al., 2021) and COSMO (Massidda et al., 2024). More importantly, the work of Charpentier et al. (2022, DP-DAG) and Annadani et al. (2023, BayesDAG) are not cited; DP-DAG also uses the Gumbel-Sinkhorn operator and Hungarian algorithm for differentiable permutation learning and supports interventional data. The paper should compare experimentally and theoretically with these differentiable approaches for interventional data.
   - A more thorough literature review on causal discovery from interventional data and on differentiable ordering learning is expected.

4. **Efficiency Analysis is Missing**
   - Despite “efficiency” in the title and a claim that Intersort does not scale beyond 100 variables while DiffIntersort scales to 2000, no algorithmic complexity analysis is provided for either method.
   - No execution time comparisons are reported in the experiments, whether against Intersort or other baselines (e.g., DCDI, GIES, DP-DAG). A reviewer specifically requests wall-clock time measurements under varying numbers of nodes.
   - The claim of scaling to 2000 variables is not backed by theoretical or empirical runtime analysis.

5. **Experimental Reproducibility and Details**
   - Code for CausalDisco and the full experiments is not provided (only code for DiffIntersort scoring). This hinders reproducibility, especially given sparse method descriptions.
   - Hyperparameters are insufficiently reported: the optimization algorithm is not specified; values of \(\lambda_1\), \(\lambda_2\), and \(c\) are not given (except \(\epsilon=0.3\), \(\gamma=0.5\)). The effect of varying \(\lambda_2\) (regularization strength) is not ablated.
   - Data generation details are lacking: for the nonlinear case, it is unclear which structural causal model from Brouillard et al. (2020) is used (additive noise or not) and how \(\hat X_i\) is computed; for random Fourier features, no reference or appendix discussion is provided; for linear mechanisms, weight sampling is not described.
   - Only two noise distributions (Gaussian and Laplacian) are tested, which is limited; the paper claims broad inference across noise types but does not explore other distributions (e.g., nonparametric or heavy-tailed).
   - Experiments use very sparse graphs (expected edges up to 200 for 2000 nodes). Denser settings should be considered.

6. **Method and Presentation Issues**
   - The “constrained” version of CausalDisco (Eq. 8) is poorly integrated: the loss actually uses regularization, not a constraint, and the relationship between the constrained and regularized formulations is not clarified. A bilevel optimization perspective would be more appropriate.
   - Definition 2 (top order divergence) is presented early in the method section but serves only as an evaluation metric, which distracts readers.
   - Handling of tied potentials \(p_i = p_j\) is not explained: would the soft permutation matrix connect nodes bidirectionally or remove the edge?
   - The name “CausalDisco” conflicts with an existing library of the same name (Reisach et al., 2021, 2023).
   - Figure 3 incorrectly labels the method as “causaldisco” (lowercase) rather than “CausalDisco”.

7. **Minor Typos and Formatting**
   - Line 220: Kuhn (1955) should use `\citep{}`.
   - Line 316: Albert & Barabasi (2002) should use `\citep{}`.
   - Line 408: the reference should be to Figure 9, not to a citation.
