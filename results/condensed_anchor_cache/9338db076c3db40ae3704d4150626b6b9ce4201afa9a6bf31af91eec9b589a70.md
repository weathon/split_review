## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces B‑MoCA, a benchmark for evaluating mobile device control agents. Built on Android ADB, it defines 131 common daily tasks (ranging from simple to multi‑step) and incorporates environment randomization (icon placements, wallpapers, languages, device types) to test generalization. The authors benchmark several (M)LLMs (both closed‑ and open‑source) and custom agents. While agents perform well on straightforward tasks, they struggle with complex, multi‑interaction tasks. The benchmark is open‑source. Reviewers acknowledge its realistic design and comprehensive evaluation but uniformly criticise its limited novelty, narrow task/app scope, lack of comparison with existing benchmarks, missing state‑of‑the‑art agents, and shallow conclusions.

### Strengths
- Well‑structured benchmark grounded in the Android OS, providing an authentic evaluation environment.
- Defines 131 daily tasks with realistic scenarios, covering a spectrum from simple operations (e.g., opening apps) to complex multi‑step interactions (e.g., web searches, device setting changes).
- Environment randomization (layout, language, wallpaper, device type) prevents agents from merely memorising fixed configurations and assesses generalisation across diverse settings.
- Evaluates a diverse set of agents (closed‑ and open‑source LLMs, multi‑modal LLMs, custom models) under a unified platform.
- Open‑source code and resources facilitate reproducibility and future adoption.
- Addresses an important gap by focusing on environmental diversity, making it a useful resource for improving agent robustness.

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty and shallow conclusions.**  
   - The work was already presented at an ICLR 2024 workshop; the full‑conference submission lacks fresh innovation.  
   - Conclusions are well‑known (e.g., poor generalisation in UI element understanding and manipulation). AppAgent (one of the code bases used) already documented VLMs’ limitations, showing performance drops even with GPT‑4V and Qwen‑VL, and used tricks like structured XML/document references to improve comprehension.  
   - The paper does not provide inspiring findings or deep insights that advance the field, and the experimental conclusions lack depth.

2. **Narrow task/app scope.**  
   - Despite 131 tasks, the range of mobile apps and task types tested is limited, potentially under‑representing the breadth of real‑world mobile control scenarios.

3. **Insufficient comparison with existing benchmarks.**  
   - No detailed qualitative or quantitative comparison is provided against prior benchmarks like **WebShop** (web‑based interaction, language grounding) or **Mobile‑Env** (GUI interaction for mobile agents). B‑MoCA appears to be an extension rather than a novel evaluation paradigm; the authors should explicitly highlight what is new or improved in methodology or evaluation framework.

4. **Missing state‑of‑the‑art agents and full GUI agent evaluations.**  
   - Only LLMs and MLLMs are benchmarked; many open‑source GUI agents (e.g., **AutoDroid**, **You Only Look at Screens**, **CoCo‑Agent**, **AppAgent**) are not included. This limits the benchmark’s comprehensiveness and makes it harder for future works to adopt B‑MoCA for comparison.

5. **Multimodal model challenges not explored.**  
   - The paper does not analyse specific difficulties of multi‑modal models (e.g., image‑based inputs), such as increased computational complexity and latency, which are critical on mobile devices. No metrics are designed to evaluate these aspects.  
   - Recent work on optimising multimodal models for mobile devices (arXiv:2405.12107) is cited as a relevant challenge that B‑MoCA does not address.

6. **No training of new models on collected data.**  
   - The benchmark collects a rich training dataset with varied environments, but only pre‑existing models are evaluated. Training a new agent on this dataset could directly test whether the environment diversity improves generalisation, a natural next step that is missing, weakening the contribution.

7. **No exploration of mitigation strategies for randomisation.**  
   - While randomisation degrades performance, the paper does not test adaptive fine‑tuning or other methods to mitigate the decline. The authors are asked: have any additional measures been tried to address randomisation effects?

8. **Justification for open‑source vs. closed‑source model comparison.**  
   - Given the well‑known performance gap between open‑source and closed‑source VLMs, the paper does not elaborate on the motivation or insights gained from including both in the evaluation.
