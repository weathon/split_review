## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Unified Uncertainty Calibration (U2C), a framework that combines aleatoric and epistemic uncertainties in multiclass classification. Instead of the standard reject-or-classify (RC) recipe (abstain if epistemic uncertainty is high, classify otherwise), U2C relabels the 5% most epistemically uncertain in-distribution validation examples as a new c+1 class (OOD). It then learns a non-linear calibration function τ_u on the epistemic uncertainty score u(x) and concatenates it as the logit for the OOD class, producing a unified (c+1)-class softmax vector. The calibration function is trained via cross-entropy on the relabeled validation set. The paper presents theoretical analysis (Lemmas 5.1, 5.2) and experiments on ImageNet across architectures and uncertainty estimators, showing improvements over RC.

### Strengths
- The paper is very well written, with a clear overview of the field and explanation of why reject-or-classify is not ideal. (Reviewer 1)
- The proposed U2C approach is simple (in a good sense), well-motivated, and applicable to all classification settings. (Reviewer 1)
- Experimental results show efficacy in a variety of settings, with consistent gains on ImageNet across different network architectures, uncertainty estimators, and data distributions. (Reviewers 1, 3)
- The problem of separating aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty is interesting, and U2C attacks one aspect by considering calibration and out-of-sample detection jointly. (Reviewer 2)
- The U2C framework identifies a new problem formulation for uncertainty calibration under the OOD setting. (Reviewer 4)
- The idea is natural and intuitive: constructing epistemic scores comparable to aleatoric scores, avoiding inconveniences of the RC method. (Reviewer 4)
- The algorithm is adaptive and flexible, benefiting from a good epistemic uncertainty estimator and a high-performance calibration function. (Reviewer 4)

### Weaknesses
- **Philosophical tone and incorrect statements** (Reviewer 2): Several parts have a philosophical feeling with vague, unsubstantiated claims. Examples:
  - Eq. (2) gives an incorrect definition of Expected Calibration Error (ECE). ECE does not have an inner expectation over p ~ [0,1]; the outer expectation is over x ~ P_X, not (x,y) ~ P.
  - Page 4: “a classifier models aleatoric uncertainty if it is well calibrated” – neither cited paper (Guo et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2021) claims this; it is an unsubstantiated opinion.
  - Section 3.1: “aleatoric uncertainty arises due to irreducible sources of randomness in the process of labeling data” falsely implies all aleatoricity is annotator noise.
  - Section 3.2: “epistemic uncertainty is an unsupervised quantity… more challenging to estimate than aleatoric uncertainty” – no citation provided.
- **Notational inconsistencies** (Reviewers 2, 3): Notation changes from τ_u to τ^u; Eq. (8) uses τ^u while preceding text uses τ_u. (Reviewer 2 also notes the central function u(x) is mentioned inline only as part of examples listed for completeness.)
- **Lack of clarity for key components** (Reviewer 2): What is an example of a “non-linear” τ̃_u in Eq. (7)? It is so central that it should be transparent at first reading.
- **Theoretical results not noteworthy** (Reviewer 2): There are two lemmas but no theorem. Lemma 5.1 is a simple decomposition; Lemma 5.2 verifies that RC does poorly on an illustrative synthetic example. (Reviewer 4 adds: Lemma 5.2’s critique of RC is simplified; practical implementations often cap extreme losses, so more nuance is needed.)
- **Discrepancy in Section 5 analysis** (Reviewer 3): The statement “if P_in has a lot of mass in C but little in B, then RC outperforms U2C” appears incorrect based on Figure 1 and Lemma 5 – U2C should outperform RC in that case.
- **Condensed description of U2C** (Reviewer 3): An algorithm in pseudocode would significantly improve clarity for the relabeling, blending, and learning steps.
- **Lack of comparison with prior unified uncertainty work** (Reviewer 3): No comparison with methods like Malinin and Gales (Dirichlet-based modeling of both uncertainties); discussion of trade-offs (retraining vs. computational overhead) is missing.
- **Weak justification for discarding 5% in-distribution data as OOD** (Reviewers 3, 4): 
  - Reviewer 3: The rationale is unconvincing; these points are still in-distribution; more justification on why they “look most like OOD” is needed.
  - Reviewer 4: The approach relies on the assumption that OOD samples exhibit comparable epistemic uncertainty to in-distribution outliers. This may fail if the epistemic estimator gives low uncertainty to genuine OOD inputs (e.g., mouse image misclassified as dog with high confidence). U2C’s performance is intimately tied to the epistemic uncertainty estimator’s quality.
- **Minor points on calibration definition** (Reviewers 1, 4): 
  - Reviewer 1 asks whether adding τᵤ as the logit for class c+1 could mess up calibrated logits for classes 1…c.
  - Reviewer 4 notes that stating “neural networks with small ECE produce calibrated confidence scores” is inaccurate because low marginal ECE does not guarantee conditional calibration.
- **Missing experimental details** (Reviewer 4): It is unclear whether models in the experiments are pre-trained or trained from scratch; providing details would aid reproducibility.

**Reviewer Disagreement**: Reviewer 4 is highly positive (rating 8, contribution 3), finding the idea natural and the problem formulation novel. Reviewer 2 is strongly negative (rating 3, soundness 1, contribution 2), criticizing philosophical content, incorrect definitions, and lack of novelty. Reviewers 1 and 3 are moderate (both 5), with Reviewer 1 adjusting score after discussion with other reviewers, sharing similar concerns. This contrast shows that while the core idea has appeal, concerns about rigor, notation, theoretical depth, and practical assumptions weigh heavily.
