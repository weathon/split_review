## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents a self-evolving spiking neural network with Hebbian plasticity to study how orientation maps (salt-and-pepper vs. pinwheel) emerge in primary visual cortex across species. It identifies the degree of feedforward input overlap, neuronal density, and connectivity balance as critical factors. Pinwheel structures are shown to have lower wiring cost, enhanced sparse coding, and greater robustness to noise. The model reproduces maps compatible with rodent, cat, and primate physiology.

### Strengths
- **Biological plausibility:** The recurrent spiking network with plasticity is well supported by neurobiological experiments and mirrors conventional models used in neuroscience (R1).
- **Rigorous and timely:** The paper is technically correct, methodologically rigorous, and brings potentially important new insights into why biological networks organize along a salt-and-pepper to pinwheel continuum (R2).
- **Novel modeling approach:** The model is well motivated, mathematically formalized, and includes realistic anatomical factors (retinotopy, morphology, connectivity) that allow it to reproduce species-specific orientation maps (R3).
- **Key finding:** The degree of synaptic organization crucially depends on feedforward input overlap; learned pinwheel structures are stable and promote efficient visual processing (R2).
- **Clear explanation:** The model components are described in sufficient detail to understand the proposed mechanism (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity and presentation issues:**
  - The text contains long, grammatically incorrect sentences, typos, and mislabeled figure references (e.g., page 9 cites Figure 6 but likely means Figure 4; line 262 refers to “third panel” of Fig. 2a but likely the first) (R2).
  - The abstract claims the subject is “largely unexplored,” which is too strong given existing work on orientation map emergence from haphazard wiring or pooling in predictive coding networks (R3). A more extensive literature review and explicit comparison with Stevens et al., 2013 is needed.
  - The title in the PDF differs from the title on OpenReview, suggesting poor preparation at submission stage (R1).
  - Excessive use of non-standard abbreviations (other than V1, E-I, FF, SESNN) impedes readability despite the list in Appendix A.7 (R2).

- **Spiking dynamics and contribution to AI:**
  - The spiking dynamics appear unnecessary because all learning rules depend only on firing rate, not spike-timing. No result relies on spike timing; a rate-based model might suffice. The authors should explain what is gained from the spiking mechanism, as spike-timing-dependent plasticity could yield different effects (R1, minority more negative).
  - The contribution to ICLR is unclear: the learning rules and network architecture are not new, and the paper does not explain how the results can be incorporated into deep learning algorithms (R1).

- **Lack of justification for modeling choices:**
  - The difference between Hebbian Oja’s plasticity (used for E-E) and Correlation Measuring plasticity (used for E-I and I-I) is insufficiently motivated. The only stated reason is that Oja’s rule normalizes E-E weights; why not use Oja’s for E-I and I-I? This choice is crucial to the results and needs deeper explanation (R2).
  - The energy cost (Eq. 7) is defined as inversely proportional to connection strength—this is not intuitive for metabolic cost. The information capacity computed as entropy of weight distributions (Eq. 8) also lacks justification; references and reasoning are needed (R2).
  - The reliability measure (Eq. 10) is based on single-neuron encoding, but cortical signal processing is better captured at the population level (R2).
  - The claim that hyperparameters are “consistent with empirical findings” (lines 173-174) is vague; specific experimental evidence should be cited (R2).

- **Unclear or missing variables and equations:**
  - The variable \(z_j(t)\) in Eq. 3 is not defined (R1, R2). The purpose of Eq. 3 versus Eq. 4 is unclear (R2).
  - Why is the maximum weight (neural connectivity parameter) higher for E than I neurons, despite the claim that E-I connectivity should be stronger than E-E? (R2)

- **Missing comparisons and ablations:**
  - No comparison with the model of Hansel & Vreeswijk (J. Neurosci., 2012) (R1).
  - No comparison of Eq. 8’s coefficient with the cross-correlogram measure (Bair et al., J. Neurosci., 2001) (R2).
  - A recent study (Koren et al., eLife, 2024) showed efficient encoding in a spiking E-I network without E-E connectivity; the current paper suggests E-E is necessary, but this discrepancy is not addressed (R2).
  - Is the tiling of receptive fields a possible factor in map formation? (e.g., https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2016.07.015 ) (R3)

- **Overly simplistic conclusions:**
  - The paper concludes that pinwheel structure is more efficient than salt-and-pepper, but salt-and-pepper is observed in biology and unlikely to be inefficient. The results only show that each structure arises from different parameter regimes (e.g., small input overlap). The conclusion should be more nuanced: there are multiple efficient solutions depending on parameters, not a single best structure (R2).

- **Limited evaluation:**
  - The model uses only static images on a Cartesian grid; extensions to dynamic images or a polar grid would be relevant (R3).
  - Evaluation is limited to comparing orientation maps with experimental data; a more quantitative assessment on other tasks would strengthen the claims (R3).
