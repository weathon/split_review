- Scores: 6, 6, 5, 8

## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes an active learning method for inference set design (ISD) in hybrid screening, where the goal is to select a small subset of compounds for experimental testing such that a model can accurately predict the remaining compounds in a finite library. The method uses uncertainty-based acquisition functions (least confidence and query-by-committee) and a stopping criterion based on a lower bound on accuracy. Experiments on image and molecular datasets (MNIST, QM9, Molecules3D, RxRx3) show cost reduction. Reviewers generally found the problem relevant and well-motivated, but noted limited technical novelty and insufficient baselines. Scores ranged from 5 to 8; one reviewer (R4) initially raised major concerns about the proof and clarity but after rebuttal raised their score to 8.

### Strengths

- Problem close to industrial application, addressing a real-world challenge in biological screening where experimental costs are a significant bottleneck (R1, R3).
- The paper is generally clearly written and easy to follow (R1, R2).
- The main idea is clear, useful, and effective (R2). The proposed inference set design method shows promising potential for strategically targeted selection based on model uncertainty (R3).
- Good experimental design; experiments are quite detailed and demonstrate benefits of the approach on diverse real-world datasets: QM9, Molecules3D, and RxRx3 (17,063 CRISPR knockouts and 1,674 FDA-approved compounds) (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Originality: The submission tackles an interesting problem where the goal is not to maximize model generalization but to ensure model correctness on a specific subset of data. This is a first step towards better theoretically-backed approaches in biology (compared to the LINCS L1000 dataset where a fixed subset was selected based on prior knowledge) (R4).
- The experimental results look good, with appropriate baselines (random selection of new samples, or adding the next batch in the original order) (R4).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited technical novelty**: The method describes classical active learning evaluated with a non-traditional performance metric (though the metric is well motivated). The technical novelty is limited; it is mostly an application of existing methods (R1, R2). One reviewer questions the key difference from existing uncertainty-based active learning methods, noting it appears to be mainly that the evaluation criterion is performance on the library rather than generalization (R2).
- **Insufficient baselines and comparisons**: The experimental comparison is severely limited, with only a random agent as a baseline. The paper would benefit from comparisons with other heuristics that capture the "difficulty" of observations (e.g., molecule size, complexity) and other active learning methods (e.g., Bayesian Active Learning by Disagreement (BALD) [Houlsby et al., 2011], reinforcement learning approaches with proper reward modeling) (R2, R3). R3 considers the absence of comparison to BALD a major oversight, as BALD is explicitly designed to select samples that reduce uncertainty across the dataset, directly relevant to this work. R2 also notes that for Molecules3D, comparing with data ordered by smallest molecule size first goes against the idea of training on difficult examples first; ordering by decreasing size would be more interesting.
- **Assumption of no measurement error**: The paper assumes no measurement error (zero aleatoric uncertainty), which is not reasonable in practical biological assays. Reformulating the problem with measurement noise is not trivial (R1).
- **Stopping criterion and theoretical guarantees**: The paper argues that the stopping criterion provides a lower bound on target accuracy assuming the model is weakly calibrated (i.e., the order of uncertainty is correct). However, how reliably this assumption holds in practice is unclear (R2). Moreover, the proof of Lemma 1 is incorrect or has serious logical gaps (R3, R4). R4 details that the definition of _b_ (or _N<sub>b</sub>_) is inconsistent across the paper; the proof uses confusing notation (_Ŷ_ vs. _Y_, _p̂_ = _f̂_(X)), displays the opposite sign of the inequality, and lacks rigorous justification via Bayes’ theorem and induction. R3 similarly finds the theoretical explanation unclear, with logical gaps and notation issues (_i ∈∈ Xᵗ<sub>inf</sub>_, identical left and right sides of inequality). R4 also notes that the result in Lemma 1 is not empirically tested (no verification that error < γ with probability 1-δ at stopping), and code is not shared, making the research not reproducible.
- **Clarity and presentation issues**: The paper exceeds the ICLR page limit and has formatting issues (blank space on page 5, broken text on lines 392-393) (R1, R2, R3). Multiple reviewers note unclear notation and missing definitions: _b/N<sub>b</sub>_ is never defined and used inconsistently; _N<sub>target</sub>_ appears without explanation (R3); eqs. (3) and (4) have mismatched indices and left/right hand side (R2); the sentence about treating performance on _X<sup>t</sup><sub>inf</sub>_ as IID is unclear (R2); "weakly calibrated" vs. "approximately calibrated" terminology (R4); the abbreviation IID is not defined on first use (R3). R4 suggests putting all variable definitions in a single paragraph after the introduction. The name "inference set design" is questioned as not descriptive of the method (R2).
- **Query-by-committee rationale and acquisition function choice**: For the committee approach, why focus on voting entropy rather than entropy of the mean predictive distribution? (R2). R4 also questions why query-by-committee is mentioned if there is no theoretical guarantee associated with it. R2 finds no description in the main text of the ML models used in the experiments.
- **Appropriateness of accuracy metric**: The choice of accuracy measure may not be appropriate for imbalanced data (common in drug discovery); area under the curve (AUC) is more suitable (R4).
- **Practical constraints**: The method's effectiveness is constrained in a low data regime and complex task (as indicated by the RxRx3 dataset results). If so, how can it be practically useful for real-world biological applications that commonly encounter these challenges? (R3). R4 also raises concerns about the computational expense of retraining the model each round and evaluating on the inference set; the budget of measurements is important but minimizing the number of selection rounds may also be desirable.
- **Other reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms**:
  - Can the authors point out a few practical scenarios where ISD is relevant while Bayesian optimization is not? (R1)
  - How can the method handle measurement error? (R1)
  - Were any meaningful patterns observed in the hard-to-predict examples in the public chemical datasets (HOMO-LUMO gap and energy prediction)? (R1)
  - Is there a closed-form expression or upper bound on the stopping time τ? Is the resulting budget realistic (sublinear in N for reasonable γ)? (R4)
  - Why not a more adaptive approach where the acquisition batch size is not constant? (R4)
  - Please define KL and the result cited in eq. 5 (R1).
  - R4's specific questions about the experimental plots: What do the shadowed orange parts, "saving" in Figure 1, and the threshold γ represent? Can the theoretical guarantee be empirically verified?
  - R3's specific questions about Lemma 1: same inequality on both sides; notation i ∈∈; logical circularity in lines 212-213.

Note: R4 initially rated soundness 1, presentation 1, contribution 2 due to the proof issues, but after rebuttal raised the overall score to 8. Other reviewers gave scores of 6, 6, 5. This contrast is preserved.
