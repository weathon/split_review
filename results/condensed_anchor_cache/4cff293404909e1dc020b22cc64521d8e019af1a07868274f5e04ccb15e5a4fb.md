## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Attribution with Intrinsic Information (AII), a gradient-based feature attribution method that accumulates gradients over all class predictions to address two purported biases: “information ignorance” and “extra information.” It also introduces a new feature-removal strategy (CFA) and metrics (insertion confusion, deletion confusion). Reviewers disagree strongly: two reviewers (scores 5) find the method effective and experiments convincing, while two reviewers (scores 3) consider the presentation poor, the key concepts ill-defined, and the method insufficiently described or novel. The positive reviewers praise the clear illustrative examples and strong empirical performance (especially high insertion scores); the negative reviewers highlight missing formal definitions, short algorithm description, and no comparison to contrastive/counterfactual explanation methods.

### Strengths
- **Novel method and interesting setting** (R1, R4). AII proposes a new way to aggregate gradient information over all classes for feature attribution.
- **Clear illustrative examples of two attribution biases** (R2, R3). Figures 1 and 2 effectively show information ignorance (omission of important features) and extra information (incorrectly highlighting irrelevant features), helping to motivate the work.
- **Extensive experiments with many baseline methods** (R1, R2, R3, R4). Comparisons include a variety of gradient-based and other attribution methods. Code is available (R1). AII achieves high insertion scores and visually less noisy attributions (R2), outperforming traditional methods (R3). The positive reviewers find the empirical results convincing and thorough.
- **Empirical resolution of the identified problems** (R3). The proposed AII method is shown to resolve information ignorance and extra information in the presented experiments.

### Weaknesses
- **Poor presentation and lack of formal definitions** (R1, R3, R4). The key concepts “information ignorance” and “extra information” are not defined mathematically; they are illustrated only through examples (Figures 1, 2) and never quantified (R1). A diagram or Venn diagram would help (R3). Notation is inconsistent and erroneous (R4): Equation 2 uses index *i* but term has *j*; \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) are undefined; superscript/subscript confusion (\(x^t\) for pixels sampled from univariate uniform). Figure captions (1–5) do not specify the model used and sometimes omit the attribution method (R4). Table 2 caption has a typo: “U-INS, U-DEL” should be “F-INS, F-DEL” (R3). Minor: “the larger the attribution result” should be “the larger the *absolute value*” (R4).
- **AII algorithm description too short and lacking mechanistic detail** (R1, R4). The algorithm is described in only half a page (Section 3.4) (R1). The description is “extremely curt” given it is the main contribution (R4). Without formal definitions of the biases, it is unclear how gradient accumulation over all class predictions mitigates them (R1, R3). No rigorous mathematical treatment links the method to the identified problems (R1). Some reviewers see limited innovation: essentially averaging gradients over all class predictions, reminiscent of existing averaging approaches like Integrated Gradients (R4). No discussion or comparison with contrastive/counterfactual explanation methods (e.g., CEM) is provided (R4). More intuition is needed on why AII resolves extra information (R3) and why the entropy‑maximization feature removal helps (R2, lines 300–304).
- **Questionable claims and experimental concerns** (R1, R2, R3, R4). The statement (lines 157–158) that “the loss function L(f(x), y) only contains the class information of y” is inaccurate: cross‑entropy loss also depends on logits of non‑target classes, and gradients for those classes are non‑zero (R1). The example in Figure 2 tries to show that other attribution algorithms wrongly highlight an irrelevant square region, but this assumes that the model does not actually use that region; a control experiment is needed to verify that the model ignores it (R2). The metrics used in experiments may not capture ambiguous low‑confidence cases like Figure 1 because scores do not require reconstructing the exact low‑confidence prediction (R2). The experiments are restricted to image classification on popular datasets, which could bias results (R3). No timing comparisons are provided, though CFA and AII optimizations appear more expensive than baseline attribution methods (R4). The CFA optimization (Equation 2) is a hard problem and computationally intensive per image (R4). The paper only considers turning pixels black for feature removal, whereas other null values (e.g., average pixel) are common in the literature; this is not a new insight (R4).
- **Applicability and understanding gaps** (R2, R3). AII may not help explain less ambiguous images (e.g., Figure 3, MNIST where most digits are clear) (R2). The observation in Figure 3 raises the question whether it is a feature of the model or a bug in the attribution (R2). More examples showing that the problems are resolved would be helpful (R3). The difference between high‑confidence and low‑confidence predictions used in the evaluation is not defined (e.g., 90% vs 10% or 50‑50?) (R3).
