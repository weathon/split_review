## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Multi-Marginal Flow Matching (MMFM), a method that extends Flow Matching to model system dynamics across multiple time points and experimental conditions using smooth spline-based interpolation and classifier-free guidance. The authors evaluate MMFM on synthetic data and one real-world single-cell RNA-seq dataset with around a hundred chemical perturbations, showing improved imputation of missing time points against baseline methods.

### Strengths
- The motivation for MMFM is well-founded: it directly addresses challenges in biological experiments where data is fragmented across time points and conditions. The multi-step transport approach is an efficient way to capture temporal and conditional complexities.
- The extension of (Conditional) Flow Matching to the multi-marginal setting is a natural and well-justified progression. The framework leverages inter-dependencies across conditions, enhancing generalizability.
- The use of optimal transport and Schrödinger Bridge to integrate multiple marginals is insightful, leading to improved model robustness.
- The introduction of cubic splines for smoother interpolation (as opposed to piece-wise linear interpolation) significantly boosts performance and efficiency; this is well supported by experiments.
- The method expands on prior work in a parsimonious manner. The approach is simple, intuitive, and has sound theoretical backing.
- The authors know the literature well, provide ample citations, and design experiments that include sufficient baselines.
- The paper is well-written and organized; the main text gives minimally sufficient information while the appendix provides complementary details.
- The method is evaluated on a real-world dataset involving cellular responses to drugs, demonstrating applicability to practical biology and medicine.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited empirical breadth and rigor**: The method is evaluated on only one real-world RNA-seq dataset and one synthetic dataset. This limited scope is concerning given the broad range of real-world settings claimed in the introduction. One reviewer specifically suggests including additional genomics datasets from Tong et al. (2024) to increase the contribution. (Note: the authors addressed this in their rebuttal with extra experiments.)
- **Title overclaim**: The title “Modeling Complex System Dynamics” overstates the focus, as the paper primarily addresses biological single-cell dynamics. It would be strengthened by demonstrating applicability to broader domains (e.g., climate, finance, physics) or at least discussing such possibilities and limitations.
- **Lack of clear problem definition**: The paper lacks a centralized notation or formal problem statement, making it difficult to follow the methodology and assess novelty.
- **Incomplete methodological details**:
  - The loss function for training is not fully explained; the MMFM loss (Eq. 7) appears identical to the CFM loss (Eq. 3) without clear differentiation.
  - Key formulas (e.g., derivatives of mean and variance functions, definitions of \(c_{\phi}\), \(\mu_t'(x)\), \(\sigma_t'(x)\)) are missing or vague.
  - The specific implementation of optimal transport (how joint distributions are constructed from pairwise couplings) is not described clearly.
  - The network architecture \(\bar{v}_t\) is not specified.
- **Scalability and efficiency trade-offs**: The multi-marginal OT solution must be pre-computed. Although mini-batches are mentioned, more discussion is needed on the performance–efficiency trade-off between L-MMFM and MMCF, especially as OT solvers scale with dimensionality.
- **Unclear or missing experimental details**:
  - The origin of the formulas used for the synthetic dataset is not explained, nor its purpose clearly stated.
  - Figure 2 lacks y-axis labels, hindering interpretation.
  - For the real-world drug-response dataset, the evaluation metrics (e.g., imputation performance criteria) are not defined, making it difficult to assess effectiveness or reproduce results.
  - Comparisons with baselines lack statistical significance tests and detailed quantitative results.
- **Assumption of observed conditions**: The method requires conditions \(\{c\}\) to be given by data. It is unclear how to proceed when conditions are latent or unobserved—a common real-world scenario.
- **Limited extrapolation discussion**: The paper does not address whether MMFM can handle extrapolation beyond given time points or non-uniformly sampled time points. (One reviewer asks if methods like CSDI could be adapted; MMFM’s capability for forecasting is not discussed.)
- **Unclear notational step**: In Eq. (11), it is unclear why condition \(c\) is multiplied by a scalar when it is just an identifier.
- **Questions awaiting clarification**: Several methodological details remain unanswered—specific OT implementation, derivation of the spline interpolation, and network architecture choices.
