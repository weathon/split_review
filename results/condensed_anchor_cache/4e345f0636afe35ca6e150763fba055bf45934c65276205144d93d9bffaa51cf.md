## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a generative method to create privacy-risky fake data in order to mislead membership inference attacks, while protecting real privacy-safe samples. It introduces a sample-level privacy categorization (privacy-safe, privacy-vague, privacy-risky) based on attack success rates, and uses CycleGAN-style transformations to convert samples between these domains. The approach is evaluated on CIFAR-100 and TinyImageNet, comparing against SELENA and RelaxLoss. Reviewers disagree on overall merit: two reviewers (scores 5) find the idea innovative and the empirical work solid but point to limitations in scope and trade-off quantification; two reviewers (scores 3) are more critical, raising serious concerns about theoretical rigor, method validity, writing clarity, and insufficient baselines.

### Strengths
- The core idea of generating privacy-risky fake data to confuse attackers is novel and elegant (R1, R2).
- The sample-level privacy analysis (categorizing samples into privacy-safe, privacy-vague, privacy-risky) is an interesting and potentially valuable empirical insight that goes beyond dataset-level privacy (R1, R2, R3).
- The motivation is clearly established, starting from data complexity and model capacity asymmetry, making the problem and the proposed solution easy to follow (R1, R4).
- The method is validated through extensive experiments on popular datasets (CIFAR-100, TinyImageNet) and compares favorably against established defenses (RelaxLoss, SELENA) in several settings (R1). Reviewer R1 found the rigorous validation a strength.
- The application of CycleGAN to transform data between privacy-safe and privacy-risky domains is creative (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited data scope:** Experiments are restricted to image datasets (CIFAR-100, TinyImageNet); privacy risks are context-dependent and the approach’s effectiveness on text, tabular, or time-series data is unknown (R1, Q1).
- **Privacy-utility trade-off insufficiently addressed:** The paper notes a decrease in test accuracy when privacy-risky fake data is introduced, but provides little quantification or analysis of the trade-off; more detailed discussion and possible mitigation strategies are missing (R1, Q2; R2).
- **Computational overhead:** The method requires multiple independent model runs to identify privacy-safe samples and a sophisticated GAN-based generative step; overhead is not compared with baselines like SELENA or RelaxLoss, and no optimization techniques are discussed (R1, Q3; R2, overhead question).
- **Unclear advantage over baselines:** In several scenarios (e.g., correctness-based attacks on ResNet18 CIFAR-100 in Fig. 7, and multiple settings in Fig. 7 overall) SELENA performs better or the standard cross-entropy model is similar; the paper does not convincingly argue when and why the proposed approach should be preferred (R2, Qs on advantage; R3, Comment 4).
- **Absence of comparison with formal privacy:** No discussion or comparison with Differential Privacy or other formal guarantees, leaving the overall privacy claim as an empirical property (R2).
- **Writing and presentation need significant improvement:** The paper contains unclear or incomplete sentences (e.g., start of Section 3), insufficient explanation of figures (e.g., Fig 1, attack success metric “M-Entropy” hidden in caption), and a related-work section that is a list of summaries without proper contextualization (R3, Comment 1; R4). Terminology such as “data complexity” and “model capacity” is used without precise definition (R3). A clear algorithm outline for the fake data generation pipeline is missing (R2).
- **Lack of theoretical/analytical rigor:** Several claims are speculative and unsupported by formal reasoning or evidence. For example, the conjectures in Section 3.1.2 are presented as insights but rely on unverified assumptions about model memorization; the “Dataset Size vs. Complexity” argument in 3.1.3 oversimplifies the interplay of regularization, sample distribution, and outliers (R3, Comment 1). The claim that “RelaxLoss always gives priority to protecting some samples with specific characteristics” is vague and not quantified (R4).
- **Method validity concerns (sample-level privacy instability):** The privacy level of a sample is empirically measured based on attack success, which depends on all other input samples and the chosen model (R3, Comment 2; R4 about “data outlier effect” not defined). Consequently, when fake data are added to the training set, the “environment” changes, so samples that were originally privacy-safe or risky may shift – the transformation may not achieve the intended privacy level. This undermines the core assumption that the GAN targets well-defined distributions for privacy-safe/risky domains (R3, Comment 3).
- **GAN-based generation issues:** Using a true data sample as generator input (instead of noise) limits diversity and risks overfitting to specific patterns, potentially carrying over identifiable features from original samples (R3, Comment 3). The assumption that privacy-safe and privacy-risky subsets follow well-defined probability distributions is not justified; subsets defined by empirical attack behavior may exhibit distributional shifts (R3, Comment 3).
- **Limited baseline comparison:** The experiments only compare against simple augmentations (Random Cropping & Random Flip, not explicitly shown in plots); more diverse baselines would be needed to assess effectiveness (R3, Comment 4).
- **Experimental methodology unclear:** The paper does not specify how many models form the ensemble, the voting mechanism, or whether the M-Entropy attack is used for privacy-level assessment (R4, Q on ensemble). The distinction between “fake data” and “fake data & transformed data” is not clearly described; the number of input data samples for each experimental condition is not reported (R3, Q2–Q6). The plot in Fig. 2 showing “data outlier effect” lacks a proper definition of what constitutes an outlier (R4).
