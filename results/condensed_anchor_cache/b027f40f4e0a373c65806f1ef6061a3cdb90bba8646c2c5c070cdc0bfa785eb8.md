## Merged Review

### Summary

Reviewers agree the paper targets an important goal (foundation model for ECG interpretation) and leverages a large, multi-source pretraining dataset (over six million ECGs from three continents). However, opinions diverge sharply on novelty, experimental rigor, and scope. Two reviewers (R1, R5) rate the work highly (score 6), praising the temporal augmentation strategy and its effectiveness on small/medium datasets. Two reviewers (R2, R3) are moderate (score 3), raising concerns about incremental novelty and flawed comparisons. Two reviewers (R4, R6) are strongly negative (score 1), citing lack of originality, insufficient baselines, missing statistical tests, and shallow analysis. There is consensus that the method is essentially patient-contrastive learning (PCLR) with added temporal augmentations (zero-masking and random cropping). Most reviewers find this insufficient technically, and the experimental design is widely criticised for fragmented comparisons, empty table cells, and missing hyperparameter details.

### Strengths

- Large-scale, multi-source pretraining dataset ( >6 million ECGs from four cohorts in three continents: MGH, BIDMC, BCSV) is a key strength (R1, R2, R4, R5, R6), supporting good generalization across demographics and health conditions.
- The work addresses an important problem: developing foundation models for medical data to reduce reliance on expensive labels (R3, R4, R5). The high cost of physician-provided labels motivates self-supervised learning in this domain (R3).
- The temporal augmentation component (zero-masking and random cropping) is considered original by some (R1, R5) – R1 notes not having seen random cropping applied in this way for ECG, and R5 finds it enriches clinically relevant representations.
- The method demonstrates superior performance over fully-supervised methods on small to medium labeled datasets (e.g., up to 100k samples), adding practical value for data-scarce clinical settings (R1, R5).
- R1 appreciates the concise literature overview of existing pretraining approaches and the intuitive explanation of the methodology.
- The evaluation covers multiple downstream tasks (age, sex, five-year mortality, diagnostic super-classes) and includes an external validation cohort (PTB-XL), showing generalizability (R4, R5).
- Data scaling experiments show TA-PCLR outperforms fully-supervised baselines on smaller labeled sets (R1, R5).
- The analysis of how pretraining dataset distribution (healthy vs. diseased cohorts) impacts downstream performance is a useful exploration of domain effects (R1, R5).

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of novelty / incremental contribution** (R2, R3, R4, R6): The method directly builds on PCLR (Diamant et al., 2022) by adding only generic temporal augmentations (zero-masking and random cropping). These augmentations are not ECG-specific (e.g., they do not preserve intervals like PR, QRS, QT or the isoelectric line) and are already used in time-series literature (R3, R6). The loss function (InfoNCE) is standard (R3, R4). R6 states the framework is “very similar to existing frameworks” and lacks originality. R1 (a positive reviewer) notes the paper introduces a novel method but does not convincingly present a foundation model. R2 and R3 question whether the method is more than a “general contrastive learning strategy.” **Minority contrast**: R1 and R5 consider the temporal augmentation novel, but this view is not shared by the majority.

- **Incomplete and poorly designed comparisons** (R2, R3, R4, R5, R6):
    - In Table 2, PCLR is pretrained only on MGH and BIDMC, while TA-PCLR uses BCSV (the largest cohort). This prevents a direct head-to-head comparison of the augmentation benefit (R2, R3). Why not pretrain PCLR on BCSV as well? (R2, R3)
    - Table 3 compares only to ResNet, an older architecture, and does not include PCLR as a baseline (R2, R3, R4). R2 asks why no transformer-based or attention-based SOTA models are compared (e.g., Song et al., 2024; McKeen et al., 2024; Bickmann et al., 2024).
    - Tables 4 and 5 have many empty cells (missing results) without explanation (R2, R3, R4). R3 suggests the study is incomplete or rushed. R6 recommends comparing against a wider range of existing ECG foundation models (e.g., those in https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.05178) and other self-supervised frameworks (e.g., MAE, MoCo).
    - R6 proposes additional comparisons: contrastive alternatives (MoCo) and non-contrastive methods (MAE) to isolate the contribution of the contrastive learning strategy.
    - Only one external validation set (PTB-XL) is used; R5 questions generalizability beyond that dataset, especially regarding geographical and racial diversity.

- **No ablation study of pretraining components** (R1, R6): The paper does not ablate the effect of individual augmentations (zero-masking vs. random cropping) or compare to simpler augmentations. R6 notes limited exploration of augmentation techniques from prior work (e.g., https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3511808.3557591, https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04360). R1 suggests the ablation (only comparing to PCLR without temporal augmentation) is insufficient or poorly explained.

- **Missing hyperparameter tuning details** (R1, R4): It is unclear whether hyperparameter tuning was performed for TA-PCLR or baselines. The final values in the appendix are not enough; a description of the tuning methodology is needed (R4).

- **No statistical significance testing or confidence intervals** (R4): The paper reports only single metrics per task (e.g., AUROC, accuracy) without measures of variability, making it hard to assess whether improvements are meaningful.

- **No interpretability analysis** (R1, R6): There is no analysis of learned representations (e.g., t-SNE/PCA plots of embeddings, feature correlation, clustering by clinical conditions). R6 suggests this would help demonstrate the model’s robustness and whether it consistently represents different data types (e.g., ECG rhythms, pathologies).

- **Ambiguous or overstated claims** (R1, R3):
    - Counting 12-lead ECGs as 12 separate recordings may inflate the reported dataset size (R3: “6 Mil ECGs”).
    - R1 points out inaccuracies in the literature background (e.g., mislabeling ECG-FM as using generative pretraining, incorrect generalizations about contrastive learning definitions).
    - R1 notes a mismatch between calling it a “foundation model” and the limited experimentation (no model scaling analysis, small architecture used without justification that it can fully utilize 6M ECGs).
    - R1 finds the phrase “first investigation of its kind” for the dataset distribution analysis is overstated.

- **Presentation and organizational issues** (R1, R2):
    - Figure 2 colors are too dark (R2).
    - Empty cells in Tables 4, 5, 6 should be marked with “-” and explained (R2, R4).
    - Some methods (Song et al., 2024; 3KG) are mentioned in results but not described in related work (R1).
    - Typos exist (e.g., “same train/test splits..”, “Unique patients1”) (R1).
    - The paper lacks non-tabular figures (e.g., data scaling curves) (R1).

- **Missing analysis of potential bias** (R1): The training scheme samples one random ECG per patient per epoch to avoid bias toward patients with many ECGs, but this could underrepresent chronic patients with more recordings (who may be most clinically important). This trade-off is not discussed (R1).
