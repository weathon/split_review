## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates when next-word prediction capabilities of LLMs can be adapted to numerical domains (e.g., time series forecasting) through the lens of isotropy. It argues that the shift-invariance of the softmax function causes problems for numerical tasks, and that isotropic embeddings preserve the underlying representation structure, thus enabling effective adaptation. The claim is supported by a theoretical proof (Theorem 1) and experiments with GPT-2 on two synthetic wireless time series datasets (TDD and FDD). One reviewer (rating 5) found the study well-motivated and the argument plausible; the other three (ratings 3) considered the work too narrow, the causal link unsubstantiated, and the presentation unclear.

### Strengths
- Provides a novel perspective on the role of isotropy in LLM embeddings for numerical/time series tasks, building on prior work.
- The interdisciplinary motivation (applying LLMs to finance, energy, climate, etc.) is timely.
- The paper is well written (Reviewer 1), and the intuition that isotropy resolves the softmax shift-invariance problem is reasonable (Reviewer 2).
- The study is well motivated and attempts a theoretical proof (Reviewer 4).
- The argument for why isotropy matters is plausible and grounded in prior analysis (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited experimental scope.** Only two synthetic datasets (TDD, FDD) are used, both from wireless communications, with a weak model (GPT-2). No experiments on popular benchmarks or real-world datasets from the domains listed (finance, energy, retail, climate, etc.). The generalizability of the findings is therefore unclear (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).
- **Causal link not established.** The claim that poor performance on Dataset 2 is due to low isotropy is speculative; other factors could be responsible. No experiments vary model architecture or data properties to isolate isotropy as a causal factor (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Poor readability and overcomplication.** The paper is not well written (Reviewer 2). Key concepts are overcomplicated: Theorem 1 is deemed unnecessary by Reviewer 4; the introduction of a “Student Model” without a “Teacher Model” is confusing (Reviewers 2, 4). Section 3.2, expected to be the most valuable, is unclear and largely reuses prior analytical methods (Cai et al., 2021). Formatting issues include improper line breaks, incorrect spacing, and figures lacking clear annotations (Reviewer 4).
- **Unclear methodological choices.** The rationale for framing regression as a classification task is not justified (Reviewer 4). Construction of the “time series vocabulary” (line 161) is not detailed – it is unclear if GPT-2's existing numeric vocabulary is used and how basic units are determined (Reviewer 4). Equation 4 has a likely subscript error ($T_l+1$ vs. $T_{l+1}$) and a problematic KL divergence formulation (expectation inside an extra summation; entropy term issues) (Reviewer 4).
- **Lack of impact argument.** The paper does not convincingly argue why the studied setting (adapting pre-softmax outputs for numerical tasks) is widely used or important for the listed application domains (Reviewer 2). Missing evidence that this approach is adopted in practice.
- **Missing dataset details.** The two datasets (TDD, FDD) lack citations, descriptions, and justification for why synthetic wireless data was chosen over open-source datasets from other domains (Reviewers 2, 4).
- **Questions that overlap with criticisms.** 
  - Why is softmax shift-invariance specifically problematic for numerical data compared to other downstream tasks? (Reviewer 3)
  - Can isotropy be shown to be a causal factor with more extensive experiments (multiple models, controlled variations)? (Reviewer 3)
  - Related to missing ablation: no experiment compares varying levels of isotropy on the same dataset (Reviewer 3).
