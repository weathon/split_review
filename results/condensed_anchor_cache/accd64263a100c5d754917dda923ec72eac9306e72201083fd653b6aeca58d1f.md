## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a novel RL benchmark (RLP) based on Simon Tatham’s Portable Puzzle Collection, wrapping 40 configurable logic puzzles as Gymnasium environments. It evaluates several model-free RL algorithms (PPO, A2C, DQN, TRPO, etc.) on a subset of puzzles and reports that current RL agents struggle to outperform a random policy on many tasks. The code is publicly available.

### Strengths
- RLP is a novel, well-motivated benchmark that could be useful for the community. It addresses an important problem: evaluating neural algorithmic reasoning in RL. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- The benchmark covers a wide variety of logic puzzles (40 games) with configurable difficulty levels and known polynomial-time optimal solutions. This allows for measuring performance gaps. (R1, R3)
- The paper evaluates multiple commonly used RL algorithms (PPO, A2C, DQN, TRPO, and variants) under different observation types (internal state vs. RGB pixels). (R1, R2)
- The source code and environments are publicly available, making the benchmark easily usable. (R2, R3, R4)
- The empirical study appears correctly executed. (R4)
- The experiments show that many common RL algorithms fail to beat a random policy on several puzzles, highlighting the need for further research in algorithmic reasoning for RL. (R3)

### Weaknesses
- **Missing critical comparisons:** The experiments do not include model-based or state‑of‑the‑art RL algorithms (e.g., MuZero, DreamerV3) nor algorithms specifically designed for hard puzzle games or neural algorithmic reasoning. Even after the rebuttal added MuZero and DreamerV3, their performance is merely stated without any discussion, training curves, or comparison on hard tasks. They reportedly “still cannot pass the human easy setting of any puzzles” and perform worse than PPO on some puzzles, yet no explanation is given. (R1, R2, R4)
- **Inadequate reward/stopping design:** All experiments report mean episode lengths instead of mean discounted returns. This is problematic for games like “Mines” that can end in failure states (failed episodes are truncated, biasing length). (R1)  
  Furthermore, no time limit is imposed on environments; for episodic RL agents, the stopping criterion is unclear. A preset time limit (e.g., based on an upper quantile of random policy completion time) is necessary for fair evaluation. (R4)
- **Selection of evaluated difficulties is poorly justified:** The paper restricts experiments to puzzle difficulties where a random policy can “find a solution” (i.e., obtain some reward), claiming this is necessary for learning. This claim is incorrect – many RL algorithms (PPO, HER, RND, etc.) can solve tasks with extremely sparse rewards where random exploration succeeds only with vanishing probability. (R1, strongly argued after rebuttal)  
  Moreover, only the easiest difficulty level is used; no results on harder difficulties are provided, which would be necessary to demonstrate scaling laws and the benchmark’s true challenge. (R1, R3)
- **Lack of detailed analysis:** The paper does not analyze why certain puzzles (e.g., “fifteen”) are solvable while others (e.g., “pegs”, “pearl”, “solo”) are not. No correlation is drawn between puzzle properties (state space complexity, optimal solution length, branching factor) and agent performance. (R3, R4)  
  The paper also does not categorize puzzles by the logical reasoning capabilities they require, nor does it evaluate RL agents that are explicitly endowed with such capabilities. (R4)
- **Poor presentation of results:** Figures with “length bars” are crowded and hard to read; error bars are nearly invisible. Aggregate summary plots or tables with bold for best performers would be far clearer. Figure 5 lacks clear added value and could be relegated to an appendix. (R3, R4)
- **Missing standardized difficulty settings:** Pre‑defined configurations for varying difficulty levels are not provided; without them, future comparisons across studies will be inconsistent. (R3)
- **Missing table reference:** Table 3 is referenced on page 9 but does not exist. (R1)
- **No comparison to optimal solutions:** Although the benchmark’s games have known polynomial‑time optimal solutions, the evaluated algorithms are not compared against them. It is unclear whether the benchmark includes these optimal solutions. (R1)
- **Benchmark scope and motivation:** The paper does not convincingly argue why algorithmic reasoning in puzzles is important for real‑world RL applications. (R4)  
  It also does not propose a new method or open a clear set of RL problems that this benchmark specifically addresses. (R2)
- **Timeliness of baselines:** The tested methods are not the newest developments; one is from 2022, another from 2020, and the rest are from 2017 or earlier. (R2)
- **Speed concerns:** The environment uses Pygame bindings; it is unclear whether training is fast (e.g., like Brax with C‑backend) or slow. A JAX interface for parallel execution would be valuable. (R3, R4)
- **Minority contrast:** One reviewer (R1, score 3) is particularly critical, emphasizing that the paper’s claim about random policy necessity is fundamentally wrong and that the experiments do not support the benchmark’s challenging nature. Another reviewer (R3, score 6) finds the benchmark relevant but notes presentation and analysis shortcomings, while R4 (score 5) stresses design issues and missing reasoning evaluation.
