## Merged Review

### Summary
The reviews converge on the importance of the fairness problem and FairCoT’s empirical gains, but diverge sharply on novelty, evaluation validity, and technical clarity. Reviewers 2 and 4 (scores 5) see the CoT adaptation as a simple, model-agnostic contribution that achieves good debiasing without quality loss; Reviewers 1 and 3 (scores 3) find the contributions incremental, the iterative refinement process unclear, and the evaluation flawed due to heavy reliance on CLIP. All reviewers raise concerns about computational inefficiency, missing baselines, and insufficient justification for key design choices.

### Strengths
- **Important and timely problem**: Reducing bias in text-to-image models is crucial for both research and real-world deployment; the paper addresses a topic that has led to usage restrictions (e.g., DALL·E, Gemini). [R1, R2, R3, R4]
- **Model-agnostic and flexible**: The method works with both open-source (Stable Diffusion variants) and closed-source models (DALL·E) without retraining or parameter tuning. [R3, R4]
- **CoT adaptation to fairness**: Successfully transfers Chain-of-Thought reasoning from LLMs to the debiasing domain. [R4]
- **Comprehensive evaluation**: Tested across multiple diffusion models and several debiasing baselines; covers multiple socially sensitive attributes (gender, race, age, religion). [R1, R2, R3]
- **Maintains image quality**: FairCoT improves fairness/diversity metrics without significantly degrading CLIP-T scores. [R1, R4]
- **Extensive related work overview** and **evaluation beyond single-person/single-concept prompts** (common in prior work). [R3]

### Weaknesses
- **Limited technical novelty**: Iterative CoT refinement has been explored before (e.g., IDA [1]); the paper does not clearly differentiate its mechanism from prior iterative strategies. [R1, R2]
- **Missing baseline comparisons**: IDA [1] and other relevant debiasing methods are not compared. [R1]
- **Unclear iterative CoT refinement process**: How CoT updates and drives fairness improvements is not concretely explained; a clearer example of the refinement logic is needed. [R1]
- **Flawed reliance on CLIP**:  
  – CLIP is used both in the pipeline (attribute prediction/entropy scoring) and as a key evaluation metric; this creates a circular evaluation because CLIP’s own biases are not accounted for. [R2, R3, R4]  
  – CLIP was not designed as an exhaustive demographic classifier; its ability to accurately classify attributes like age, religion, or cultural attire is unvalidated, potentially leading to skewed results. [R4]  
  – The paper does not explore the impact of CLIP’s known biases on the fairness conclusions. [R4]  
  – Reviewer 3 urges a complete redesign of the evaluation to avoid using CLIP-T as both a metric and part of the method.
- **Qualitative quality issues**:  
  – In Figure 6, FairCoT images appear “weird” and of poor quality; are artifacts (e.g., green noise) common? [R1, R4]  
  – FairCoT often generates multiple people in a single image compared to baselines showing only one person—does this sacrifice prompt‑faithful control? [R4]  
  – The presence of green noise suggests instability or artifacts that need investigation. [R4]
- **Computational inefficiency**: The pipeline requires multiple T2I model calls; no analysis of the typical CoT path length, number of inferences, or diminishing returns after several iterations is provided. Practical application is hindered. [R2, R4]
- **Bias in underlying MLLMs/CLIP**: The method assumes the MLLM and CLIP are unbiased, but bias in those models has been documented (citations [1,2]). This is not addressed. [R2]
- **Lack of established benchmark evaluation**: Instead of using existing fairness datasets (e.g., [1]), the authors use self‑selected prompts; this reduces comparability and reproducibility. [R3]
- **Missing annotation details**: Section 4.3 lacks information on who labeled images (annotators’ qualifications, criteria, process) and whether the hand‑labeled dataset will be shared. [R3]
- **Inappropriate representation of religion via attire**: Using “attire” to represent a religion (Figure 2, a man in a suit) is an overly broad generalization; a person of a given religion may not wear a specific attire. [R2]
- **Pipeline compatibility with SD models unclear**: Unlike DALL·E (integrated with GPT‑4), Stable Diffusion models cannot be prompted in the same way; how FairCoT adapts to them is not explained. [R2]
- **Presentation issues**:  
  – Figure 3 diagram is unclear. [R1]  
  – Captions of figures and tables are too sparse. [R2, R3]  
  – Excessive use of bullet points makes the paper appear unprofessional/outline‑like. [R4]  
  – Minor: “MLLM” abbreviation introduced multiple times; missing spaces (e.g., line 266 “(Figure1)”, line 410 “fairness.Further”). [R3]  
  – Section 3.1 uses “training stage” but no training occurs; “bias discovery stage” is more accurate. [R3]  
  – Number of prompts per category should be stated in the main text (currently only in appendix). [R3]
- **Reproducibility gaps**: The exact version of GPT‑3.5 is not specified; no open‑weight language models were tested to reduce reliance on proprietary models. [R3]
