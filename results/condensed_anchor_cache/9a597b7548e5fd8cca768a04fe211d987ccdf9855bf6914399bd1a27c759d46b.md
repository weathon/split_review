## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates whether image classification models exhibit human-like progressive difficulty understanding, where correct/incorrect responses follow a structured hierarchy (e.g., failing easy implies failing hard; passing hard implies passing easy). To test this, the authors create a synthetic dataset (36,000 images) using GPT-4 to generate prompts combining 100 ImageNet categories, 10 attributes (e.g., occlusion, viewpoint, lighting), and 3 difficulty levels (easy, medium, hard), then render images via DALL-E 3. They evaluate ~12 pretrained models (ImageNet, LAION) and find that models follow the expected hierarchical pattern ~80–90% of the time. The paper also proposes an adaptive evaluation method inspired by the GRE: a two-round test (first round: 1 easy, 2 medium, 1 hard; second round: 5 images chosen based on first-round results) to estimate overall accuracy with fewer samples. Reviewers note the interesting question and clear presentation, but raise major concerns about difficulty label validation, lack of human evaluation, overlooked existing difficulty-labeled datasets, arbitrary adaptive testing heuristics, missing statistical rigor, and insufficient situating in related work.

### Strengths
- The paper investigates an interesting and novel question about the structure of model errors in relation to difficulty.
- Writing is clear, well-organized, and self-contained; experiments and presentation are easy to follow.
- The synthetic dataset could be useful for the community (e.g., curriculum learning, robustness) if validated properly.
- The central result that models exhibit a hierarchical learning pattern consistent with the hypothesis is convincing to some reviewers (R3, R4) and demonstrated across multiple models and pretraining datasets.
- The adaptive evaluation method (GRE-inspired two-round testing) is innovative and shows precision gains over static sampling (e.g., Table 1), with some ablation validation.
- Good ablation studies (e.g., per-attribute analysis in Table 3, Appendix A4) support the analysis.

### Weaknesses
- **No human evaluation of dataset difficulty.** There is no validation that the prompt-driven difficulty labels (easy/medium/hard) correspond to human perception. Examples in Figure 2 suggest human accuracy would be near 100% even on “hard” images, undermining the core difficulty concept. (R1, R2, R4)
- **Difficulty label assignment is arbitrary and inconsistent.** The labels assume generated images reflect intended difficulty, but results (Table 3, Appendix A4) show that attributes inherently differ in difficulty (e.g., some are always harder) — the labeling scheme does not capture these cross-attribute differences, violating the claim of balance. (R2) Multiple reviewers propose alternative labeling via user studies, model-based dropout, or leveraging existing difficulty-annotated datasets. (R2, R3)
- **The claim “no real data labeled with ground-truth difficulty” is false.** Existing datasets provide difficulty-relevant annotations: PASCAL VOC 2012 has per-bounding-box occlusion/truncation; ObjectNet (Barbu et al. 2019) and SCEGRAM (Öhlschläger & Vo 2017) include difficulty information from human cognitive science. (R1, R3, R4)
- **The main hypothesis has already been validated.** Saxena et al. (NeurIPS 2019) showed that during training models learn easier samples earlier, a related hierarchical learning phenomenon. This prior work is not adequately distinguished. (R2)
- **Adaptive testing heuristic is unjustified and lacks robustness.** The choice of 4 images in round 1 and 5 in round 2 with a specific distribution (Figure 5) appears arbitrary; no justification or sensitivity analysis is provided. Different model rankings could arise from this small sample (Table 2). (R2)
- **Missing statistical rigor.** Standard deviations are not reported for any experiment (especially important due to randomness in adaptive testing and dataset sampling). (R2, R4)
- **Missing implementation details.** Confidence computation (Figure 6) is not specified; it is unclear how accuracy is estimated in Table 2 for adaptive testing (cannot simply be accuracy on 9 images). (R2, R3) The confidence measure may be uncalibrated (e.g., softmax scores). (R3)
- **No compositionality guarantee in the dataset.** The human example (2×3 vs. 2×3×4) implies compositional difficulty; it is unclear how the image attributes (occlusion, style, etc.) compose similarly across difficulty levels. (R3)
- **Related work omissions.** The paper omits relevant adaptive testing work (Gao et al., ICCV 2023) and general image-difficulty studies (Mayo 2023, Meding 2021, Jiang 2020, Kwok 2024). (R1, R3)
- **Figure 6 aggregates confidences across all models, hiding per-model differences**; it should instead show distributions per model. (R4)
- **Memorization claim in abstract (“Anything else hints at memorization”) is speculative.** The paper does not check nearest training points or duplicates. (R2)
- **Terminology issue:** “learning dynamics” on page 2 refers to post-training analysis, not training behavior. (R2)
- **Prompt generation error:** The paper mentions “nine level of variability” in a prompt (p.5), which may indicate a typo or inconsistency. (R2)
- **Real-world utility of adaptive testing is unclear**; testing is rarely a bottleneck, and reducing test size may bias results. (R2, R4)
