## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a framework centered on win rate as the only evaluation metric grounded in the preference data sampling distribution. It defines a family of Direct Win Rate Optimization (DWRO) objectives and relates existing methods to DWRO: RLHF is a KL-regularized DWRO, DPO approximates the same target distribution but uses a different objective, and SFT is not a DWRO objective but can still improve win rate. The authors provide closed-form expressions for expected win rate improvement, characterize target distribution sharpness via β and function h, and present empirical results on HH and OASST datasets using Pythia-2.8b as judge.

### Strengths
- **Theoretical grounding**: Formal proofs show that only win rate is grounded in the preference data sampling distribution, justifying the DWRO framework (R1, R3, R4).
- **Unifying lens**: The framework provides a clear, well-structured way to interpret RLHF, DPO, and SFT under a common objective, and draws an original parallel to black-box variational inference (R3, R4). One reviewer notes this is an “original and valuable tool” (R3).
- **Empirical analysis**: Experiments evaluate various DWRO-KL variants (different h and β) and show that alternative objectives can outperform RLHF, supporting the framework’s utility (R1, R3). Ablation studies on h and β are appreciated (R4).
- **Closed-form solutions**: Expressions for expected win-rate improvement offer insights into the role of the starting model and sharpness (R3). Several reviewers (R2, R4) question the practical utility, but this is listed as a strength by R3.

**Contrast**: While R3 and R4 praise the framework as useful and novel, R2 strongly contests originality, stating that the DWRO objective is identical to IPO (Equation 6 in Azar et al., 2024) and that the win-rate-centric framework is not new but essentially general preference learning from prior work (Munos et al., 2023; [1], [2], [3]). R1 similarly notes that all h types in Table 3 have been considered before.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and prior work**: Multiple reviewers (R1, R2) argue that the core contributions are not new. The DWRO objective matches the IPO objective ([1]), and fixing one response to optimize the other is exactly each iteration of Nash learning (Munos et al., 2023). All variants of h in Table 3 have been studied previously. R2 states “the major contribution, DWRO, is not new”; R1 says “novelty of this work is little.” R3, however, finds the framework original – this disagreement is noted.
- **Missing comparison to Nash learning**: Based on the theory of Munos et al. (2023) and [3], fixing one response and optimizing the other may not achieve the best performance, as one iteration does not reach the Nash equilibrium. R1 strongly recommends comparing to Munos et al. and [3].
- **SFT analysis is unclear**: Section 3.3 lacks justification for the target distribution \(p(\mathbf{y}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}, \ell=1)\). It is unclear how the other response \(y_0\) is sampled (fixed reference distribution?). The derivation from Eq. 15 to Eq. 16 is given but the origin of Eq. 16 is not explained (R2). The insight that SFT improves win rate is interesting but its practical value is not made clear (R3).
- **Insufficient experimental details**:
  - No explanation of how the reference model is trained or how preference pairs are constructed (R2). Appendix G only contains proofs, not implementation details (R2).
  - The purpose of experiments is unclear: comparing DWRO variants against a fixed reference model is less meaningful than having them compete directly (R2).
  - Judge model Pythia-2.8b is lightweight and achieves only 68.8 on the evaluation set; stronger judges (e.g., GPT-4) are standard for win-rate evaluation (R2, R4). R2 and R4 express serious concern about judge reliability.
  - DPO is discussed in analysis but not included in empirical results (R4).
  - Beta parameter importance is acknowledged but further studies on its impact are missing (R4).
  - Appendix mentions Mistral 7B but no results are provided (R4).
  - Number of samples per experiment is not stated (R4).
- **Presentation issues**:
  - Poor writing, especially in the contribution section: too many points (7) and confusing wording; should use mathematical formulas and fewer points (R1). Grammatical issues hinder comprehension (R1, R4).
  - Notation inconsistencies: anchor distribution is sometimes \(q\) and sometimes \(p\) (R2, R4). The mapping of function \(h\) is not defined (R1). The variance notation in line 354 is unclear (R1). Use of “.5” is unprofessional; should be 0.5 or 1/2 (R1).
  - Figure 1 is unclear and not discussed in the results section; guidance on interpretation is needed (R3).
  - Section 2.2 discusses evaluation function \(\phi\) but it does not appear later (R2).
- **Framework limitations**:
  - Does not account for unpaired/ranked preference methods (R4).
  - Does not address biases in preference learning, such as length bias (R4).
  - Focuses only on DWRO-KL variant; other possible variants are not explored (R4).
  - The claim that DPO and SFT may not improve win rate (Section 5) is trivial since they were not designed for general preference learning (R2).
- **Technical concerns**:
  - Sequence-level KL divergence for DWRO-KL vs. token-level for PPO makes results potentially incomparable; authors should comment (R4).
  - R2 notes that the goal of IPO is the best response to a reference model, while Nash equilibrium learning is more general; the paper’s focus on best-response objectives is limited.
- **Missing clarifications**:
  - Why fine-tune on dispreferred outputs? Which step does that correspond to? (R4)
  - Clarify anchor distribution: is it solely based on \(y_0\) as in Definition 2? (R4)
  - In Definition 2 base case (\(\ell=1\)), no \(y_1\) is provided – explain (R4).
  - Why is preference limited to \(\ell=1\)? Why not include \(\ell=0\) where \(x_0\) is preferred? (R4)
  - What is “Property 3” in the proof of Proposition 1? (R4)
- **Practical impact**: The closed-form solutions and alternative DWRO objectives yield limited actionable insights; RLHF still performs best in experiments, questioning the additional flexibility’s value (R3).
