- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 5, 3, 3, 5, 5

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes EVF-SAM, an early vision-language fusion approach for text-prompted Segment Anything Model (SAM) for referring expression segmentation. It replaces CLIP with BEIT-3 as a multimodal encoder that generates prompt embeddings from image and text inputs via early fusion, then feeds them to SAM’s prompt encoder. The method achieves state-of-the-art on RefCOCO/+/g with 1.32B parameters (82% fewer than prior large multimodal model based SAM methods). Five reviewers rated the paper scores 5, 3, 3, 5, 5, with mixed views on novelty and evaluation breadth but agreement on strong experimental results.

### Strengths
- **Strong empirical results**: EVF-SAM achieves state-of-the-art performance on RefCOCO, RefCOCO+, and RefCOCOg (all reviewers).
- **Comprehensive ablations**: Tables 3–6 systematically ablate fusion methods, trainable modules, feature representations, and foundation model choices; Table 3 shows clear advantage of early fusion (all reviewers highlight this).
- **Efficient architecture**: 1.32B parameters, ~82% fewer than LLM-based alternatives, and a lightweight multimodal encoder (BEIT-3) outperforms heavier LLMs (Reviewers 2, 3).
- **Key insight**: Investing in a strong multimodal encoder is more effective than scaling the segmentation model (e.g., larger BEIT-3 > larger SAM, see Section 4.4) (Reviewer 3).
- **Clear presentation**: Well-organized figures summarizing prior methods (Fig. 1) and architecture (Fig. 4) (Reviewers 1, 5).
- **Interesting finding**: Early-fusion multimodal encoders (BEIT-3) are beneficial for prompting SAM, which may guide future design (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty**: The core idea—adding a multimodal encoder (BEIT-3) to SAM—is incremental. Early fusion of image/text has been widely explored in prior works (e.g., Grounding DINO, GLIP, LAVT, CoupAlign, MaskGrounding). No new modules or techniques are introduced, and the paper does not clearly differentiate from these existing methods. Some reviewers view this as a simple backbone swap (Reviewers 1, 3, 4, 5).
- **Unclear early-fusion contribution**: It is ambiguous whether “early fusion” is a proposed novelty or a property inherited from BEIT-3’s architecture. The paper provides only a high-level diagram (Fig. 3) and a vague statement (line 230). The mechanism is not explained in detail. Using a special [CLS] token after projection as prompt embeddings seems similar to the special token approach that the paper claims causes instability (Reviewer 5). The performance gain may be specific to BEIT-3: early fusion with ViLT yields modest improvement (66.6 vs 51.7 average) whereas for BEIT-3-Large it jumps from 52.3 to 78.0 (Reviewer 5). Moreover, early fusion experiments with CLIP are absent (Reviewer 5).
- **Unfair comparisons in main table (Table 2)**:
  - Comparison methods use different encoders (e.g., CLIP, GPT-4, LLaVA), while EVF-SAM uses BEIT-3, which is not a contribution of this paper. If ViLT replaces BEIT-3, performance on RefCOCO val drops from 82.1 to 73.9 (Reviewer 1).
  - Training data varies across methods; EVF-SAM uses 1,920k samples (128/iter × 15k steps) vs. LISA-7B’s 800k samples. This discrepancy should be ablated (Reviewer 4). Fair comparisons under the same training data (e.g., same as PSALM or LISA) are needed (Reviewer 5).
  - Many comparison methods (LLM-based) are capable of additional tasks (reasoning, VQA, generic segmentation), so claiming superiority in referring segmentation alone is insufficient (Reviewers 2, 5).
- **Narrow evaluation scope**:
  - Only COCO-based datasets (RefCOCO/+/g); no in-the-wild testing on ReasonSeg (LISA) or ReasonSeg-Ins (LISA++) (Reviewer 1). Additional tasks (zero-shot semantic/instance/open-vocabulary segmentation) are needed to demonstrate robustness and avoid overfitting to RES benchmarks (Reviewer 2).
  - No comparison to other SAM text-enhancement methods such as Open-Vocabulary SAM, SAM-CLIP, AlignSAM (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing details in comparisons**:
  - Table 2 does not report parameter counts, training data volumes, or inference speeds for compared methods (Reviewers 1, 2). Table 6 should include inference speed (Reviewer 1). Table 7 should report mIoU for ADE20K instead of cIoU (Reviewer 5).
- **Insufficient exploration of vision-language models**: Only CLIP and BEIT-3 are tested. The effectiveness of early fusion for other multimodal models (e.g., VLMO, CoCa) is unproven (Reviewer 2). Computation comparison between BEIT-3 and CLIP is missing, weakening the conclusion that early fusion (vs. late fusion) is the cause of improvement—BEIT-3 may have more compute (Reviewer 3).
- **Weaknesses in writing and related work**:
  - References omitted for claims about previous text-prompted SAM works (line 051) and statements about “uncontrolled length of answering query” (Reviewer 3).
  - Vague descriptions in Section 2.2 (e.g., “matches” similarity, and short sentence-per-method listings) (Reviewer 3).
  - Contradictory reasoning: traditional RES models are criticized for not integrating foundation models (citing LLaVA), then later LLM-based methods are criticized for heavy computation (Reviewer 3).
  - The “semantic conflict” motivating the [semantic] token is not explained (Reviewer 3).
- **Specific unanswered questions** (reviewers’ requests for ablations or explanations):
  - Why use zero-initialized sparse embeddings concatenated to multimodal embeddings? (Reviewer 3)
  - Why is it necessary to train/fine-tune SAM’s prompt encoder when performance gain is only 0.4 cIoU (Table 4)? Could the multimodal embedding go directly to the mask decoder? (Reviewer 3)
  - Why is the image [CLS] token most effective in Table 5? (Reviewer 3)
  - Attention maps in Figure 5 show [CLS] attending to irrelevant image patches—could this be due to missing “register” tokens (see Darcet et al., ICLR 2024)? (Reviewer 3)
  - Is early fusion applicable to decoder-only LLM-based architectures like PSALM and LISA? (Reviewer 5)
  - Code and data should be released to aid reproducibility (Reviewer 5).
