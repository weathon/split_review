## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates robust overfitting in adversarial training (AT), attributing it to learning “non-effective features” from normal data, and proposes two measures—attack strength adjustment and data augmentation—to mitigate it. Experiments on CIFAR-10/100 show improved robustness. Reviewers gave scores of 6, 3, 3, 3, 1. The majority (four reviewers) raised significant concerns about clarity, reproducibility, lack of theoretical support, and insufficient comparisons, while one reviewer (score 6) found the work original, rigorous, and clearly written. One reviewer (score 1) strongly criticized undefined terminology, lack of evidence, and poor presentation.

### Strengths
- Addresses the important and poorly understood problem of robust overfitting in adversarial training.
- Provides a novel perspective by separating normal data and adversarial perturbations, and designs insightful factor ablation experiments (e.g., Figure 1 comparing removing small-loss adversarial examples with removing only the perturbation).
- The discussion of the robust overfitting phenomenon, especially in Section 3.2, is clear and well-motivated.
- The proposed method is plug-and-play and tested with multiple attack and training methods (AT, AWP, TRADES, MLCAT) using AutoAttack as evaluation.
- One reviewer (score 6) found the paper well-structured, clearly written, and making a significant contribution to understanding robust overfitting.

### Weaknesses
- Key concepts, including “non-effective features”, “learning state”, and “similarity of learning states between training and test data”, are left undefined and vague. They require formal definitions with equations and should be empirically or theoretically validated in the context of adversarial training.
- Many central claims (e.g., about the gap in learning states and its evolution) are presented without objective experimental or theoretical evidence. The paper relies heavily on intuitive reasoning.
- The proposed methods (attack strength adjustment and data augmentation) are not described with sufficient detail: no equations, pseudo-code, or precise explanation of how to adjust attack strength, how to use AugMix, what loss threshold defines “small-loss”, and what the specified threshold is. This hinders reproducibility.
- Experiments are confined to CIFAR-10/100 with simple CNN architectures; evaluations on larger datasets (e.g., ImageNet) and modern architectures are missing, limiting generalizability.
- There are no empirical comparisons with existing techniques for mitigating robust overfitting (e.g., weight averaging, early stopping, SWA, etc.), despite being mentioned in related work.
- The proposed methods are not novel: data augmentation is a standard regularization technique, and attack strength adjustment (e.g., scheduling or adaptive budgets) is already common in adversarial training (e.g., PGD-based AT). The paper does not differentiate sufficiently.
- The ablation studies show that OROAT can have a negative effect on robustness under certain conditions; the paper acknowledges this but does not provide a detailed analysis or practical remedy.
- The writing is unbalanced: the analysis and method sections are lengthy, while the experimental section is too condensed and lacks important details (hyperparameters, training procedures, attack configurations) for reproducibility and assessment.
- The paper focuses on explaining robust overfitting rather than achieving state-of-the-art robust accuracy; the practical gains are modest.
- One reviewer (score 1) strongly emphasized the lack of evidence and poor presentation, while another (score 6) found the paper clear and rigorous; this reflects a substantial disagreement on presentation quality.
- The analysis does not include quantitative measures of the “gap” between training and test learning states, nor theoretical bounds on when robust overfitting occurs.
- Limited ablation on the proposed method’s own hyperparameters (e.g., attack strength schedule, AugMix intensity) to isolate their effects.
