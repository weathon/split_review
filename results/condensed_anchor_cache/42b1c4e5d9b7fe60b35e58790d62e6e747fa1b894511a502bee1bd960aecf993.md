## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a template-content (T-C) framework to explain LLM reasoning, formalized as hierarchical decomposition during autoregressive generation. It provides theoretical existence proofs for template and content transformers and presents experiments on last-letter-concatenation and SingleEQ tasks. Reviewers find the framework interesting but criticize the assumptions, weak experimental support, and unclear practical significance. Ratings span 3, 5, and 6, indicating substantial disagreement: Reviewer 1 (score 3) is most critical of the theory’s assumptions and experiment quality; Reviewer 2 (score 5) questions the practical relevance of the existence proofs; Reviewer 3 (score 6) finds the paper clear but seeks stronger validation.

### Strengths
- The proposed template-content framework is interesting, and the hierarchical extension is more flexible in explaining model’s abilities in solving complex reasoning tasks (R1).
- The paper provides proof of the existence of template transformers and content transformer functions for the T-C decomposition (R2).
- The explanations are clearly written in simple terms (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Unrealistic assumptions in theory** (R1, strong criticism): The paper assumes a “well-trained” model that perfectly memorizes all answers, claiming this is “not challenging for prevailing LLMs.” However, existing work shows that even largest models struggle with tail knowledge [1]. This sidesteps the core generalization challenge—handling unseen data—rather than memorized instances. The framework does not address how novel template-content combinations are handled.
- **Combinatorial explosion in hierarchical extension** (R1): The hierarchical decomposition assumes the model can efficiently search a large space of sub-template/sub-content combinations, especially with label-consistency variants (Appendix A.1). No mechanism is given for navigating this space, nor does it address multiple valid decompositions or varying degrees of memorization. The theory therefore does not convincingly explain solving very complex tasks.
- **Weak experimental support** (R1, R2, strong agreement):  
  - The two tasks (last-letter-concat, SingleEQ) are very simple. To support claims of flexibility and generalizability, more complex tasks like GSM8K or AQuA should be tested (R1).  
  - The T/C classification in Figure 4 (right) is ad-hoc: some green bars are too short to see, making it unclear which model “exhibits the clearest T/C distinction.” No quantitative analysis of the green/blue bar ratios is provided; the ratios appear not significantly different between models, so judging “reasoning capability” from them is unjustified (R1).  
  - In Figure 5 (SingleEQ), the proposed classification already conflicts with human intuition, raising doubts about practical use, especially for more complex tasks. The definition of content as “names, objections, and Arabic numbers” is ad-hoc; the paper does not explain how to apply T-C decomposition generally to any task (R1).
- **Practical significance of existence proofs unclear** (R2, strong criticism): The paper proves existence of template and content transformers but does not explain why this decomposition is important for practical NLP. The functions \(f_T\) and \(f_C\) could be more complex than a single transformer model (e.g., decomposing a 7B model might require 70B functions), potentially negating any benefit. No analysis of computational cost, model size, or training efficiency is provided.
- **No experimental validation of benefits** (R2, also noted by R1, R3): The paper lacks empirical results showing that T-C decomposition improves task performance or reduces computation. There is no demonstration on general NLP tasks like summarization, translation, or standard benchmarks (e.g., HELM, lm-evaluation-harness) (R2). Reviewer 3 also requests examination of misclassification cases and comparison with alternative explanation models using numerical experiments.
- **Presentation issues** (R1, R3): Crucial details are frequently relegated to the appendix, disrupting reading continuity (R3). The paper is not easily readable (R2).  
- **Missing reference** (R1): The paper omits discussion of “Text and Patterns: For Effective Chain of Thought, It Takes Two to Tango” (Madaan et al. 2022), which addresses how text (content) and patterns (template) affect reasoning.  
- **Reviewer questions overlapping with criticisms** (treated as weaknesses):  
  - (R1) How to define template and content in general for any task?  
  - (R1) Can the authors provide quantitative analysis on the ratio between green/blue bars in Figure 4?  
  - (R2) Can \(f_T\) and \(f_C\) be more complex than a single transformer model?  
  - (R2) Will such decomposition benefit tasks in computation or performance for general NLP benchmarks?  
  - (R3) Are there alternative explanation models in the literature? A comparison with numerical experiments would be beneficial.
