## Merged Review

### Summary
The authors introduce DarkBench, a benchmark of 660 prompts across six categories (brand bias, user retention, sycophancy, anthropomorphism, harmful generation, sneaking) to detect dark design patterns in LLMs. They test 14 proprietary and open models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Mistral, and Google, using a mix of LLM annotators (Claude Sonnet, Gemini Pro, GPT-4o, Llama3 70b) and human annotation. Results show dark patterns in 48% of cases, ranging from 30% (best) to 61% across models.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles a crucial and novel issue—defining and measuring dark patterns in LLMs—that has not been addressed in prior research.
- Extensive comparison of 14 proprietary and open-source models on the benchmark.
- The paper is well-written and well-organized; the direction is important for evaluating model safety.
- Human review and validation were performed for both the LLM-generated prompts and the LLM-generated evaluations, adding credibility.
- The selection of the six patterns is relevant and the problem is well motivated; the DarkBench dataset itself is a valuable asset.
- Figures are relatively clear and concise.
- One reviewer expressed excitement about the direction of evaluating LLM behavior based on established design patterns, acknowledging the difficulty of this task even with limitations.

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical engagement with the concept of “dark patterns” is insufficient.** It is not convincingly argued that the six tendencies qualify as dark patterns (intentional designs harmful to users and beneficial to the designer). Specific misalignments: “harmful generation” and “sycophancy” are broader than dark patterns; anthropomorphism is not necessarily harmful (e.g., beneficial in mental health contexts); brand bias may be incidental from preference tuning or system prompts (e.g., Google ranking its own content is not a dark pattern). One reviewer noted this as a primary concern unlikely to be resolvable during review.
- **LLM annotator bias is not addressed.** Using LLMs to annotate dark patterns may introduce their own dark patterns (e.g., brand bias causing more favorable evaluations of own-brand responses). No statistical test was performed to check whether an LLM’s scores for its own responses differ significantly from those it assigns to others.
- **Krippendorff’s Kappa is reported as low/poor but key details are missing.** The exact Kappa score and the threshold used for “poor agreement” are not specified. The claim that “summary statistics remain consistent” is insufficiently justified; the implications of low inter-rater agreement on the reliability of the benchmark results are not thoroughly discussed. It is also unclear what the agreement scores were within/between model judges and human annotators.
- **Decision criteria for classifying a model as exhibiting a dark pattern are unclear.** The paper states “48% of all cases” (p.5 line 243) but does not define the cutoff, whether it is a simple proportion, or whether metrics like recall/precision would be more informative. No statistical significance testing or group differences are reported to strengthen the conclusions.
- **Methodology description is vague in several areas:**
    - It is unclear if humans reviewed all 660 DarkBench prompts for quality; rephrasing occurred but its purpose and nature are not explained.
    - Benchmark application parameters (e.g., number of responses per model: one or sampled multiple times, temperature, etc.) are not specified.
    - No information is given on correlation between different annotator models, how the final yes/no answer was generated, or whether positional bias in LLM-as-a-judge was accounted for.
    - The results of human reviews on the annotator model outcomes are not presented.
- **LLM generation and validation lack rigor.** Details are missing on the extent of mode collapse in prompt generations, comparisons across generations from different models, and the use of subcategories within the six categories (a priori or through clustering). The validation burden for LLM-generated benchmarks is high and not adequately met.
- **LLM evaluation concerns:** The poor inter-rater agreement noted above undermines trust in the LLM judges; moreover, it is unclear how different model judges (e.g., Claude vs. Llama) were compared and aggregated. One reviewer noted that the benchmark may be picking up on friendly tuning (e.g., Claude’s heavy friendliness tuning) rather than meaningful dark patterns, which would limit novelty and relevance.
- **Lack of stability testing across prompt variations.** No evidence is provided that the findings are consistent when prompts are paraphrased (e.g., the prompts in Table 1).
- **Lack of qualitative examples.** The results section would benefit from including actual responses from the models to illustrate dark patterns.
- **Lack of detail on human annotators.** The paper does not report annotator demographics (age, gender, expertise) or clarify whether LimeSurvey annotators were laypeople or experts, making it difficult to assess generalizability to typical LLM users.
- **Potential negative impact of reducing anthropomorphism.** One reviewer raised concern that reducing anthropomorphism—as called for by the benchmark—could harm legitimate uses of LLMs in social science, such as simulating human samples (e.g., Argyle et al. 2023; Törnberg et al. 2023). This tradeoff is not discussed.
- **Minor issues:** The origin of the term “dark pattern” is misattributed (it was coined in a 2010 blog post by Harry Brignull, not the creation of darkpatterns.org). The paper contains numerous typos. Figure 4 ordering does not appear to be “by least to highest frequency of dark patterns” as stated. “Appendix 5” is referenced but does not appear to exist in the provided material.
