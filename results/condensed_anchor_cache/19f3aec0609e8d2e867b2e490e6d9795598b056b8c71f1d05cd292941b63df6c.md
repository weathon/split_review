## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies the role of discrete tokenization as reconstruction targets in masked image modeling (MIM). It provides theoretical analysis, connecting MIM to contrastive learning and showing that tokens aligned with downstream class labels yield better generalization. A new metric, Token-Class Alignment Similarity (TCAS), is proposed to measure tokenizer quality. Based on this, the authors introduce ClusterMIM (originally called K-MIM), a clustering-based discrete tokenizer that achieves strong performance on ImageNet-100 and 200-epoch ImageNet-1k experiments with ViT backbones.

### Strengths
- The core question – how discrete tokenization affects MIM downstream performance – is interesting and necessary.
- The theoretical analysis using a graph-based view of MIM is novel and provides formal grounding for the role of tokenization.
- The TCAS metric is cheap to compute and appears predictive of tokenizer effectiveness; it can help the community study MIM tokenizers.
- The paper theoretically and empirically demonstrates the superiority of class-wise tokenization (tokens that align with class structure).
- The proposed tokenizer is simple, especially the pixel-based variant (K-MIM pixel), which achieves significant gains without requiring a separate pre-trained model.
- The paper is well-organized and clearly articulated for the most part (though some reviewers found the introduction could be smoother).

### Weaknesses
- **Experimental scale**: Experiments are limited to ImageNet-100 and a short 200-epoch schedule on ImageNet-1k. This is insufficient to fully validate K-MIM’s impact; results on larger-scale benchmarks and longer training are needed. Reported TCAS numbers are also limited to a few settings.
- **Applicability of Theorem 1**: Theorem 1 suggests that an image classifier (tokens perfectly aligned with classes) gives the best downstream error bound. However, in practice MIM often outperforms classification-based pretraining. One reviewer doubts the general applicability of the theorem and the TCAS metric across diverse MIM tasks. Clarifying when the bound holds (e.g., two-class setting) and reconciling with empirical results is needed.
- **Theoretical scope**: The analysis is restricted to two classes. It is unclear if the results extend to multi-class settings. The derivation connecting graph connectivity to the error bound lacks sufficient detail (e.g., why bounds in Eq. 7 do not depend on \(l\)).
- **Tokenizer design and assumptions**: Discrete tokenizers like dVAE/VQGAN use ConvNets/ViTs to produce interrelated tokens that incorporate context from multiple patches. The paper treats tokens as independent local information, which may not correctly reflect their nature. This assumption needs justification or relaxation.
- **Relation to distillation**: Using DINO features to create tokens (K-MIM DINO) effectively supervises the MIM model with DINO. Yet K-MIM DINO achieves a linear probe accuracy of 67.4%, far below DINO’s 78.2% (ICCV 2021). The efficiency argument for K-MIM DINO should account for the cost of training DINO itself (the pixel-based variant avoids this). The paper should discuss the relationship to distillation targets (e.g., [B] Exploring Target Representations for Masked Autoencoders) and the expected performance relative to the teacher.
- **Ablation and metric consistency**: The TCAS metric is only reported in Table 2; it should also appear in Tables 3 and 4 to help explain performance variations (e.g., how TCAS changes with \(K\) in Table 4). More ablation on clustering numbers (e.g., \(K = 10, 25, 200\)) in Table 4 is required.
- **Performance discrepancies**: The reported fine-tuning result for MAE in Table 3 is 82.9%, while He et al. (CVPR 2022) report 83.6% – please explain the gap. The sharp drop in K-MIM performance with larger token books (Table 4) is unexplained, and contrasts with DINO’s trend; TCAS may shed light.
- **Missing comparisons**: The paper should report TCAS and MIM performance when using an image classifier (e.g., DeiT) as the tokenizer. It should also explore other feature sources for clustering, such as ImageNet-supervised ViT or MAE features.
- **Naming and readability**: The term “Mixture Tokenizer” is confusing (sounds adversarial/ad-hoc); a clearer name is suggested. The introduction jumps directly into specific methods without background on MIM or tokenization, reducing readability for newcomers.
- **Optimization for TCAS**: While K-MIM achieves a good TCAS score, it is not explicitly optimized for TCAS. Is there a tokenization method that directly maximizes TCAS? This is a natural next step.
