## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a backdoor attack on end-to-end autonomous driving systems using multi-vehicle trajectories as dynamic triggers, generated and evaluated via temporal logic (DiffSpec). The authors also introduce a negative training strategy with patch trajectories to improve stealth. While some reviewers find the approach novel, comprehensive, and practically important (scores 5), others raise serious concerns about over-assumed attacker capability, high poisoning rates, lack of physical-world validation, and insufficient justification of the dynamic multi-vehicle trigger’s necessity (scores 3). There is clear reviewer disagreement on the overall plausibility and impact of the work.

### Strengths
1. **Novel dynamic multi-vehicle trigger design** – The paper breaks from static triggers (e.g., images, markers) by using multi-vehicle trajectories, making detection by conventional defenses more challenging (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). The dynamic nature better reflects real driving scenarios and is more practical to deploy (Reviewer 3).
2. **Trajectory generation framework with temporal logic** – Combining behavior-driven generation with temporal logic evaluation (DiffSpec) efficiently enforces temporal and spatial constraints to produce realistic, complex multi-vehicle interactions (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). This integration of TL for attack synthesis/evaluation is considered interesting (Reviewer 4).
3. **Negative training mechanism** – Incorporating non-trigger “patch trajectories” that share similarities with triggers but do not activate the backdoor enhances stealth and accuracy (Reviewers 1, 3).
4. **Comprehensive evaluation** – The attack is tested under various trigger patterns, RL algorithms, target actions, and against existing defenses (data smoothing, DP-SGD), with metrics ADE and MVR significantly compromised (Reviewers 2, 3). An ablation study validates design choices (Reviewer 3). One reviewer also appreciates the ethical stance of evaluating defenses (Reviewer 2).
5. **Importance of the problem** – Analyzing vulnerability of offline RL agents for backdoor attacks is recognized as significant (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Over-strong attacker assumptions** – Reviewers 3 and 4 question the threat model: the attacker is assumed to have full access to the static training dataset, can poison trajectories and manipulate rewards, which is unrealistic given that AD companies tightly control data (Reviewer 3). The scope and assumptions (e.g., knowledge of MDP, state/action space) are also unclear (Reviewer 4).
2. **High poisoning rate** – Both Reviewers 2 and 3 highlight that ~15% poisoning is too high to be stealthy; Reviewer 2 notes this resembles a “front door” rather than a backdoor (e.g., if 85% of data is normal weekday driving, a 15% weekend trigger is improbable). The choice of poisoning rate is questioned (Reviewers 2, 3).
3. **Lack of physical-world / closed-loop validation** – The evaluation is limited to the ADE/MVR metrics in simulation; no real-world case studies or closed-loop experiments are provided to prove the realizability of the generated triggers or robustness to real-world noise, lighting, weather, and trajectory inaccuracies (Reviewers 1, 2). Representative successful case studies are needed (Reviewer 2).
4. **Insufficient justification for dynamic multi-vehicle trigger necessity** – Reviewer 2 states that the paper fails to demonstrate why static or single-vehicle triggers are ineffective for this domain, citing that inference-time adversarial attacks (Cao et al., Zhang et al.) might suffice. The claim that static triggers are impractical is not well supported beyond a single citation (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 3 also asks why not use a single-vehicle trajectory trigger for easier activation.
5. **Limited defense evaluation and consideration of safe/robust learning** – Only data smoothing and DP-SGD are tested; Reviewers 2 and 4 call for testing against more advanced defenses. Moreover, Reviewer 4 notes that the effectiveness of the attack is not discussed when safe learning or robust learning methods are integrated into the RL agent.
6. **Temporal logic framework limitations** – Only a single temporal logic specification (DiffSpec) is used; Reviewers 1 and 4 question this choice. Reviewer 4 asks why DiffSpec is chosen over other TL formulations and suggests discussing hyperproperties (since DiffSpec normally evaluates single trajectories, but triggers involve multiple trajectories). Reviewer 1 notes that more varied temporal/spatial constraints could support more complex stealthy patterns.
7. **Need for more comprehensive literature comparison** – Reviewer 4 requests a table comparing the proposed method with existing works on temporal logic-based attacks, specifically citing Jiang et al. (2024, ICCPS and ESWEEK) on vulnerability analysis and backdoor attacks in safe RL-cyber-physical systems.
8. **Generalization and statistical rigor** – Reviewer 4 questions whether negative training generalizes to unseen trajectories in the real environment, and asks how uncertainty in the training dataset and real environment is measured. Multiple runs with statistics (e.g., reporting best performance) are suggested but absent.
9. **Presentation issues** – Reviewer 3 notes that font sizes in Table 4 and Figure 3 are too small to read numerical results clearly.
10. **Motivating example ambiguity** – Reviewer 4 is concerned that the given motivating example (Section 3.2) is actually a common real-life scenario, making it unclear how the backdoor avoids being triggered during normal deployment.
