## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes TransFusion, a framework for zero-shot cross-lingual information extraction (IE). The key idea is to translate a low-resource language input into English, then instruct the model to first generate annotations on the English translation before predicting annotations on the original low-resource input (a “translate, annotate, fuse” approach). For decoder-only models like GoLLIE, this is done in a single prompt; for encoder-only models, a two-step pipeline is used. The authors fine-tune GoLLIE on 4‑way parallel data (source English text, target translation, and their respective annotations) to create GoLLIE‑TF. Experiments across 12 multilingual IE datasets covering 50 languages show gains over GoLLIE, GPT‑4, and translate‑train baselines, especially for low-resource languages.

### Strengths

- The approach is simple and well motivated, with a clear explanation of the data generation and training procedure (all reviewers). However, one reviewer (R3) finds the paper requires thorough reading and is not clearly explained at first pass.
- TransFusion is effective in multiple configurations: prompting decoder‑only models (including GPT‑4), fine‑tuning decoder‑only models (GoLLIE‑TF), and fine‑tuning encoder‑only models (R1, R2, R3, R4). It is also easy to combine with API‑gated models without fine‑tuning (R1).
- Evaluation is extensive, covering NER, relation extraction, and slot filling across many datasets and 50 languages, with particular focus on low‑resource African languages (R1, R2, R4).
- Ablation studies evaluate the contribution of the “annotate in English first” step and the impact of different machine translation model sizes (R1, R2, R4).
- Manual error analysis is provided (R2).
- The method is reproducible (R1) and includes a stronger baseline (TransTrain) that uses the same translated data but does not train on the fusion task (R2).
- GoLLIE‑TF improves over GPT‑4 prompting and translate‑train baselines in most languages (R2, R3, R4).
- The work establishes a clear path for IE on low‑resource languages, which could be impactful for applications (R2).

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty concerns.** Translation‑based cross‑lingual transfer is not new; many prior works use external MT for cross‑lingual transfer or multilingual instruction tuning (e.g., EMNLP 2023, NAACL 2024, EACL 2024 findings, ACL 2024 findings). The paper overstates novelty and omits related works such as Paolini et al. (Structured Prediction as Translation) (R1, R2, R4). R1 rates contribution as 2, while R2 and R4 also flag limited innovation.
- **Missing baselines.** The translate‑test baseline (annotating the translated English text directly, without fusion) is not formally evaluated for either GPT‑4 or GoLLIE, making it hard to isolate the fusion step’s contribution (R1, R4). Improved translate‑test variants from recent literature are also absent (R1). State‑of‑the‑art scores on these datasets are not provided for context (R1).
- **Limited model scope.** Experiments use only GoLLIE‑7B; the method should be validated on other open‑weights models (e.g., Llama‑3, Gemma 2) and larger sizes (e.g., 70B) to verify whether gains diminish with scale (R1). R3 notes that the three “flavours” of TransFusion are really different models, not a single approach.
- **Clarity issues.** The notation for source/translated language is inconsistent between data generation (Section 3.1) and inference (Section 3.2), causing confusion (R2). Figure 1 does not clearly show the translation, English‑model prediction, and fusion steps (R3). The description of training data is ambiguous: L238‑L241 suggests 19k English examples and 891 translated examples, but Section 3.1 states all training data must be 4‑way parallel (R2). Minor presentation errors: “lagnauge”, double parentheses in language lists, Gemini reference formatting (R3). The paper does not fill the 10‑page limit (R3).
- **Applicability beyond IE.** The method is tailored to IE tasks; the paper does not discuss whether it would benefit other sequence‑labeling tasks (POS, dependency parsing) or NLU tasks (NLI, QA) often used to evaluate cross‑lingual transfer (R1).
- **Computational cost.** The cost of the additional translation and generation step is only discussed in the appendix (Table 7), not in the main paper (R4). R1 asks whether the cost can be reduced and whether skipping the translation step during inference after fine‑tuning would work (R4).
- **Fusion comparison and alignment.** The fusion step is not compared against standard rule‑based aligners (R2). R4 asks if training on translation+fusion alone (without translating test inputs) could already improve performance.
- **Language coverage and properties.** It is unclear whether the method works for languages unseen by NLLB (R1). The effect of language proximity/distance to English on performance is not analyzed (R1). The paper also does not explain why varying the MT model size does not significantly affect results (R1).
- **Disagreement among reviewers.** R1 is much more critical (score 3, contribution 2) than R2, R3, and R4 (scores 6,6,5). R3 finds the exposition unclear despite R1 and R2 describing it as well explained. R3 also flags that the three configurations are essentially separate models, while R1 and R2 treat them as a unified approach.
