## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes PE2, an automatic prompt engineering method that uses a hand-designed meta-prompt to iteratively propose and refine prompts via an LLM. The meta-prompt incorporates detailed instructions, a two-step task description, a step-by-step reasoning template, context specification, and optimizer-inspired concepts (batch size, step size, momentum, hard negative sampling, backtracking). Experiments on mathematical reasoning (MultiArith, GSM8K), instruction induction, and counterfactual tasks show that PE2 outperforms existing baselines (APO, APE) and a human-engineered prompt. The paper also ablates components, finding that the prompt tutorial and optimizer concepts like step size and momentum are not consistently beneficial and are excluded from the final PE2.

### Strengths
- The problem of automating prompt engineering is important and of wide interest (R1, R2, R4).
- The meta-prompt design includes several well-motivated components (detailed instructions, step-by-step reasoning, context specification, optimizer inspiration) that are empirically ablated (R1, R3, R4).
- Experimental results show consistent improvements over existing automatic (APO, APE) and human baselines on the tested datasets (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- The paper provides illustrative examples and the appendix contains sufficient implementation details to reproduce the method (R2).
- One reviewer (R3, score 8) praised the extensive empirical evaluation: component choices are justified with real examples, and even discarded components (prompt tutorial, momentum) are systematically tested before exclusion – a sign of thoroughness.
- The idea of enriching the meta-prompt with explicit instructions and context is seen as a novel contribution (R4).
- The method handles poor initializations, hallucination, and instruction-ignoring problems, and can make targeted prompt edits (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and thin algorithmic contribution.** The main innovation is the hand-crafted meta-prompt itself, with little further insight. Reviewers note: “algorithmic contribution seems a bit thin beyond the meta-prompt design” (R2). The optimizer-inspired components (step size, momentum, prompt tutorial) do not consistently help, making it unclear what the actual contribution is (R1). The iteration limit (t=3) is not well justified, and the paper lacks analysis of why performance plateaus so quickly or what prompts are generated at the end (R2, R4). One reviewer (R3) acknowledges this but still values the empirical grounding; another (R2) suggests the main paper should only describe the final PE2 and move negative experiments to the appendix – a point of disagreement.
- **Insufficient evaluation scope and task diversity.** Most ablation and analysis (Tables 1,2,3) are limited to simple math datasets (MultiArith, GSM8K). Harder math datasets (e.g., MATH) and more representative task categories (QA, text summarization) are not tested (R1). For GSM8K, the gap to SOTA (90%+) raises concerns about effectiveness on stronger foundation models (R1). The main evaluation uses Text-Davinci-003, but the meta-prompt and prompt proposal use GPT-4; baselines (APO, APE) are evaluated only with Text-Davinci-003, and it is unclear whether using GPT-4 for their paraphrasing would change reported numbers (R3). Results with GPT-4 or GPT-3.5 as the main model are not provided (R3). “Does the approach work on a more general use case?” (R1).
- **Missing comparisons to recent prompt optimization methods.** The paper does not compare with meta-prompt optimization (Section 4.2 of arxiv 2309.03409) or prompt design to optimize demonstrations (arxiv 2305.14106) (R1).
- **Presentation and clarity issues.** The paper describes many components (tutorial, step size, momentum) that are later discarded, making it “hard to separate the wheat from the chaff” (R2). The clarity of the gradient-based update for hard prompts is insufficient – how are gradients accessed? (R4). The use of optimization terminology (“batch”, “step size”, “momentum”) is confusing because “batch” refers to prompt examples rather than fine-tuning batches, and step size/momentum are not used in the final PE2 (R2). The term “concept” in the optimizer analogy is not formally defined (R4, footnote 3). Figures 3 and 4 show performance across timestamps rather than at the final or converged step (R4).
- **Specific methodological questions and missing details.** (Many of these are phrased as questions but overlap with criticisms or missing experiments.)
    - How are the examples for hard negative sampling selected (Line 42, B.4)? Does `batch_size` refer to total examples or only negative examples? (R2)
    - Appendix C.1.1 shows more in-context examples (3, 10) improve performance – why then is `batch_size` set to 2 in PE2? (R2)
    - Where is the validation set \( D_\text{dev} \) used in Algorithm 1? (Possibly in Select-Best?) (R2)
    - Why does prompt performance plateau so quickly by t=3? How much performance would be lost with only one iteration (t=1)? (R2)
    - What effect does the choice of examples in a “batch” have on performance? Could active learning be incorporated? (R2)
    - Can the meta-prompt itself be optimized with PE2? (R3) [Noted as a future direction but relevant.]
