## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes hierarchically branched diffusion models for class-conditional generation. Branch points between classes are determined from the forward diffusion process based on pairwise class similarity, and separate reverse diffusion is learned for each branch. The framework is claimed to offer advantages over classifier-free guidance: efficient incorporation of new classes in a continual-learning setting, analogy-based conditional generation (transmutation), and interpretability of the generation process. Experiments are conducted on image (MNIST, letter-recognition), single-cell RNA-seq, and molecular graph datasets.

### Strengths
*   **Novelty and elegance (R2, R3, R4):** The approach is a creative, highly novel use of the diffusion forward process itself to control generation in a structured way, largely unexplored in prior work.
*   **Clear writing and visualizations (R1, R2, R4):** The paper is well-written, easy to understand, and benefits from well-crafted visualizations that aid comprehension.
*   **Meaningful advantages demonstrated (R1, R2, R3, R4):** The method shows promising benefits over standard class-conditional diffusion:
    *   Competitive or better FID on some datasets compared to classifier-free guidance (R2).
    *   Multi-class sampling is considerably more efficient (R2, R4, Table S9).
    *   Can incorporate new classes without full retraining, and the observation that training new classes on only subsets of the diffusion forward process avoids catastrophic forgetting is a genuinely new and striking effect (R2, R3).
    *   Enables transmutation (analogy-based conditional generation) across data modalities (R2, R3, R4).
    *   Provides a degree of interpretability via averaged noisy data at branch points (R1, R4).
*   **Potential to inspire future work (R3):** The ideas could motivate more structured diffusion generative models. One minority reviewer (R3) rated contribution as only “fair”, but still acknowledged the creative potential.

### Weaknesses
*   **Insufficient experimental support for key claims (R2, R3):** Most claims are supported by limited experimentation.
    *   Claims of “similar or better” generative performance relative to state-of-the-art are based on only two simple character-based image datasets (MNIST, letter-recognition) and one RNA-seq dataset; no outperformance was observed for the RNA-seq data. The phrase “in many cases” overstates the findings (R2).
    *   Only one baseline (classifier-free guidance, Ho et al., 2021) is used for comparison (R4).
    *   The new class / continual learning experiments are limited: starting with 3 classes on MNIST and 2 classes on RNA-seq before adding a third. It is unclear whether the effect extends to more realistic scenarios with more steps or when the “1” class is already included (R2, R3).
*   **Scalability concerns not addressed (R1, R2, R4):** Experiments are limited to datasets with small numbers of classes. No results or complexity analysis are provided for larger class counts (R1, R4). It remains open how well the approach scales to more challenging, less-structured image classes where the hierarchy may not be clear (R2).
*   **Branching point threshold selection is not guided (R2):** The definition of branch points involves a threshold, but no concrete recipe is given for its selection, and there is no investigation of how the threshold impacts performance.
*   **Analogy-based generation (transmutation) is not convincingly validated (R3):**
    *   For the RNA-seq experiment, the evaluation is unclear: what is the formal objective, and how is success measured beyond showing that some marker genes changed? The claim that “genes with high correlation did not change” is not properly interpreted (R3).
    *   For the molecule dataset, it is not clear what properties of the original molecule are retained; the generated molecules may be a random mixture of the desired property and some atoms from the original (R3).
    *   A key ablation is missing: comparing transmutation using a regular diffusion model that noises the data partially and then regenerates with the changed label (R3).
*   **Interpretability claims are unclear in utility (R3, R4):** The meaning and usefulness of the average branching points are not well explained. For example, the observation that letters with larger feature values tend to transmute to letters with larger feature values is hard to interpret from Figure 3b (R4). The visualization of branch points in Figure 4 is intuitive but questions remain about more upstream branch points (R4).
*   **Unsubstantiated or overly broad claims (R2, R4):**
    *   The statement that “images and image-like data are the only modalities that suffer from this issue” (regarding class-defining features being at multiple locations) is likely missing a “not” and is otherwise unsupported (R2).
    *   The claim that “this limitation on images may be avoided by diffusing in latent space” is given without evidence (R2).
    *   In the continual learning setting, it is unclear whether not fine-tuning upstream branches actually helps or harms performance; no empirical results support the claim (R4).
*   **Metric reliance on FID only (R2):** FID can provide an incomplete or misleading picture; other approaches (e.g., precision and recall) would be valuable. Also, in Figure S3c, the FID for branched and linear models on RNA-seq is exactly the same, which is not explained (R4).
