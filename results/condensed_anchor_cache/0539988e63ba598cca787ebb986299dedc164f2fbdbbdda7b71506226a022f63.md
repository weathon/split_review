## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper systematically examines how the corruption method (Gaussian Noise GN vs Symmetric Token Replacement STR) and evaluation metric (probability vs logit difference) affect activation-patching results in mechanistic interpretability of language models. It also studies sliding window patching. The paper finds that GN produces out-of-distribution inputs that break internal mechanisms, while STR preserves in-distribution inputs; logit difference is recommended because probability can overlook negative model components; and sliding window patching can inflate logit plots. Recommendations for best practices are provided.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important need for standardization in activation patching, where many variants exist with little prior consensus (R1).
- Motivation is strong: understanding how hyperparameters (corruption, evaluation, sliding window) change interpretability results is crucial (R2).
- Convincingly demonstrates that these design choices can lead to different interpretations, using the Name Mover analysis to illustrate issues with GN (R2).
- Thorough experimentation across multiple settings: sliding window, localizing factual recall, and circuit discovery (R3).
- Abstract and introduction are well written, making problem statement and contributions easy to follow (R3).
- Section 6 provides actionable recommendations for activation patching (R2).

### Weaknesses
- Novelty is limited; the paper is largely a study of existing methods without proposing new corruption or improvement techniques. One reviewer (score 8) found the work convincing and relevant, while others (scores 6) felt it could be better suited to a workshop (R3, with contrast from R2).
- Corruption method coverage may be insufficient: only GN and STR are considered. Missing alternatives such as adversarial perturbations or targeted token substitutions that might reveal different causal relationships (R1). Additionally, it is unclear how many papers actually use GN beyond Meng et al., questioning how widespread the identified issue is (R2).
- STR is recommended but is more difficult to implement (requires semantically similar substitutions). The paper does not suggest a flexible approach that avoids GN biases while being easier to apply (R2).
- The analysis of evaluation metrics (probability vs logit difference) does not delve into their limitations, such as sensitivity to the specific prompt or potential masking effects due to averaging over multiple positions (R1).
- Sliding window investigation is limited in scope; different window sizes or aggregation strategies are not explored (R1).
- Clarity issues: Table 1, especially the “negative detection of 0.10 under GN” discussion, is hard to parse and lacks sufficient explanation. Terminology such as “Name Mover” assumes prior knowledge of Wang et al. (R2, R3). How the content of Table 1 was computed is not clearly described (R3).
- A reviewer asks: how much do different substitutions for STR or different noise samples for GN change the interpretation? Are the methods at least consistent within themselves? This highlights a missing variability analysis (R2).
- Minor: typo “use its own the method” (presumably “use its own method”) (R3).
