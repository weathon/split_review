## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Multi-domain Prototype-based Federated Fine-Tuning (MPFT), a one-round federated learning (FL) framework for Federated Domain Adaptation (FDA). Instead of aggregating models, each client generates prototypes (domain-specific embeddings) via sampling strategies (mean, cluster, random) and sends them to the server, which uses them to train a global adapter. Experiments on two datasets show performance improvements (~2% accuracy) over conventional methods with high communication/computation efficiency. Differential privacy (DP) is applied to protect prototypes.

### Strengths
1. **Significant performance improvement**: Achieves ~2% accuracy increase on two datasets compared to conventional methods (R1, R3).
2. **Communication and computational efficiency**: Requires only a single communication round and transmits only prototypes, greatly reducing costs (R1, R2).
3. **Intuitive approach**: Uses three simple prototype sampling strategies (mean, cluster, random) that are easy to understand (R1).
4. **Comprehensive experimentation**: Extensive experiments covering multiple scenarios, various sampling strategies, and different DP configurations (R2).
5. **New evaluation metric**: Introduces a metric that assesses both out-of-domain and in-domain accuracy to balance knowledge retention and adaptation (R3).
6. **Clear presentation**: Writing and logic are easy to follow; problem formulation is clear (R3).
7. **Interesting novel idea**: One-round multi-domain fine-tuning using prototypes is considered an interesting contribution. *Note: One reviewer (R2) strongly disagrees on novelty – see Weakness 4.*

### Weaknesses
1. **Impractical assumption**: The method assumes clients already have a well-pretrained model, which may be unrealistic in privacy-sensitive domains (e.g., healthcare, finance) where FL is most needed. Authors should discuss specific applications where this assumption holds or propose adaptations when pretrained models are unavailable (R1 question).
2. **Vague experimental setup**: It is unclear whether baselines (FedAvg, FedProx) are trained from scratch or from the same pretrained backbone as MPFT. This should be specified (R1 question).
3. **Insufficient privacy guarantees**: Privacy protection via Gaussian noise on prototypes is informal. A rigorous analysis is required:
   - Formal definition of the threat model (R2).
   - Quantification of noise levels using established DP metrics/frameworks (R1, R2).
   - Theoretical conditions on noise variance to satisfy DP (R2).
   - Explanation of the claim that specific noise configurations reduce bias (R2).
   - DP-related analysis is missing from the convergence proof (R3).
4. **Overly simplistic method (minority view – R2)**: One reviewer (R2) finds the method overly simplistic, relying only on basic prototype sampling (mean, cluster, random) and deviating from traditional FL principles (no local training on client data). The gains are attributed to the strong pretrained feature extractor rather than the method itself, and the approach is considered lacking novelty. This contrasts with other reviewers’ positive view. R2 suggests removing the powerful pretrained backbone and using a simpler model (e.g., ResNet, ConvNet) to evaluate the method’s own contribution.
5. **Missing theoretical analysis of sampling strategies**: No explanation of the core differences among mean, cluster, and random sampling in terms of learning mechanisms, communication efficiency, personalization, or trade-offs between computational complexity and accuracy. Their performance under varying degrees of non-IID data, class imbalance, and sample sparsity is not discussed (R2).
6. **Convergence analysis not meaningful**: The theoretical convergence proof is trivial because training on aggregated prototypes is equivalent to centralized training. The paper should instead provide a validity proof that the adapter on aggregated prototypes achieves comparable performance to training on aggregated raw data, or derive an upper bound on the performance gap. The proof also lacks DP-related analysis for MPFT with DP (R2, R3).
7. **Outdated baseline comparisons**: Experimental comparisons are mostly from 2017–2022. Recent aggregation-based methods addressing data heterogeneity (e.g., FedCP KDD 2023, FedFed NeurIPS 2023, FedGH MM 2023) are not included, even though the paper criticizes averaging-based aggregation. Stronger support for the drawbacks of such methods and comparisons with recent advances are needed (R3 question).
8. **Missing details**:
   - The choice of parameter *s* in Section 5.6 is not explained (R3 question).
   - In the Appendix B proof, class *k* is missing from the formulation despite being defined earlier (R3 question).
9. **Lack of deep experimental insight**: Although experiments cover many configurations, they fail to reveal functional differences among prototype sampling methods under different data distributions. No summary is provided on how performance varies with the number of prototypes per class or imbalanced class distributions (R2).
