## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces PHYSICO, a benchmark that assesses LLM understanding of physical concepts at different levels: low-level tasks (definition recall) and high-level tasks (interpreting abstract grid representations). LLMs match or exceed humans on low-level tasks but fall behind by ~40% on high-level tasks, which the authors attribute to the stochastic parrot phenomenon. In-context learning and fine-tuning on grid-formatted data yield little improvement, suggesting the difficulty is not solely due to grid unfamiliarity.

### Strengths
- Novelty of using grid/matrix representations (inspired by ARC) for physical concept understanding is novel (Reviewer 3). The benchmark includes both low-level and high-level tasks grounded in Bloom’s taxonomy, forming a two-tier design (Reviewer 1, 3).
- The task design is well-structured and clearly presented, with research questions ordered for readability (Reviewer 2, 3).
- Comprehensive evaluation covering multiple frontier LLMs (GPT-4, GPT-4o, Llama 3, etc.) and a human baseline, including human studies to verify basic grid comprehension (Reviewer 2, 3, 4).
- Targeted domain is intriguing and relevant to the stochastic parrot debate; results showing large human–LLM gaps offer insights for future LLM improvement (Reviewer 3).
- Experiments demonstrating that grid unfamiliarity is not the sole cause of poor performance—ICL and fine-tuning produce little gain—are a positive design feature (Reviewer 3). However, one reviewer questions the strength of this evidence (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses
- **Claim of no prior quantitative verification of stochastic parrot is inaccurate.** Multiple reviewers note that the claim in Section 1 (“there are no quantitative experiments to verify the stochastic parrot phenomenon in LLMs”) is false. There exists substantial quantitative work, e.g., “The Generative AI Paradox” (West et al., 2024) showing models generating data they cannot understand, and Dziri et al. (2023) on compositionality limits, plus a broader literature on LLM capabilities and probing (Reviewer 1, 4).
- **Theoretical framing and motivation are problematic.** The paper assumes LLMs could achieve human-like understanding without adequate justification; quantitative methods may be unsuitable for testing something current architectures cannot achieve (trained for regression) (Reviewer 4). The connection from “stochastic parrot” to physical concept understanding to abstract grid reasoning has two gaps (Reviewer 2), and the results are seen as expected given known LLM limitations (Reviewer 2, 4).
- **Insufficient engagement with existing benchmarks.** Why a new physical reasoning benchmark is needed over existing ones like PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020) for physical commonsense or ARC/ConceptARC/L-ARC/BabyARC for core knowledge is not adequately discussed (Reviewer 1, 2). One reviewer notes that ARC already showcases LLMs’ inability in core knowledge reasoning (Reviewer 2).
- **Grid format confound and evidence for familiarization is weak.** Doubts remain: pre-training biases against grids may not be overcome by limited ICL/fine-tuning (Reviewer 1). The human study for grid familiarization only used GPT-4o, not open-source models like LLaMA, making it unrepresentative (Reviewer 3). No automatic reproducible familiarization study is provided; human study alone is insufficient (Reviewer 3). Grid size could be a confounder (Reviewer 1: “would grid size be a confounder for performance? … Does performance decrease sharply when increasing the grid size?”). Ambiguity in grid representations is also noted (e.g., Figure 1: why is the light red bar not falling?) (Reviewer 2).
- **Dataset imbalance and insufficient instances per concept.** For example, reference frame has 12 instances but nuclear fusion only 2, which may harm statistical significance. Each physical concept should ideally have at least 10–30 instances (Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of per-concept results.** The paper does not break down LLM performance by physical concept; results comparing rare vs. common concepts versus humans would test generalization (Reviewer 3).
- **Human study details are unclear.** The number of participants is not stated (Reviewer 2). How the task was explained to humans and whether the prompt matched the LLM prompt is unclear (Reviewer 4).
- **Writing quality issues.** While one reviewer praised clarity (Reviewer 3), another finds the writing lacks flow and polish, making it hard to follow the overall argument (Reviewer 4). The presentation score from Reviewer 1 is low (1).
