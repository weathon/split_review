## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes AddSR, a blind super-resolution method that applies Adversarial Diffusion Distillation (ADD) to accelerate Stable Diffusion (SD)-based BSR. It introduces a Timestep-Adaptive ADD loss (TA-ADD) to address the perception-distortion imbalance caused by naive ADD, and a prediction-based self-refinement (PSR) strategy that uses the estimated HR image from previous steps as a conditioning input to improve high-frequency detail without additional modules. The method achieves 7× speedup over SeeSR (50→4 steps) and demonstrates good perceptual quality on non-reference metrics, though with notable fidelity drops.

### Strengths

1. **Strong perceptual quality and efficiency**: AddSR produces images with better perceptual quality than most diffusion-based SR models while requiring fewer inference steps and less time (e.g., 7× faster than SeeSR). Qualitative results demonstrate high-quality details (R1, R2, R5).

2. **Innovative acceleration approach**: The use of adversarial diffusion distillation to accelerate SD-based BSR is meaningful and engaging; the prediction-based self-refinement and TA-ADD are innovative aspects that improve inference speed while maintaining quality (R3, R5).

3. **No extra modules for conditioning**: PSR does not require additional modules to pre-clean LR images; it uses the HR image estimated from Equation (2) (R1). The teacher model substitutes x_LR with x_0 to regulate the output, which implicitly forces the student to learn high-frequency information of HR images (R1).

4. **Straightforward and powerful design**: The proposed methodologies appear straightforward yet deliver superior performance in both qualitative and quantitative analyses (R4).

5. **Meticulous experiments and clear writing**: Experiments are designed to evaluate performance across various degradation scenarios and datasets, showing robustness. The writing is clear and well-structured, making complex details accessible (R5).

6. **Leading perceptual-oriented metrics**: The method achieves top results in perceptual-oriented metrics (R2, R3).

### Weaknesses

1. **Severe perception-fidelity trade-off and low PSNR/SSIM**: The framework fails to push the perception-distortion trade-off boundary. It achieves improved perceptual quality by heavily sacrificing fidelity, as indicated by low PSNR/SSIM scores (R2). There is a noticeable performance gap between teacher and student: LPIPS drops from 0.2124 to 0.2953, a significant decrease. LPIPS is a more crucial metric than non-reference metrics where the method shows improvement (R3). In Table 1, the proposed method significantly underperforms in PSNR and SSIM compared to existing SD-based methods; in Table 2, it does not deliver comparable perceptual quality scores to SUPIR (R2). One reviewer notes that SD-based SR can trade off fidelity and quality with inference tricks (e.g., manually added text prompts), but this paper does not specify the inference setup of each baseline and does not achieve promising results with an acceptable fidelity drop (R2).

2. **Lack of comparison with efficient and one-step SD-based methods**: Missing comparisons with other efficient SD-based methods (e.g., OSEDiff) makes it hard to verify effectiveness under similar efficiency (R2). The latest diffusion-based SR methods have accelerated inference into a single step (SinSR, One-Step Effective, You Only Need One Step, Degradation-Guided One-Step, TAD-SR), yet AddSR obtains optimal results only with 4 steps, raising questions about advantage and practicality (R5, R4). One reviewer requests a diverse comparison with these one-step methods in Table 1 and specifically asks about the advantage of self-refinement when AddSR-1 is used (R4).

3. **Missing visual comparisons and details**: No visual comparison with SUPIR (R1). Visual comparisons are not thorough (R1). The texts in Figure 6 and Table 1 are too small (R1). Figure 2 caption lacks essential information, PSR details are not provided, and the input for Figure 3 is unspecified (R3). The type of GPU and inference time during testing are not reported (R1). Inference times for each SotA in Table 1 are not provided (R1).

4. **Notation errors and confusing terminology**: LPIPS is labeled as non-reference in L271-272, but it is actually reference-based (R3). The term "predicted HR" is used (e.g., Figure 3 caption), but typically "HR image" refers to ground truth; since the method also uses ground truth HR as a condition for the teacher model, mixing the term "HR" can cause misunderstanding (R2).

5. **Missing LPIPS in Table 3 and fidelity comparisons**: LPIPS is absent from Table 3, and the fidelity performance appears much lower than existing SOTA models such as ResShift, SeeSR, and current single-step SR models. Comparisons with these are missing (R3).

6. **Error accumulation in PSR**: When the estimated HR images are rough or distorted in larger steps, PSR may transfer distortion errors to the next step. LR is necessary to ground the output, but how is error reduced? (R5). Since SD-based SR methods often hallucinate (Figure 5(b)), did the authors consider error accumulation and apply any additional processing (e.g., blurring) to the intermediate output to prevent it? (R2). How do high-frequency components necessarily provide better guidance when hallucinations also contain high frequencies? (R2).

7. **Lack of theoretical analysis for ADD fidelity drop**: The paper only observes that directly applying ADD reduces fidelity but does not provide a theoretical analysis for why this occurs (R5).

8. **Empirical nature of TA-ADD weighting**: The time-step aware weighting function d(s,t) appears too empirical. TAD-SR’s weighting is simpler and more effective, similar to diffusion time-step embedding (R5). One reviewer questions the novelty: since other works (Distribution Matching Distillation, Latent Adversarial Diffusion Distillation) also use adaptive weighting functions, what advantage does Equation 3 offer? A comparison with their weighting functions is requested (R4).

9. **Novelty concerns**: PSR’s concept of using previous output as condition resembles RePaint; what distinguishes the proposed approach? (R4). The TA-ADD loss design is questioned: using the student output as input to the teacher model to enforce similarity between teacher and student outputs implies the teacher should learn identity, but since the teacher has no learnable layers, this design cannot achieve such an effect (R2).

10. **Missing ablations and hyperparameter studies**: The training scheme appears overfitted to the 4-step setting, yet ablation on the number of steps (e.g., using the trained student for 5 or more steps) is missing (R2). The ratio of GAN loss to distillation loss may vary with different scenarios; how is it adjusted and does it need to change per image? (R1). The effects of using only GAN loss and only distillation loss are not described (R1). The ablation study on "Effectiveness of Refined Training Process" is questioned because during inference there is no HR input, so what is its significance? (R1). The experiment on how PSR controls intermediate and final output is missing (R2).

11. **Questions about prompts**: Does AddSR use manually added prompts (e.g., "clean," "high-resolution," "8k") like SeeSR? Since these prompts greatly influence the perception-fidelity trade-off, their use may affect performance when intermediate results are crucial conditions. Comparison with and without prompts is requested (R2).

12. **Speedup calculation**: Reducing steps from 50 to 4 should ideally yield 12.5× speedup, but only 7× is achieved. Why? (R2).

13. **One-step self-refinement advantage**: For AddSR-1, what advantage does self-refinement bring? (R4).

14. **Figure 4 discrepancy**: In Figure 4, the output from step 1 (using step 2’s output as condition) appears more similar to step 4 than to step 2. What explains this discrepancy? (R4).
