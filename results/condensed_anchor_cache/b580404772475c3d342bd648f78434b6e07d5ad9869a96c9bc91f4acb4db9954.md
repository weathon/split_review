## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes FTP (FFN Token Pruning), a method to accelerate the prefilling stage of long-context LLM inference. It observes that the FFN module accounts for over 60% of inference time. FTP prunes non-critical tokens before the FFN forward pass, using attention scores in each layer to determine token importance and the pruning ratio. The pruned tokens’ information is partially preserved through the residual connection. On LongBench, Qwen2-7B-Instruct with FTP achieves 1.24× speedup with 1.30% performance drop; Qwen1.5-32B-Chat reaches 1.39× speedup. Two reviewers rate the paper 5, two rate it 6 (Confidence 3–5). The experimental section, baseline selection, fairness of comparisons, and literature review are the primary weaknesses.

### Strengths
1. **Innovative and clearly motivated idea** – Accelerating the prefilling stage by pruning tokens specifically in the FFN layer is novel. The motivation is well-supported by wall-time breakdowns showing FFN as the dominant component during prefilling on short decode lengths.
2. **Simple, straightforward, and easy to apply** – The method is technically sound and requires minimal changes to the model.
3. **Strong performance results on LongBench** – Speedups of 1.24–1.39× with generally small accuracy degradation; the ablation on larger models (32B) adds practical value.
4. **Detailed experimental analysis** – The experimental setup is well-explained, and the analysis of results is thorough.
5. **Elegant design** – The approach is considered elegant and offers a reasonable accuracy-speedup trade-off by most reviewers.

### Weaknesses
1. **Unfair and limited baseline comparisons** – Multiple reviewers note that the comparison with PyramidInfer is unfair because FTP uses FlashAttention while PyramidInfer uses a PyTorch attention implementation, inflating FTP’s TTFT advantage. An apples-to-apples comparison (e.g., both with PyTorch attention, or separate tables) is needed. Baselines are too few: only PyramidInfer and LLMLingua2 are compared on LongBench. Missing comparisons with KV-cache eviction methods (H2O, NACL), dynamic layer-skipping methods (MoD), and other token pruning methods. More benchmarks (e.g., BigBench, ZeroScrolls, L-Eval) should be included.
2. **Attention-score pruning lacks justification for FFN** – The method applies attention-score-based token pruning to the FFN module without evidence that attention scores are suitable for predicting token importance in FFN computations. A specific design tailored to FFN is missing. (Reviewer 1, Question 1 overlaps as a criticism.)
3. **Major accuracy drop on code completion with Llama3** – Table 2 shows a ~20% performance drop on code completion tasks for Llama3, far exceeding baseline degradation. This undermines the claim of “negligible performance decrease” and limits broader applicability. (Reviewers 3 and 4 both highlight this.)
4. **Loss of intermediate information** – Figure 5 shows FTP retains only the first few and last few tokens, discarding intermediate content for some layers. Previous work (e.g., NACL) shows this leads to sampling bias and accuracy loss, which the paper does not analyze. (Reviewer 1, Question 3 overlaps.)
5. **Missing computational cost analysis** – The overhead of computing the Sum Attention Score for token selection is not discussed. The paper does not address whether this cost offsets the FFN speedup.
6. **No discussion of memory impact** – The method’s effect on memory usage (reduction or unchanged) is not reported, even though many prior pruning methods motivate memory reduction.
7. **Weak literature review and imprecise positioning** – The paper does not discuss related token pruning works like MoD or earlier works outside LLMs. The distinction from prior methods (e.g., “direct pruning from the whole layer” vs. FTP’s dynamic selection) is not sharply defined – it should clarify that earlier methods prune across both attention and FFN, or apply permanent pruning.
8. **Unclear derivation of pruning ratio and some results** – How the best reserved ratio per layer is determined from attention variability is not explained. Additionally, conclusions 2 and 3 from Figure 5 (lines 232–236) are not clearly justified. Figure 7 is uninterpretable (no labeling of η or compression rates for other models). (Reviewer 2, Questions; Reviewer 4, Questions.)
9. **Lukewarm overall trade-off** – The speedup (1.2–1.4×) may not be sufficient to justify the observed accuracy loss, especially when large drops occur on code tasks. The random baseline should be framed as an ablation, not a competitor. Tables 1, 2, and 4 contain repeated information and could be consolidated.
10. **Typos** – Line 267: “and the dataset” should be “dataset”.
