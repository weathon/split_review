## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper challenges the hypothesis that the success of iterative pruning methods is due to improved mask identification or implicit regularization. Instead, it argues that increased training epochs with cyclic learning rate schedules drive most of the improvement for pruning at initialization (PaI). The paper shows that cyclic training boosts PaI methods (random, SNIP, Synflow) across datasets, but at high sparsity, cyclic training alone is insufficient—a strong coupling between learned parameter initialization and mask is required. The authors propose SCULPT-ing, which combines cyclic training of an initially sparse mask (pruned via PaI to 20-70% sparsity) with a single final pruning step to high sparsity, aiming to match iterative pruning methods like learning rate rewinding (LRR) at reduced computational cost.

### Strengths

- Connects the choice of learning rate schedule (cyclic) to sparse training performance, showing that PaI methods are significantly boosted by longer cyclic training, regardless of the mask source (Fig. 4). This effect is most clear at lower sparsities.
- Proposes SCULPT-ing, a simple sparse training framework that combines cyclic training with one-shot pruning. In several cases (e.g., ResNet-50 on ImageNet, some CIFAR cases), SCULPT-ing matches LRR performance, and it does so with reduced computational cost relative to iterative pruning (LRR).
- Provides empirical analysis of training dynamics using linear mode connectivity (Fig. 3) and approximate loss landscape sharpness (largest eigenvalue of Hessian) to understand how cyclic training improves PaI.
- Challenges existing explanations for why dense-to-sparse methods (e.g., AC/DC) outperform—suggesting improved mask-parameter coupling rather than better mask topology.
- The topic (improving PaI) is well-motivated and the paper covers relevant literature. Writing is clear and concise overall.
- (Minority: Reviewer 2 notes that SCULPT is a "straightforward framework" that matches LRR at high sparsity in some settings, and that the paper's focus on learning schedules is "intriguing." Reviewer 1 notes Fig. 4 provides "clearest results," and Reviewer 3 praises the thorough empirical exploration via multiple analysis techniques.)

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty concerns:** Several reviewers note that the benefits of cyclic learning rate schedules for sparse training have been previously established (e.g., Yin et al. 2022, Jin et al. 2022, Kurtic & Alistarh 2022). Similarly, extending training durations to improve sparse network accuracy is known (Sreenivasan et al. 2022, Evci et al. 2021, Yuan et al. 2021). The paper’s primary contribution—rigorous evaluation of extended cyclic training for PaI—is considered of modest significance, especially given SCULPT's performance limitations.
- **Coupling insights not novel:** Section 5’s conclusion that mask-parameter coupling is crucial at high sparsity has been extensively discussed in prior work (Frankle & Carbin 2019, Zhou et al. 2019, Chen et al. 2020, Paul et al. 2023). The paper does not substantially advance this understanding; the only addition is the use of cyclic training. A direct comparison of cyclic vs. non-cyclic training with the LRR mask is missing (Fig. 5), and Fig. 6’s loss landscape comparisons only cover cyclic training. The claim that “cyclic training is required” is not properly tested.
- **Limited model and dataset diversity:** Experiments are restricted to ResNet models (ResNet-20, ResNet-50) on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet-1k. No results on Transformer architectures (e.g., ViT) or other CNN families (MobileNet, EfficientNet) are provided. Using ImageNet ResNet for CIFAR-100 is known to be overparameterized, making high sparsity easier. Reviewers request experiments on at least one more architecture to demonstrate generalizability.
- **SCULPT performance is weak relative to baselines:** SCULPT underperforms LRR in many cases (e.g., ResNet-20 on CIFAR-10 across all sparsities). On ImageNet, SCULPT at 20% initial sparsity achieves ~75.5% accuracy with 450 epochs, while dynamic sparse training (DST) methods like RigL (5×) achieve 76.4% at 90% sparsity from the start—a large FLOP benefit. Even dense-to-sparse methods like AC/DC likely outperform SCULPT in an iso-FLOP comparison. SCULPT’s 20% initial sparsity yields negligible memory/compute savings in practice (only ~14% parameter memory reduction assuming 16-bit weights plus bitmask). A comparison with gradual magnitude pruning (GMP*) using accelerated cubic learning rate is requested.
- **Insufficient baselines and framing:** The paper does not compare SCULPT against strong contemporary baselines such as RigL, AC/DC, GMP*, or Rare Gems (Sreenivasan et al. 2022). The abstract and title suggest PaI is competitive, but the actual results show PaI still falls off at high sparsity before LRR does. The green band in figures (2,4,5,7,8,9) is described in text but never labeled in the figures.
- **Presentation issues:** SCULPT is not presented as a formal algorithm (pseudocode). Figure 1 shows two pruning cuts, but the text sometimes suggests pruning only at initialization. The paper lacks theoretical analysis. The “Insights into mechanism” section is hard to follow; hypotheses are not clearly separated and answered. The delta improvement plot (cyclic vs. default) is missing to support claims like “the boost is larger at higher sparsity” (lines 294-295).
- **Unclear claims and missing controls:** Line 213 states “Increased training improves generalization,” but experiments only compare cyclic training, not simply longer training. The statement that “it can only match … ImageNet at 20% sparsity beyond which the effect of pruning becomes dominant” (lines 298-299) is ambiguous—both LRR and PaI prune the same amount; why would the “effect of pruning” differ? For Fig. 3, Reviewer 1 asks whether colors are swapped (orange line appears to have error barriers, blue does not). For Fig. 8, ImageNet results are not extended to higher sparsities, leaving it unclear whether SCULPT continues to match LRR.
- **Missing background references:** Rare Gems (Sreenivasan et al. 2022, NeurIPS 2022) and related works from its Table 1 are not discussed.

**Contrast:** While Reviewer 2 (score 5) finds SCULPT promising and the learning schedule connection interesting, Reviewers 1, 3, and 4 (scores 3 each) view the contributions as modest, the novelty as low, and the empirical evaluation as insufficient. The majority agree that the paper requires major revisions to improve clarity, broaden experiments, and demonstrate competitive performance against strong baselines under fair FLOP comparisons.
