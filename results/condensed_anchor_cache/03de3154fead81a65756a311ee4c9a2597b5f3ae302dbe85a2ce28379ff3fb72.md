## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper shows that shallow transformers can use a combination of linear and MLP associative memories to achieve near-optimal storage capacity, with proof that storage scales linearly with parameter count (up to log factors). It introduces a synthetic factual recall task involving subject-relation correlations and proves that a single-layer transformer with linear attention can obtain 100% accuracy when either self-attention or MLP parameters scale linearly with number of facts. Gradient flow analysis on a simplified linear attention model reveals a “hallucination” phase where the model ignores the subject token and predicts based only on the relation. One reviewer (R3, score 6) was notably more critical than the others (R1 and R2, both score 8), expressing stronger reservations about the narrowness of the synthetic task, the lack of experimental detail, and limited discussion of prior work.

### Strengths

- **Solid theoretical contributions.** The proofs that linear and MLP associative memories achieve storage capacity scaling linearly with parameter count are rigorous (R1, R2, R3). The demonstration that a single-layer transformer can trade off between value matrices and MLP as associative memory is a distinct result (R1, R2).
- **Formal definition of hallucination and insightful gradient dynamics.** The paper defines hallucination at line 455–460 as the stage where the model ignores the subject token and predicts based only on the relation, outputting a plausible but incorrect answer (R1). The gradient flow analysis identifying an intermediate hallucination phase during training adds depth to understanding training dynamics (R1, R3).
- **High potential impact (contingent).** If the results extend to deeper transformers with softmax attention and real datasets, the framework could unite the LLM community with the theoretical associative memory community (R1). The mathematical rigor is a strength (R3).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited applicability to real-world transformers and datasets.** The analysis uses only a single attention layer, linear attention (softmax dropped), and a synthetic dataset where noise tokens and subject tokens are disjoint (R1, R2, R3). The disjointness assumption is a strong simplification—in practice, the last token often carries crucial contextual information (R2). It is unclear how the theory generalizes to multi-layer architectures, which are a key component of modern transformers (R1, R2, R3). The theoretical results need to be extended to deeper models and real-world data distributions to be more relevant (R1, R2).
- **No comparison to empirical scaling laws.** The paper does not compare its derived scaling laws to established empirical results such as the Chinchilla scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022). It should discuss whether the theoretical framework can reproduce the observed relationships between model size, dataset size, and performance (R1).
- **Insufficient experimental detail.** The description of Figure 1 is incomplete; error bars, failure cases (e.g., storing one-hot vectors, line 130), and different choices of σ in the MLP are missing (R1). Key training details such as weight initialization distributions, learning rate schedules, and batch sizes are not clearly stated (R3). This hampers reproducibility.
- **Associative memories are single-step updates, not energy-based.** The linear associative memory is not formulated as an energy-minimization problem; memory capacity would not scale linearly in a recurrent or energy-based setting. The paper should explore the recurrent case (R1).
- **Missing or insufficiently explored components.** Section 4.2 omits LayerNorm; it is unclear if theoretical results hold with LayerNorm included (R3). The choice of nonlinearity σ in Eq. (2) is critical for memory capacity in Dense Associative Memories (arXiv:1606.01164), but its role is not justified or investigated (R1).
- **Clarity issues in theoretical statements.** The claim on line 117 that “the number of associations scales linearly with the number of parameters (up to log factors)” is unclear—without an injective assumption, the number of tokens, not associations, scales linearly (R3). The explanation for why the model learns the conditional distribution of relations rather than the conditional distribution of subjects (Theorem 6) is not intuitive and should be expanded (R2). The plot in Figure 5 (left) appears too smooth, and the loss does not change for the first 2000 gradient steps—this counterintuitive behavior needs explanation (R2).
- **Lack of discussion of related work.** The paper does not sufficiently contextualize its findings within prior literature on neural networks and attention mechanisms as associative memories [1,2], limiting the assessment of novelty (R3).
- **Generalization not studied.** The framework focuses on memorization; extending it to study generalization abilities of transformers would be valuable (R1, Q1). The theory is only developed for causal language modeling; extension to masked-token (BERT-style) tasks is not considered (R1, Q2).
