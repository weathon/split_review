## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proves two mechanisms (feature masking in the lazy-training limit and parameter-dimension reduction around symmetric solutions) by which symmetries in the loss function trap models in low-capacity states (“collapse”). It proposes **syre**, a model‑ and symmetry‑agnostic algorithm that adds a fixed random bias to the parameters and replaces standard weight decay with a norm defined by a positive diagonal matrix **D**. Experiments on supervised learning, variational autoencoders, self‑supervised learning, and continual learning (including an RL setting) show improvements, especially when models are prone to collapse (e.g., posterior collapse in VAEs, loss of plasticity in continual learning).

### Strengths
- **Theoretically grounded.** The paper proves concrete mechanisms (Proposition 3 shows symmetry reduces parameter dimension; two distinct mechanisms are proven). It nontrivially builds on (Ziyin 2024).
- **Simple and broadly applicable.** syre requires only minimal code changes, no architectural modifications, and no knowledge of the underlying symmetries. It is claimed to be applicable to arbitrary architectures.
- **Promising empirical results.** In several settings, syre outperforms baselines and another symmetry‑removal method, particularly in continual‑learning scenarios (improved plasticity and maintained performance) and in self‑supervised learning.
- **Innovative approach.** Using static random biases and a modified weight‑decay term to break symmetries is novel and provides a new way to connect symmetries with model expressivity.
- **Clear exposition.** The paper is well‑structured and the core concepts are explained clearly. The theoretical analysis is rigorous (reviewers 1, 4).
- **Addresses a practical problem.** Symmetry‑induced collapse affects multiple important domains (VAEs, continual learning, SSL), making the work impactful.

### Weaknesses
- **Conceptual confusion about symmetries (strong criticism from one reviewer).**  
  The analysis focuses on fixed points where \(P\theta_0 = 0\) and discusses “symmetric points” rather than the full symmetry orbits (group actions). For example, in a two‑layer linear network \(F(x)=UVx\), the relevant symmetry is the \(GL(h)\) orbit \((U,V)\to(Ug,g^{-1}V)\), not just fixed points. By concentrating on fixed points, the paper neglects the continuous nature of symmetries and the degeneracies they create in the loss landscape. Other reviewers did not raise this concern, creating a notable disagreement.

- **Overly restrictive theoretical framework.**  
  The condition \(P\theta' = \theta'\) for reflection symmetry is too restrictive; a valid reflection in 2D (e.g., \(\theta'=(0,1)\), \(P = ((1,0),(0,0))\)) violates it. The definition therefore does not capture all possible reflection symmetries.

- **Questionable argument about gradient vanishing.**  
  The claim that symmetric points trap models because gradients vanish is not universally valid. In the \(UV\) example, fixed points along the \(U=V\) line have nonzero gradients except at the origin and minima. Gradients naturally vanish *along* symmetry orbits (level sets of the loss), but this does not imply trapping. The paper does not differentiate between gradients vanishing along orbits and at specific points.

- **Limited analysis of the regularization effect.**  
  The D‑norm modification is essentially ellipsoidal weight decay, and the \(\theta_0\) shift changes the center of the regularization. These may simply select a different point within the same symmetry orbit (as standard weight decay does) rather than fundamentally breaking the symmetry structure. The paper does not provide sufficient justification that the proposed modifications actually remove symmetries, as opposed to acting as a different form of regularization.

- **Experimental shortcomings.**  
  *Lack of proper baselines:* There is no systematic comparison with standard weight decay. Improved performance in continual learning could be due to a regularization effect that prevents catastrophic forgetting, not to symmetry removal. The effect of symmetry removal is not isolated.  
  *Narrow scope:* Experiments are limited to small‑ or medium‑scale models and toy tasks. High‑impact benchmarks (e.g., ImageNet) and large‑scale architectures (e.g., transformers) are missing, so the method’s real‑world applicability is unsubstantiated.  
  *Weak validation of the theoretical mechanisms:* Only one case (Fig. 1) tests Propositions 2/3. A systematic analysis across architectures and datasets is needed to show that more/less symmetries correlate with more/less collapse, and that syre mitigates this in a way consistent with the claimed mechanisms.  
  *Missing ablations:* No ablation studies comparing syre with standard weight decay, with/without the random bias, or with/without the D‑norm modification. The reader cannot attribute improvements to symmetry‑breaking.

- **Missing discussion on generalization.**  
  Symmetry sometimes acts as a regularizer. The potential downside of completely removing symmetries (e.g., harming generalization) is not discussed.

- **Additional requests and minor issues.**  
  *Scalability:* How does syre scale to large models (transformers)? What is the computational overhead?  
  *Hyperparameters:* How sensitive is syre to hyperparameters (e.g., the scale of the random bias, the diagonal matrix **D**)? Heuristics for practitioners would be useful.  
  *Adaptive vs. static bias:* Would an adaptive bias improve the method?  
  *More complex environments:* The method should be tested in less controlled or more challenging settings (e.g., the LMC experiments of Lim 2024).  
  *Formatting:* Headings are too close to previous paragraphs, hurting readability (reviewer 1).  
  *Typo:* Line 65–66: “\(\|\theta\|_D^2:=w^T D w\)” should be “\(|w|_D^2:=w^T D w\)”.
