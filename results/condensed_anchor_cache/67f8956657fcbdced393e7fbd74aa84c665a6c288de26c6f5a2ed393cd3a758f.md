## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Twinned Interventional Flows (TIF), a generative model for continuous-time, partially observed dynamical systems with irregularly-sampled data. TIF augments partial observations with latent variables via a “twinning” mechanism and uses conditional continuous normalizing flows (CNFs) to model system dynamics, enabling tractable log-probability estimation. It claims to handle unobserved confounding, missing data, and counterfactual prediction by combining observational and interventional data. A novel penalty mitigates stiffness in the neural ODE. Experiments are conducted on a Half-Cheetah reinforcement learning benchmark and a tumor growth treatment effect prediction task.

### Strengths
- **Novel twinning concept and versatile framework**: The “twinning” idea (augmenting partial observations with additional latent variables including confounders as a state) is original. The approach naturally extends to counterfactual inference, missingness, and irregular sampling, making it applicable across diverse settings (anomaly detection, RL, treatment effect prediction). The authors present solid theoretical analysis, notably Theorem 3.1 (marginalisation of the infinitesimal change of variables).
- **Flexible generative model for continuous-time irregular data**: TIF provides a tractable log-probability at each time step and does not suffer from limitations of fixed-dimensional embeddings. It supports both observational and interventional data during training.
- **Novel stiffness penalty**: A new penalty is introduced to mitigate stiffness in the underlying neural ODE, which is a technical contribution that addresses a known challenge in training continuous-time flows.
- **Under-explored setting**: Learning to model temporal potential outcomes in continuous space with partially missing observations is an important problem, and the paper proposes a model with provable benefits from auxiliary non-interventional data.

> **Minority contrast**: One reviewer (score 6) found the approach innovative and theoretically sound with high potential impact. Two reviewers (score 3 each) agreed on the originality of the setting but raised major concerns about rigor, clarity, and experimental support.

### Weaknesses
- **Poor presentation and clarity**: The paper is difficult to follow—lacking intuitive illustration of the “why” behind design choices, with scattered motivation across two pages. Key concepts are introduced without definition (e.g., “twinned space”, “privileged data”, “sub-flows”, “stiff ODE”, “stiff regions”). The POMDP is never formally defined, and its use ignores that rewards are required for a decision process (if removed, it reduces to a graphical model). Many essential assumptions (e.g., causal sufficiency, identifiability conditions) are buried in the appendix and should be surfaced. The related work section is unfocused (e.g., causal discovery is discussed without relevance).
- **Lack of rigor in causal claims**: The paper simultaneously targets reinforcement learning and time-varying causal effect estimation without explaining the connections or reconciling different assumption frameworks. Specifically:
  - The interventional benchmark (tumor generator) relies on assumptions from Seedat et al. (2022) that are not addressed within TIF (e.g., sequential randomization in continuous time). Unobserved confounding is claimed to be handled, but without additional assumptions (e.g., additive latent noise) causal effects are non-identifiable; no sensitivity analysis or partial identification is discussed.
  - Counterfactual prediction is conflated with interventional prediction (e.g., “counterfactual trajectories” used for interventional queries). The terms “interventional” and “counterfactual” denote fundamentally different causal quantities; this confusion undermines the claims.
  - The training with “observational” (hidden confounders) and “interventional” (all confounders observed) data is described unclearly, and it is not explained how hidden confounding could facilitate interventional predictions beyond infinite-data asymptotics.
  - The paper confuses causal Markov condition with Markov property of POMDP.
- **Insufficient experimental evidence and unfair comparisons**:
  - Claims that TIF outperforms low-dimensional embedding methods are not substantiated by experiments. No comparison with other density estimators is provided to support “accurate density estimates”. The counterfactual benchmark lacks fair baselines (e.g., [2] is missing). Anomaly detection results are not compared to alternatives.
  - On the RL benchmark (Half-Cheetah, Fig. 6), TIF does not significantly outperform VAE-RNN, and the plot does not match the published baseline performance; variance (standard error) is not shown for TIF. No implementation details, hyperparameter tuning process, or source code is provided, making it impossible to verify fairness or reproducibility.
  - Table 1 mixes “apples and oranges” (causal inference vs. RL methods, time-varying vs. cross-sectional) and contains incorrect entries (e.g., De Brouwer et al. (2022) is marked as not providing log-probability despite having a likelihood model; Seedat et al. (2022) is marked as handling hidden confounding, which TE-CDE does not).
- **Scalability and complexity concerns** (raised by one reviewer): The normalizing flow design requires transformations in the same dimensions (no lower-dimensional encoding), leading to O(n³) time complexity due to matrix inversion, which limits scaling to high-dimensional data (e.g., images, EHR).
- **Missing ablations and unclear design choices**:
  - The penalty for stiff ODEs is not ablated or clearly motivated (stiff ODEs are not defined; their relevance to the setting is not demonstrated).
  - It is unclear how to choose latent dimensionality (n−m) or the number of Monte Carlo samples; the stability of the Monte Carlo estimate used in training is not discussed.
  - The purpose of the concatsquash block is not justified—possible simpler alternatives are not considered.
- **Terminology and notation issues**: Notation like \(\hat{Q}_{t_j, t}\) in Eq. 1 is undefined. “Online prediction” is not defined. The POMDP formulation is said to not require rewards, which defeats its purpose. The term “privileged data” is introduced without definition.
- **Specific questions left unanswered** (overlapping with weaknesses above require clarification/experiments): What is the precise setting? Why does this setting suffer from stiff ODEs? How is the half-cheetah experiment related to the claimed setting? What is the take-away from that experiment? What is meant by “online prediction”? Why is causal discovery discussed in related work? Is the focus on offline data—if so, is offline RL relevant? What is the “twinned space”?
