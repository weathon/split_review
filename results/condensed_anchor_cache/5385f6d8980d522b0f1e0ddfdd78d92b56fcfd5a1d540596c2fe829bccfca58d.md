## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Time-MoE, a decoder-only transformer foundation model for time series forecasting that uses a sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) architecture. It activates only a subset of parameters per prediction, reducing inference cost while allowing scaling to 2.4B parameters. The model is pre-trained on Time-300B, a newly collected dataset of over 300 billion time points from nine domains. It supports flexible forecasting horizons and variable input lengths. Extensive experiments demonstrate that Time-MoE outperforms dense baselines and prior LLM-based approaches in both zero-shot and fine-tuned settings, and scaling laws are validated for time series.

### Strengths
- **Originality**: First application of sparse MoE to time series forecasting at billion-parameter scale, addressing both efficiency and the overfitting seen in dense models (R1, R3).
- **Scalability and Efficiency**: MoE reduces computational cost significantly for larger models, enabling inference on lower-memory hardware; activated parameter count is decoupled from total parameters (R2, R1).
- **Multi-Resolution Forecasting**: The multi-resolution head handles various forecasting horizons efficiently; flexibility is critical for real-world applications (R2).
- **Rigorous Methodology**: Architecture, training, and evaluation are described in detail, supporting reproducibility (R1).
- **Empirical Validation**: Strong improvements over dense models and prior LLM-based methods in zero-shot and fine-tuned settings across multiple benchmarks; scalability analysis comparing Time-MoE with its dense counterpart is a contribution due to sheer parameter size (R2, R3).
- **Practical Impact**: Applicable to finance, energy, and climate forecasting; universal forecasting and flexibility make it valuable for decision-making (R1).
- **Clarity**: Clear writing, well-organized sections, and figures (especially Figure 2) make the architecture accessible (R1, R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited comparison to SOTA and missing benchmarks**: Only a narrow set of baselines/datasets are used. Missing comparisons with contemporary models like Temporal Fusion Transformers (TFT), AutoTimes (Liu et al., NeurIPS 2024), and benchmarks like M4, UCI, Monash dataset, and LOTSA (used in time series foundation models). Evaluating only two related domains (Temperature, Weather) is insufficient for generalizability claims (R1, R3). The rationale for excluding certain datasets from Time-300B collection appears arbitrary (R3).
- **Insufficient ablation on design choices**: No isolation of contributions from gating mechanism, expert network size, multi-resolution head, or expert allocation/configuration strategies (R1, R3). The choice of Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE) lacks a comparative ablation against sinusoidal/learned embeddings for long-term forecasting—specifically error over extended horizons, robustness, and computational efficiency (R2, R3). Also missing: ablation on loss functions (L1 before settling on Huber?) (R3). RoPE is not clearly labeled in Figure 2 (R3).
- **Lack of standard deviations**: No error bars or standard deviations reported for main results, weakening statistical reliability of claims (R3).
- **Missing analysis of MoE routing and expert balance**: The gating mechanism for selecting top-k experts and the criteria for load balancing are not fully detailed (e.g., mathematical formulation, thresholds). Techniques to prevent expert collapse (auxiliary loss, dynamic routing) are not discussed (R1, R2). Underfitting risk when few experts are activated—there is no analysis of performance with e.g., 2, 4, or 8 active experts (R1). Failure cases such as expert specialization collapse and load balancing issues should be referenced (R1).
- **Dataset quality and domain imbalance**: The data-cleaning pipeline for Time-300B is only briefly described; metrics like missing data rate, outlier thresholds, signal-to-noise ratio are not provided. Different domains may need specific preprocessing (e.g., stationary vs. non-stationary). The impact of spurious correlations (e.g., P2S from Kraus et al.) is not addressed. Underrepresented domains in Time-300B may disproportionately affect performance (R2, R3).
- **Computational cost analysis is missing**: Concrete metrics such as training time per epoch, GPU memory usage, and inference time across model sizes are absent. There is no breakdown of MoE layer cost versus rest of network, nor comparison to baselines like TFT and N-BEATS (R1).
- **Interpretability gap**: No visualization of which experts activate for different input characteristics (e.g., high-frequency vs. low-frequency data). No ablation showing how different experts contribute to predictions (R1).
- **Contradiction in inference speed**: Table 5 shows that removing resolution layers increases speed, which is counterintuitive if multi-resolution layers are supposed to be efficient. Clarification needed (R3).
- **Performance variability across model sizes**: Time-MoE_large performs worse than dense models in some 100B-token cases. Authors should explain why (R3).
- **Tokenization concerns**: Only point-wise tokenization is used; neighboring time steps have similar information—consider tokenizing multiple time steps as a unit to improve context efficiency. Hybrid tokenization (single + multiple) could increase vocabulary size (currently 10) (R3 reviewer query).
- **Missing MAE results**: Present MAE values, especially for ablations, to aid robustness evaluation (R3).
- **Minor issues**: The paper proves that flash attention is fast and BF16 is reasonable—this could be moved to the appendix, replacing with performance analysis of different data sampling strategies during training (R3). The forecastability table lacks its proper reference (Wang et al., 2024) (R3).
