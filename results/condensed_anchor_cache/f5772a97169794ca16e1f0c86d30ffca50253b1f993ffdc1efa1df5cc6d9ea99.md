## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes HardPatch, a hard-label (black-box) adversarial attack for large vision-language models (LVLMs). The method splits images into uniform patches, masks each to assess sensitivity via output changes, then iteratively selects the most vulnerable patch, initializes noise, and estimates gradients using Monte Carlo random noise queries. Untargeted and targeted attacks are performed by maximizing similarity to a target text. Evaluations on BLIP-2, MiniGPT-4, LLaVA-1.5, and InstructBLIP across MS-COCO and ImageNet demonstrate effectiveness. The method also extends to universal attacks.

### Strengths
- The method is well-motivated and neat: it targets the practical hard-label setting where attackers lack gradients and model details, with potential applicability to proprietary LVLMs like GPT-4o. It uses few hyperparameters and relies solely on querying the model.
- The localized patch strategy (altering only a few vulnerable patches) is computationally efficient compared to global noise, potentially harder to detect, and reduces perceptibility modestly.
- Innovation includes using black patches (0 pixel values) as masks to test sensitivity, inspired by MAE’s global semantic invariance under local context masking, and Monte Carlo gradient estimation for the noise patch optimization.
- The paper is well-written, logical, and easy to follow. Experiments are comprehensive, with comparative and ablation studies (patch size, number of patches, universal attack), and results show effectiveness across four victim LVLMs even with low noise size.
- The research question is highly relevant: real-world attackers often lack model access, and this work expands understanding of adversarial effectiveness in realistic contexts.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited model evaluation**: All four victim LVLMs (BLIP-2, MiniGPT-4, LLaVA-1.5, InstructBLIP) are relatively small and use the same frozen CLIP ViT image encoder. This makes it unclear whether HardPatch exploits this architectural dependency (e.g., via text similarity). Results on recent SOTA open-source models (Qwen2 VL, Llama3.2 Vision) and proprietary models (GPT-4o, Claude3.5) are missing, reducing generalizability. One reviewer notes that transfer-based attacks (Zhao et al. 2024, Dong et al. 2023) represent an even harder black-box setting and are not compared, challenging the claim of being the first hard-label attack.
- **Computational cost and scalability**: The attack requires excessive queries (e.g., one reviewer reports >5 hours and 50 GB memory per attack). The paper does not specify whether reported GPU hours are per image or per dataset; if per image, scalability is poor. Frequent querying risks detection by server-side defenses (e.g., rate limiting), and the high cost limits practical deployment.
- **Stealthiness concerns**: The adversarial patches cause significant visual changes (e.g., resembling QR codes in Figures 1 and 5), easily noticeable by humans, contradicting the standard imperceptibility requirement for adversarial perturbations. Multiple patches further enlarge the masking area and destroy important semantic parts of the image.
- **Unclear or missing details and unfair comparisons**:
    - The $p$-norm used in Equation 1 is not specified. The method completely changes 1–4 patches, likely violating $\ell_\infty$ constraints (large $\ell_\infty$ difference). No perturbation budget $\varepsilon$ is enforced in Algorithm 1 or Equations 2–6.
    - Comparison with Zhao et al. (2024) (MF) is unfair: MF uses $\ell_\infty$ perturbation with $\varepsilon=8$, while HardPatch uses $\varepsilon=16$ with an unknown $\ell_p$ norm. Also, MF produces pixel-level perturbations, not patches, making direct comparison problematic.
    - In Tables 4 and 5, the perturbation budget $\|x-x'\|_p\leq \varepsilon$ is not explicitly kept. The scaling experiment (varying patch number and size) should control for total perturbed pixels (e.g., 4 patches of $5\times5$ vs 8 patches of $7\times7$ vs 13 patches of $9\times9$ to equalize pixel count). The conclusion that more patches indicate easier attack is not properly controlled.
- **Incomplete ablation**: Only patch number and size are ablated; other hyperparameters (optimization steps $T$, number of additive noises $K$, targeted similarity threshold $\tau_1$, untargeted threshold $\tau_2$, random seeds, randomized functions for $\delta_m$ and $\Delta_k$) are not analyzed. The choice of normalized uniform distribution $u \cdot \exp(u-1)$ for $\Delta_k$ is not justified theoretically or empirically.
- **Unsuitable target phrases**: In targeted attacks, phrases like "I am sorry" or "I cannot answer" are problematic because success may reflect model limitations rather than true attack success. A visual example for a concrete target like "Bomb" (including the model’s response) is needed to verify attack success.
- **Minor writing issues** (line 131 and 310 formatting), but these do not affect scientific content.
