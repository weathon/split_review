## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies the global convergence of RLHF for aligning LLMs, employing a bi-level optimization framework to address distribution shift between reward learning and policy learning. Under a weak gradient domination assumption, the authors propose a first-order algorithm for neural network parameterized settings and derive a sample complexity of \(\epsilon^{-7/2}\). The paper provides theoretical convergence rate bounds and global optimality in a parameterized RLHF framework, moving beyond previous tabular or linear assumptions.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-structured with a clear introduction and a proof sketch that is easy to follow.
- The theoretical analysis is novel for applying penalty-based bilevel optimization to RLHF, providing convergence rate bounds and global optimality (one of the first works to do so under neural parametrization).
- The sample complexity of \(\epsilon^{-7/2}\) is a notable improvement over prior RLHF results.
- Thorough theoretical support, including convergence proofs, gives a solid foundation for future empirical work.

### Weaknesses
- **Unrealistic assumptions (minority view: some reviewers find the theory well-rounded, but others strongly criticize the assumptions):**  
  - Assumptions 1 and 3 (weak gradient domination) are not justified for neural networks, even two-layer NNs, let alone Transformers. Without justification, the derived results are not meaningful.  
  - Assumption 2 (Lipschitz and smoothness) is considered relatively easy to satisfy, but the combination of weak gradient domination and neural parametrization assumptions may limit generalizability.
- **Limited significance and motivation of distribution shift:**  
  - The concept and significance of distribution shift are not clearly defined or demonstrated. The paper argues it is a key issue but provides no concrete numerical or theoretical evidence.  
  - One reviewer questions whether the bilevel formulation is even needed: when a KL-regularized lower-level is used (as in prior work), it has a closed-form solution, undermining the motivation.
- **Writing clarity and presentation issues:**  
  - From Section 5 onward, the paper is poorly written and hard to follow.  
  - Extensive complex notation without a summary table, poorly explained notations (e.g., sigma, Log, norm, bracket in assumptions and lemmas).  
  - Multiple typos in theoretical results: Assumption 3 line 389 “\(J(\lambda.\phi)\)” instead of \(J(\lambda,\phi)\); Assumption 4 extraneous period; Lemma 1 “\(J(\lambda),\phi)\)”; Theorem 1 exponentials incorrectly placed outside big-O (e.g., \(K=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon)^{-1}\)).  
  - The neural network parameterization (activation function, architecture) is not specified.
- **Algorithmic and practical limitations:**  
  - The penalty reformulation is adapted directly from Kwon et al. (2023); Algorithm 1 is not practical for large-scale LLM/RLHF pipelines.  
  - Algorithm 2 is a standard way to estimate fitted Q-values; overall algorithmic contribution is limited.  
  - The proxy objective (Eq. 10) requires \(\lambda^{*}\), which seems impractical, and it is unclear how the proposed method bypasses the need for hypergradients and Hessians.  
  - Although policy fine-tuning is defined as a KL-regularized problem, KL regularization is not implemented in the proposed algorithm.  
  - Practical guidelines for tuning parameters (e.g., gradient step sizes) are missing.
- **Incremental theoretical contribution (minority view: some reviewers consider the theory novel):**  
  - The bi-level formulation is largely based on prior works (Chakraborty et al., Ding et al., Kwon et al.). The main proof draws from existing analysis techniques: actor-critic analysis from Xu et al. (2020), bi-level optimization results from Chen et al. (2024), and NTK analysis from Wang et al. (2020) for shallow over-parameterized feed-forward NNs.  
  - The analysis is limited to shallow single-hidden-layer feed-forward NNs with NTK, not transformers used in modern LLMs.
- **Missing empirical validation:**  
  - No numerical experiments are provided to validate the algorithm’s superiority, handling of distribution shift, or practical applicability.  
  - The paper claims to handle distribution shift but offers no evidence (theoretical or empirical) that the bilevel framework does so.
- **Lack of clarity in theoretical results:**  
  - The sample complexity in Theorem 1 uses squared distance; it is unclear how this compares to other references in Table 1 (which may use different metrics).  
  - Dependence on horizon \(H\) and state/vocabulary space size is not given.  
  - The main results in Section 6.3 lack sufficient explanation; the paper initially compares different RLHF methods but then only discusses actor-critic algorithms.  
  - Reliance on the actor-critic framework of Xu et al. (2020) (ICML 2024) with the main adjustment being substitution of policy optimization by solving Eq. (10) – the differences and challenges in the analysis are not elaborated.
- **Reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms:**  
  - How can neural networks (including Transformers) satisfy the weak gradient domination assumptions?  
  - Why are numerical results absent if the algorithm is claimed to be superior?  
  - Can the proposed approach be applied to large-scale RL applications?  
  - How does the method differ from PARL (Chakraborty et al., 2023)?  
  - What would change if the algorithm did not follow the hypergradient formulation?
