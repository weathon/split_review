## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Auto-Demo Prompting, a method for batch prompting that reformats the prompt to have the model output each question followed by its answer in sequence, aiming to combine the efficiency of batch processing with the quality of few-shot demonstrations. Two reviewers (R1, R2) find the idea interesting and note some empirical improvements over standard batch prompting. The third reviewer (R3) is strongly critical, rating the paper 1 (confidence 5), stating it is not up to conference standards and unlikely to change opinion even after author responses.

### Strengths
- Auto-demo prompting outperforms standard batch prompting on 7/10 tasks across batch sizes (R1).
- The method is interesting: repeating the question in the batch helps the model answer each question more accurately (R2).
- Improving inference-time efficiency via prompting techniques is an important direction (R1).
- Experiments cover five commonly used NLP datasets (R2, R3), and the idea of exploiting similarity across questions for efficiency gains is interesting (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing crucial baselines**: No comparison against few-shot prompting (with or without retrieval-based demonstration selection), which is central to the claim of “bridging” batch and few-shot prompting (R1, R2, R3).  
- **No evaluation of efficiency/cost**: The method generates more tokens by repeating questions, increasing cost; yet the paper provides no efficiency analysis, despite citing efficiency as the original motivation for batch prompting (R1, R2, R3).  
- **Minor and unclear improvements**: Gains are often small (e.g., 0.2% reported on some tasks, Lines 370–371) and the overall performance improvement is marginal relative to added complexity (R1, R2, R3).  
- **Missing ablations**: The role of the embedding space for demonstration selection is not ablated; other metrics for data selection are not explored (R1). Standard deviations are absent from Figure 3 (R2), and the x-axis/batch-size range is inconsistent across experiments (R2).  
- **Limited originality**: The method directly combines existing ideas (batch prompting and few-shot demonstration selection) without deeper insight. The intuition that sequential (q, a) pairs align with training data is straightforward (R1). The Batch Data Selection with Retrieval approach is not novel (R2, R3).  
- **Clarity issues** (R2, R3, and partially R1): The definition of batch prompting is unclear in the intro (Lines 43–48); it is not explained how questions are batched; model-generated answers as few-shot prompts are introduced abruptly (Lines 100–102); terms like “proper design” (Line 105) are vague; why 0 to N‑1 additional demos help with long context is not justified (Lines 114–115); the contribution list (Lines 123–141) omits efficiency despite that being the initial motivation; unnecessary details appear (Lines 213–219). Reviewer 1 adds that Method 1 and Method 2 descriptions appear to misstate the generation order (e.g., $a_i$ not conditioned on $q_i$; output preceded by $q_1 q_2$ rather than all previous questions).  
- **No comparison to related efficiency methods**: For example, Hydragen (arXiv:2402.05099) shares KV-cache on shared prefixes without increasing context length (R2, R3).  
- **Reviewer disagreement is sharp**: Reviewer 3 is strongly negative (rating 1, confidence 5), deeming the work not up to conference standards and unlikely to be salvageable. Reviewers 1 and 2 are moderately positive (rating 3) but share many of the same concerns, indicating consensus on major weaknesses.
