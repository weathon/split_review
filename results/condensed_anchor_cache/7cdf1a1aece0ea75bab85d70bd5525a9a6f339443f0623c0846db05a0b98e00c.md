## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper formalizes the problem of benchmarking NLP systems when scores are missing on entire tasks or instances, and proposes a method that uses a compatible partial ranking approach (counting permutations consistent with observed partial orders) to impute missing data, then aggregates via Borda count. Two refinements are given for task-level and instance-level scores, and a large benchmark of over 131 million scores is released. Validation on synthetic data (20 systems, 20 tasks, 20 instances) and on an extended real-world benchmark shows improved robustness compared to a simple-mean baseline that ignores missing values. Reviewers agree the problem is important, but diverge on the contribution’s originality, strength of baselines, quality of presentation, and thoroughness of experimental analysis.

### Strengths
- The problem is significant and increasingly relevant: benchmarking general-purpose models requires combining results across many datasets and tasks, yet missing scores are common due to cost, private systems, or computational limits.
- The method is clever and well formalized: it combines (1) estimates based on the proportion of total orders compatible with the observed partial order, (2) Borda count to aggregate task orderings, and (3) confidence intervals on the resulting rankings. A practical O(n³) algorithm for counting compatible permutations is provided, solving a non-trivial combinatorial problem.
- Both task-level and instance-level evaluations are covered, extending the scope of the prior work (Colombo et al., 2022).
- The method empirically outperforms a simple baseline (mean aggregation that ignores missing data) and yields rankings that remain much closer to the reference ranking as the proportion of missing scores increases.
- A large dataset of instance-level evaluation results is released, intended to foster further research. The results on both synthetic and real-world data are convincing for several reviewers.
- The paper is clearly written and easy to read according to one reviewer (score 8), though others disagree on readability.

### Weaknesses
- **Originality and baseline comparison**: Several reviewers note low originality; the main contributions are an efficient implementation of a combinatorial approach that extends two pre-existing methods (Colombo et al., 2022) to missing scores, and enlargement of an existing dataset. The method is compared only to a weak baseline (mean aggregation that ignores missing values). This baseline confounds two issues: ignoring data and using scores from different scales. It is unclear whether the proposed method’s advantage comes from using ranks instead of scores, or from modeling missingness. Stronger baselines (e.g., imputation methods that leverage observed correlations) are missing.
- **Motivation and assumptions**: The uniform distribution over all total orders compatible with the observed partial order is described as “particularly naïve.” It is sub-optimal when system performance is correlated (e.g., if system i usually outperforms j when both are observed, setting M_ij = 0.5 ignores that signal). The justification for averaging estimated ranks (rather than using a different aggregation) is lacking.
- **Borda aggregation issues**: Borda count yields very different rankings from mean aggregation even when there are no missing values; this needs investigation before the resulting rankings can be accepted as truthful. The degradation from missing values is the same for Borda and mean aggregation in realistic scenarios with less than 20% missing values, potentially limiting practical interest.
- **Presentation and readability**: The paper feels rushed. Figures are generally too small and often unreadable (e.g., Fig. 2, 3, 5). Their positioning is odd (e.g., Fig. 2 referenced on p. 9). Multiple typos (e.g., “Our includes…”, missing article, messed-up superscripts, “in Ssec 5.3” likely 5.2). Citations lack brackets in many places. Reference missing for “[4]”. The x-axis in Fig. 2 and Fig. 5 is described as showing the proportion of scores observed, but the text discusses it as proportion removed, causing confusion.
- **Experimental analysis**: The experimental section is not well justified. Choice of datasets and evaluation protocol are insufficiently explained. Lack of error bars makes it hard to assess statistical significance. The toy experiment (Sec. 4) only checks robustness scaling and pairwise confidence; it is not clear why other factors (e.g., number of tasks) are not varied. Scaling corruption (Fig. 3) is not detailed: how tasks are selected for scaling, how many are scaled, what the evaluation metric is, and why there are two regimes starting at 1 or 0 when η=0. The confidence analysis (Fig. 6) is not compared to other methods such as ANOVA and could be removed for more analysis of earlier experiments.
- **Instance-level vs. task-level performance**: The two-level method (sigma_2l) does not consistently outperform the one-level method (sigma_l); in fact, instance-level information appears harmful on three datasets (Fig. 5). This is only briefly mentioned in the conclusions and not analyzed. The correlation of sigma_l vs. sigma_2l differs greatly across datasets without explanation.
- **Missing details**: The proportion of missing scores in the comparison of rankings by sigma_l and sigma_mu (Tables 1 and 2) is not stated. It is not clear from Fig. 5 whether “# scores (%)” is percentage removed or kept—the discussion is misleading. The method’s handling of misleading evaluation results (e.g., dataset sample bias, imperfect metrics) is not addressed.
- **Task correlation**: A closer look at task correlation is missing, since similar tasks may be easier to predict.
- **Not NLP-specific**: The problem is general, yet experiments are restricted to NLP.
- **Disagreement among reviewers**: Three reviewers (scores 5, 6, 5) rated the paper lower, citing low originality, weak baselines, poor presentation, and lack of thorough analysis. One reviewer (score 8) was more positive, finding the approach convincing and the paper clearly written, but also raised many of the same concerns (e.g., Borda/mean difference, missing details, figure readability).
