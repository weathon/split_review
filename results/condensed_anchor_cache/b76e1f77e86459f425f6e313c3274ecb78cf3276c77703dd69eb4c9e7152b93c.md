## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces LongSafetyBench, a benchmark with 1201 multiple-choice questions covering 10 safety-related tasks at an average length of 41,889 words, designed to evaluate the safety of long-context LLMs. Experiments on eight (Reviewer 2 says eight, Reviewer 3 says ten) popular models find insufficient safety capabilities and show that fine-tuning on a small amount of long-context safety data can improve performance. Reviewer 1 is more positive (score 5, confidence 5) than the other three (each score 3, confidence 4–5). All reviewers note the importance of the topic, but raise substantial concerns about task design, evaluation methodology, validation, and dataset availability.

### Strengths
1. **Novel benchmark for an important gap.** The paper introduces the first benchmark specifically designed to evaluate the safety of long-context LLMs, addressing a timely and underexplored problem. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
2. **Comprehensive large-scale experiments.** Evaluation of 8–10 popular long-context models across 10 task categories provides strong evidence of the benchmark’s utility and reveals insufficient safety capabilities in existing models. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
3. **Fine-tuning insight.** The finding that supervised fine-tuning with a small amount of automatically constructed long-context safety data significantly improves safety performance in open-source models is valuable and has practical implications. (R3, R4)
4. **Concern about long-context safety issues is valid.** The paper highlights that long-context safety is a particularly challenging problem and that models tend to overlook harmful content in lengthy texts. (R1)

### Weaknesses
1. **Questionable task design and lack of systematic taxonomy.**
   - The selection of the ten task types lacks justification; it is unclear why these specific tasks were chosen and to what extent they provide a comprehensive safety evaluation. (R2)
   - The distinction between “fully harmful context” and “partially harmful context” is not clearly defined or objectively justified. (R2)
   - Some tasks (e.g., HarmfulExtraction, HarmfulTendency, ManyShotJailbreak) appear similar; it is not clear whether they measure distinct safety perspectives or are just different topics. (R2)
   - The “seemingly safe and reasonable scenario” terminology is ambiguous. (R2)

2. **Corpus size and domain coverage insufficient.**
   - The benchmark is not large enough to be a proper safety evaluation; it would be better to create domain-specific benchmarks (e.g., medical, political) to mitigate corpus size and address domain biases. (R1)
   - HarmfulExtraction uses crime novels without distinguishing fact from fiction; fictional actions that are impossible in the real world should not be included. A corpus built with input from law enforcement officers would be more appropriate. (R1)
   - HarmfulAdvice should be constructed in consultation with real professionals (e.g., medical professionals) and account for cultural, legal, and regional variations. (R1)
   - Synthetic/generated data raises concerns about how well results translate to real-world tasks. (R4-Q2)

3. **Evaluation via multiple-choice scheme is problematic and does not measure generative safety.**
   - The benchmark uses only multiple-choice questions with four options representing varying safety degrees. This measures the model’s *awareness* of harmful context rather than its ability to generate safe responses. (R2, R3, R4)
   - In tasks like CountingCrimes (“What crimes has Devil committed?”), providing the correct answer might not be unsafe; the definition of “safe” behavior is debatable. (R3)
   - The safe response (SR) metric is misleading because it only indicates selection of the safest option, not text generation safety. (R2, R4)
   - Options are generated by Qwen2 according to “predefined option construction standards” that are not described, and there is no validation process for these options. (R2)
   - The paper acknowledges that models may detect but ignore harmful content, but equating this with a weakening of safety is questionable; more nuanced metrics are needed. (R4)

4. **Claims about long-context vs. short-context safety not properly validated.**
   - The paper claims that safety performance degrades with longer context, but this is not tested on the own benchmark: no comparison across different length ranges is provided. (R2, R3, R4-Q1)
   - The claim that “models perform worse in long-context than in short-context” is based on a comparison with a third-party leaderboard, which uses different tasks, metrics, and scopes—this is an invalid comparison. (R2)
   - No contrastive evaluation with the same questions under short-context and long-context conditions is conducted. (R2)

5. **Fine-tuning experiments lack rigor and generalizability.**
   - The SFT experiment uses the same dataset for tuning and evaluation, making the reported improvements questionable. (R2)
   - Only one closed-source model (Claude-3.5-sonnet) is used as a baseline for comparison; the conclusion that fine-tuned models are comparable to SOTA closed-source models is not convincing. (R2)
   - There is no analysis of whether fine-tuning negatively impacts non-safety-related tasks or performance in long-context scenarios. (R4-Q3)

6. **Insufficient analysis of failure reasons.**
   - The paper reports evaluation results but lacks deep analysis of why models fail. For example, what causes safety issues: text length, multiple-choice format, or other factors? (R3)
   - Chain-of-thought or explanation generation for failure cases would provide insight into how models fail and how safety could be improved. (R3)

7. **Dataset and replication material not provided.**
   - No access to the benchmark or replication data is available, and there is no commitment to make it public. (R2, R3)

8. **Presentation issues.**
   - Many grammar errors and ambiguous statements exist (e.g., “memtioned” line 404, “imporce” line 537). (R2, R4)
   - Table 2 and Figure 6 are not discussed in the text. (R2)
   - Metrics (HA, SR) are mentioned early without explanation. (R2)
   - Figure 2 text is hard to read. (R2)
   - External methods (ManyShotJailbreak, SafetyBench) are not sufficiently explained, making the paper less self-contained. (R4)
   - Figure 3 uses a score instead of the metrics (HA, SR) used elsewhere; consistency is needed. (R4)
