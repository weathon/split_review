## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes HyperGCL, a novel contrastive learning framework for graphs that operates in hyperbolic space (Poincaré ball). It introduces an alignment loss based on hyperbolic distance and a uniformity term that forces node embeddings to populate the ball uniformly. To implement uniformity, embeddings are mapped to the tangent space where an isotropic Gaussian distribution is enforced, preventing dimensional collapse. The authors analyze collapse modes (leaf collapse, height collapse) and introduce the effective rank for evaluation. Experiments on node classification (8 datasets) and collaborative filtering show that HyperGCL consistently achieves state-of-the-art results among contrastive and hyperbolic methods.

### Strengths

- **Novel and intuitive idea.** Applying contrastive learning in hyperbolic space with a tailored uniformity loss is new and well-motivated for graph data that exhibits hierarchical structure.
- **Clear presentation.** The mathematical concepts are clearly explained, and the taxonomy of collapse modes (leaf/height collapse) is well articulated.
- **Theoretical foundation.** The paper provides a solid theoretical analysis, including effective rank and connections to dimensional collapse, which grounds the proposed method.
- **Extensive empirical evaluation.** HyperGCL is benchmarked on 8 datasets for node classification and collaborative filtering, consistently outperforming existing methods (both Euclidean and hyperbolic). Ablation studies and analysis of effective rank and Gaussian distribution choice are included.
- **Ablation and analysis.** Pertinent experiments on the choice of hyperbolic space for alignment/uniformity and on effective rank support the design decisions.
- **Detailed appendix.** Experimental details are provided, enhancing reproducibility.

### Weaknesses

**1. Marginal and possibly unrealistic performance gains.**
- The improvement over baselines is only about 1 percentage point on all datasets. Several reviewers question whether such a small margin justifies the added complexity.
- Reviewer 1 notes that Table 1/2 shows HyperGCL taking first place on every single dataset, which looks “too good to be true.” It must be clarified whether all baseline numbers were obtained by running the code (with consistent settings) or taken from original papers.  
- The practical impact is limited given the small gains and lack of clear computational complexity advantage over Euclidean contrastive methods.

**2. Missing comparisons and incomplete benchmarking.**
- Several strong baselines are missing: **GraphMAE** and **CCA-SSG** (the latter directly addresses dimensional collapse in Euclidean space via covariance to identity). CCA-SSG is especially relevant because it achieves comparable performance to HyperGCL using higher embedding dimensions, raising doubts about the necessity of hyperbolic space.
- **HGCL** (Table 1) is also a hyperbolic contrastive learning method, but the paper does not explain how HyperGCL differs from it or whether it is the only other hyperbolic contrastive baseline.
- No comparisons in semi-supervised learning settings, even though HyperGCL’s low-dimensional hyperbolic embeddings might be better suited for label efficiency.
- No fully unsupervised evaluation (e.g., clustering, link prediction) is provided; only supervised evaluation via a linear classifier is used. The paper should include unsupervised metrics to directly validate the learned topology.
- Sensitivity to the encoder architecture is not analyzed. A comparison of the 2-step linear classifier evaluation against a fully supervised GNN backbone (including heterophilous settings) is missing.

**3. Lack of sensitivity and hyperparameter analysis.**
- The perturbation hyperparameters (drop edge/node rates) are not validated, and the method’s sensitivity to them is not reported.
- No sensitivity analysis of the curvature parameter (c) for node classification. Since this parameter needs fine-tuning, it would help to know how robust the method is.
- The choice of embedding dimension (fixed at 50) is not compared to higher dimensions used by competing Euclidean methods (e.g., CCA-SSG).

**4. Theoretical and conceptual issues (major concern for Reviewer 4).**
- **Contrastive learning is not defined.** The paper jumps into (X,A) pairs without explaining what real data these correspond to or what the formal objective is. The symbols \(p_\text{pos}\) and \(p_\text{data}\) in Equation (1) are not defined.
- **Theorem 1 is misinterpreted.** It states that hyperbolic distances can be approximated by a tree metric, but the paper then claims the converse (“any tree can be embedded into a Poincaré disk with low distortion”), which is not what Theorem 1 says.
- **Equation (9) is incomplete.** \(p\) and \(D\) are not defined. \(D\) appears to be KL divergence, but the distributions involved are not specified; it seems \(p\) is meant to be the standard normal, but this is never stated.
- **Theorem 3 is trivial or irrelevant.** The inequality \(\mathcal{L}_U^\mathcal{T} \geq -Erank\) is vacuous because the left side is always non-positive and the right side is always non-negative. Moreover, the KL divergence in \(\mathcal{L}_U^\mathcal{T}\) is not a suitable proxy for effective rank: effective rank is scale-invariant (depends only on singular value distribution), while KL divergence is scale-sensitive. The paper should have directly reported \(\mathcal{L}_U^\mathcal{T}\) values in Table 3 instead of Erank, but even then the connection is not justified.
- **Uniformity motivation is flawed.** The isotropic Gaussian imposed in the tangent space decays exponentially (in Euclidean norm), but the surface area of the Poincaré ball grows exponentially in radius, leading to a double-exponential concentration effect that dominates any uniformity effect. This is not discussed.
- **Figure 4 is misleading.** The claim that panel (b) is “best” is questionable because it ignores the tree topology (panel (d) appears more uniform under hyperbolic distance). The paper does not clarify why the original data topology is irrelevant for evaluating uniformity.
- **Scalability.** The method involves a determinant calculation (from the Gaussian log-likelihood), which does not scale well to high dimensions. While many applications use low dimensions, this should be acknowledged.

**5. Writing and presentation issues.**
- Many typos throughout the paper (noted by multiple reviewers).
- Figure 1 and Figure 2 are unclear; their explanations need improvement and could be moved to the supplementary material.
- Definition 2 has a notational mismatch (\(\mathbb{D}^n_c\) should be \(\mathbb{D}^d_c\)) and is hard to follow.
- Figure 4: collapse modes should be labeled in subplots, and the clean tree should be isolated.
- Figure 5: the paper says “drop nodes” but the figure appears to mask features instead.
- The reason for projecting embeddings to the Poincaré ball in Section 3.1 is not explained in the main text; only a reference to Appendix B is given.

**6. Related work and optimization choices.**
- Mapping from hyperbolic space to Gaussian distributions has been studied earlier (e.g., [A]), which mitigates the novelty of Theorem 2. This should be explicitly acknowledged.
- Efficient Riemannian optimizers have advanced beyond RSGD, but the paper does not compare with alternatives (e.g., [B], [C]).
- Optimization in Riemannian manifold is not justified by simple benchmarks; the choice of RSGD seems arbitrary.

**7. Reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms (to be addressed as weaknesses).**
- (R1) How are baseline results obtained? (see point 1)
- (R1) How does HyperGCL differ from HGCL? (see point 2)
- (R2) What are the computational complexity advantages over existing methods? (see point 1)
- (R3) Are embeddings normalized or just centered? (affects Gaussian assumption)
- (R3) Why use a linear classifier for evaluation? A 2-MLP with ReLU would better test non-linear separability.
- (R4) What does \(\mathcal{T}\) in \(\mathcal{L}_U^\mathcal{T}\) stand for?
- (R4) What are \(p_\text{pos}\) and \(p_\text{data}\) in Equation (1)?
- (R4) Could contrastive learning be formally defined consistent with the method’s notation?
