## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a topological masking method for transformers on graph-structured data, parameterizing the mask as a learnable function of a weighted adjacency matrix. By approximating with graph random features (GRFs), the method achieves linear time and space complexity with respect to the number of tokens, supporting attention masking on graphs with >30k nodes. The paper provides concentration bounds for GRFs (claimed as first known) and sparsity guarantees. Experiments on image classification (ImageNet, iNaturalist2021, Places365), point cloud dynamics prediction, and robotics tasks show performance gains over baselines. Reviewers are split: three rate 8 (positive overall, with requests for additional experiments and clarification), three rate 6 (citing theoretical flaws in the complexity claims, limited related work, and dense presentation). The main theoretical concern is that the O(N) complexity claim relies on O(1) sparsity of GRFs, which does not hold for general graphs (e.g., complete graphs) without a bounded-degree assumption.

### Strengths
- The method is the first to achieve **O(N) time and space complexity** for topological masking on general graphs under the low-rank attention framework, improving over previous O(N log N) methods. (R1, R2, R3)
- Provides the **first known concentration bounds for GRFs** (Theorem 3.1) and rigorous sparsity guarantees, with theoretical insights potentially extending beyond transformers. (R1, R3, R6)
- Demonstrates **strong performance gains** on diverse real-world tasks (image, point cloud, video, robotics) and scales to >30k nodes where quadratic attention is intractable. (R1, R5, R6)
- Offers both **symmetric and asymmetric GRF variants**, with different efficiency–variance tradeoffs. (R1)
- Paper is **well-organized, clearly motivated, and well-illustrated** according to several reviewers; the reasoning (from low-rank attention to kernel masking to GRF approximation) is logical. (R4, R5)
- Includes **detailed ablation studies** and some analysis of the impact of the number of random walks. (R1, R6)

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical flaw in O(1) sparsity claim for GRFs** (Reviewer 1). The bound in Lemma 3.2 depends on graph size N, degree, and parameters (number of walks n, halt probability p_halt, δ). For a complete graph, the number of non-zero entries grows with O(N), not O(1). The authors implicitly assume bounded-degree graphs (lines 278-280: “assuming that c remains constant (i.e. we fix a maximum edge weight and node degree as the graph grows)”), which contradicts the claim of applicability to general graphs (line 82). This assumption must be made explicit and the complexity analysis corrected; current experiments only on low-degree grids do not validate the claim for dense or high-degree graphs. (R1)
- **True time complexity O(Nmd) omitted** from most of the paper (Reviewer 5). The factors m (hidden dimension) and d (feature dimension) can be large, affecting realistic scalability. The paper should discuss the practical implications of large m and d for memory and computation. (R5)
- **Insufficient efficiency experiments**: The paper reports accuracy but not runtime, memory, or FLOPs. Several reviewers request training time, total computation cost, and the impact of the number of random walks on runtime. (R2, R4, R5) This makes it hard to validate the claimed efficiency gains. (R4, R5)
- **Lack of evaluation on traditional graph datasets**. Reviewers 2 and 6 request experiments on standard benchmarks like Cora, Citeseer (node classification) to demonstrate generalizability beyond image/point cloud data. (R2, R6)
- **Limited discussion of method limitations** (Reviewer 3): no analysis of how random walk length or halting probability affect approximation quality; no guidance for graphs with varying connectivity/diameter; no discussion of dynamic/evolving graphs where recomputing walks is costly; no systematic hyperparameter sensitivity study. (R3)
- **Inconsistent and confusing notation** (Reviewer 1, 3, 6): sequences (f_k) mis-specified as subset of ℝ instead of functions; weighted adjacency matrix defined as normalized (w_ij = 1/√(d_i d_j)), which discards edge weights; missing explicit assumptions about graph structure and maximum degree; line 148 size notation confusing (𝒩 vs N). (R1, R6)
- **Presentation is mathematically dense and unclear** (Reviewer 3). Lines 184–254 are notation-heavy, McDiarmid’s inequality is used without sufficient context, and the link between theory and practice is weak. Algorithm 1 is too abstract to follow. This harms accessibility, especially for readers unfamiliar with GRFs. (R3)
- **Limited related work comparison** (Reviewer 6). The paper does not adequately differentiate its approach from methods that learn latent graph structures or use structural information via relative positional encodings. Improved time/space complexity has been previously discussed (references [A],[B] unspecified). The paper should clarify its novelty relative to these. (R6)
- **Missing comparisons to linear attention baselines** such as Performers (Favor+) and Nyströmformer (Reviewer 1). (R1)
- **Citation issue**: The reference Borgwardt et al. (2005) is about graph kernels, not graph node kernels; the paper should justify the connection or use a more precise citation. (R5)
- **Additional requested analyses**: Explain why accuracy improvement in Figure 5 (GRF Interlacer) drops after the initial timestep (Reviewer 4). Clarify convergence of the power series in Eq. (4) and assumptions on α (Reviewer 3). Clarify if the deconvolution in Remark 3.1 is always guaranteed or an assumption (Reviewer 3). Address whether the method is a reinvention of graph neural networks by integrating structure information into Transformers (Reviewer 5). (R3, R4, R5)
