## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies bias and fairness in person detection, evaluating Mask R-CNN and YOLOv3 on the MIAP and BDD datasets. It analyzes how real-world image manipulations (blur, grayscale) and model reliance on size/contrast/brightness affect fairness across race, gender, and age. Reviewers agree the topic is important and experiments are extensive, but have significant concerns about novelty, methodology, and analysis depth.

### Strengths
- The topic of algorithmic bias and fairness in person detection is important for safety-critical applications (autonomous vehicles, security monitoring) and could guide future dataset and model design [all reviewers].
- Extensive experiments are conducted on two datasets (MIAP, BDD) with two detectors (Faster R-CNN / Mask R-CNN, YOLOv3), including controlled image manipulations and a biased detection pipeline, using established fairness metrics (e.g., statistical parity, equal opportunity, fairness through awareness) [R1, R2, R3].
- The paper provides a novel analysis of the impact of image quality (blur, grayscale) and model feature biases on fairness across race, gender, and age demographics [R3].
- The literature review well frames the motivation; quantitative tables and figures (AP, FDR, FNR, TPR) are good; qualitative results in the appendix clearly show YOLO is generally less biased than Faster-RCNN and disparity across attribute groups [R4].
- Figures 1–3 clearly illustrate the methodology; the results section gives a comprehensive description of both quantitative and qualitative findings [R4].

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and contribution**: One reviewer rated the paper a 1 (others 3) and considered novelty limited—many person detection datasets (CrowdHuman, TinyPerson, aerial datasets) already address low-quality/crowded images, and the paper reads more like an experiment report than an academic paper with theoretical analysis or technical contribution. The selected detectors (Mask R-CNN, YOLOv3) are outdated; reviewers suggested using DINO-based detectors, YOLOv8+, and specialized pedestrian detectors (CSP, PRNet) [R1, R2]. Developing an improved detector based on the findings was expected to strengthen technical contribution [R1].
- **Dataset and model generalizability**: Only two datasets (MIAP, BDD) are used, lacking popular pedestrian datasets like Caltech, CityPersons, or NightOwls (which offers real-world low-light bias). The analysis should discuss how findings generalize to other datasets and models, and how dataset size/diversity affect biases [R1, R2, R3].
- **Experimental comparison missing**: Minimal comparison is made between the four experimental settings, making it hard to assess how image manipulations (Exp2) or injected bias (Exp3) actually affect fairness relative to baseline (Exp1). For example, under blur/grayscale, performance gaps between skin-tone demographics appear to narrow, which is counterintuitive and needs interpretation. Without comparing settings, the research questions are not satisfactorily answered [R4]. Reviewers requested ablation-style analysis or visualizations (e.g., ablation-CAM) to explain how different demographics are affected [R4].
- **Fairness discussion insufficient**: Section 7 does not sufficiently discuss fairness metrics relative to the research questions. The finding that males satisfy statistical parity while females fail is confusing given the definition provided; the treatment of the “unknown” group in fairness metrics is unclear [R4]. The paper identifies biases but does not propose or evaluate specific mitigation strategies or algorithms [R3].
- **Methodology details omitted**:
  - No description of how bias is injected into NMS for Experiment 3 [R4].
  - Fairness Through Awareness metrics are only mentioned through the Aequitas toolkit; the distance metric used is not specified, and the choice is critical [R4].
  - It is unclear whether models were retrained on biased data or only tested [R4].
  - The number of instances per demographic subgroup (especially dark/light skin in BDD, where 99% are unknown) is not reported, raising questions about statistical significance [R4].
- **Presentation issues**:
  - All quantitative tables and most figures are placed in the appendix, making the main paper not self-contained; important results (attribute-wise accuracy) should be in the main text, possibly summarized in a unified figure [R1, R2].
  - Figures 4–15 in the appendix are blurry [R2].
  - Typo on page 2: "While most of the works reviewed in Section 2" should be Section 3 [R2].
  - Other typos: p2/3 dataset formatting; MIAP misnamed as "More Inclusive Images for People" instead of "More Inclusive Annotations for People"; double author names; "potentialyl" (p7.1); "lends" instead of "lens" (p3.3); Fig.2 caption grammar; Fig.3 uneven box widths [R4].
- **Missing references**: The paper should cite work on image compression (e.g., JPEG) and its effect on racial bias (Dodge & Karam 2016), and the recent FACET benchmark (Gustafson et al., 2023) which analyzes fairness on person images [R4].
- **Ethical discussion**: The paper would benefit from deeper discussion of ethical implications of algorithmic bias in safety-critical applications [R3].
- **Unclear claim**: The statement “YOLO may also have benefited from higher image quality within MIAP” is ambiguous; the fairness of the comparison between Faster-RCNN and YOLO performance needs clarification [R4]
