## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes FiRST, an algorithm that reduces LLM inference latency by using layer-specific routers to adaptively skip transformer layers per input sequence during decoding (the prompt decides which layers to skip). The method is compatible with KV caching and uses LoRA adapters for fine-tuning. Experiments on machine translation and summarization tasks with Llama-3-8B show latency reduction while retaining competitive performance compared to the base model and unified skipping.

### Strengths
- The idea of using input-adaptive routers to dynamically skip layers for efficient LLM inference while maintaining performance is promising for resource-constrained deployment.
- The inclusion of LoRA adapters for fine-tuning helps mitigate potential performance degradation from layer skipping and aids selection strategy optimization.
- The method is compatible with KV caching and batch decoding, avoiding additional computation for these processes.
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow.
- FiRST is evaluated on two different tasks (machine translation and summarization).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient evaluation scope and benchmarks:**  
  – Only one dataset per task (machine translation, summarization) is tested; more datasets are needed.  
  – Experiments are limited to Llama-3-8B; more model architectures should be evaluated.  
  – No zero-shot performance is reported (e.g., perplexity and accuracy on lm-evaluation-harness, WikiText2, C4, commonsense reasoning benchmarks like BoolQ, PIQA, HellaSwag, WinoGrande, ARC-easy, ARC-challenge, OpenbookQA, MMLU, BIG-bench hard).  
  – The method is claimed to be general, yet only tested on classical translation and summarization; broader reasoning tasks are missing, which hurts the generality motivation.

- **Insufficient baselines and comparisons:**  
  – Baselines are only the base model (with/without LoRA) and unified layer skipping. Many related efficiency methods are not compared, e.g., SLEB, QUEST, SliceGPT, and other layer pruning/streamlining works (Compressing LLMs by Streamlining the Unimportant Layer, Shortened LLaMA, Sheared LLaMA, Learning-to-Cache).  
  – No ablation study for LoRA-only fine-tuning is provided; Tables 1-3 suggest LoRA gives strong improvements, but without a LoRA-only baseline it is unclear whether gains come from FiRST or LoRA.  
  – No ablation study for router hyperparameters: how do λ and α in the loss affect performance? λ is fixed at 0.01 without justification; α (lora_alpha) settings are not discussed.  
  – The paper does not discuss how the number of skipped layers is determined (purely by router or user-defined?) nor the speed-up comparison with unified skipping.

- **Missing analysis of overhead and training cost:**  
  – The overhead of the proposed routers (additional parameters, training time, inference cost) is not discussed. A simple baseline of random layer skipping should be reported.  
  – The two-step training process (router fine-tuning + LoRA fine-tuning) is time-consuming per task; methods supporting post-training or zero-shot capabilities would be more desirable.  
  – No discussion of how the router handles dependencies between layers, especially when earlier layers are skipped, nor the effect of probabilistic layer skipping on gradient flow and training stability.

- **Novelty and related work:**  
  – Layer redundancy has been extensively explored (e.g., Compressing LLMs, Shortened LLaMA, Sheared LLaMA, router selection for DiT in Learning-to-Cache), but none of these are compared; the proposed method introduces additional parameters and training without clear efficiency gains over parameter-free pruning methods.  
  – The connection to quantization-aware training is unclear.

- **Presentation details:**  
  – Font in Figures 2 and 3 is too small.
