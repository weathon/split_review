## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes MetaProx, a meta-learning method that estimates per-task model parameters using an unrolled neural network derived from proximal gradient descent (PGD) on a regularized ERM problem. The regularization term is learned by representing its proximal operator as a coordinatewise piecewise linear function. Theoretical error bounds are provided for both smooth and non-smooth proximal functions. Experiments on few-shot image classification (miniImageNet) show improved performance over several optimization-based meta-learning baselines (e.g., MAML, LEO, ANIL). The learned proximal operators are visualized.

**Reviewer disagreement:** Two reviewers (R3, R4) were highly positive (score 8) while two reviewers (R1, R2) were more moderate (score 6). R4 expressed low confidence (2) in their positive assessment; R2 had high confidence (4) in their moderate assessment; R1 and R3 had confidence 3.

### Strengths
- **Novelty of idea (R2, R3, R4):** Applying algorithmic unrolling to proximal gradient descent for meta-learning is new and interesting. R3 emphasizes that this allows learning a broader range of complex and adaptable priors, including non-smooth/discontinuous functions, enhancing expressiveness and interpretability compared to traditional approaches that impose explicit priors (e.g., Gaussian/Laplace in LEO, ANIL). R4 notes algorithmic choices (piecewise linear approximations) are well-motivated.
- **Theoretical analysis (R2, R3):** The paper derives rigorous error bounds for both smooth and non-smooth proximal operators, offering practical guidelines for hyperparameter tuning (e.g., parameter C). R2 considers this theoretically sound with technical depth.
- **Empirical performance (R1, R2, R3, R4):** Consistent performance gains are demonstrated on few-shot classification benchmarks (miniImageNet) against a wide range of optimization-based meta-learning algorithms. R1 notes favorable results with standard datasets and benchmarks. R4 highlights additional ablations that underline the effectiveness of design choices.
- **Presentation and context (R1, R4):** The paper provides a clear, robust overview of background and prior work, making contributions easy to understand. R4 finds the theoretical exposition and experimental evaluation well fleshed out and well-written. R1 appreciates the mixture of theory and experiment.
- **Visualization of learned nonlinearities (R1):** The results in Section 4.4 on learned proximal operators are an interesting consequence of the method.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited scope of evaluation (R1, R3, R4):** Experiments are restricted to few-shot classification (mostly miniImageNet). R3 requests additional experiments in other few-shot domains (regression, reinforcement learning, time series forecasting) to verify generality. R4 asks for results on other benchmarks. R1 notes that the methodology’s connection to specific practical tasks (e.g., robotics) is not argued, weakening claims of “expressivity” and “interpretability”.
- **Missing related work and comparison (R1, R2, R3, R4):** R2 notes the paper lacks a dedicated related work section, making it difficult to locate contributions within the state of the art. R1 is unclear how MetaProx differs from other meta-learning approaches (novelty seems focused on algorithm unrolling, not the meta-learning formulation) and asks whether it is the first work connecting equation (1) to proximal gradient descent. R3 requests comparison with [1] (proximal layers for deep regularization). R4 asks for discussion of relation to fully Bayesian approaches (Probabilistic MAML, Bayesian MAML). R1 also raises the “apples-to-apples” comparison in experiments: are some SotA methods excluded, and could MetaProx’s advantage come from being a multilayer residual network with learnable nonlinearities?
- **Theoretical claims not empirically validated (R2):** The paper claims the error bounds are tight, but this is not verified in toy settings where the bounds can be numerically computed. R1 also questions whether Theorem 3.2 is mainly an application of known one-dimensional piecewise linear approximation results.
- **Clarity and presentation issues (R1, R2):** R2 finds the introduction too heavy on motivation before explaining contributions; Section 3.1 (algorithmic unrolling) is difficult to follow and needs thorough revision. R1 notes minor errors: page 3 “(2b)” should be “(1b)”; dangling clause before equation (11); nonstandard notation \(\mathbb{C}^k\) for continuously differentiable functions; Theorem 3.2 should reference Assumption 3.1.
- **Interpretability/explainability claim is vague (R1, R2):** R2 is unsure how the explainable prior can be useful in practice beyond the results in Section 4.4. R1 notes the link between the learned piecewise linear proximal operators and task-specific structure is not made concrete. R3 suggests visualizing the evolution of the learned prior at each unrolled PGD step (not just averaged) to deepen understanding.
- **Methodological concerns (R1):** The implementation uses 1D piecewise linear functions that may not correspond to the prox of any function; the necessary monotonicity constraint is not addressed. R1 also asks about the initialization of \(\zeta\) parameters (missing detail). The notion of “optimal” regularizer \(\mathcal{R}^*\) is introduced without definition.
- **Missing ablations and analyses (R3):** No analysis of trade-off between regularization and loss in Eq. (1b). No discussion of careful initialization/scheduling of \(\lambda\) to avoid instability. No evaluation on more challenging settings (e.g., 20-way classification).
- **Low reviewer confidence in positive assessment (R4):** R4’s vote for acceptance is with low confidence (2), noting they might decrease score during rebuttal.
