## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses causal discovery in the presence of latent variables under linearity and non-Gaussianity assumptions. It proposes PO-LiNGAM, a generalization of LiNGAM that allows latent variables anywhere in the causal structure, without constraints on their positions or number. The key idea is to cluster variables into atomic units—basic building blocks of the causal graph—and use high-order statistics (GIN condition) to identify latent variables from their measured descendants via measurement surrogate variable sets. A three-phase iterative algorithm is presented: phase (I) identifies leaf nodes and their parents; phase (II) discovers atomic units; phase (III) refines atomic units. The authors establish identifiability conditions and demonstrate the method on synthetic and real data.

### Strengths
- The method allows a very general causal structure where measured and latent variables can be interlinked arbitrarily (no constraints on latent variable presence or position).
- Overlapping variables within discovered latent sets are handled, extending earlier LiNGAM variants.
- The theoretical identifiability is established under two clear conditions, and the results are sound.
- The use of linear transitivity via GIN condition to infer latent variables is novel and interesting.
- The algorithm is clearly presented, with multiple examples that help understanding.
- Sufficient experimental results (synthetic and real) demonstrate the method’s validity.
- The work is well situated in the causal structure learning literature, and the graphical assumptions are novel.

### Weaknesses
- The identifiability condition requires each atomic unit to have a “sufficient number of pure atomic unit children.” This is a strong assumption; the authors acknowledge it and list relaxing it as future work, but its practical plausibility is not explored.
- There is no discussion of when the PO-LiNGAM assumptions would realistically hold or be violated; concrete real-world examples would help assess practical relevance.
- Notation clarity is a concern: explanation of notations is limited to Table 1 in Figure 1, which is insufficient. Several definitions are unclear:
  - Definition 3’s “pure atomic unit child” is introduced without clear explanation.
  - Definition 5’s description of surrogate variables (terms “can be”, “can have”, and “non-redundant set”) is hard to parse.
  - The simultaneous use of V, boldfaced V, and script V (all denoting sets) is confusing, especially in Definition 3.
- Reviewers raised multiple technical questions that reflect missing details or potential issues:
  - Q1: Is the decomposition of atomic units among latent variables not unique? If so, how is uniqueness proven? Could non-uniqueness affect results?
  - Q2: How do the identifiability conditions compare to existing conditions for similar models?
  - Q3: How does condition (a) imply each latent variable has measured descendants?
  - Q5: What are “decomposable atomic units” in Section 3.3? They are not atomic units by definition; how do they arise from Phases I and II?
  - Q6: Does Section 3.4 assume an oracle independence test? (This should be stated.)
  - Q7: In Corollary 3, what “parts” of Phase I & II need to be specified? Also, does the last sentence mean “no latent variables” rather than “no latent confounders”?)
- Computational time of the algorithm is not reported.
- Minor typo: Remark 1, 2nd sentence: “we remove them form” should be “we remove them from”.
- Abstract: “fragile” might be better as “sensitive”.
