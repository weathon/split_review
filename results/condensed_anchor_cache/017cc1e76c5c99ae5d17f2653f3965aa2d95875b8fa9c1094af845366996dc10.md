## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces a Preference-Aware Multi-Objective Multi-Armed Bandit (MO-MAB) framework where user preferences are modeled as explicit vectors, and the overall reward is the inner product of the preference vector and the arm reward vector. Unlike classical Pareto-centric MO-MAB, this framework optimizes within the Pareto front based on individual preferences. The authors study three preference structures (known, unknown stationary, non-stationary with breakpoints, and hidden) and propose UCB-style algorithms (PRUCB variants) for each, providing theoretical regret bounds. The work is motivated by real-world examples and presented with clear figures.

### Strengths
- The Preference-Aware MO-MAB framework is novel and well motivated by limitations of Pareto bandits, with illustrative real-world examples.
- A systematic and comprehensive study of multiple preference settings (known, unknown stationary, non-stationary, hidden) is provided.
- Tailored algorithms are designed for each setting, and sublinear (logarithmic / instance-dependent) regret bounds are proven.
- The presentation is clear and easy to follow, with intuitive examples and visual aids that enhance understanding.

### Weaknesses
1. **Theoretical solidity of the non-stationary setting (Theorem 5)**: It is unclear how the best arm \(a_t^*\) can be identified before the preference is known when preferences change arbitrarily over time. The regret definition in Eq. (2) appears ill-posed for this scenario; the claimed regret \(O(\delta \log T + D^{1/3}T^{1/3})\) is highly suspicious—in adversarial-like settings, regret should be linear in \(T\). The argument needs rigorous justification.

2. **Lack of algorithmic novelty**: Algorithms 1–4 are essentially direct applications of standard bandit techniques (UCB, sliding window, linear regression) with preference estimation appended. Specifically:
   - Algorithm 1 (known preference) reduces to a single-objective bandit and adds no complexity.
   - Algorithm 2 (stationary unknown preference) uses standard UCB with independent reward and preference; no technical difficulty is apparent.
   - Algorithm 3 (non-stationary) uses a sliding window, a well-known adaptive method; simpler restart techniques could also be used.
   - Algorithm 4 (hidden preference) estimates the preference using linear regression (features = multiple rewards, reward = overall reward), a mature approach.
   The algorithms differ only in the preference estimation component, yet the paper claims regret bounds similar to basic scalar bandits without explaining how the additional unknown preference vector is handled without extra difficulty or tighter analysis.

3. **Preference estimation rationale**: The design of Eqs. (5), (8), and (9) for preference estimation is not adequately justified. In particular, what is \(c_{t-1}\) in Eq. (5)? The underlying reasoning for these choices is missing.

4. **Proposition 1 and comparison with preference-free baselines**:  
   - It is unclear whether Proposition 1 uses the same regret definition (Eq. 2) that accounts for preferences, given that the referenced algorithms are preference-free.  
   - The fairness of comparing preference-free algorithms under a preference-aware regret metric is questionable.  
   - Reviewer 4 notes that for conflicting objectives (e.g., arms [0,1] and [1,0]), any preference-free algorithm incurs \(\Omega(T)\) regret for at least one objective; the proof in Proposition 1 may offer no technical novelty.

5. **Missing experimental validation in the main paper**: No experiments are included in the main text. The paper should provide empirical comparisons with existing MO-MAB baselines (e.g., S-UCB, S-MOSS, Pareto-UCB, Pareto-TS) and ideally on real datasets, along with a discussion of when and why a practitioner should adopt the proposed PRUCB approach. Synthetic experiments, while helpful, are insufficient; real data would reveal practical advantages or limitations.

6. **Assumptions and generality**:  
   - The connection between the setup in Section 5 (non-stationary preferences) and contextual bandits needs to be clarified; the paper may be essentially solving a contextual bandit problem.  
   - Alternative modeling approaches (e.g., treating preferences as quality constraints, as in Sinha et al. 2021) could be more sample-efficient and deserve discussion.  
   - The paper assumes independence between reward and preference, and that the overall reward is observed as an inner product. Removing these assumptions (e.g., only observing overall reward) would significantly strengthen contributions.

7. **Presentation clarity for algorithms and regret orders**: The paper lacks a summary table or dedicated section that clearly delineates the differences among the algorithms, their pseudocode, and the precise regret orders (including prefactors). The prefactors are currently vague and hard to interpret.
