## Merged Review

### Summary
Under a controlled protocol with consistent base models (three 7B-class LLMs) and extension data, this paper evaluates eight long-context extension methods: four RoPE-adaptation techniques (e.g., NTK-Dynamic, CLEX) and four attention-approximation methods (e.g., LongLora, Landmark, streaming-LLM). Experiments use metrics like perplexity, needle-in-a-haystack (NIAH), and downstream tasks (e.g., LongBench). The study concludes that (1) perplexity remains a general-purpose performance indicator for long-context tasks; (2) approximate attention methods systematically underperform; (3) exact fine-tuning methods are effective within their extension range but struggle with extrapolation beyond the trained length (32k). Models and checkpoints are open-sourced.

### Strengths
- The topic is timely and important; the paper provides the first fair and comprehensive comparison of long-context extension methods under a controlled setting, addressing a gap where previous work used different base models and data.
- Systematic evaluation of many methods (8) with consistent base models, extension data, and a variety of metrics/tasks.
- The background section (Section 3) offers a clear, unified view of the different context-extension methodologies.
- The study open-sources codebases, models, and checkpoints to promote reproducibility and further research.
- The controlled protocol and standardized evaluation are a useful contribution to the field.

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited model diversity and context length range**  
   - Only three base models (all 7B-class) are used; results may not generalize to larger models (e.g., 13B) where prior experience shows inconsistent conclusions (Reviewer 1).  
   - Experiments are capped at 32k tokens; generalization to longer contexts (128k, 1m) is not addressed (Reviewer 3).  
   - No evaluation on more recent architectures (e.g., Qwen2.5) (Reviewer 3).  
   *Missing experiments that could strengthen or refine findings.*

2. **Insufficient evaluation of short-text performance and knowledge forgetting**  
   - The impact of extension methods on short-text capabilities is not assessed. Reviewer 1 suggests adding standard benchmarks (e.g., Open LLM Leaderboard); Reviewer 2 specifically calls for checking whether methods preserve original performance (e.g., comparing NTK by parts vs. NTK-rope on short-text tasks).  
   *Missing ablation needed to judge practical trade-offs.*

3. **Misleading classification of “approximate attention” methods**  
   - Grouping diverse approaches (streaming-LLM, LongLora, Landmark, MinInference, self-extend) under one label is too coarse and may be misleading (Reviewer 2). For example, self-extend shares more with NTK-F than with typical approximate attention, and recent MinInference-type work shows promising performance. The blanket conclusion “approximate attention is poor” may therefore be overstated (Reviewer 2).  
   *Reviewer 2 notes this threatens the robustness of the paper’s arguments.*

4. **Questionable claim about perplexity as a general-purpose indicator**  
   - The linear trend in Figure 4 appears largely driven by LongLora and Landmark; when these are removed, the correlation weakens (Reviewer 4). In the low-perplexity region (below 6), differences are small while downstream performance varies substantially. The paper should report rank correlation and consider that the claim may hold only within a certain region (Reviewer 4).  
   *Reviewers 1 and 3 accepted the claim, but Reviewer 4 strongly disputes it.*

5. **Missing full quantitative ranking**  
   - The paper identifies NTK-Dynamic as the best method but does not provide a full ranking of all eight methods, despite citing “lack of quantitative rankings” as a motivation (Reviewer 4).

6. **Hyperparameter sensitivity not explored**  
   - Hyperparameters are fixed across methods; a deeper exploration of how different settings (e.g., rotation scaling, block sizes) affect results is needed (Reviewer 3).

7. **Limited novelty**  
   - Several insights (e.g., perplexity correlation, effectiveness of fine-tuning within range, difficulty of extrapolation) have been discussed in prior work. Reviewer 3 states they “did not gain any new takeaways from it.” Reviewer 2 also notes contribution may be limited if arguments are not sufficiently robust.

8. **Presentation and organizational issues**  
   - The paper lacks visualizations to highlight main results and a summary table of key characteristics of the eight compared methods (Reviewer 4).  
   - Line 194: CLEX is mentioned without introduction; Reviewer 4 asks for a description.  
   - Line 221: “key matrix” should be “key and query matrices” (Reviewer 4).  
   - Line 505: The section title “Context extension hurts in the short term and gains in the long term” is unclear—what do “short term” and “long term” refer to? (Reviewer 4).

9. **Inference speed trade-off not analyzed**  
   - No information is provided on inference speed differences between methods (e.g., whether attention-approximation methods are faster). Reviewer 4 notes this is critical for practical decision-making.

10. **Unexplained discrepancy for LM-Infinite**  
    - The original LM-Infinite paper reports an ~80% pass rate on Passkey Retrieval (similar to NIAH), yet Figure 1 in this paper shows LM-Infinite failing most NIAH cases. Reviewer 4 asks for an explanation of this gap.

11. **Minor classification concern**  
    - Reviewer 2 observes that self-extend is more naturally classified as a RoPE-adaptation variant (like NTK-F) than as an approximate attention method, implying its inclusion under “approximate attention” may skew results.
