- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 5, 5, 1, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes "erase-and-check" (EC), a defense against adversarial prompts that add malicious tokens to bypass LLM safety guardrails. EC exhaustively erases individual tokens (or token subsequences) and checks all resulting subsequences with a safety filter (Llama 2 or DistilBERT). If any subsequence is flagged harmful, the original prompt is labeled harmful. The method considers three threat models — adversarial suffix (tokens appended), adversarial insertion (contiguous block inserted mid-prompt), and adversarial infusion (tokens at arbitrary positions) — and claims certifiable safety: a harmful prompt will not be mislabeled as safe if the number of added tokens is at most a given threshold d. Experiments measure safe-prompt accuracy and runtime for different d values, and three empirical defenses (RandEC, GreedyEC, GradEC) are also introduced.

Reviewers disagreed sharply on the paper’s contribution and soundness (scores: 5, 5, 1, 3). Some viewed EC as a novel and timely certified defense; others found the core idea to be an exhaustive search with impractical assumptions and weak evaluations.

### Strengths

- **Novel problem and approach**: The paper is among the first to study certifiable safety guarantees against adversarial prompting. The erase-and-check idea is new, intuitive, and extends certified robustness (e.g., L0 robustness, RS-Del) to the LLM safety domain. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4)
- **Consideration of multiple threat models**: The method addresses adversarial suffixes, insertions, and the more general infusion setting, with experiments across all three. This is a necessary step for generalizability. (Reviewer 2)
- **Provable detection**: The idea of providing provable guarantees (deterministic under the safety filter) against token‑addition attacks is a valuable contribution that may spur future research. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4)
- **Simplicity and clarity**: The overall writing is clear, the methodology is easy to follow, and the use of Llama‑2 as a harmfulness filter is an efficient way to check subsequences. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4)

### Weaknesses

**1. Overclaiming and unclear scope of the guarantee**  
   - The title “Certifying LLM Safety against Adversarial Prompting” is too broad. The method only defends against token‑addition attacks (appending, inserting, infusing tokens). It does not address other attack types (e.g., jailbreak via mismatched generalization, token replacement/deletion, re‑phrasing). The paper should explicitly state which attacks are covered and which are not. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4)  
   - The meaning of “certified” differs from the adversarial‑robustness literature. Here the guarantee holds only with respect to the specific safety filter (Llama‑2), not against any objective notion of harm. Since safety is subjective, the terms “certified” and “guarantee” should be used with caution. (Reviewer 2; echoed by Reviewer 3)

**2. Unrealistic assumption that the attack mode is known**  
   - EC requires prior knowledge of the threat model (suffix vs. insertion vs. infusion) and the maximum number d of added tokens. In practice an attacker can choose any mode; the certificate is attack‑specific and does not transfer. By contrast, randomized smoothing certifies against any perturbation within a norm ball. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4)  
   - Moreover, an attacker who can add a suffix can just as easily insert the same string elsewhere, rendering a suffix‑only defense ineffective. (Reviewer 4)

**3. Exhaustive search is neither novel nor practical for general attacks**  
   - The core idea is an exhaustive search over all subsequences. While deterministic certified defenses exist, the novelty is limited: for infusion, the search space is combinatorial (O(n choose d)). With a prompt of 100 tokens and d=5, this is ≈75M checks. The paper does not report the original prompt length n, making it hard to assess practicability. (Reviewer 3; also noted by Reviewer 2 on query complexity)  
   - Two reviewers found the defense straightforward given the exhaustive‑search formulation; one reviewer considered it a clear novelty. (Reviewers 1, 2 vs. Reviewer 3)

**4. False‑positive rate escalation due to multiple tests**  
   - Because the prompt is labeled harmful if *any* subsequence is flagged, the overall false‑positive rate grows rapidly with the number of checks. For a safety‑filter FPR of p and d checks, the overall FPR is 1− (1−p)^d. Even with p=0.01, d=20 yields ≈18.2% FPR. For combinatorial search this becomes impractical unless the filter has an exponentially low per‑check FPR. (Reviewer 3)  
   - The paper does not report the safety filter’s FPR or discuss how this escalation affects practical use. (Reviewers 1, 3)

**5. “Fundamental property” of subsequences is not universally true**  
   - The claim that “subsequences of safe prompts are also safe” has counterexamples (e.g., “Do not write [X]” is safe, “write [X]” is not). The property requires a precise definition of safety and does not hold for all safe prompts. (Reviewers 1, 2)  
   - Even if conceptually true, no classifier can perfectly realize it: subsequences of safe prompts are not always predicted as safe. (Reviewer 3)

**6. Missing key evaluation on harmful prompts**  
   - The paper reports only safe‑prompt accuracy in plots; the crucial metric — percentage of harmful prompts correctly labeled as harmful (certified detection rate) — is not shown. Theorem 1’s left‑hand side is not empirically evaluated. The only harmful‑prompt result is a single 93% accuracy on 500 AdvBench prompts, which largely reflects the underlying Llama‑2 performance. (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)  
   - The evaluation is therefore incomplete; AUC‑ROC, F1, and true/false positive/negative breakdowns are recommended. (Reviewers 2, 4)

**7. Insufficient experimental details and small sample sizes**  
   - For adversarial suffix/insertion experiments, the method for generating adversarial prompts is unclear: does it use the GCG attack or random suffixes? A comparison to GCG is claimed but the numbers may come from different settings. The 20‑token length used in the paper likely corresponds to characters, not the 150–200 character GCG tokens, misrepresenting the attack strength. (Reviewer 2)  
   - The infusion scenario uses only 30 samples, too small for statistically significant conclusions. (Reviewer 4)  
   - The safe prompts were generated via ChatGPT without describing the criteria; the generation process needs to be specified. (Reviewer 1)

**8. Ambiguity about “token”: inconsistency with past work**  
   - The paper uses “token” to mean character, but the GCG attack uses tokens as integers that expand to many characters. This discrepancy makes comparisons with prior work misleading and should be clarified. (Reviewer 2)

**9. Determinism of the safety filter not addressed**  
   - The certified guarantee assumes the `is‑harmful` function is deterministic. However, Llama‑2’s output depends on hyperparameters (temperature, etc.). The paper does not discuss how determinism is ensured in experiments. (Reviewer 1)

**10. Baseline comparison (Randomized Smoothing) is unfair and poorly justified**  
   - The “randomized smoothing” baseline drops a fixed set of tokens and uses majority vote, which is always weaker than EC’s max‑based approach. A more natural adaptation would randomly drop/add tokens and then apply majority vote. (Reviewer 3; also noted by Reviewer 2)

**11. No discussion of adaptive attacks**  
   - The paper does not consider adaptive attacks that target the defense itself (e.g., by exploiting the differentiable nature of the Llama‑2 filter). This is a standard requirement in the certified‑robustness literature. (Reviewer 2)

**12. Defense only handles token addition, not replacement or deletion**  
   - The method assumes the adversary only adds tokens. It cannot certify safety if the attacker replaces or deletes tokens, which is a major limitation. (Reviewer 4)

**13. Minor presentation issues**  
   - Figure 3(b) shows similar running times for d=10,20,30 despite O(d) complexity; the meaning of the plot needs clarification. (Reviewer 1)  
   - The `is‑harmful` prompt (zero‑shot, few‑shot, etc.) is not specified in the supplement. (Reviewer 1)  
   - Notation inconsistency: n is used in Section 3 but appears earlier; notation switches in Section 5. (Reviewer 2)  
   - Abstract language is harder to parse than the clearer description on page 2; the latter should replace the abstract. (Reviewer 1)
