## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes MIMOSA, a multimodal concept-based model that uses a shared concept embedding with modality-specific decoders to improve interpretability. Reviewers have mixed opinions: one (score 5) finds the approach novel and the analysis detailed; two (score 3) see value but note limited innovation and insufficient experiments; two (score 1) consider the contribution minimal and the experimental validation weak. Key disagreements concern novelty, interpretability gains, and the thoroughness of the evaluation.

### Strengths
- Extends concept bottleneck models to the multimodal domain, addressing interpretability in multimodal models (R2, R5). Reviewer 1 finds the use of a single concept embedding with individual concept decoders novel.
- Provides detailed analysis in sections on shared vs. separate embeddings, intervention efficacy, and qualitative examples (e.g., interpreting a 4 as 3) (R1). The method is intuitive and easy to understand (R2).
- Offers multi‑dimensional interpretability via image and text decoders, enabling visualization of modality‑specific concepts and providing new insight (R3, R5). Independent concept decoding is seen as a strength (R5).
- Paper is well‑structured and clearly written (R1, R2). Placing related work at the end helps maintain focus (R1).
- Demonstrates performance on benchmark datasets (MNIST+, cdSprites+, CUB) and shows improved accuracy and interpretability compared to unimodal concept methods (R1, R5). Reviewer 5 notes performance on MNIST++ (dataset name mismatch with other reviewers) and CUB.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and contribution**: The method is a straightforward application of CEM to multimodal data using sum fusion and reconstruction decoders, lacking innovative design (R2, R4). Multimodal reconstruction losses already exist (e.g., MVAE, R4) and the decoding visualization is common (R2). The paper does not clearly distinguish itself from recent label‑free CBMs (LaBO) or ResCBM (R5). One reviewer (R1) finds the approach novel, creating a split in opinion.
- **Insufficient experimental validation**:  
  – Improvements on MNIST+ and cdSprites+ are marginal (R2). On CUB200 MIMOSA does not achieve top performance (R2, R4). The datasets are small‑scale; no large‑scale multimodal dataset is tested (R2, R5). Two of the three datasets are simple and near‑perfect scores are reached by multiple methods (R4).  
  – No ablation studies are provided to isolate the contribution of individual modules (R2, R3, R4, R5).  
  – Baseline comparisons are limited: only CBM and CEM are compared, and it is unclear whether those comparisons are fair because those methods were designed for unimodal data (R2, R3). Important baselines (Post‑hoc CBM, LaBO, ECBM) are missing (R5). The “E2E” baseline is not described (R4). The “txt only” benchmark in Table 1 is not motivated (R3).  
  – Negative intervention experiments are absent (R5); intervention experiments should cover both image and text modalities (R3). Figure 3 shows a suspicious trend where task accuracy increases with stronger intervention, which one reviewer believes is an error (R3).
- **Missing implementation and training details**:  
  – The full training objective is not specified; only a decoder loss \(L_{dec}\) is mentioned, but the supervised task objective and how losses are combined are omitted (R1, R4).  
  – It is unclear which components are pre‑trained and which are trained jointly (e.g., encoders, decoders, Stable Diffusion decoder) (R1, R4). The use of Stable Diffusion as an image decoder is not explained with training details (R4).  
  – Hyperparameters (learning rate, optimizer, number of repeats, how standard deviations are obtained) are missing (R4).  
  – The definitions of “Concept Accuracy” and “Concept mismatches” in Table 2 are not provided, making the results uninterpretable (R3, R4). In Eq. (1), a summation of unimodal representations yields two outputs and the role of \(\hat{c}\) is unexplained (R4).
- **Unclear or inadequate interpretability claims**:  
  – Reconstructing inputs from the shared embedding is not equivalent to explaining decisions; the concept vectors lack any objective that guarantees semantic interpretability (R4). The multi‑dimensional interpretability is not contrasted with existing methods from the interpretability perspective (R3).  
  – The definition of “concept” is ambiguous: in MNIST+ the concepts are more like descriptions rather than well‑defined prototypes (R2). For CUB200, how text descriptions were generated or obtained is not clarified (R3, R5).  
  – The evaluation of concept accuracy is problematic because the method’s generated concept descriptions differ from the data annotations (R3).
- **Presentation and consistency issues**: Inconsistent notation (decoders called \(\Psi_i\) and \(\bar{h_i}\); \(\Psi(c)\) vs \(\Psi_i(c)\)), dataset name inconsistency (“cdSprites” vs “cdSprites+”), unclear alignment of text in Fig 4, and confusing diagram in Fig 2 (R4). Figure 1 does not align well with the text; the network \(\psi\) is not clearly located (R5). The paper would benefit from including recent references (LaBO, BotCL, ResCBM) (R5).
