## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes ATraDiff, a data augmentation method that uses a generative diffusion model trained on offline data to produce synthetic trajectories for online reinforcement learning. The method is designed to be adaptable: it handles varying trajectory lengths and mitigates distribution shifts between offline and online data. ATraDiff can be integrated with any online RL algorithm that uses a replay buffer. Empirical evaluations show performance improvements on several benchmarks, particularly in complex environments with sparse rewards. Two reviewers (R1, R3) gave high scores (6,6); two gave low scores (3,3); one gave 5. One low-scoring reviewer (R5) gave confidence 5, while the high-scoring reviewers had confidence 4.

### Strengths

- Addresses an important problem in RL: improving data efficiency with sparse rewards using generative (diffusion) models for data augmentation (R1, R4, R5).
- Novelty: using a diffusion model to generate synthetic trajectories as a data augmentation approach (R2, R4).
- Generality: ATraDiff works as a plug-in with any online RL algorithm that has a replay buffer (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Handles varying trajectory lengths and online/offline distribution shifts via a coarse-to-precise strategy and an online adaptation mechanism (R1, R4).
- Promising performance improvement across a diverse set of environments and ablations (R1, R3, R5).
- Interesting approach: fine-tuning Stable Diffusion with stacked images of projected low-dimensional states (R5).
- The approach is straightforward and can be combined with a wide family of RL methods (R2).

### Weaknesses

- **Computational cost not measured**: No data on wall-clock time or additional latency from running the diffusion model. Continual training of the diffusion model on new experiences is also absent despite being an important detail (R1, R3, R5). One reviewer (R5) explicitly asks how long online image generation takes.
- **Assumes ground-truth reward function**: The method requires access to the environment reward function to compute rewards for synthetic state-action pairs, which is impractical if reward evaluation is expensive (R2). Another reviewer (R4) asks how reward is obtained if the action is not executed, or if the reward function is assumed accessible.
- **Missing discussion of offline dataset assumptions**: The paper does not clarify what quality of offline dataset is used (medium, low, optimal) in the D4RL benchmark, which likely influences performance (R2).
- **Fair comparison in online setting**: The online RL experiments give the proposed method access to additional offline data, but baselines do not use it. Learning-from-demonstration baselines would be a fairer comparison; it is unclear whether improvements come from the extra data or the method itself (R2).
- **Incomplete description of image conversion** (R5): Converting low-dimensional states to images and back is claimed without justification. Missing: pictures of rendered states, error analysis of the conversion, details of how Stable Diffusion was fine-tuned, and how low-dimensional actions are generated or rewards obtained from the image model.
- **Missing comparison with SynthER** (R3, R4, R5): SynthER (NeurIPS 2023) is the closest transition-based diffusion augmentation method and was evaluated on the same MuJoCo locomotion environments in Figure 2, yet no comparison is made. A full comparison on both performance and computational requirements is requested.
- **Missing comparison with trajectory-based diffusion method**[3] (R5): Another method (Diffusion Model for Multi-Task RL, NeurIPS 2023) proposes trajectory generation and is not discussed.
- **Benefit of trajectories vs. transitions not justified** (R5): The paper claims full trajectories provide more comprehensive information but provides no evidence or comparison to transition-based augmentation.
- **Why project states to images?** (R5): Generating a sequence of low-dimensional states directly (as in [2,3]) is a simpler alternative; it is unclear why the image-based approach is chosen, and why the paper does not instead up-sample from visual offline datasets (e.g., from [4]).
- **Incomplete experimental description** (R5): The setup for Figure 2 is unclear (e.g., is each plot the average of 4 D4RL datasets?). The environments or datasets used in Figure 5 are not explained.
- **Vague sampling scheme description** (R5): The reward- or TD-error-based pickup strategy in Appendix A.1.1 is described extremely vaguely.
- **Plagiarism concern** (R5): The description of the state diffusion model in Appendix A.2 is copied nearly verbatim from Appendix B.2 of SynthER [1]; authors should reword and cite.
- **Figure quality** (R3): Line names and colors in figures are not intuitive.
- **Lack of analysis on generated trajectories** (R3): Would like to see analysis of the temporal coherence of generated image trajectories, e.g., whether they form coherent videos.
- **Missing ablation studies** (R3, R4): Ablation of the random dropping strategy in Section 4.3 is absent. Comparison with the state-based diffuser on more tasks is desired.
- **Reproducibility concerns** (R3): Many implementation details are missing.
- **Technical questions** (R4): In Section 4.2, is the synthetic trajectory actually $(s_L, a_L)$ instead of $(s_t, a_t)$? Does the random dropping strategy in Section 4.3 have any ablation support?
- **Missing comparison with SynthER** (repeated across reviewers).
