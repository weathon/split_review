## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a two-stage analogical prompting method for LLMs to solve linguistic puzzles from extremely low-resource languages using the modeLing dataset. Reviewers generally found the idea interesting and the experimental setting comprehensive, but raised significant concerns about limited scope (only machine translation tasks, single dataset), lack of conceptual novelty, insufficient explanation of the method and evaluation details, and missing analysis of the underlying grammar rule learning. One reviewer rated the paper very low due to poor presentation and confusing explanation, while others gave moderate scores and noted the work as a valuable benchmark.

### Strengths
- The analogical prompting approach is an innovative method for evaluating LLM linguistic reasoning on multilingual tasks, especially for extremely low-resource languages; this extends prior analogical reasoning work beyond English. (R1, R4)
- Comprehensive experimentation across multiple LLMs (GPT-4o, Llama-3.1-405B, Aya-23-35B, etc.) and prompting strategies (zero-shot, CoT, analogical), with clear presentation of results and quantified gains: up to 8.1% for GPT-4o and 5.9% for Llama-405B over CoT. (R1, R3)
- The finding that analogical exemplars help larger models but not smaller ones suggests an interesting capability difference: larger models can better adapt analogical examples to the linguistic problem. (R2)
- Highlights significance for multilingual AI and low-resource language preservation. (R1)
- There are limited existing works on solving Linguistics Olympiad problems, making this methodology valuable as a benchmark for future studies. (R3)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited scope**: The method is tested only on machine translation (Rosetta Stone) tasks from modeLing, ignoring other IOL formats (multiple-choice, cloze, morphological analysis, syntactic parsing). (R3) One reviewer (R1) also noted that only modeLing was used and suggested incorporating other benchmarks like LINGOLY or PuzzLing Machines, even if only to highlight limitations.
- **Lack of conceptual novelty**: Analogical prompting primarily augments prompts with self-generated information; the idea of leveraging related languages is well-known. The paper provides no deeper theoretical understanding of why certain languages are more effective exemplars or how specific linguistic features are shared. The contribution is primarily empirical and not very inspiring. (R3)
- **Insufficient analysis of grammar rule learning**: The claim that models deduce from inductively learned rules is unsupported. No detailed study of what grammar rules are at issue, no testing on constructed examples that violate those rules, and no demonstration that models actually learned rules. (R2)
- **Clarity and explanation issues**: One reviewer (R4, minority more negative) found Figure 1 very confusing and of poor quality; Section 2 lacks examples and formalization, confusing the reader; experiments are poorly introduced and the overall thread is not understood. Other reviewers also noted the need for more detail on the test problems (R2) and the role of rationales (R2).
- **Missing evaluation details**: The manual exact-match evaluation lacks information on number of responses reviewed, number of evaluators, inter-annotator agreement, and whether evaluation was blind to model/prompt. The granularity of the exact match metric (e.g., penalization of morphological variations) is unclear. (R1)
- **Missing ablations/analyses**: No examination of performance when language family is omitted but examples are provided (R2). No exploration of why the mixture setting (different generator and deducer) outperforms self-generation (R1). No explanation for near-zero zero-shot scores despite lenient BLEU (R3). No analysis of accuracy of language family identification and its correlation with problem-solving success (R1). No discussion of performance boundaries when languages are only distantly related or features are not easily transferable (R3).
- **Unanswered question**: One reviewer (R3) questioned whether Aya-23-35B's superior performance as generator when GPT-4o is the deducer implies it excels at language identification rather than translation; this is left unaddressed.
- **Minor**: No discussion of code release plans. (R4)
