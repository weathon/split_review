## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes CommFormer, a method that learns the communication graph among agents in multi-agent reinforcement learning using a learnable adjacency matrix with continuous relaxation and attention units. A bi-level optimization is used to update the encoder/decoder parameters and the adjacency matrix while maintaining a sparsity constraint on the communication links. Experiments on StarCraftII (SMAC) across Easy to Super Hard tasks show CommFormer significantly outperforms several baselines and is adaptable to both value-based and policy gradient (PPO) methods.

### Strengths
- Novel and well-formulated approach: modeling communication as a learnable graph with continuous relaxation and bi-level optimization (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Clear motivation (Figure 1) and well-written paper (R1, R2).
- Strong experimental results on SMAC: CommFormer outperforms most state-of-the-art baselines across a range of difficulties (R1, R2).
- Ablation studies demonstrate the importance of sparsity (R1).
- Adaptable to various actor-critic frameworks (R1).
- Solid formalization of the communication graph problem (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing recent graph-based MARL baselines in comparisons and related work.** Several reviewers note that important graph-based communication methods are not cited or compared: QGNN (R1); Pesce & Montana 2022 (R2); Seraj 2022, Niu 2021, Bettini 2023, Meneghetti 2020 (R3); Liu et al. 2022 Temporal Dynamic Weighted Graph Convolution (R4). The paper needs to include these and other SOTA graph-based distributed communication methods in both the literature review and experiments (R3, R4).
- **Limited evaluation domain.** All experiments are only on SMAC. It is unclear if the method generalizes to other multi-agent coordination tasks (e.g., social interactions, heterogeneous teams). If it does not, this should be discussed as a limitation (R3). Additional domains are needed to demonstrate generalizability (R3, R4).
- **CTDE vs. CTCE concern.** The method uses communication at test time (agents can create/destroy links), which contradicts the CTDE paradigm typically assumed in MARL. The paper should discuss whether CTCE is a more appropriate framing and compare with CTCE-based methods, especially graph-based ones (R1, R4). R4: “There are still some communications among agents for the execution of policies. It seems that CTCE is more suited for the proposed method.”
- **Agent ordering assumption.** In Section 3.2, restricting agent i to only communicate with agents j < i imposes an implicit ordering. This introduces additional design choices that need justification (R1).
- **Fixed communication graph after training.** The paper states that dynamic adjustments are out of scope, but this limits applicability to non-stationary or time-varying communication needs. Performance comparisons with dynamic graph methods would be valuable (R2). R2 also questions whether the method overfits to the communication graph and what a train/test split looks like.
- **Sparsity phrasing confusing.** The text “as task complexity increases, higher level of sparsity becomes necessary” is contradictory; the matrix should be *less* sparse (more connections) for complex tasks (R2).
- **Missing task 8m in Figure 4.** The “Sparsity” paragraph of Section 4.3 mentions 8m, but it does not appear in Figure 4 (R2).
- **FC upper bound questionable.** In Table 1, the fully connected (FC) graph is used as an upper bound, but a 100% win rate is not a practical bound since performance depends on opponents. The value 93.8% for FC is unexplained (R4). R4 asks: “How is the value 93.8% obtained in the FC column as the upper bound?”
- **Physical feasibility and environment constraints.** The learned communication graph may assign links between agents that are far apart or otherwise infeasible. A hybrid approach incorporating environmental constraints (e.g., communication range) might be more realistic (R3).
- **Attention for credit allocation not entirely novel.** The second contribution (using attention units for credit assignment to received messages) is already done by TarMAC (R3).
- **Lack of limitations discussion.** The paper does not discuss limitations (R3).
- **Runtime complexity.** With S = 0.4, the method is still quadratic in the number of agents. It may be more relevant to evaluate sparsity as the number of non-zero entries rather than a proportion (R2).
- **Figure 6 anomalies.** On some tasks, fully connected (FC) massively outperforms MAT (which allows unrestricted communication), which seems counterintuitive (R2). R2 also asks why additional environment steps fix the sparsity problem on 25m.

*Minority contrast:* The two higher-scoring reviewers (8,8) find the paper novel and well-executed, with strong results, but also note the CTDE assumption and missing QGNN baseline. The two lower-scoring reviewers (6,6) are more critical about the narrow literature review, limited domains, and lack of comparison with other graph-based methods; they also raise the feasibility and CTCE concerns more strongly. One reviewer (R3) voted weak rejection pending revisions but indicated willingness to raise the score if issues are addressed.
