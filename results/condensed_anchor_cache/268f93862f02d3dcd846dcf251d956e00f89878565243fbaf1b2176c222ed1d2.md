## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a bilevel optimization framework that integrates a neural solver (upper-level task assignment) with a problem-specific solver (lower-level routing, e.g., TSP solver) to solve combinatorial problems like the min-max CVRP. A surrogate network is trained to predict solution quality from the lower-level solver, enabling gradient-based training of the neural assignment module. Evaluation on realistic city-map-based instances shows the framework outperforms LKH-3 in solution quality and computation time. However, there is significant overlap with prior work, limited novelty, insufficient experimental comparisons, and questions about generality beyond CVRP.

### Strengths
- The idea of using bilevel optimization with a neural solver for the upper level and a problem-specific solver for the lower level is relevant and interesting (Reviewer 3, S1). The framework can outperform the pure problem-specific solver LKH-3 on min-max CVRP (Reviewer 2, S2).
- The use of a surrogate network to enable backpropagation through the non-differentiable lower-level solver is a commendable approach (Reviewer 1, Strength 1; Reviewer 4, Strength 1).
- The use of realistic city maps for generating training instances enhances practical relevance (Reviewer 1, Strength 2; Reviewer 2, S1; Reviewer 3, S2). One reviewer (R3) notes that while the random sampling of delivery points does not fully resemble real human behaviors, this effort is valued.
- Visual presentation of test results across three cities is clear with specific highlights (Reviewer 1, Strength 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and insufficient discussion of related work**:
  - Bilevel optimization for vehicle routing is not new; several works (e.g., L2D [1], GLOP [2], RBG [3]) also use a neural solver for upper-level decomposition and a problem-specific solver (like LKH-3) for lower-level subproblems. This paper lacks comparisons with and even discussion of these approaches (Reviewer 2, Weaknesses 1-2; Reviewer 4, W1).
  - The probability map prediction in this paper is similar to GLOP [2]; the surrogate network is similar to the idea in L2D [1]; LinSatNet is a direct adaptation from existing work (Reviewer 2, W2).
  - The related work section is too general, focusing on bi-level approaches rather than specifically covering problem decomposition and task assignment methods, which is the paper’s core contribution (Reviewer 1, Weakness 1).
  - A minority reviewer (R4, score 5) acknowledges the surrogate network as a strength but also states that similar two-layer optimization approaches exist (e.g., Generalize learned heuristics to solve large-scale VRPs in real-time, ICLR 2023), so the framework is underperforming in innovativeness.

- **Insufficient experimental comparisons**:
  - The method is compared only with LKH-3, not with other learning-based bilevel methods [1,2,3] or end-to-end methods like DPN [4] (Reviewer 2, W4; Reviewer 3, Q1; Reviewer 4, W2).
  - No comparison with representative decomposition methods (Reviewer 1, Weakness 2.(1)) or with using Google OR-Tools alone to highlight the framework’s additive benefit (Reviewer 1, Weakness 2.(2)).
  - The paper does not compare with other metaheuristic algorithms for CVRP, which can achieve good results within the 120-second time limit (Reviewer 3, W1, Q2).
  - The framework is only applied to one problem (min-max CVRP), limiting assessment of versatility. At least one example with a different problem scenario or different TSP solver should be provided (Reviewer 1, Weakness 2.(3); Reviewer 2, W3; Reviewer 3, W1, W3).
  - Experiments are limited to instances of size 100; performance on larger realistic instances (thousands of nodes) is not tested (Reviewer 3, Q3).
  - The comparison results are derived after applying local search on the output of each method; the extent to which this operation affects evaluation is not discussed (Reviewer 1, Questions 4: first bullet).
  - Table 3 shows that using training data from a given city does not always yield the best performance on that city's test data, raising questions about how to select training data (Reviewer 1, Questions 1).

- **Problematic or unsupported claims**:
  - The abstract and introduction claim that heuristic algorithms do not balance accuracy and computational time well, which is incorrect; metaheuristics remain a first option with quick execution and reasonable quality. The claim is not properly supported, especially since comparison is limited to LKH-3 (Reviewer 3, W1).
  - Statement that “high-quality solutions share a similar structure” when solving similar problems repeatedly is not always true; phase transitions exist even for TSP (Reviewer 3, Q5).

- **Framework limitations and unclear technical details**:
  - The framework's applicability to more constraint-laden VRP variants is unclear: new constraints often require a new lower-level solver and thus a new surrogate network, and the problem-specific solver’s efficiency may degrade in complex scenarios (Reviewer 1, Weakness 3 – first bullet). How training time and efficiency are maintained in more complex cases is not discussed (Reviewer 1, Questions 2: first bullet).
  - Generality beyond routing is not demonstrated; when applying to other scenarios, component networks likely need to be replaced, and framework stability during training is unaddressed (Reviewer 1, Questions 2: third bullet).
  - The concept of the “cost estimation network” is hard to understand: it produces a probability map for vehicle assignment, which seems more related to assignment than cost estimation (Reviewer 2, Questions 1).
  - The concept of “feature matrix” in Section 3 is unclear and lacks formal definition; it corresponds to the vehicle assignment matrix in the case study but its meaning under general combinatorial optimization is not explained (Reviewer 2, Questions 2).
  - The problem instance Z in line 292 only contains coordinates, not node demands (Reviewer 2, Questions 3).
  - In Problem 2.1, x-type variables may be redundant since they can be derived from y-type variables (Reviewer 3, Q4).
  - It is not described how the surrogate model produces solutions (one-off output or sequential decision mode) (Reviewer 1, Questions 2: second bullet).
  - Training process details are missing: are the two trainable modules trained jointly or is the lower-level pre-trained? If jointly, how is loss distributed? What is the sampling strategy for the problem-specific solver, and how is sampling sufficiency evaluated? (Reviewer 1, Questions 3).
  - When integrating different problem-specific solvers, no criteria or requirements for selection are provided (Reviewer 1, Questions 4: second bullet).

- **Presentation issues**:
  - Typo in line 307: missing blank in “…… ofthe cost estimation ……” (Reviewer 2, W5).
  - The use of module components in the framework is not specifically discussed or analyzed, affecting readability (Reviewer 4, W3).

- **Missing convergence analysis**: The paper does not provide convergence analysis or deeper investigation into how the neural approach improves bilevel optimization paradigms (Reviewer 3, W3).
