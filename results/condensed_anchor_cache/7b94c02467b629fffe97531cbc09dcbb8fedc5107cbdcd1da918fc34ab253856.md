## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers generally found the paper well-written and addressing an important scalability problem, with strong empirical efficiency and theoretical guarantees. However, concerns were raised about missing baselines (including AMIE, differentiable rule learning methods, and several structure learning approaches), overstatement of the “probabilistic” framing, definitional clarity, and lack of scalability experiments on very large knowledge graphs. One reviewer was notably more critical (score 3, confidence 4), citing insufficient comparison with prior work.

### Strengths
- The problem of scalable rule learning is highly important and well-motivated.
- SPECTRUM demonstrates strong empirical efficiency: it outperforms RNNLogic, NCRL, LSM, BOOSTR, and PRISM in computation time while achieving competitive or superior accuracy on real-world datasets (5 KG completion databases; IMDB and WebKB).
- The paper provides theoretical guarantees: completeness and approximate optimality of the pattern mining phase, and computational cost for a given error bound on utility estimates.
- The method runs on CPU yet yields comparable or better results than GPU-based methods, showing practical industry value.
- The structure is clear and easy to follow, with well-defined metrics and explicit experiments.
- The utility measure (precision, recall, rule utility) is designed to be cheap to compute, and the rule-learning complexity is linear-time (pattern mining) plus quadratic-time (ranking).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing baselines / insufficient related work:**  
  - No comparison with Poole et al. (2014) on MLN scalability, Gradient Boosted RDN (Natarajan et al. 2012), B‑RLR (Ramanan et al. 2021), NNRPT (Kaur et al. 2020), α‑ILP (Shindo et al. 2023), or AMIE3 (Galárraga et al.) – the latter is a fast rule miner using heuristic measures.  
  - No comparison with advanced differentiable rule learning methods that rely less on templates and offer better scalability, such as Glanois et al. (2022, NS‑HR).  
  - For knowledge graph completion, no comparison against NeuralLP (Yang et al. 2017) or DRUM (Sadeghian et al. 2019) on standard large KGs (UMLS, Kinship, FB15K‑237); SPECTRUM’s scalability on these datasets is not demonstrated.  
- **Overstated “probabilistic” framing:** SPECTRUM itself does not learn probabilistic rules; the emphasis in the title and throughout is somewhat misleading.  
- **Definitional clarity issues:**  
  - Notation for isomorphic rules uses \(v_i\) to denote both a node and an edge inconsistently (lines 99 vs 106).  
  - “Instantiations of a rule” (line 163) and “symmetries” (line 144) are not defined.  
  - The difference between “ground patterns” and “substitutions” in precision calculation is unclear.  
  - Definitions of precision and recall for a rule lack comparisons to existing definitions (Rocktäschel & Riedel 2017; Gao et al. 2024; Qiao et al. 2021) – their novelty and differences should be clarified.  
- **Limited rule form:** The “precision” measure only considers Horn clauses; in MLNs, more general rules (e.g., contrapositives like ‘not b → not a’) are important, and it is unclear how SPECTRUM handles such cases.  
- **Justification of greedy optimization:** After obtaining M candidate rules (typically tens to hundreds), a quadratic-time greedy optimization is used. It is unclear whether a more precise optimizer would become a bottleneck, and the rationale for this choice is not fully justified.  
- **Missing experimental details:** How the mined rules are leveraged in the link completion task (Section 7.2) is not described (e.g., whether a linear model is used).  
- **Minority reviewer criticism:** One reviewer (score 3, confidence 4) was notably more critical, particularly regarding the missing baselines and insufficient comparison with prior work.
