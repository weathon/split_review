## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes using tensor-train (TT) low-rank decomposition to compress high-dimensional point clouds, enabling out-of-distribution (OOD) detection and approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search. Training uses a Sliced Wasserstein loss and a nearest-neighbor loss. The TT representation is said to create a hierarchy of “median” representations for clustering/ANN and OOD via density comparison. One reviewer (Reviewer 2, score 6) was substantially more positive than the others (scores 3, 3, 3), praising the demonstration of TT’s usefulness and the meaningful GNO-IMI comparison. The other three reviewers raised major concerns about clarity, baselines, experimental rigor, and contribution.

### Strengths
- The paper shows that TT decomposition can achieve high compression rates for high-dimensional data (R1, R2, R3).
- Demonstrates that the compressed TT representation is usable for two applications: OOD detection and ANN search (R1, R2).
- The idea of using the TT format for efficient K-means clustering and hierarchical search is insightful (R4).
- Using the Sliced Wasserstein loss to learn the TT cores is an interesting contribution (R3, R4).
- The background on tensor trains, ANN beam search, and the training loss are well-delineated (R3).
- The TT decomposition is amenable to random sampling (R3).
- Experiments comparing against GNO-IMI yield results that track that baseline, showing the technique is not completely off (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Presentation and clarity are poor.** The paper is difficult to follow; sections feel disconnected, concepts (e.g., reshape operations, Eq. 8, the relation of TT to clustering levels) are not explained clearly, and there are multiple typos (e.g., “reveals” → “reveal”, “wide popular” → “widely popular”) (R1, R2, R4).
- **Main contribution is unclear.** The TT representation itself is well known; Section 2–3 read like a review without stating clearly what is novel. The introduction of the Sliced Wasserstein loss is abrupt—it is not justified why this loss should be preferred over conventional TT training losses, nor how it differs from existing density estimation losses (R1). The paper also alternates between presenting the method as a proof-of-concept and as immediately effective, making it hard to assess its true value (R3).
- **Baselines are weak and comparisons are unfair or incomplete.** For ANN, only GNO-IMI is compared—a weak baseline at the 10M scale; many scalable hashing methods (LSH, learned hashing, FAISS, ScaNN) exist and should be included (R1, R3). The ANN evaluation uses only a subset of 10M vectors from Deep1B, whereas the original GNO-IMI paper (Babenko & Lempitsky 2016) uses the full 1B dataset, making the comparison unfair and raising doubts about scalability (R1). For OOD, only a coreset method on MVTec is used, not SOTA, so the benefits of TT are not convincingly demonstrated (R1).
- **Missing key metrics and ablations.** Training time is not reported; without this, benchmarks are meaningless—simple k-means with 12k clusters (similar parameter count) would likely give better recall but may be more expensive, and the quality-compute tradeoff is not established (R3). Training memory and FLOPs should be stated (R3). The effect of the loss weight α (Sliced Wasserstein vs. nearest-neighbor loss) is not ablated—e.g., α=0 or α=1 (R3). Only 2nd–3rd order TTs are used; higher orders are not explored, leaving questions about potential issues or performance saturating (R4, R2).
- **Experimental results are weak.** Figures 3e and 3f show PCA projections that any naive compression method would produce—they do not provide evidence of TT’s superiority (R3). Figure 2 is confusing: the number of orange points varies across subfigures without explanation, and the meaning of colors is unclear (R3). For ANN, the paper does not compare on the number of points actually compared (empirical recall-vs-analysis), nor on index construction time (R2). It remains unclear whether TT methods can ever outperform clustering-based methods like k-means, and in which settings they would be preferable despite higher compute costs (R2).
- **Probability estimates are questionable.** In Eq. 19, a uniform probability is assumed independent of centroid distance, which does not appear correct (R2).
- **One reviewer (R2, score 6) was notably more positive**, but even that reviewer noted missed opportunities to draw deeper conclusions (e.g., longer tensor trains, comparisons beyond GNO-IMI, cost analysis). The other three reviewers (scores 3, 3, 3) found the contributions fair to poor, the soundness good but the presentation poor, and the overall evidence insufficient to support practical use.
- **Reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms directly become weaknesses:**  
  - Why is only a subset of Deep1B used for ANN (R1)?  
  - How does α=0 or α=1 perform (R3)?  
  - What happens with higher-order TTs (R4, R2)?  
  - Can TT outperform clustering methods (R2)?  
  - Are there comparisons on index construction time (R2)?  
  - The paper claims “replacing the normal point cloud with TT-compressed point cloud is straightforward” but gives no justification (R4).
