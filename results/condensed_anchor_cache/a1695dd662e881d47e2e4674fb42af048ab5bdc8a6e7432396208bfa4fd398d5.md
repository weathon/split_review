## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes that emergent abilities in large language models arise from the regularity of the optimal response function \( f^* \). The authors claim that models smooth regions with large derivatives, with smoothing decreasing as model size \( N \) increases. They provide a theorem linking \( N \) to approximation quality, and experiments on a ResNet fitting a trigonometric function and on Qwen models performing arithmetic tasks (multiplication, summation, addition word problems). Reviewer opinions are split: two reviewers (scores 5) found the theoretical framework interesting and the synthetic experiment compelling, while two reviewers (scores 3) found the derivative definition in token space problematic, the experiments limited, and the writing poor. All reviewers noted significant weaknesses in connecting the theory to LLM tasks.

### Strengths
- The theoretical framework linking regularity (Siegel & Xu, 2020) to emergence offers a fresh perspective. (R1)
- The main theorem effectively illustrates how model size relates to approximation quality in regions of complex behavior. (R1)
- The synthetic experiment with ResNet is particularly compelling. (R3)
- The perspective that parameter size controls the threshold for predicting an irregular function is interesting. (R3)
- The central claim about smoothing behavior influenced by derivative magnitude is interesting and could explain zero-shot task changes. (R2)
- The idea of relating expressiveness to approximating steeper functions is potentially interesting. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- Derivative in token space is not rigorously defined. The input domain is discrete (integer-valued tokens), not continuous; the authors need a clear definition and justification for the derivative in this setting, and to show why the embedding preserves metric structure. (R2, R4)
- The paper is very poorly written, with defective English, grammatical errors, and general sloppiness (e.g., line 424 name switch from “Grace” to “Tina”, undefined variables in line 219), making it hard to follow beyond vague ideas. (R4; also noted as presentation issues by R1 and R3)
- The theory does not provide precise thresholds or scaling rules to predict when emergence occurs, only that it should happen when the model is large enough, making it difficult to test predictive power beyond qualitative trends. (R1, R3)
- The title “Regularity Explains Emergence” is too strong; the burden of proof is high given the extensive literature on emergence (e.g., Big-Bench) and the lack of strong causal connection to natural language tasks. (R3)
- The experimental evidence is very limited and disconnected:
  - The ResNet toy model does not match the LLM setup (autoregressive transformers), and the theory’s applicability to any model type actually highlights its limitations. (R1, R2)
  - The arithmetic tasks (multiplication, summation) do not clearly connect to the theory’s focus on derivatives; the observed U-shaped trends can be explained by task structure alone, and there is no systematic investigation of the derivative or smoothed function across diverse language tasks. (R1, R2, R3)
  - The Qwen experiments use very small datasets (e.g., 128 examples?) and lack error bars, making Figures 1–4 inconclusive. (R4; also mentioned by R3 for average error noise)
  - It is unclear how Figures 1 and 2 demonstrate any emergence. (R4)
  - Lemma 3.2 (derivatives of middle digits larger) may have confounding issues (e.g., guessing parity, allowing zero as first digit). (R4)
- The contributions beyond prior work (Siegel & Xu, E et al., Wu) are not clearly articulated. (R4)
- The presentation needs improvement: figure captions lack necessary information, color schemes are inconsistent. (R1, R4)
- Questions that overlap with missing experiments or ablations:
  - Can the theory predict specific model sizes or conditions where emergent behavior happens? (R1)
  - Could the authors design an experiment with an autoregressive transformer? (R1)
  - Can the error bounds predict error rates for different model sizes? (R1)
  - Can the authors systematically evaluate the derivative and smoothed function on a more general class of language models across model sizes? (R2)
  - Why was summation of single digits chosen instead of regular \(d\)-digit addition? How would results change for simpler/harder subsets? (R3)
  - What explains the noise in average error results across model scales? Report standard errors. (R3)
  - Why is the assumption that digit embeddings preserve metric structure true? (R4)
  - What are the bars above variables starting line 282? (R4)
  - In Section 4.2 (CoT), can the authors say anything more specific beyond speculation? How exactly do derivatives multiply and reduce? (R4)
  - Why is a 2-component function needed in the first example? (R4)
- Minor writing issues (typos listed by R3) should be fixed.
