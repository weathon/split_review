## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes replacing the ReLU loss function with a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss for training Neural Control Barrier Certificates (NCBCs), aiming to achieve faster training, better scalability, and elimination of post-hoc verification via Lipschitz-based validity conditions. Reviewers acknowledge the practical direction and the use of Lipschitz continuity to prove barrier function applicability over the state space, with experiments on six benchmark systems up to 6 dimensions. However, significant concerns are raised about the novelty being incremental, poor presentation, lack of rigorous motivation for MSE over ReLU, reliance on hard-to-obtain and often loose Lipschitz bounds, scalability limitations from the epsilon-net gridding, and incomplete engagement with the extensive Lipschitz neural network literature. Reviewer 1 is more positive (score 6) about the technical soundness and practical results, while Reviewers 2 and 4 (score 3) and Reviewer 3 (score 5) highlight major theoretical and presentation gaps.

### Strengths
- Practical formulation that does not assume much structure on dynamics and control (only state-feedback), and uses a weaker definition of CBC that does not require incremental decrease in level sets.
- The MSE loss provides smoother gradients and faster convergence, enabling training with larger neural networks and handling systems up to 6 dimensions in the provided case studies.
- The approach uses Lipschitz continuity to provide a formal safety guarantee across the state space without post-hoc verification (e.g., SMT solvers), simplifying the pipeline and reducing computational overhead.
- The benchmark examples are adopted from prior work, allowing direct comparison and showing favorable results over existing methods in terms of training time and network size.
- The idea of using Lipschitz analysis to derive barrier function certificates is noted as interesting and original (though not sufficiently connected to existing Lipschitz network theory).
- Six numerical case studies are presented, adding empirical support.

### Weaknesses
- **Low novelty**: The primary contribution—replacing ReLU loss with MSE—is incremental. Several reviewers question why MSE is beneficial beyond smoothness and whether the improvement actually comes from Lipschitz control rather than the loss function itself. The paper ignores a large body of prior work on Lipschitz neural networks (e.g., Miyato et al. 2018, Fazlyab et al. 2019, Prach et al. 2022, Wang & Manchester 2023, and many others listed by Reviewer 2), which could immediately strengthen or reframe the results.
- **Poor presentation and missing details**:
  - The paper uses discrete-time control but the original CBC formulation (Prajna) is for continuous systems; this is not clarified or justified.
  - Functions ReLU(·,·) and MSE(·,·) are never defined.
  - Algorithm 1 is confusing: notation like B3? and references to losses L_i in equations (7)–(9) that do not exist.
  - Lipschitz continuity of neural networks is not defined in the self-contained description.
  - Minor typos throughout hinder assessment of correctness.
  - Attached code only includes pendulum examples, not the full set; experiment settings in Section 5.2 miss many hyperparameters.
- **Scalability concerns with gridding and Lipschitz estimation**:
  - The method requires an epsilon-net (Eq. 6) over the state space, whose point count scales exponentially with dimension. The paper does not analyze how the required number of training points grows with system dimension and Lipschitz constants of dynamics and the network.
  - Upper bounds on Lipschitz constants L_x and L_u are needed for validity; these are non-trivial to obtain unless known analytically, and the spectral normalization used is conservative. Reviewers note that these bounds are often extremely loose, especially for larger input regions, undermining the practical guarantee.
  - For high-dimensional or perception-based control (where large networks are truly needed), the epsilon-net scaling may become prohibitive, even if the method scales better with network size.
- **Weak motivation of MSE loss and missing performance objective**:
  - The MSE loss tends to push B(x) toward the decision boundary (B(x) = -η), rather than acting as a constraint like the ReLU loss. It is unclear how this affects the learned barrier landscape and whether the additional smoothness is desirable.
  - The control problem formulation trains a controller only to avoid unsafe states, without any performance objective (e.g., stability to a fixed point). Adding such an objective would compete adversarially with the MSE barrier loss, making trade-offs difficult. The setting would be more natural if a controller were already given and the barrier function is learned to certify it.
- **Verification reliance on loose Lipschitz bounds**:
  - The algorithm’s safety guarantee rests entirely on the Lipschitz bounds being correct and tight. Reviewers point out that these bounds are often loose in practice, especially when the input region is not small, and the paper does not discuss whether this looseness was observed or mitigated in the experiments.
- **Lack of ablation and physical intuition**:
  - No ablation study separates the effect of the MSE loss from the effect of Lipschitz regularization. Reviewer 2 specifically requests a baseline that uses advanced Lipschitz control (e.g., via orthogonal layers or SDP bounds) without MSE loss to attribute improvements.
  - The paper lacks physical intuition: why are inverted pendulum examples harder? Why is the pendulum not symmetric in θ and θ̇? How were network architectures chosen? For small systems where analytical barrier functions exist, how do the learned results compare? How is the margin η chosen and is it annealed during training?
- **Similarity to prior work**: The formulation and technical results are similar to Nejati 2023, beyond what is cited.
- **Minor issues**: The significance of Theorem 8 is unclear (Reviewer 4); validation relies solely on Lipschitz upper/lower bounds which are acknowledged as potentially very loose (Reviewer 4).
