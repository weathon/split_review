## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SRL, a representation learning approach that introduces a weak bisimulation metric for sparse-reward RL. It relaxes the traditional bisimulation metric by replacing the reward difference term with a trainable Gaussian distribution (to prevent representation collapse) and strengthens transition distribution differences via a discounted sum of future distances. Experiments on DMC, MetaWorld, and Adroit with sparse rewards show SRL consistently outperforming baselines.

### Strengths
- The motivation for addressing representation collapse in sparse-reward settings is compelling and clearly explained.
- The weak bisimulation metric is novel, straightforward, and could be easily adapted to other bisimulation-based algorithms.
- The paper is generally well-structured and easy to read, with a clear explanation of the problems with traditional bisimulation metrics.
- Experiments are extensive (DMControl, MetaWorld, Adroit) and SRL consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines.
- One reviewer (score 1, confidence 5) found only the empirical results encouraging, while other reviewers (scores 5 and 6) also praised the method’s novelty, motivation, and presentation.

### Weaknesses
- **Related work and baselines.**  
  - Missing discussion of prior methods addressing bisimulation in sparse rewards: intrinsic rewards with inverse dynamics (Kemertas et al. 2021), action-based bisimulation (e.g., [2]), MICo (Castro et al. 2022), RAP [5].  
  - The central representation collapse issue is incorrectly attributed to Liao et al. (2023); it was first studied by Kemertas et al. (2021) (Lemma 2 with \(c_R=1, c_T=\gamma\)), who are only mentioned at end of Sec. 4.3.  
  - Baseline selection lacks representativeness: DrQ-v2 and DrM are not bisimulation-focused; missing comparisons to MICo, RAP, and especially Kemertas et al. (2021) whose modifications of DBC (embedding normalization, intrinsic rewards, inverse dynamics) substantially improved performance in sparse rewards and would be a stronger baseline.  
  - Comparing SRL (built on DrQ-v2) with DBC is unfair because DrQ-v2 already significantly outperforms DBC.  
  - The replay buffer size of 2e5 may disadvantage baselines without justification.  

- **Clarity, errors, and ambiguous claims.**  
  - Eq. (5) and Eq. (17) incorrectly use “−” instead of “+”.  
  - Line 402 wrongly classifies *Walker* and *Quadruped* as sparse reward tasks (they are dense in [3]); clarification needed if definition differs.  
  - Definition 4.2 is not strict: it defines a deterministic map using a random variable, and the distance can be negative and non-zero for identical states.  
  - Key concepts in Theorem 3.1 (e.g., \(\mathbb{M}\), \(\pi\)-simulation, least fixed point) are not defined in paper or appendix.  
  - Vague/incorrect language throughout: “possess the superiority” (L13), “intractable reward difference” (L19) – Monte Carlo estimation is tractable, “pure distribution internally” (L23), “fundamentally” (L52), “equivalent task-relevant features” (L53), “certain favorable properties” (L75), “effective relaxation and strengthening in specific aspects” (L78), “stacking frames does not lift partial observability” (L160), “In optional \(d_\pi\)” (L216), “But seriously,...” (L240).  

- **Motivation and theoretical grounding.**  
  - Replacing the reward difference with Gaussian noise seems arbitrary and poorly motivated; it is unclear how task-relevance (which depends on reward) is preserved.  
  - The Gaussian assumption lacks strong theoretical justification in the bisimulation context; sensitivity to the variance is not explored.  
  - The meaning of \(\mu_c\) in Eq. (5) is unclear – appears to be a constant without practical significance.  
  - Assumption A.1 (sparse-reward expectation bounded by sufficiently small constant \(C_1\)) is unquantified and not in the main text; its validity needs empirical support or discussion of violations.  
  - Theorem 4.1 reasons about the sup over all states, but the logic in lines 234–235 uses it for specific state pairs, which may be overstated.  
  - The T-step unrolling of transition distribution differences seems like a hack, susceptible to compounding modeling errors and not well motivated.  

- **Experimental shortcomings.**  
  - No ablation study evaluating the impact of the Gaussian noise term (with vs. without).  
  - No experiments in environments with noisy backgrounds, nor qualitative analysis of how redundant information / noise affects representations.  
  - The method is called “Scalable” but scalability is not distinguished from prior work like MICo.  
  - The continuous differences in transition distribution may introduce instability or sensitivity to hyperparameters, not thoroughly addressed.  
  - Insufficient exploration of different noise distributions or parameterizations for the Gaussian; the choice lacks generalizability evidence.
