## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes DNALONGBENCH, a benchmark suite of five tasks that require modeling long-range DNA dependencies up to 1 million base pairs: enhancer–target gene interaction, expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) prediction, 3D genome organization (Hi-C contact maps), regulatory sequence activity (Enformer-style profiles), and transcription initiation signal. The authors evaluate five baseline methods on each task: a task-specific “expert” model, a simple CNN, and three fine-tuned DNA foundation models (HyenaDNA, Caduceus-Ph, Caduceus-PS). Across tasks, the expert models generally outperform the foundation models, and the foundation models do not consistently beat the lightweight CNN. The paper curates and provides datasets for download.

### Strengths
- **Biologically significant and well-chosen task set.** The five tasks cover important areas of regulatory genomics, chromatin organization, and variant-effect prediction (e.g., eQTLs), testing trans effects and long-range dependencies in the genome. The focus on tasks with different dimensionalities (1D profiles, 2D contact maps) and granularities (binned vs. base-pair resolution) is novel relative to existing benchmarks such as BEND and LRB. (Reviewers 1, 2)
- **Addresses a key gap in evaluating DNA language models.** The paper provides a much-needed, realistic test of whether current DNA LLMs (HyenaDNA, Caduceus) can handle biologically meaningful, long-range tasks, rather than simplistic classification. The results reveal important limitations of these models, even after fine-tuning, which is a valuable finding. (Reviewers 1, 2)
- **Clear writing and well-organized presentation.** The biological motivation and significance of each task are well explained, and results are presented concisely. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)
- **Data collection and curation effort.** The authors collected and curated data from multiple sources and provide it for download, which constitutes a useful resource for the community. (Reviewer 3)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty; tasks and datasets mostly reused from prior work.** All five tasks and corresponding datasets have been used in previous publications and benchmarks (e.g., BEND (Marin et al. 2023), LRB (Kao et al. 2024)). For example, the “Regulatory sequence activity” task directly uses Enformer’s training/validation/test sequences. The main contribution is consolidating these tasks, not introducing new data. The impact would be increased by aggregating multiple similar datasets (e.g., for enhancer–target gene interaction, combining several publicly available experimental datasets to reduce bias and increase size). (Reviewer 1)
- **Insufficient model coverage.** The benchmark tests only two DNA LLM families (HyenaDNA and Caduceus) plus a basic CNN and one expert model per task. Many other relevant architectures that can capture long-range interactions are omitted (e.g., Enformer, Borzoi, DanQ, and other transformer-based models). Without these, the paper essentially benchmarks only DNA LLMs, and it is unclear whether the observed limitations are specific to those LLMs or apply more generally. (Reviewers 2, 3)  
  *Minority viewpoint: Reviewer 1 also questioned the choice of “expert models,” suggesting Enformer or Borzoi should be used for the enhancer–target gene task instead of Activity by Contact, and noting Borzoi outperforms Enformer for eQTL prediction. (Reviewer 1)*
- **Unclear whether tasks truly require long-range context.** Several pages of the paper argue for the need to model long-range dependencies, but no evidence is provided that the existing tasks actually benefit from context beyond short windows. A CNN with limited receptive field often outperforms the long-context foundation models (e.g., RSAP Human/Avg, WB, SNSES, MS in Tables 4 and 5), undermining the premise. The paper would benefit from ablation studies (e.g., varying context lengths) or an overview of the minimal sequence spans needed for each task. (Reviewers 2, 3)
- **Limited exploration of model advantages and disadvantages.** The benchmark mainly reports performance rankings without analyzing *why* certain models succeed or fail. Missing analyses include: the effect of reverse-complement augmentation/architectures, the contribution of fine-tuning vs. task-specific pre-training, the role of model size/expressivity (e.g., comparing an Enformer-like architecture of high capacity on each task), and whether poor long-range prediction is due to context length or to difficulty focusing on specific signals in long sequences. (Reviewer 2)
- **Terminological and baseline selection issues.** (Reviewer 1)
  - The term “Expert model” is misleading; e.g., Enformer is called an “expert model” for eQTL prediction even though it was not tailored to that task. “State-of-the-art model” would be more appropriate.
  - The classification “DNA foundation model” is applied inconsistently: Avsec et al. 2021a (Enformer) is cited as a foundation model early in the paper (L146) but later treated as an expert-model baseline, despite not being pre-trained on DNA alone.
- **Codebase and documentation insufficient for a benchmark.** (Reviewer 3)
  - The code lacks thorough documentation (only a README and one dataloader notebook). No clear API is provided to easily integrate new methods (e.g., a simple `predict` wrapper). A benchmark should allow a user to evaluate their model with minimal code changes, but current integration requires adapting to DNALONGBENCH’s structure.
  - Data repositories at Harvard Dataverse lack READMEs explaining the organization of files relative to the appendix descriptions.
- **Writing and clarity issues.** (All reviewers)
  - Typos: “tetails” → “details” (L363), “complied” → “compiled” (L278), “Additional the” (L479), en-dash/hyphen errors (e.g., “enhancer-target” → “enhancer–target”), “megabase pairs” → “mega base-pairs” (L220). (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)
  - Unclear phrasing: L154–155 (“It has shown promising performance … despite the problem itself is not well defined in real applications”). (Reviewer 1)
  - In tables, the best model is highlighted but the second-best should also be marked due to the large gap to the expert model. (Reviewer 2)
  - Table 2 describes the Hi-C output as a 99681-vector, but contact maps are 2D; dimensionality should be clarified. (Reviewer 2)
  - Task ordering is inconsistent between Table 2, main text, and supplement. (Reviewer 2)
  - SNP abbreviation is introduced only later; it should be defined earlier. (Reviewer 3)
- **Missing details on data processing and splits.** (Reviewer 2)
  - Stratified sampling approach used for enhancer–target gene and eQTL prediction is not fully explained (stratification basis).
  - Potential data leakage: could the same genomic coordinate appear in multiple splits (e.g., examples from the same gene in both train and test)?
  - When masking out other enhancers in the enhancer–target gene task, what were they masked with?
  - The reasoning for removing genes with too few positive/negative pairs in enhancer–target gene and eQTL prediction is not given.
