## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes CLA-RA, an active learning framework for crowdsourcing that selects both instances to label and annotators to perform the labeling. It comprises an annotator disagreement-based instance selection mechanism, an instance-dependent annotator confidence model, and a similarity-based annotator inference method. Evaluated on 13 datasets (11 with simulated multiple annotator labels, 2 with real multiple ratings), the method shows improvement over prior multi-annotator active learning approaches in 10 of 13 datasets. Reviewer 2 is notably more positive (score 5) than the other three reviewers (scores 3, 3, 3), praising the intricate mechanisms; all others consider the paper technically sound but with significant experimental and expositional weaknesses, and one reviewer (R4) raises scope concerns for ICLR.

### Strengths
- The approach of splitting instance selection and annotator selection is sensible and intuitive (R1).
- The paper provides thorough citations (R1).
- The suite of mechanisms—knowledge repository, instance-related selection, annotator selection—is highly advantageous for implementing crowdsourced learning in practical settings (R2).
- The framework is clear, easy to follow, and technically sound (R3, R4).
- The comprehensive insights into each component of the proposed method are interesting and make the presentation solid (R3, R4).
- The experimental evaluation covers a range of standard UCI ML datasets; numerical gains are large enough that one reviewer (R4) believes they are significant despite the absence of a statistical analysis (R4).
- The overall idea of combining crowdsourcing with active learning and using majority-vote-based annotator confidence is clever and effective (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Notation and clarity issues (R3):** Notations are not clearly defined; the usage is confusing. For example, features of an instance are too similar to the instance notation; the last $x$ in Section 3.1 is misused; the condition in Section 3.5 is mathematically incorrect (index $j$ compared to vector $z$). The lack of distinction between an instance $x$ and its feature representation makes mathematical formulations difficult to follow.
- **Experimental section overly concise and missing critical details:**
  - The simulation of multiple ratings is only given a passing reference; the authors should explain how they generated the multiple ratings in addition to referencing the paper (R1).
  - Dataset characteristics (class distributions, feature types, number of instances, etc.) are not provided, making it hard to assess generalizability (R3, R4).
  - The number of annotators used is too small; this may not reflect real-world scenarios and could artificially benefit the proposed method, which relies on modeling annotator behavior (R3).
  - In most figures, the Y‑axis does not start at 0, inflating the visual improvements (R1).
  - For the two non‑simulated datasets, the proposed framework barely surpasses past approaches (R1).
  - Figures 5 and 6 (or 5 and 7) lack any comparative baseline (e.g., random annotator/instance selection), so the claimed efficiency of the example selection strategy is not convincingly demonstrated (R1, R2).
- **Missing ablation studies and parameter analysis:**
  - No ablation study isolates the contribution of the three key components (instance disagreement selection, annotator confidence model, annotator inference method). It remains unclear which step is pivotal (R2).
  - Essential parameter analyses are absent: exploration threshold $\epsilon$, confidence threshold $\eta$, similarity threshold, ratio of training data, annotation budget, etc. No explanation is given for the specific values used, and no validation set or cross‑validation approach is described (R2, R3).
  - There is no mechanism to guarantee that the annotator model $f_{\theta}$ converges to a good solution; limited training data or biased annotator responses could lead to suboptimal performance (R3).
- **Lack of statistical significance testing:** The paper reports numerical gains but does not perform any statistical significance analysis (R1, R4). One reviewer (R4) believes the gains are large enough to be significant, but this is not formally established.
- **Evaluation measure:** The paper uses accuracy; for imbalanced datasets, macro F1 would be more appropriate, but no justification is given (R4).
- **Missing comparisons:** The experimental setup does not include comparisons against strong baselines that utilize similar active learning strategies with multiple annotators (R2).
- **Scope and impact concerns (R4):** ICLR focuses on representation/deep learning, but this paper is core ML without covering those topics. Active learning with crowdsourcing is a somewhat saturated field; the approach, though clever, may not be of substantial interest to the ICLR audience unless linked to recent deep learning / representation learning applications.
- **Assumption about majority being correct:** The method relies on the majority being correct for building annotator confidences; it is unclear if it would work in cases where the majority is incorrect (R1).
- **Hyper‑parameter sensitivity and time complexity:** No analysis of time complexity when the number of workers increases; hyper‑parameter values are not justified, raising robustness concerns (R3).
- **Why not work as well on some datasets:** The method underperforms CEAL on Mushroom and Vehicle; no explanation is provided (R1).
- **Rationale for weighted majority:** The paper uses a conventional weighted majority algorithm for confidence computation without justifying why over more recent methods (R2).
