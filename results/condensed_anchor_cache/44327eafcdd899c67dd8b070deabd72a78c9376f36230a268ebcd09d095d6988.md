## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates how faithfully human proxies (preference data and reward models) reflect true human preferences in LLM alignment. It categorizes proxies into Level-1 (direct preference data) and Level-2 (reward models), re-labels the HH-RLHF dataset to create CHH-RLHF, and evaluates the impact of cleaner proxies on DPO and PPO training. Reviewers acknowledge the re-labeled dataset as a practical contribution and note the novelty of the two-level framework and the high opposite re-label rates. However, they question whether the findings extend beyond the obvious conclusion that better data and reward models yield better alignment, criticize the lack of labeling-process details, unclear experimental design choices, missing comparisons, and inconsistencies between claims and empirical results (e.g., Starling‑34B’s strong performance). The discussion of known issues (Goodhart’s Law, reward over‑optimization) is seen as not novel. Reviewer 3 is more positive (score 6) than the other three (score 3 each), praising the experimental design and correlation analysis but also noting the unclear narrative and framing gaps.

### Strengths
- Novel two‑level classification of human proxies (direct preference data vs. reward models) that provides a clear framework for analysis. (Reviewer 2)
- High opposite re‑label rates discovered in HH‑RLHF (e.g., three out of four annotators chose the originally rejected response as better) critically highlight dataset reliability issues. (Reviewer 3)
- Systematic correlation experiments between human and model evaluations are particularly valuable. (Reviewer 3)
- The re‑labeled dataset (CHH‑RLHF) is a concrete community resource that can improve preference‑learning reliability. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4)
- Exhaustive set of empirical experiments across DPO, PPO, and PRO, and evaluation of a wide range of reward models. (Reviewer 4)
- Raises awareness that practitioners systematically under‑account for annotation noise and over‑rely on proxies without verification. (Reviewer 1, 2)

### Weaknesses
- The main scientific insight reduces to “cleaner data → better models” and “better reward models → better alignment”, which is expected and already known; the paper offers little new conceptual understanding beyond the re‑labeling effort. (Reviewers 1, 4)
- The labeling process is not described: number of annotators, annotation protocol, agreement metrics, or scalability to other domains. Without this, the pitfalls taxonomy and re‑labeling cannot be assessed for generalizability. (Reviewer 1)
- Several analyses lack clarity or completeness:
  - PPO experimental settings are unclear; it is not stated whether reward models trained on CHH‑RLHF were compared against RMs trained on original HH‑RLHF for downstream performance. (Reviewer 3)
  - The choice of specific models per experiment is not justified (e.g., why use Starling‑7B/6.9B when Starling‑34B outperforms in Figure 4 and Table 4? Why include GPT‑J?). (Reviewers 2, 4)
  - Correlation scores are missing from Figure 4; basic dataset statistics (size, original annotation process) are omitted. (Reviewer 4)
- The claim that current reward models are inadequate is contradicted by the paper’s own results: Starling‑34B shows high reward‑model accuracy, good correlation with humans, and leads to better aligned models—consistent with the trivial finding that better RMs improve alignment. (Reviewer 4)
- Table 4 includes DPO as a reward model, which is not reasonable (prior work shows policy models learn different representations than reward models). Removing DPO entries would weaken the claim that many RMs perform worse than 50 % accuracy. (Reviewer 4)
- Discussion (Section 5) on Goodhart’s Law and reward over‑optimization is well‑known and does not add new insights; it reads more like a related‑work summary. (Reviewers 1, 2)
- The paper’s framing fails to adequately situate itself among existing critical investigations of reward over‑optimization and proxy faithfulness, making the overall narrative unclear. (Reviewer 3)
- The paper does not meet the standard for ICLR; it feels better suited to a workshop or Findings venue. (Reviewer 2, implied by Reviewer 1)
