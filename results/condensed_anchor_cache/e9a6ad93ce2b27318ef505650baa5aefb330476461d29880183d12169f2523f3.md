## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes MEAD, a model-based algorithm that uses an object-centric abstraction (Ab-MDP) where abstract states are sets of items and attributes, and actions are behaviours (low-level policies that perturb attributes). MEAD learns a discriminative world model predicting the success probability of behaviours, and plans with count-based intrinsic reward, MCTS for exploration, and Dijkstra for goal reaching. Experiments on 2D crafting and MiniHack environments show MEAD significantly outperforms low-level baselines (e.g., IMPALA, RND, ICM) and model-based/free methods using the same abstraction (Dreamer-v3, PPO, MuZero). All reviewers praise the clear writing, strong empirical results, extensive ablations, and the study of transfer. However, they raise serious concerns about strong assumptions (pre-given object mapping and competent low-level policies), limited applicability to dynamic/continuous environments, and lack of clarity in baseline descriptions and experimental details (seeds, error bars, legend readability). Minorities (scores 8) are more positive about efficiency and novelty, while others (scores 6) emphasize the manual feature engineering and restricted scope.

### Strengths

- Clear presentation with good intuition, motivation, and helpful illustrations. Related work is thorough. (All reviewers)
- Very efficient method for solving high-level problems of the Ab-MDP structure; significantly outperforms low-level methods (IMPALA, RND, ICM) and model-based/free methods using the same abstraction (Dreamer-v3, PPO, MuZero). (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5)
- Novel combination of object-centric abstraction, discriminative world model, count-based intrinsic reward, and planning (MCTS + Dijkstra) for efficient exploration and world model learning. (R3)
- Comprehensive analysis with numerous ablations (R1, R4, R5), detailed appendix (R1), and study of zero-shot and few-shot transfer across item types and environments (R1, R2, R3).
- Interpretable world model due to object-centric abstraction. (R3)
- Contributions listed and addressed. (R2)
- Learning from complex abstract behaviours is an important problem; results show problem definition can be as important as underlying method. (R5)

### Weaknesses

- **Strong assumptions on abstraction and low-level policies:** The approach relies on a pre-defined object-centric mapping (item-id, attributes) and competent object-perturbing behaviours. This is not discovered, and the paper does not adequately discuss the practical challenges and overhead of acquiring these in complex/real-world scenarios. (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5) Specifically:
    - Learning the mapping via supervised learning is only briefly mentioned; collecting diverse (S,X) pairs and sensitivity to mapping quality are not explored. (R3)
    - Pre-trained low-level policies can be as hard to obtain as solving the original task; the number of required policies (N*m) can become prohibitive. (R4) The paper does not report how many were learned, what fraction were competent, or cumulative training steps vs. world-model training. (R4, Q4)
    - The abstraction is manually engineered for the tested environments; a general analysis of what constitutes a good abstraction level for an Ab-MDP is missing, risking overfitting to specific domains. (R5)
- **Limited applicability to dynamic/continuous settings:** The method assumes usually only one element changes (or none); replanning helps only partially. Environments needing object/agent position modelling on the high level would not work. (R1) Efficiency drops with high-frequency changing attributes (e.g., positions) because abstract state space grows. (R1) Attributes are required to be discrete, which limits use in robotics with continuous position, speed, orientation. (R4) At inference, Dijkstra planning is feasible for static environments, less for procedural ones where object positions change. (R5) The paper should illustrate how performance degrades as the forward model is trained (e.g., overfitting to environment). (R5)
- **Clarity and experimental details insufficient:**
    - Exploration in Section 4.1: not explained whether count-based exploration is used there; task return appears to be from Dijkstra planning. (R1, R1 Q1)
    - Number of seeds per experiment not explicitly stated (appendix hints at ≥3). (R2)
    - Figure 4b: overlapping lines of IMPALA, RND, ICM make them indistinguishable. (R2)
    - Figure 5: black lines have no error bars; possibly single-seed runs; this should be stated. (R2)
    - Figure 5a: blue line starts high; need explanation. (R2)
    - Baseline descriptions in main paper are superficial; lack of 2-sentence summaries or a table showing differences (access to behaviours, world model type, inference strategy). (R4) This makes it difficult to attribute performance gains. (R4, R4 Q1, Q2)
    - Unclear if PPO and Dreamer-v3 are applied to Ab-MDP or low-level MDP. Caption in Figure 4 mentions also low-level exploration methods, causing confusion. (R3) Comparison with Dreamer-v3 may be unfair because Dreamer’s latent space is tailored to continuous states, not discrete abstract states. (R5) For transfer experiments, it is unclear if Dreamer/MuZero ran enough dream updates; if policy is reset, more updates are needed before interacting. (R1)
    - No comparison with methods closer to MEAD (e.g., from Related Work) in the same abstract MDP setting. (R3, R3 Q3)
- **Distinction between Ab-MDP and Options:** The Ab-MDP should not be equated to options; it is a higher-level planning space where behaviours (not the Ab-MDP itself) can be seen as options. Current framing risks conflating these concepts. (R2)
- **Missing justification for using Dijkstra over value iteration.** Dijkstra assumes deterministic transitions; value iteration could handle stochastic outcomes. (R1 Q2)
- **World model training details missing from main paper:** Architecture and loss should be mentioned. (R1)
- **Transfer distribution shift:** Why MEAD does not suffer distribution shift while Dreamer does? Possibly due to discriminative model or other assumptions – needs explanation. (R4 Q3)
- **Typos and proofreading needed:** Inconsistent wording (abstract: “reinforce learn” and “supervise learn”), l086, l143, l196, l251 (95% confidence interval), l270 (missing details), l313-314, l331-332, l404-405, l316 (“observe”), l325 (“representatin”), l331 (“themin”). (R1, R2, R3)
