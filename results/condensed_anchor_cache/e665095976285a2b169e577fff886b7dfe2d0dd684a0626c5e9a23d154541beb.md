## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces Task-Adaptation Curriculum Learning (TaCL), a method to bridge the gap between a pre-trained model and a low-resource target task by fine-tuning on a sequence of intermediate tasks selected via a graph search formulation. Two search algorithms, greedy best-first search (GBFS) and Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS), are used to estimate transferability and select task sequences. Experiments on two NLP benchmarks (20-task and 6-task graphs) show TaCL outperforms naive fine-tuning and random task ordering. Reviewers are split: two (score 5) find the approach effective and the graph-search perspective novel, while three (score 3) raise concerns about limited novelty, sparse baselines, computational cost, and missing experimental details.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5).
- The motivation is sound: addressing distribution gap and overfitting in low-resource target tasks via intermediate tasks (R1, R2, R5).
- Framing the task curriculum as a graph search problem provides a fresh perspective for transfer learning (R1, R5).
- Experiments demonstrate that the proposed task sequence selection methods (GBFS, MCTS) improve performance over full fine-tuning and random order (R1, R3, R4).
- The authors emphasize computational cost and propose efficiency improvements (e.g., limiting training steps) (R1, R4).
- Target task performance of TaCL is better than baselines (R3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty and reliance on existing algorithms** (R1, R2, R5).  
   - The contribution is vague: using two classic search algorithms (GBFS, MCTS) makes the methodological novelty limited (R2, R5).  
   - A deeper analysis of how these algorithms are adapted or modified for the task curriculum problem is missing (R5).  
   - The work closely resembles existing methods such as "Don't Stop Pretraining" (Gururangan et al.) and should be compared explicitly in methodology and experiments (R5).

2. **Sparse and insufficient baselines** (R1, R2, R4).  
   - Missing comparisons with advanced curriculum learning and transfer learning methods, including anti-curriculum learning (e.g., [1707.09533], [1811.00739]) (R1).  
   - No comparison with augmentation-based methods or generative models (e.g., LLM-based data generation from arXiv:2403.02990) to address data scarcity (R1).  
   - Parameter-efficient fine-tuning like LoRA is not considered, even though it could reduce overfitting by limiting updated parameters (R1).  
   - No comparison with joint multi-task learning with adaptive weights, a simpler and more straightforward alternative (R4).  
   - Baseline task embedding methods are included but perform worse; more explanation about how they select tasks (single task vs. sequence) is needed (R4).  
   - Evaluation domains are limited to NLP; performance in CV or robotics is not shown (R2).

3. **Computational burden and lack of quantitative analysis** (R4, R5).  
   - The search algorithms require training on several extra tasks; the paper lacks a quantitative comparison of training time, steps, and sample counts between full fine-tuning, GBFS, and MCTS (R4, R5).  
   - The trade-off between performance gains and computational cost is not discussed; return on investment is unclear (R5).

4. **Requirements for a predetermined task graph** (R1, R2).  
   - The method requires a precomputed graph of tasks; it is unclear how the 6-task graph was constructed (brute-force?), and the 20-task graph uses results from prior studies (R1).  
   - In many real-world domains, such a task graph may not be available, and the paper does not address how to construct one from scratch (R1).

5. **Missing implementation details and analysis** (R1, R2, R3).  
   - Important details about the method are missing, making full understanding difficult (R2).  
   - In Equation 6, what does Q(v’) represent? Are intermediate tasks generated or pre-designed? (R2).  
   - The analysis of the six-task graph (Figures 5 and 6) focuses only on the importance of MNLI, neglecting the role of other tasks and their interactions. A more granular analysis of which task pairings consistently help or hinder performance is needed (R3).  
   - The heuristics (validation loss/accuracy) may be unreliable when the validation set is itself limited in a low-resource setting (R1).  
   - The impact of reducing training steps for transferability estimation is not examined; phenomena like grokking [2201.02177] and double descent could affect scores (R1).

6. **Missing connections to related fields** (R1, R3).  
   - The work is strongly related to meta-learning (e.g., [1911.10600] uses meta-learning to reveal task relationships up to 400 tasks) and should discuss similarities and differences, especially the lack of a meta-test update in TaCL (R1).  
   - Connections to continual learning (e.g., [2205.13323] on task ordering and curriculum) are missing and would strengthen the paper (R1).  
   - The work of Taskonomy (CVPR 2018) on discovering task graphs in computer vision is highly relevant and should be cited (R1).  
   - The related work section cites mostly older papers; more recent work should be included (R3).

7. **Forgetting and safety risks** (R3).  
   - Fine-tuning on multiple tasks may exacerbate safety issues (e.g., compromising model alignment) compared to standard fine-tuning; the paper does not discuss or experiment with this (R3).

8. **Sequential vs. parallel and alternative training strategies** (R4, R5).  
   - The paper only explores sequential fine-tuning on one task at a time. Alternative strategies like parallel training (e.g., fine-tuning on groups of tasks together) or joint fine-tuning on all auxiliary tasks before the target are not considered or compared (R4, R5).  
   - A parallel strategy might alleviate forgetting better; the paper lacks discussion of its potential benefits and drawbacks (R5).
