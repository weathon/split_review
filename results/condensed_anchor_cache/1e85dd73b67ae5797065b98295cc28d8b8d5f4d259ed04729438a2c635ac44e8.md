## Merged Review

### Summary

Reviewers overall find the paper’s topic (extending the Information Bottleneck to causal settings) timely and interesting, but they strongly disagree on the depth of the contributions. One reviewer (score 8) considers the work original, well-written, and with a clear formal exposition, despite simple examples. The other three reviewers (scores 5, 5, 6) agree that the paper addresses a good question but judge the theoretical and empirical contributions as very limited, the experiments as too simplistic, the assumptions too strong, and the lack of comparisons to existing causal representation learning methods a major shortcoming. Even the reviewer who leaned toward weak accept (score 6, rebutted) notes that the strong assumptions and small-scale experiments limit practical applicability.

### Strengths

*   **Well-written and well-structured** (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). The paper provides a clear formal description, defines concepts like causal entropy and causal information gain, and places the CIB naturally as an extension of the standard IB. References and appendix are adequate.
*   **Original and timely problem** (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). Extending IB to a causal setting in a principled manner is a novel task; the topic of causal abstractions is important and timely.
*   **Sound formulation and definitions** (Reviewer 4). The CIB Lagrangian follows standard conventions, the backdoor adjustment formula for the representation is soundly defined (allowing identification given a known adjustment set), and the trade-off between predictive performance, compression, and retained causal information is clearly formalized.
*   **Demonstrated continuous transition** (Reviewer 4). The experiments successfully show a continuous transition between retained causal mutual information and predictive performance under varying regularization.
*   **Impressive results on simple domains** (Reviewers 1, 3). The illustrative examples, though simple, convincingly showcase the conceptual workings of the method.

### Weaknesses

*   **Limited experimental scope and strong assumptions** (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4). All experiments are on toy/synthetic problems with small variable sets and full knowledge of the causal graph; the method assumes all variables are discrete and the joint distribution is known. There is no evaluation on larger or higher-dimensional domains (e.g., synthetic images), no robustness or noise ablations, and no study of the effects of incomplete or incorrect causal graph knowledge. The backdoor adjustment requires the adjustment set \(Z\) to be known and observed, yet the motivational example involved selection bias on \(Z\) – the realism of this scenario is questionable, and missing/partial confounders are not addressed.
*   **Missing baseline comparisons** (Reviewers 2, 4). The paper does not compare against VAE, \(\beta\)-VAE, Graph-VAE, or any existing causal representation learning methods such as those of Subramanian et al. (2022), Liu et al. (2022), nor against tau-abstraction (e.g., [1,2]) or identifiability results for latent causal models (e.g., [3,4]). A downstream task comparison (e.g., intervention prediction) to validate the causal benefit is also absent.
*   **Unclear assumptions for causal discovery and trivial propositions** (Reviewer 2). No statement is made about standard assumptions like faithfulness; Assumption 8 is introduced but seems to simplify the problem considerably. Several propositions (notably Proposition 6) follow directly from definitions and add little theoretical value.
*   **Interpretability claim not justified** (Reviewer 3). The paper claims representations are causally interpretable, but the experiments do not provide any direct evidence of interpretability (e.g., visualization or human evaluation).
*   **Unaddressed design choices** (Reviewers 3, 4). The Lagrange multiplier \(\beta\) is fixed rather than learned; the interventional decoder uses a uniform prior, which is a generic choice – more complex priors would be more interesting. The intervention decoder (eq. 4) is unclear: the terms \(p^*\) and \(q\) are not explicitly conditioned on any intervention, making it hard to see how interventions actually enter the loss.
*   **Scalability and failure conditions not discussed** (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). The authors do not comment on the scalability of the algorithm, how often the conditions (a),(b),(c) need to be checked, or when the method is expected to fail.
*   **Minor issues** (Reviewers 1, 4). The term “representation” is defined only on page 6 but used earlier without reference; the statement that “atomic interventions are the most common” overstates typical practice; Figure 1 is hard to read due to small text/line widths; the phrase “conserve space” (line 62) is unclear.
