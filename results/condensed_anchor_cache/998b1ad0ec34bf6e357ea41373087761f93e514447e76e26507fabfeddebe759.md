## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes “DeepInception,” a black-box jailbreak method that uses nested prompts to create multi-layered fictional scenarios, exploiting LLMs’ personification and instruction-following capabilities to bypass safety guardrails. The approach is inspired by the Milgram experiment on authority influence. Experiments show high harmfulness rates across open- and closed-source LLMs (Llama-2, Llama-3, GPT-3.5, GPT-4, GPT-4o) as well as multimodal models (GPT-4V), and demonstrate a continuous jailbreak effect that persists in subsequent interactions. The method is compared against baselines such as GCG and PAIR and tested against defense strategies (self-reminder, in-context defense).

### Strengths
- The method is innovative and effectively bypasses ethical restrictions in a black-box environment; it achieves leading harmfulness rates compared to existing jailbreaks (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 4).
- Evaluation is impressively thorough: tests cover state-of-the-art open- and closed-source models, multimodal LLMs (GPT-4V), and recent model o1. Attack effectiveness is also validated against defense methods like self-reminder and in-context defense (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4).
- The paper draws an analogy with human psychological experiments (Milgram) and provides a theory that LLMs can be “hypnotized” into a self-loss state, increasing instruction-following behavior. This psychological framing offers a new perspective and some interpretability (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3). *Note: Several reviewers question the accuracy and relevance of this connection — see Weaknesses.*
- The method induces a continuous jailbreak state, where the model remains compromised in later interactions (Reviewer 2).
- Reproducibility is supported: source code and the specific prompt template are shared (Reviewer 4).
- An interesting multimodal application is presented, extracting identities/locations from photos (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited technical novelty:** The core idea of nested/role-playing prompts is similar to existing work (e.g., Ding et al. 2024, “Visual Adversarial Examples” jailbreak) and previously known virtualization strategies. The paper offers a specific template rather than a general new attack class, so its contribution to understanding LLM vulnerabilities is questioned (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4). *Minority contrast: Reviewer 1 considers it innovative.*
- **Misinformed and tenuous link to the Milgram experiment:** Several reviewers point out critical disconnects. The experiment involved direct, escalating commands (“You have no other choice, you must go on”), not indirect persuasion as claimed. The authority figure mechanism is not incorporated into the method; instead the method “tricks” the model through fiction, which is the opposite of the experiment’s clear awareness of harm. This framing may mislead readers and does not align with the actual method (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4). Reviewer 3 provides a detailed criticism of the claimed analogies.
- **Inaccurate statement about GCG:** The paper claims GCG is not black-box applicable, but GCG can be adapted for black-box settings by optimizing on open-source surrogate models (Reviewer 2).
- **Unreliable model-based evaluation:** Harmfulness ratings rely on automated model-based scoring, which can produce false positives. A human study to validate whether outputs are genuinely harmful, and whether DeepInception responses are more harmful than baselines, is needed (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing variation in prompt templates:** Only one specific nested prompt structure is tested. Given the success, exploring variations (e.g., different nesting depths, scene types) would strengthen the work. This also limits insight into which aspects of nesting matter (Reviewer 4, also implied by Reviewer 3).
- **Clarity and presentation issues:** The appendix is overly detailed; the “Multi-Modal Attack” and “Chat Histories” sections should be moved to supplementary material (Reviewer 1). Figure 7 appears before Figure 6 (Reviewer 2). The title uses “Gloss” without explanation, which obscures the paper’s content (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing clarification on evaluation metric conversion:** The appendix defines a five-category harmfulness rating but does not explain how these categories are converted into the harmfulness rates/scores reported in experiments (Reviewer 1).
- **Perplexity analysis questioned:** The paper interprets lower PPL as favorable, but lower perplexity does not necessarily indicate a better jailbreak; it may reflect length differences or model confidence unrelated to harmfulness. A response is harmful regardless of its perplexity (Reviewer 2).
- **Counterintuitive defense result:** In Table 3, self-reminder actually *helps* the attack on GPT-3.5, increasing the harmfulness score. This unexpected result is not explained (Reviewer 3).
- **Unclear formalization of “jointly inducing” property:** The concept appears to simply formalize what other jailbreak methods implicitly do (e.g., forcing the model to start with “Sure, here is how”) and does not add new insight (Reviewer 3).
