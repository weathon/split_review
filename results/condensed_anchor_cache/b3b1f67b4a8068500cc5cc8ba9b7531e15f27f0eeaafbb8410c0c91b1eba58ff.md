## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Summarize and Score (SASC), a method that generates natural language explanations for black‑box text modules (functions mapping text to scalar values). SASC works in three steps: probing a corpus for high‑scoring n‑grams, prompting an LLM to produce candidate explanations from a subset of those n‑grams, and scoring each explanation by comparing the module’s response to LLM‑generated positive and negative phrases. The method is evaluated on synthetic modules (where ground truth is known), on modules within a pre‑trained BERT model (dictionary‑learned transformer factors), and on fMRI voxel responses to natural language stimuli. The authors report that SASC often recovers ground‑truth explanations, enables inspection of BERT internals, and allows fine‑grained brain mapping. Reviews are mixed: one reviewer (score 8) finds the method novel, theoretically grounded, and promising; two reviewers (scores 5 and 6) raise significant concerns about evaluation rigor, missing baselines, the scoring step, and clarity.

### Strengths
- SASC addresses a timely and important problem: interpreting black‑box text processing modules, especially LLMs.
- The method is novel and does not require differentiability or access to model internals; it uses a separate LLM (GPT‑3) as an explanation generator.
- The corpus‑based n‑gram scoring approach is more sound than token‑wise scoring (e.g., as in the OpenAI neuron‑explanation paper).
- Weak assumptions are used; SASC can be applied even in noisy settings (e.g., fMRI data).
- Three diverse evaluation contexts are explored: synthetic modules (ground‑truth target via INSTRUCTOR‑XL embeddings), BERT transformer factors with human baselines, and fMRI voxel responses on a passive listening task.
- The results on synthetic data show that SASC often recovers ground‑truth explanations; the BERT analysis provides interpretable insights; the fMRI experiments illustrate potential for fine‑grained brain mapping.
- One reviewer highlighted that the experiments reveal fascinating patterns and that SASC could be a useful tool in social science, medicine, and neuroscience.

### Weaknesses
- **No comparison to existing interpretability methods**: SASC is not compared to state‑of‑the‑art explanation techniques such as LIME or SHAP, making it hard to gauge relative performance.
- **Scoring step confound**: The explanation score uses \(E[f(\text{Text}^+)-f(\text{Text}^-)]\), which conflates the quality of positive examples with the quality of the LLM‑generated negative examples. Using \(E[f(\text{Text}^+)-f(\text{Text}^{\text{all}})]\) would be more appropriate. This is particularly problematic because the flawed score is used as a key comparison metric in Table 4, Figure 3, Table 6, the fMRI experiments, Table A4, and Table A8.
- **Lack of ablations and complexity analysis**:
  - No ablation study on corpus size (e.g., 10k, 20k, 40k, 80k n‑grams) to show its effect on explanation scores.
  - The “Default” synthetic module experiment (54 modules) uses a corpus known to contain the relevant n‑grams; the evaluation should use the same random n‑gram selection as the other settings.
  - The synthetic scoring design is heuristic with no theoretical justification or ablation study to validate its components.
  - Computational complexity and efficiency of SASC (e.g., scaling with corpus size) are not discussed.
- **Evaluation reliability**:
  - The paper relies on manual inspection by the authors to assess explanation correctness, with no controlled human study (e.g., via Amazon Mechanical Turk or Prolific). This introduces potential bias; an automated evaluation using a different embedding model (e.g., bge‑large) and cosine similarity could be less subjective.
  - The criteria for determining explanation correctness are unclear. For instance, in Table 3, why is ‘facts’ considered equivalent to ‘information or knowledge’, while ‘language’ is considered unequal to ‘ungrammatical’? No formal procedure is given.
- **Missing details**:
  - The n‑gram summarization baseline used in Table 1 is not properly described; the cited references (Kadar et al., Na et al.) do not perform n‑gram summarization (one uses omission, the other parsing+replication).
  - The method for computing the Spearman rank correlation standard error of the mean (SEM) is not specified (e.g., bootstrapping, t‑statistic). This is needed given the low correlation values.
  - For the BERT experiments, the “transformer factors” are not clearly defined, the ground truth for them is not stated, and the computation of the SASC win percentage (61%) is not explained.
- **Model choice and generalizability**:
  - The choice of GPT‑3 as the helper LLM is not justified; using a more advanced model (e.g., GPT‑3.5) could change results.
  - SASC appears to capture only semantic selectivity; its applicability to low‑level text patterns (e.g., syntactic or orthographic) is unclear, and this limitation is not adequately discussed.
- **Unexplained trends and coverage**:
  - Figure 3 shows that explanation scores decrease as BERT layer depth increases; no explanation or analysis is provided for this trend.
  - The method explains only the top module responses in isolation, not the relationships between modules (e.g., how different modules interact or compose).
- **Potential overestimation on synthetic data**: The synthetic experiment’s “Default” setting uses a corpus that is guaranteed to contain the correct n‑grams, likely inflating performance.
