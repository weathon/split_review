## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents RankNovo, the first deep reranking framework for de novo peptide sequencing. It uses a list-wise approach that models candidate peptides from multiple base models as multiple sequence alignments and applies axial attention. Two new metrics, PMD (Peptide Mass Deviation) and RMD (Residual Mass Deviation), are introduced to quantify mass differences at the sequence and residue levels. Experiments show RankNovo surpasses its base models and achieves state-of-the-art on 9-species-V1 and V2 benchmarks, with zero-shot generalization to unseen models. Reviewers found the motivation meaningful and the metrics novel, but raised concerns about modest improvement, computational cost, unclear mathematical notation, limited novelty of the core algorithm, and insufficient discussion of limitations and future work.

### Strengths
- The paper is written very clearly and is easy to understand (Reviewer 1).
- Highly meaningful motivation: revealing that different de novo models have distinct advantages and challenging the traditional single-model paradigm (Reviewers 1, 2).
- The framework is general and adaptable to any de novo peptide sequencing model (Reviewer 1).
- Experiments are thorough, demonstrating state-of-the-art performance on two datasets (9-species-V1 and 9-species-V2) and surpassing each individual base model (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- Strong zero-shot generalization to unseen models whose outputs were not seen during training, underscoring robustness and potential for future applications (Reviewer 4).
- Novelty of the reranking approach and introduction of PMD and RMD metrics, which are likely the first of their kind in de novo sequencing and provide valuable reference points (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).
- The list-wise reranking effectively leverages complementary strengths from multiple sequencing models (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Modest performance improvement**: RankNovo’s peptide recall only surpasses the baseline by 0.037 (V1) and 0.023 (V2). Since RankNovo is trained and inferred on base model outputs, it can theoretically only outperform them. For such minor gains, why not simply select the best prediction from a single model? (Reviewer 2). This calls for additional experiments comparing to naive ensemble methods, e.g., selecting the peptide with the highest score from a model output, the most frequently predicted peptide, or the peptide closest to precursor mass (Reviewer 2).

2. **Computational cost and efficiency analysis lacking**: RankNovo requires inference from 2–6 other de novo models during training and inference, making it substantially slower than individual models. The paper lacks analysis of parameter number, training time, and inference time (Reviewer 2). A detailed breakdown of time per pipeline stage and the impact of the number of candidates on runtime is needed (Reviewer 4). The computational overhead may limit applicability in resource-constrained environments (Reviewer 3).

3. **Ambiguity and errors in mathematical notation**:
   - The symbol ‘M’ represents both residue mass (line 270) and prefix mass (line 306); a subscript or different symbol is needed (Reviewer 1).
   - In formula (7), the term \(\overline{m}_{q\tilde{j}} - \overline{m}_{k\tilde{j}}\) is likely a typo; it should be \(\overline{m}_{qi} - \overline{m}_{k\tilde{j}}\) to correctly represent prefix mass difference (Reviewer 1).
   - In formula (10), \(L_{\text{coarse}}\) and \(L_{\text{fine}}\) are not clearly defined; they seem to correspond to PMD and RMD but the notation is inconsistent (Reviewer 4).
   - The symbol \(\lambda\) is used in both formula (1) and formula (10) with different meanings, causing confusion (Reviewer 4). The setting of \(\lambda\) in Eq. 10 is unclear (e.g., whether it differs across tasks), and no ablation or sensitivity analysis is provided to justify its value (Reviewer 4).

4. **Insufficient discussion of related transformer-based methods**: The introduction lacks adequate coverage of recent transformer-based de novo sequencing models such as HelixNovo, InstaNovo, AdaNovo, PrimeNovo, and GraphNovo (Reviewer 1).

5. **Limited novelty of core algorithm**: The heavy dependence on outputs from existing sequencing models means the method does not fundamentally advance core de novo sequencing algorithms; its performance is intrinsically tied to the quality of base models, and if those are weak the reranking will inherit limitations (Reviewer 3).

6. **Unclear rationale for ablation study design**: When studying the influence of the number of base models, five subsets were selected by sequentially removing the strongest model. The basis for this choice is not explained; removing the poorest models would be an alternative. The selection may bias results (Reviewer 4). Also, in Table 8, adding ByNovo (the strongest model) to five other models reduced average performance; this counterintuitive result requires deeper explanation, not just a statement that peptide recall is primary (Reviewer 4).

7. **Conclusion too simple**: The conclusion lacks discussion of limitations, prospects, future optimization directions, and challenges. Reviewers suggest expanding it to be more comprehensive and forward-looking (Reviewers 3, 4).

8. **Minor issues**:
   - Typo: “expriment” in section titles 4 and 4.1 (Reviewer 4).
   - Inconsistent phrasing: “The latter four models, ByNovo, R-ContraNovo, and R-ByNovo” lists only three (Reviewer 4).

9. **Robustness definition ambiguity**: The paper’s use of “robustness” is unclear—it could refer to noise in mass spectrometry data (which RankNovo may not address) or to inconsistency in available base models during training vs. testing (Reviewer 2).

10. **Rationale for selecting six specific de novo models not explained** (Reviewer 3).
