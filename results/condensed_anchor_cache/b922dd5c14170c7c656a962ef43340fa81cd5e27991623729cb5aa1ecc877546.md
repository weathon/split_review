## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an imitation learning framework for multi-agent datasets where collective trajectory desirability scores (e.g., number of incidents) are available but individual agent contributions are unknown. It introduces *Exchange Values* (EVs), a Shapley-value-inspired metric quantifying an individual agent’s contribution to the collective desirability (expected change in desirability when substituting the agent with a random one). To handle incomplete or anonymized data, the paper proposes *EV-Clustering*, which maximizes inter-cluster EV variance to infer EVs. Using these estimates, *EV-based Behavior Cloning (EV2BC)* selectively imitates agents with high EVs to learn policies aligned with desired outcomes. Experiments on a synthetic *Tragedy of the Commons* environment and the cooperative *Overcooked* game (with both synthetic and human data) show that EV2BC outperforms relevant baselines (e.g., behavior cloning from all agents, behavioral clustering, inverse reward design, oracles). The paper includes an ablation study on EV-Clustering’s robustness to dataset composition and demonstrates that clustering is crucial when trajectories lack all agent combinations.

### Strengths
- **Novel Metric (Exchange Values)** – Introduces EVs as a principled way to quantify individual agent contributions to a collective desirability score, elegantly extending Shapley Values while accommodating group-size constraints. This addresses a realistic and meaningful problem (credit assignment in multi-agent imitation learning).
- **Handling Incomplete/Anonymized Data** – EV-Clustering enables EV estimation from real-world datasets where not all agent combinations are observed and agent identities are anonymized. This is a practical advance over methods that assume full observability.
- **Clear Problem Formulation and Intuition** – The paper is well motivated and the mathematical exposition (Sections 3 and 4) is precise, consistent, and accompanied by helpful intuition (e.g., substituting an agent and estimating the average change in desirability).
- **Strong Empirical Results** – EV2BC convincingly outperforms baselines in both environments. Figure 4 (right) is particularly compelling for illustrating the necessity of clustering in the degenerate anonymized case. Results also demonstrate that higher EV agents indeed produce higher collective desirability.
- **Relevance to High-Impact Applications** – The problem of learning aligned policies from multi-agent human data is important for autonomous driving, social dilemmas, and cooperative AI. The work is a step toward selectively imitating desirable behaviors without manual labeling per agent.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited Evaluation Environments** – Only two environments (Tragedy of the Commons and Overcooked) are studied, both relatively simple in terms of agent interaction, action spaces, and observability. The paper is motivated with a driving scenario but does not test on autonomous driving benchmarks, traffic datasets, or more complex domains (e.g., SMAC, multi-camera cooperation). Reviewers (R2, R3, R4, R5) consistently note this limitation and question generalization to realistic systems with heterogeneous agents, partial observability, and long-term dependencies.
- **DVF Definition and Justification** – The Desired Value Function (DVF) is a critical input, yet the paper does not address how it is defined or quantified for each environment. Without a clear methodology, a poorly chosen DVF could lead to unintended behaviors. Reviewers (R1, R5) ask for justification and potential biases or unintended consequences. The assumption that the collective value function is a sum of individual contributions (additive) is also not thoroughly discussed; R4 questions the class of problems where this applies.
- **Assumption of Consistent Agent Behavior** – The framework assumes agents behave consistently across trajectories. This may fail in dynamic or competitive settings (e.g., opponent-dependent strategies). R1 notes this strong assumption limits real-world applicability; R4 asks about bipolar dynamics (two agents work well together but not alone).
- **Lack of Comparison with Shapley Values** – When group-size constraints are absent (e.g., Tragedy of the Commons), a natural baseline is using Shapley Values directly to guide imitation. This comparison is missing. R2 also calls for baselines that use Shapley Values as credit assignment. R5 requests reporting results with ground-truth identification or Shapley value baselines to contextualize performance.
- **Insufficient Theoretical Analysis of EV Properties** – Shapley Values have well-known properties (symmetry, dummy, additivity). No analogous analysis is provided for EVs, e.g., sensitivity to non-linear value functions or non-interchangeable agents. R2 recommends formal analysis of these properties and any new limitations.
- **Lack of Interpretation of EV Values** – The paper does not connect specific EV values to agent behavior patterns (e.g., how λ values in Tragedy of Commons correlate with EVs, or how high-EV and low-EV agents differ in Overcooked). R2 and R3 ask for such analysis to validate and interpret EVs.
- **Scalability Concerns** – Calculating EVs for each agent and performing clustering can be computationally expensive for large datasets and many agents. The paper does not address practical limitations or scalability for large-scale settings (e.g., simulated cities with thousands of agents). R3 raises this concern explicitly.
- **Confusion between Behavioral Clustering and EV-Clustering** – The ablation study does not clearly show why behavioral clustering is insufficient; results supporting the claim (in Section 5.1) are not presented. R3 finds the discussion insufficient and wants concrete failure cases of behavioral clustering.
- **Missing Experimental Details** – The number of agents and composition of datasets (e.g., D^adv) are unclear. R3 asks for clarification. R2 also asks for clarification on notation (m in Line 220, definition of cluster-centroid agents). These missing details reduce reproducibility.
- **No Use of Undesired Behavior** – The method only imitates high-EV agents and does not actively learn to avoid undesired actions. R1 points out that this could lead to policies that still exhibit unsafe or aggressive behaviors not explicitly penalized. R1 suggests future work on leveraging undesired behavior data for constraints.
- **Writing** – While generally clear, several sentences are repetitive or convoluted (R2, R3). Minor reviewer questions about notation (m, cluster-centroid) reflect occasional lack of clarity.

*Note: The most critical reviewer (R1, score 5) emphasizes the DVF ambiguity and consistent-behavior assumption, while the majority (R2, R3, R4, scores 8) are very positive but call for broader evaluation and deeper analysis. R5 (score 6) echoes the need for baselines and richer environments. All reviewers agree on the novelty of EVs and the practical importance of the problem.*
