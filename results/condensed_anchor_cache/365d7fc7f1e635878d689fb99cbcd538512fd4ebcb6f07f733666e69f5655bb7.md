## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces QuaCertBias, a framework to certify LLM bias on prompt distributions via high-confidence Clopper-Pearson bounds on the probability of unbiased responses. It operationalizes this by constructing counterfactual prompt sets (differing only in sensitive attributes) and sampling prefixes from distributions (random token sequences, manual jailbreaks, embedding-space jailbreaks). Experiments on 9 LLMs (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Mistral-7B, etc.) yield non-trivial certified bounds, revealing vulnerabilities especially to jailbreak prefixes. Three reviewers scored 8/8/6; one reviewer (score 3) is strongly critical, questioning novelty, missing baselines, and insufficient treatment of bias.

### Strengths
- **Important problem**: Quantifying bias in LLMs and providing guarantees is highly relevant to the community (R1, R4).
- **Novel framing**: Moving from benchmarks to confidence-interval-style certificates for bias on prompt distributions is a well-motivated and understudied problem, especially for black-box models (R1, R3). The strategy of encoding counterfactual information into prompt distributions is interesting (R1).
- **Simplicity as strength**: The approach is straightforward (sampling + Clopper-Pearson); simplicity is seen as a virtue by R2, who prefers confidence intervals over point-estimate benchmarks (e.g., 40–80% instead of 65%).
- **Open-source implementation** (R2).
- **Extensive experiments**: evaluates 3 prefix distributions across 9 LLMs (R4); raises insightful observations about which prompt types trigger biased responses (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns and missing related work** (R4, echoed by R3 as a minor point): R4 argues the paper ignores prior work on high-probability guarantees for LLMs, particularly Prompt Risk Control (Quach et al., 2023/ICLR 2024), which already gives bounds on bias and can handle the same settings. The claims of “first” (abstract, line 105, conclusion) are therefore incorrect, and that work is a missing baseline. R3 notes that commercial jailbreak attacks (Zou et al., 2023) are not explored, though the cited works are about learned (not random) prefixes; random prefixes had little effect.
- **Definition and motivation of “certificate”** (R1, R2): R1 finds the meaning of a certificate unclear; equation (1) gives a probability, but a certificate should be a bound on that probability. R2 says the term is not well-motivated and sees no advantage over simply saying “confidence intervals.”
- **Weak bias metric** (R3): The BOLD-based unbiasedness detector matches human judgment only 76% of the time (stated in appendix); R3 considers this low and important enough to be in the main text.
- **Limited attack distributions** (R3, R2): The proposed jailbreak prefixes do not significantly reduce %unbiased except for GPT-3.5. Stronger learned jailbreaks (e.g., Zou et al.) are not tested. R2 questions the utility of random prefixes, which are ineffective and misrepresent the cited works.
- **Missing ablations and experimental clarity** (R1, R2, R4):
  - Confidence intervals in Table 1 are wide; more samples or multiple prefixes per prompt could improve tightness (R1). How would multiple prefixes affect interval construction? (R1)
  - Impact of model temperature, top-k/top-p on the certificate is not discussed; the certificate is only valid for a specific parametrization (R2).
  - No comparison to empirical bias rates or other bounding methods; tightness of bounds is unexplored (R4).
  - No synthetic validation of whether the confidence intervals are truly valid (R3).
- **Writing and presentation shortcomings** (R1, R4):
  - Section 3: lacking specification of spaces for $\mathcal{G}, \mathcal{A}$ and operations (e.g., concatenation); Definition 1, part (3) is not clearly connected; the term “certificate” needs intuitive explanation (R1).
  - Section 4 is disjointed; a single algorithm box would help, with separate discussion of design choices (R1).
  - The treatment of bias and representational harm is insufficiently detailed (R4): e.g., what are allocative vs. representational harms? How do LLMs cause new social hierarchies? Only one set of counterfactual prompts is used; the paper would benefit from either a general framework with bias as a use case or a more thorough contextualization of the specific harm.
- **Unsupported claims** (R4): The third contribution (“novel insights into biases of popular LLMs”) is not supported by the experiments; comparisons to existing evaluation frameworks are unclear.
- **Need to sample from distribution** (R4): This requirement is a weakness compared to frameworks like Prompt Risk Control that can also handle finite-sample settings.
- **Missing model version details** (R2): For Mistral-7B, the version (instruct, guard, v0.3, etc.) is not reported. R2 also asks for a hypothesis why the main jailbreak was so effective against Mistral-7B.
- **Practical guidance missing** (R2): The paper would benefit from a section explaining how practitioners should use the toolkit and in what circumstances; releasing certificates for popular models (like a leaderboard) could be useful.
- **Disagreement note**: One reviewer (R4, score 3) is substantially more negative, finding that weaknesses (lack of novelty, missing baselines, poor motivation) significantly outweigh strengths. The other three reviewers (scores 8,8,6) are positive overall, with R3 raising moderate concerns about metric reliability and attack strength.
