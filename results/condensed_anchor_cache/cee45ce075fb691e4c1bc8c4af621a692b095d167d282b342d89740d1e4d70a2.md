## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Sylber, a self-supervised knowledge distillation method that converts speech into syllable-based embeddings. It regresses features on syllabic segments distilled from a teacher (EMA of the student), producing structured syllable-level representations. The approach yields a linear-time syllable segmentation algorithm, efficient tokenization (~4.27 tokens/sec), and shows strong performance on syllable detection, discovery, speech resynthesis, and spoken language understanding (sWUGGY/sBLIMP). The model also exhibits categorical perception aligning with human perception. However, reviewers raise concerns about evaluation completeness, training complexity, scalability, resynthesis naturalness, and the practical necessity of syllabic tokens given only minor SLU improvements.

### Strengths
- **Timely and well-motivated framework**: The paper addresses an important problem—learning linguistically-motivated large-granularity speech tokens—to reduce the high temporal rate of current SSL tokens (e.g., HuBERT K-means) that makes Transformer LMs difficult to scale. (Reviewer 2)
- **Simple and effective method**: The self-distillation with EMA teacher and segment-level averaging is intuitive and achieves strong results. (Reviewer 2, 3)
- **Linear-time segmentation**: The greedy O(n) algorithm greatly facilitates downstream utilization of syllable-level features compared to prior O(n^2) methods. (Reviewer 3)
- **Extensive evaluation**: Experiments cover syllable segmentation/discovery, categorical perception, speech resynthesis, and spoken language modeling, with strong results on all tasks. (Reviewer 2, 3)
- **Categorical perception analysis**: The discriminability index and Figure 3 clearly show Sylber’s embeddings align with human categorical perception (e.g., “lest” vs. “rest”). (Reviewer 1, 3, 4)
- **Efficient tokenization**: Achieves an average of 4.27 tokens per second, which is beneficial for compute-constrained downstream tasks. (Reviewer 1)

### Weaknesses
- **Overclaimed applicability for downstream tasks**: The claim that syllabic units are “better suited for lexical and syntactic understanding” and for SLU is not fully justified by the results (Table 4, 9). Improvements are small (<3% relative on SLU) and some baselines are missing. Reviewer 1 suggests focusing on efficiency and interpretability as promising initial results; further work is needed to show superiority. Reviewer 4 notes that Table 9 shows loss of fine-grained information, so Sylber requires separate encoders/tokenizers for complex problems, undermining the necessity of syllabic tokens.
- **Resynthesis naturalness not evaluated**: No objective or subjective evaluation (e.g., Mean Opinion Score) of the resynthesized speech quality is provided. It is unclear how natural the reconstructed speech sounds compared to HuBERT K-means units, which is critical for applications like dialog agents. (Reviewer 2)
- **Resynthesis approach is overly complicated**: The token-to-speech pipeline involves a CFM model and Articulatory Encodec, introducing unnecessary complexity and uncertainty. Intelligibility/quality are not significantly better than simpler baselines (Table 2). The paper does not justify why a simpler approach was insufficient. (Reviewer 4)
- **Missing ablation and implementation details**:
  - The method for detecting non-speech frames (norm threshold and waveform amplitude filtering) is insufficiently described. The sensitivity of these thresholds and their interaction are unknown. (Reviewer 3)
  - The impact of the segmentation algorithm on performance is unclear. Table 1 should report results with all combinations of (SSL feature, segmentation algorithm). The hyperparameter search space for the greedy algorithm and how the optimal value is determined are not described. (Reviewer 3)
- **Training effort and two-stage process**: Sylber requires two-stage training (1.15M + 500k updates) on LibriSpeech 960h, not including pre-training of HuBERT and SD-HuBERT. The gain from stage 2 is marginal (R-value 75.6→75.9 in Table 6), questioning its necessity. (Reviewer 4)
- **Scalability concerns**:
  - EMA-based self-distillation (like data2vec) is known to be unstable and hyperparameter-sensitive, which may hinder scaling. The paper does not address this, and two-stage training exacerbates costs. (Reviewer 4)
  - All experiments are on clean LibriSpeech. The segmentation algorithm’s robustness to noisy/conversational speech (e.g., Switchboard) or multilingual data is untested, casting doubt on real-world applicability. (Reviewer 4) Reviewer 1 also asks whether other SSL tokens’ lack of syllable structure is a fair criticism, since they have sub-phonemic structure.
- **Inference efficiency analysis insufficient**: Only segmentation complexity (O(n)) is reported; actual latency or real-time factor (RTF) for extracting syllable boundaries is missing. (Reviewer 4)
- **SLU comparison incomplete**: Table 4 does not report prior methods like NAST and SpeechTokenizer. The dataset used for training the uLM is not clearly stated, despite uLM performance being highly data-dependent. (Reviewer 4)
- **Bias in evaluation metric**: Since the model is explicitly trained to detect syllables, it may perform best on syllable detection/discovery metrics, making comparisons potentially biased. Further, the model is trained on 5-second segments—how does this affect performance on long utterances? (Reviewer 1, 4)
- **Minority reviewer contrast**: While most reviewers (2,3) rate 8 and emphasize strong results, Reviewer 4 (score 5) is more negative, questioning the necessity of syllabic tokens, training scalability, and real-world utility. These criticisms should be considered as a distinct, more skeptical perspective. (Reviewer 4)
