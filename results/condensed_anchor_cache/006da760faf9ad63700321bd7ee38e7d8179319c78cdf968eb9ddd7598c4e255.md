## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a scalable automated preference alignment technique for chest X-ray report generation, using an LLM-as-a-Judge mechanism on publicly available datasets to eliminate the need for radiologist feedback. The authors benchmark five direct alignment algorithms (DAAs) against a supervised fine-tuning baseline, reporting up to a 57.4% improvement in average GREEN scores and a 9.2% increase across six metrics. They also study reward overoptimization via length exploitation (reports lengthen up to 3.2×) and assess alignment tax on six additional tasks, finding no significant degradation. A reader study with four board-certified radiologists shows win rates up to 0.62 over SFT baseline, but penalizes verbosity. Reviewers agree the framework addresses a key barrier in medical AI by reducing expert annotation costs, but raise substantial concerns about bias in the automated judge, lack of generalizability, weak correlation with radiologist assessment, missing clinical efficacy metrics, and unclear explanation of results. One reviewer (R1, rating 6) considers the paper well-motivated and systematic; others (R2, rating 3; R3, rating 5; R4, rating 3) are more critical, doubting whether the approach genuinely improves factual accuracy and clinical reliability.

### Strengths
1. Well-motivated and clearly written, with thoughtfully designed figures that effectively illustrate concepts and results. (R1, R2)
2. Systematic examination of how DAAs enhance factual accuracy from three perspectives, including both improvements and potential degradations. (R1)
3. Scalable technique that reduces dependency on expert clinician annotation by leveraging an automated “LLM-as-a-Judge” mechanism. (R2, R3)
4. Significant improvements over SFT baseline: up to 57.4% in average GREEN scores and 9.2% across six domain-specific and general metrics. (R1, R2, R4)
5. Demonstrates consistent performance across six diverse tasks with no significant alignment tax, strengthening applicability to high-stakes medical domains. (R2)
6. Comprehensive comparison of five DAA algorithms, providing insights into how each uniquely contributes to model performance. (R3)
7. Detailed methodology and evaluation that serve as a valuable resource for future VLM fine-tuning research. (R3)

### Weaknesses
1. **Biased preference data collection**: The LLM-as-Judge relies on a single metric (GREEN) for generating preferences. An ablation study comparing alternative metrics is missing, and this narrow signal may limit generalizability and introduce bias. (R1, R3)
2. **Missing comparisons with prior work**: No comparison with RLAIF or Token-level DPO (TDPO). The paper should discuss methodological differences, advantages, and whether TDPO’s token-level approach could mitigate verbosity issues. (R1, R2)
3. **Single model evaluation**: Only CheXagent is tested. Experiments on other VLMs (e.g., LLM-CXR) are needed to support generalizability. (R1, R4)
4. **Low quality of generated candidates**: Precision and recall remain below 0.5; the SFT baseline struggles to produce high-quality candidates, making it unclear whether alignment pushes the model in a reliable direction. (R4)
5. **Lack of clinical efficacy (CE) metrics**: No per-disease precision, recall, or F1 scores are reported for MIMIC-CXR. GREEN is not necessarily clinically relevant; clinicians care about detection of specific diseases. (R4)
6. **Discrepancy between automated and human evaluation**: DPO and IPO achieve the highest scores in Table 3 but are rated worst by radiologists. The authors attribute this to verbosity, but R3 suspects more fundamental issues (e.g., counterfactual content). This contradicts the paper’s goal of improving factuality. (R3)
7. **Verbosity and reward hacking**: Reports lengthen up to 3.2×, showing clear over-optimization. A rule-based reward term to penalize verbosity should be tested; e.g., adding such a term to DPO/IPO could improve human ratings. (R3)
8. **Vague explanations of results**: Reasons for error increases in alignment methods (Table 5e,f) and the relationship between verbosity bias and report quality (e.g., SimPO has smallest verbosity but performs worst) are not clearly explained. (R4)
9. **Alignment tax on other tasks**: Performance drops on six diverse tasks (though not significant) raise doubts about what alignment changes—possibly just a shift to radiologist-friendly expression rather than improved image understanding. (R4)
10. **Robustness to biased/noisy data**: Not evaluated across different age groups or with artificially injected label noise. Bias accumulation in the LLM-as-Judge mechanism also requires detection and mitigation strategies. (R2)
11. **Theoretical limitations of DAAs**: The paper does not discuss specific theoretical limitations that may affect applicability to other medical or high-stakes domains. (R2)
12. **Reproducibility**: Code is not provided; a clear summary of the DAA algorithms is missing, hindering reproducibility. (R3)
13. **General metrics are not helpful**: BERTScore, BLEU-4, and ROUGE-L do not assess factual quality; consider moving them to supplementary material. (R3)
14. **Scaling cost/efficiency**: No discussion of how the approach scales in terms of cost and efficiency over time and with larger datasets. (R2)
15. **Missing justification for baselines**: The choice of baseline and comparison methods is not justified; additional relevant baselines should be included and explained. (R2)
