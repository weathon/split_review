## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a Bayesian hierarchical linear disease progression model that explicitly accounts for three types of health disparities: differences in initial severity at care entry, progression rate while under care, and visit frequency conditional on severity. The model is theoretically justified (identifiability, bias from ignoring disparities) and evaluated on simulated data and a private heart failure dataset (n=2,942, 4 features). Reviewers 1–3 (all score 3, confidence 3–4) find the problem important but raise major concerns about technical novelty, model assumptions, weak baselines, insufficient performance evidence, and limited evaluation. Reviewer 4 (score 8, confidence 3) finds the paper well written, tackling an important problem with strong theoretical justification and thorough evaluation, but notes the need for robustness checks under misspecification.

### Strengths

- **Important problem**: Addressing health disparities and fairness in disease progression modeling is clinically meaningful and socially relevant (Reviewers 1,2,3,4).
- **Interpretability and clinical insight**: The model provides interpretable characterization of disparities (initial severity, progression rate, visit frequency) and estimates covariate effects, which is valuable for clinicians (Reviewers 1,3,4).
- **Theoretical analysis**: The paper includes strong theoretical proofs showing that ignoring disparities leads to biased severity estimates (e.g., underestimating severity for disadvantaged groups) (Reviewers 2,4).
- **Empirical analysis**: Extensive evaluation on both synthetic and real-world data, with interesting insights regarding group-specific disparities (Reviewer 2). Thorough evaluation with theoretical justification is appreciated (Reviewer 4).
- **Presentation**: Well written and easy to read (Reviewer 4). Model explanation and overall structure are clear (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited technical novelty and overly simplistic assumptions**: The model is effectively a variant of a hidden Markov model with simple linear functions for transitions and emissions. The design choices (DAG, linear representation, selection of the three disparity types) appear heuristic and lack detailed justification or empirical evidence. The linear assumption constrains disease trajectories (no worsening and improvement over time), limiting realism (Reviewers 1,3,4). One reviewer (3) questions whether the technical contribution is sufficient for the venue.
- **Weak or missing baselines and comparisons**: 
  - Baselines are limited to linear regression, quadratic regression, PCA, and factor analysis—all linear methods. Comparisons to state-of-the-art approaches (neural networks, RNNs, transformers, tree-based methods with feature engineering) are absent (Reviewers 1,2,3).
  - It is unclear whether baselines include demographic/disparity information; if not, the comparison is unfair because the proposed model explicitly uses group-specific parameters (Reviewer 2).
  - For the disease progression modeling task, comparisons with other methods that can incorporate covariates to condition on ancestry or other variables (e.g., nonlinear latent trajectory models) are necessary to justify the claim that ignoring disparities specifically causes bias (Reviewer 3).
- **Insufficient performance evidence**:
  - No significant improvements over baselines in reconstruction or predictive tasks; error bars and statistical significance tests are missing (Reviewer 1).
  - Interpretability claims are only qualitatively assessed using medical knowledge; quantitative evaluation or user studies are lacking (Reviewer 1).
  - On synthetic data, the model is evaluated on data sampled from its own architecture. Experiments on data generated from more complex, realistic disease progressions (or alternative generation processes) are needed to test robustness (Reviewers 2,3).
  - Ablation studies (e.g., removing disparity types) are performed only on simulated data, not on the real dataset, so the impact of each disparity component on predictive performance is untested in a realistic setting (Reviewer 2).
- **Generalizability concerns and data limitations**: The private dataset is small (n=2,942) with only four features, raising doubts about generalizability. Validation on public EHR datasets (MIMIC, UK Biobank) is recommended (Reviewer 1). Multiple reviewers also note the need for stronger baselines that account for demographics in different ways (e.g., models with one disparity type) (Reviewer 2).
- **Model assumptions and identifiability**: The model makes strong assumptions to ensure identifiability (e.g., reference group a₀, stationary function f, linearity). It is not discussed how realistic or common these assumptions are in practice. The impact of model misspecification (when the underlying process does not satisfy assumptions) should be analyzed, including robustness to unmeasured disparities (Reviewers 1,3,4).
- **Specific questions and missing experiments (converted from reviewer questions)**:
  - How is the stationary function \(f\) (line 135) justified? Is it the same for every \(Z_t\)? (Reviewer 1)
  - Why are these three disparities considered the most significant? Hundreds of disparities exist; justification is lacking (Reviewer 1).
  - How is the reference group \(a_0\) chosen (for which \(\mu(a_0)=0, \sigma(a_0)=1\))? Does a different choice affect performance? (Reviewer 1). Similarly, for continuous \(A\), does there need to be a reference value? (Reviewer 3)
  - Describe metric aggregation: \(X_t\) includes multiple variables; how are RMSE and MAPE aggregated over them? (Reviewer 2)
  - Second disparity (faster progression) – is this due to worse clinical care for certain groups or biological differences? Would any model conditioning on ancestry infer such disparities? (Reviewer 3)
  - What happens if some disparity variables are unmeasured? Can the model still correctly infer progression? (Reviewer 3)
- **Contrast among reviewers**: Reviewers 1–3 (score 3) express significant reservations about novelty, assumptions, and evaluation, while Reviewer 4 (score 8) is largely positive, noting strong theoretical justification and thorough evaluation but still raising the need for misspecification analysis. This disagreement indicates that the paper’s contributions are not uniformly compelling.
