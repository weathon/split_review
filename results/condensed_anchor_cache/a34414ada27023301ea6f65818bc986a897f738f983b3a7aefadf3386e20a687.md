## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper integrates recent monocular depth and surface normal estimation models, categorizing them as discriminative-based (e.g., DINOv2) or generative-based (e.g., Stable Diffusion finetunes). The authors conduct extensive experiments comparing these models under unified training conditions on multiple benchmarks, including some newly introduced benchmark datasets (though these are existing datasets). They analyze performance on general depth, metric depth, surface normal estimation, and cross-view consistency. Through re-training and ablation, the paper aims to identify critical factors like fine-tuning protocol, data quality vs. scale, and pre-trained backbone. Key insights include: diffusion-based iterative refinement may be unnecessary; one-step fine-tuning suffices; discriminative models (DINOv2) slightly outperform generative ones given high-quality small-scale data; metric depth estimation requires more data than scale-invariant tasks. The paper builds a unified codebase for fair comparisons.

### Strengths
- **Exhaustive experimental comparisons**: The paper conducts a thorough comparison of discriminative and generative geometry models across a wide range of datasets and training conditions, including re-training under consistent settings (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). This provides valuable assistance to researchers in following the latest depth estimation models (R1).
- **Practical insights**: The paper offers several actionable conclusions, e.g., that a simpler one-step fine-tuning (GenPercept) is sufficient for depth and surface normal estimation, that data quality often outweighs data scale, and that metric depth estimation has different data requirements (R1, R3). The observation of Marigold’s robustness and good generalization on new data is interesting (R1).
- **Comparisons under same training conditions**: The unified baseline and retraining under identical conditions help identify superior structures, loss functions, and training methods, which is a step beyond just comparing released weights (R2).
- **One positive methodological detail**: The analysis of decoder supervision for surface normal estimation and the intervention modifying GenPercept is a positive step toward deeper analysis (R4).
- **Clear writing and easy to follow** (R3). *(Contrast: R4 finds the paper lacks clarity – see Weaknesses.)*

### Weaknesses
1. **Lack of novelty / survey-like contribution**: The paper is more an experimental report or re-evaluation than a methodological advance. It does not propose new architectures, training strategies, or datasets. The “fair baselines” simply reuse existing models on different data subsets; the “new benchmarks” are existing datasets used for evaluation, not newly created (R1, R3, R4). The contributions are not clearly distinguished from a survey or benchmark paper.
2. **Insufficient depth of analysis**: Several conclusions are based on limited experiments and may be overgeneralized. For example, the claim that complex fine-tuning protocols are unnecessary derives solely from GenPercept’s one-step fine-tuning performance, without analyzing why it works or testing more complex protocols that might challenge the claim (R1). The insights are mostly anecdotal and not causally explained – e.g., “high-quality data” is invoked but not defined or isolated as a controlled variable (R4). The finding that a strong pre-train and high-quality data are key is not novel (R4).
3. **Unconvincing conclusion on metric depth data requirements**: The statement (L419) that “large-scale datasets … are still indispensable for metric depth estimation” is not sufficiently justified because the retrained model was trained for only 30k iterations (batch size possibly 96) vs. the original Metric3D V2 trained for 800k iterations with batch 192. The difference in training budget, not dataset size, could explain the performance gap (R2).
4. **Insufficient training in experiments**: Most experiments are conducted with only 20k iterations, which may not be enough for convergence, affecting the validity of conclusions (R2). The small-scale fine-tuning underperforming official models could be due to insufficient training rather than dataset scale (R2).
5. **Missing variance/robustness metrics**: The evaluation uses only mean metrics (e.g., AbsRel, delta_1). Reporting variance or error variance across images/datasets would better represent model robustness, which is important for zero-shot generalization (R2).
6. **Need for overall performance aggregation**: Many tables (e.g., Table 9, 10) evaluate across several datasets but lack an overall average or summary metric, making it hard to compare methods holistically (R2).
7. **Lacks clarity and readability**: The paper is hard to follow due to vague terminology, insufficient context, and disorganized information flow. Sentences use strange phrasing and fail to help the reader retain key points (R4). This contrasts with R3’s positive readability comment, highlighting disagreement.
8. **Cross-view consistency evaluation concerns**: R1 questions whether testing 3D consistency solely with the Probe3D method is sufficient. This raises a missing ablation or validation of the evaluation protocol.
9. **Missing clarifications / potential errors**:
   - R2’s Q1: The claim (L404) that “our discriminative model trained on 77K data outperforms Metric3Dv2 in all three datasets” is not supported by Table 7 itself because the ViT+DPT model performs poorly on MatrixCity compared to Metric3Dv2. This suggests a misinterpretation or missing qualification.
   - R2’s Q2: Table 9 and Table 10 references in Sec. 4.1 and 4.2 may be incorrect.
   - R2’s Q3: Some methods appear in Table 9 but not Table 10 without explanation; this inconsistency needs justification.
   - R3’s question about Line 64-65: The phrase “performance distinction … when trained on the same scale and quality of data also remains unclear” is itself unclear – what exactly is being compared?
   - R4’s question: How can one judge from Table 7 whether “high-quality fine-tuning data” was used? What constitutes high-quality data? This points to a lack of operational definition.
10. **Minority reviewer contrast**: Reviewer 4 is notably more critical (score 3) than the others (scores 5,6,5), finding the paper’s contributions and scientific rigor very weak, while Reviewer 2 is more positive (score 6) and praises the exhaustive comparisons and meaningful conclusions. This disagreement is preserved here by including both praise (strengths) and severe criticism (weaknesses).
