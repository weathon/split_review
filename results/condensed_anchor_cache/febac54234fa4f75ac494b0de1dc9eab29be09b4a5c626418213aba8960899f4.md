## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper provides a theoretical analysis of sample replay in an over-parameterized continual linear regression setting (Evron et al. 2022), focusing on low replay buffer sizes. It constructs worst-case and average-case scenarios where replay of a single or few examples can increase forgetting, and verifies these findings on toy linear models and MLPs on Split MNIST.

### Strengths
- Theoretical analyses of replay-based continual learning are scarce; this paper attempts to understand when replay can increase forgetting.
- The theoretical results are verified in experiments (linear models and MLPs).
- The paper is well written, clear, and has a clear motivation.
- The studied negative impact of replay is a surprising and important topic; the theoretical results explain the reason behind the negative effect.

### Weaknesses
- **Overly restrictive assumptions:** Assumptions 2.2, 3.1, and 3.3 are too strong (e.g., constant sample norm in overparameterized linear models, orthogonality conditions, realizability). It is hard to justify how these hold in practice, weakening the significance of the theoretical results. The analysis is based on very simple models and deliberate constructions (R1, R2, R3).
- **Definition of forgetting:** The definition of forgetting in Equation (3) is based on training samples, which is problematic and non-standard (R1). The average-case analysis uses a more standard test-sample definition but is limited to two tasks (R1).
- **Limited theoretical scope:** Both the worst-case (Theorem 3.2) and average-case (Theorem 3.6) results focus on T=2 (two tasks). Theorem 3.2 is an extreme example (two tasks with three samples), making it unsurprising that a detrimental replay exists. No theoretical analysis for T>2 is provided (R1, R2, R4).
- **Insufficient experimental evidence:** Experiments are conducted only on small-scale setups (linear models and MLPs on Split MNIST with very few replay samples, typically 1-3). The paper does not include experiments on larger datasets (e.g., CIFAR, TinyImageNet) or with more than two tasks, which would be easy to do. The results show that forgetting only happens with very low replay sizes, and replay with one sample usually helps, so the claimed negative effect is not broadly demonstrated (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- **Claim about task vs. model not sufficiently supported:** The statement that "forgetting is a property of the task rather than the model" (based on observations with MNIST) is not convincingly supported by the limited evidence; the observed forgetting could be attributed to intra-task interference or overfitting in a very restricted regime (R2, R3). The relationship between interference and overfitting is not clarified, especially in Remark 3.7 where the authors claim it is not overfitting (R3).
- **Ignored related work on memory selection:** The analysis ignores a substantial body of empirical work on selecting which items to store in the replay buffer, which has reached similar conclusions in more realistic settings (R3).
- **Missing comparisons and systematic characterization:** The paper does not compare to Banayeeanzade et al. (2024, arXiv:2408.16939), who derived a closed form for forgetting and generalization error for T=2 and observed non-monotonic behavior with replay size. The paper lacks a systematic characterization of conditions under which replay is detrimental versus beneficial (R4).
- **Open questions on correlation and extrapolation:** The average-case theory does not explain how the correlation between the learned model and replay data is handled (R1). It is unclear how the results extrapolate to non-orthogonal task distributions, non-uniform class distributions, tasks with multiple sub-spaces, or out-of-distribution replay samples (R3). The paper does not discuss whether the analysis can lead to new methods for populating the memory (R3).
- **Minor issues:** Assumption labels are not updated (R2). Typo at line 115: "Inverse of X_t" (R2).
