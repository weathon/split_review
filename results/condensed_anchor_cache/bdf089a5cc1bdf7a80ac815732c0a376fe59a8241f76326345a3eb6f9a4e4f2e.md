## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Last-Layer Selective Activation Retraining (LaSAR), a post-hoc method to mitigate spurious correlations without group labels. LaSAR identifies spurious neurons via a spuriousness score (difference between median activation on misclassified vs correctly classified validation samples) and blocks them during last-layer retraining. Experiments on CelebA, Waterbirds, MultiNLI, and CivilComments show improvements in worst-group accuracy. Reviewers acknowledge the practical unsupervised setting and clear problem importance, but raise major concerns about weak theoretical justification for the spuriousness score, lack of differentiation from prior work (JTT, AFR), insufficient evidence for the core assumption that spurious features are encoded in individual neurons, and missing comparisons (e.g., tuning baselines on worst-class accuracy, fair validation protocol). One reviewer finds the method novel and results promising, while three others view the contribution as severely limited, with two giving score 3.

### Strengths

- The paper tackles the important problem of debiasing deep neural networks without requiring any annotation on spurious correlations, making it a practical and fully unsupervised post-hoc tool (Reviewers 1, 4, 5).
- The idea of using activation patterns and prediction outcomes to self-identify spurious neurons is interesting and provides a self-guided mechanism for bias detection (Reviewer 2).
- Extensive experiments are conducted across multiple data modalities (vision and text) and model architectures, showing empirical improvement over some baselines (Reviewers 3, 4, 5).
- The method is evaluated on standard debiasing benchmarks, establishing a direct comparison with prior work (Reviewers 2, 5).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited theoretical analysis and justification for the spuriousness score.**  
  The paper does not provide rigorous theoretical guarantees for why the proposed spuriousness score (difference between μ_mis and μ_cor in Eq. (5)) reliably identifies spurious features in all cases. The rationale behind the definition is not clearly explained, and the connection between this score and actual spurious feature identification lacks sufficient justification. No theoretical analysis is given for convergence or stability of the selective activation retraining process, even on synthetic data (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
  - Reviewer 3 specifically notes the WGA formula (line 157) uses argmax, which is problematic because argmax returns a group label rather than accuracy, and returns the best-performing group rather than the worst.

- **Weak empirical support for core assumptions.**  
  The method critically assumes that (i) spurious correlations are encoded in individual neurons (so they can be masked away), and (ii) spurious features can be distinguished from core features based on error density alone. Reviewers note that:
  - In complex deep networks, latent representations may not factorize cleanly into source, spurious, and noise components; neurons can learn polysemantic features (linear combinations of spurious and core features), which the method does not address (Reviewers 2, 4, 5).
  - The toy example in Section 3.2 motivates the assumption, but the pattern in the toy example (Fig. 2b) looks very different from the real data (Fig. 4), leaving the assumption unverified in practice (Reviewer 5).
  - Figure 4 appears cherry-picked; the authors should provide the distribution of spuriousness scores across all neurons for each dataset to demonstrate that spurious and core neurons are actually separable (Reviewers 1, 5).
  - Even for CelebA, the difference between core and spurious activation maps in Fig. 4 is unclear; the spurious heatmaps seem to focus on the hair (the target task) (Reviewer 4).

- **Incremental contribution and unclear differentiation from prior work.**  
  The core idea—using misclassified examples and retraining the linear head to reduce reliance on spurious features—has been demonstrated in prior methods such as JTT and AFR. It is not clear how LaSAR is fundamentally different from AFR. The spuriousness score (median among misclassified vs. correctly classified) appears equivalent to retraining the last layer while up-weighting misclassified samples. The neuron masking algorithm further assumes that spurious features are localized to individual neurons, which is a strong assumption not shared by earlier methods. The paper does not explain why masking the last layer is necessarily better than masking a middle layer (Reviewers 1, 2).  
  - Reviewer 1 also notes that JTT is misclassified as semi-supervised (line 362) when it works without group information.

- **Incomplete or unfair experimental comparisons.**  
  - JTT and AFR require group information only for validating hyperparameters and can be tuned using worst-class accuracy. The authors should compare LaSAR with these baselines when tuned on worst-class accuracy, not just with group-aware validation (Reviewer 2).
  - Retraining on a part of the validation set may give LaSAR an unfair advantage over baselines that are not trained on validation data (Reviewer 4).
  - Validation on more difficult datasets (e.g., 9-Class ImageNet / ImageNet-A) is missing (Reviewer 4).
  - The paper does not provide results in terms of balanced accuracy aside from accuracy and worst-group accuracy (Reviewer 4).
  - The related work section should be updated with relevant recent works (e.g., [1]–[6] from Reviewer 4, and variable selection methods like L1 penalty) to position the contribution more clearly (Reviewers 3, 4).

- **Unclear writing and presentation issues.**  
  The reasoning behind the method for identifying spurious features is not clearly explained (Reviewer 3). The synthetic motivating example (Section 3.2) would benefit from a real-world example to demonstrate practical relevance (Reviewer 3). Minor: line 321 says “equation 6 and equation 7” instead of “equation (6) and equation (7)” (Reviewer 3). Visualizations for the motivating example use t-SNE despite using a linear model; linear dimensionality reduction would be more appropriate (Reviewer 5).

- **Open questions that double as criticisms.**  
  How does the method ensure it does not accidentally block valid but complex feature combinations (polysemantic features)? How does it handle features that are spurious in some contexts but valid in others? (Reviewer 2). Does reweighting only the classification layer, rather than removing bias from the whole model, pose an issue in certain cases? Could the method provide pseudo-labels for supervised debiasing? (Reviewer 4). How many spurious features were masked in each experiment? (Reviewer 5).
