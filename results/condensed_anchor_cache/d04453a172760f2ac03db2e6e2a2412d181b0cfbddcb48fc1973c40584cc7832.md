## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper trains RNNs (with trainable per-neuron leak $\tau$) on two working-memory tasks whose difficulty increases with sequence length $N$: $N$-parity (XOR over last $N$ digits) and $N$-delayed-match-to-sample ($N$-DMS). Two curricula are compared: sequential training of a single-head network on one $N$ at a time, and multi-head training where a separate output head is added for each $N$ so the network simultaneously solves all $N$s. Single-head networks develop long single-neuron timescales (large, heterogeneous $\tau$) but suffer from catastrophic forgetting, while multi-head networks keep $\tau$ nearly constant ($\tau \approx k$, where for $k=1$ $\tau$ is essentially memoryless) and instead develop long network-mediated timescales ($\tau_{\text{net}}$) via recurrent connectivity. Multi-head networks show faster training, greater robustness to ablations and perturbations, and better generalization beyond the training regime; this curriculum also improves GRU/LSTM performance on large-$N$ tasks. Two reviewers (scores 8) found the analysis convincing and novel, while two others (scores 5 and 6) had significant concerns about generalization and limitations.

### Strengths
- **Originality of the question**: The paper asks whether RNNs learn long timescales through single-neuron or network-generated mechanisms depending on training curriculum – a question not previously addressed (Reviewers 1,3,4).
- **Experimental comparison**: The study is framed around systematic comparisons – two tasks ($N$-parity and $N$-DMS), two curricula (single-head vs. multi-head), two timescale types ($\tau$ vs. $\tau_{\text{net}}$) – making the results space intuitive to reason about (R2, R4).
- **Convincing core result**: The contrast between the mechanisms favoured by single-head (large, heterogeneous $\tau$) and multi-head (short $\tau$, reliance on recurrent connectivity) is compelling and supported by the data (R2, R3, R4).
- **Depth of analysis**: The paper includes ablations of neurons with short/long $\tau$, perturbations to weights and timescales, retraining experiments, and tests on GRUs/LSTMs (R2, R4). Means and standard deviations are shown across trials (R1).
- **Thoughtful methodological choices**: The authors identify potential autocorrelation bias and mitigate it by simulating network activity for long periods; they also adopt an approximation to handle non-linear dynamics (R1).
- **Clarity of setup**: Sections 2 and 3 (model, tasks, curricula) are well written and easy to follow; concepts like $\tau_{\text{net}}$ and autocorrelation are explained in an accessible way (R1, R2). Figure 7 is particularly useful and well-annotated (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited generality and toy tasks**: The experiments use only two very simple tasks and a single model class (leaky RNN with trainable $\tau$). Results are not demonstrated on more realistic or high-dimensional problems; the paper’s claims about improving RNN performance or generalizing to “sets of related tasks” (e.g., language processing) are not supported at scale (R1, R2, R4). R1 states the paper is “too limited in its experiment design quality, empirical results, and scope to recommend it for acceptance in its current state.”
- **Claims overstated**: The conclusions in the paper (e.g., that multi-head training is generally superior) seem too strong given the narrow testbed. Claims about generalizing to other task sets require broader evidence (R2, R4). R2 specifically notes the need for significance testing or baselines in Figure 8, as the curves show only a modest difference.
- **Missing connection to biology**: The introduction motivates the work with biological neural networks, but the paper never connects the investigated mechanisms back to biology (e.g., experimental predictions, relevance of heterogeneous intrinsic timescales in the brain). Two reviewers (R2, R4) highlight this gap. R4 asks whether the authors conclude that optimized heterogeneous intrinsic timescales should not be employed in the brain, and notes that other biological mechanisms (short-term plasticity [Hu et al. 2021 PLOS], spike-frequency adaptation [Salaj et al. 2021 eLife]) are not considered.
- **Key conceptual clarifications needed**:
    - The paper states that $\tau=1$ makes a neuron memoryless, yet Figure 4 shows networks solving difficult memory tasks with $\tau$ fixed at $k=1$. How is this possible? (R3) This is a major concern that undermines the central contrast.
    - The initial presentation suggests $\tau$ and $\tau_{\text{net}}$ are independent components, but later it is acknowledged that longer $\tau$ causes longer $\tau_{\text{net}}$. This needs explicit clarification (R3, R4).
    - In Section 2, the text states $\tau_{\text{net}} \le \tau$ – should this be the opposite inequality? (R4)
- **Presentation and figure issues**:
    - Section 4 (results) is meandering and hard to follow. It would benefit from claim-based subsubsections and clearer summaries (R2). R2 also notes “time-lag” is introduced without definition.
    - Figures are under-annotated in general (except Fig 7). Figure 4a in particular needs better explanation of the x-axes (R2). Figure S12 uses a log y-axis but this is not pointed out (R1). Color choices in Figure 4 (single vs. multi-head lines) may be hard for colorblind readers (R1).
    - The related work section would be better placed before the results (R4 suggestion). Supplementary figures appear scattered across appendices rather than gathered together (R4).
- **Missing explanations and missing experiments**:
    - Figure 6 uses N=30 as the maximum for linear regression – this choice is not explained, and it is unclear why the same cutoff is used for N-DMS (R1).
    - Figure 8c would benefit from a mean line and extension to larger $N$ to see asymptotic performance (R1).
    - For the multi-head case, experiments are only shown for $k=1$ (fixed $\tau$ at 1). Was $k>1$ ever tested? The claim “$\tau$s fixed at $k$ value is as good as training them” needs a specific numerical $k$ (R1, R4).
    - Figure 5d shows $\tau_{\text{net}}$ increasing up to about $N=30$ and then decreasing – why this peak? It is also puzzling that multi-head networks achieve high performance despite having $\tau_{\text{net}}$ roughly half the size of the worse-performing single-head networks (R4).
    - Figure 3a: which task is shown? Learning curves appear to still be increasing – is convergence assured? (R4) Also, was $\tau$ fixed in Figure 3, so only recurrent connectivity was optimized? This should be made clear (R4).
    - Figure 6b: the network activity appears very high-dimensional (often >10), which is surprising compared to typical trained RNN dynamics (often <3). Is this expected? (R4)
    - The motivation for choosing two tasks ($N$-parity and $N$-DMS) and their distinct computational requirements (mentioned in Fig 6b caption) is not elaborated; the differences between the tasks are not clearly presented (R4).
    - Retraining experiments use 20 epochs – why this number? (R1)
- **Methodological details**:
    - The paper uses AIC for model selection but does not justify this choice over other information criteria (R1).
    - A more formal mathematical definition of how $\tau_{\text{net}}$ is obtained per neuron should be provided in the appendix (R4).
- **Minor issues**:
    - “retraining” appears to be a typo in the paper (R1).
    - Figure 1 caption should add “(AC)” after “autocorrelation” (R4).
    - In the 4th paragraph of Section 2, “tnet” should be $\tau_{\text{net}}$ (R4). End of first paragraph of Section 4.1, remove extra comma after “K” (R4).
