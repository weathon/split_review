## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a Contrastive Grouping-based Invariant Learning (CGIL) framework for OOD generalization on graphs. CGIL clusters graph nodes into one invariant group and several contrast groups, enforces connectivity within the invariant group, and uses a contrastive loss. Experiments on four benchmark datasets (Spurious-Motif, Graph-SST5, Twitter, CMNIST) compare against eight other methods. Reviewers agree the problem is important and the experimental results indicate effectiveness, but raise substantial concerns about novelty, theoretical support, clarity, missing details, and the validity of the main claim.

### Strengths
- The problem of OOD generalization on graphs is important and invariant learning is an interesting topic. [R3]
- The paper is well-organized and written; technical details are easy to follow. [R1, R3]
- Experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of CGIL against nine state-of-the-art methods on four benchmark datasets. [R1, R4] (R2: “outperforms other state-of-the-art methods, speaks to strengths in quality and significance.”)
- The application of contrastive learning in this context is original, adding new components to existing works (Chen et al. 2022; Li et al. 2022a;b). [R2]
- Sound technical backbone. [R1]

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and theoretical support are weak.** The method builds heavily on existing techniques—graph pooling and graph contrastive learning—without sufficient novelty. Clustering and subgraph-based graph representation learning has been explored in prior works [Clear, TNNLS 2022; Multi-Scale Subgraph Contrastive Learning, IJCAI 2023; and others] that should be discussed and compared. [R3] The framework lacks theoretical support and no reasonable assumptions or theoretical explanation is given for why CGIL learns invariant subgraphs. [R3, R4]
- **Main claim is problematic.** One reviewer (R4) strongly disagrees with the paper’s central argument that existing invariant learning methods learn invariant subgraphs directly through data labels; they assert that those methods rely on environment labels and the sufficiency/invariance properties. The paper’s critique is seen as flawed, and the proposed method lacks any such grounding. [R4]
- **Missing experimental details and baselines.**
  - Standard deviations are missing from the ablation study (Table 2). [R1]
  - No code, pseudo-code, or appendix was provided. [R1]
  - Complexity analysis / running time is missing. [R1]
  - The methods GSAT and GIL, discussed in related work, are not included as baselines. [R2]
  - Section 4.3 mentions DrugOOD but the results are not shown in the table. [R2]
- **Clarity and presentation issues.**
  - The model is complicated with many components; it is difficult to follow. [R2]
  - The last two paragraphs of the Introduction should be reorganized/rewritten. [R2]
  - A table of notations and variables is needed in the Appendix. [R2]
  - Equation (1) is not mathematically clear: is Group_j a set, vector, or scalar? [R2]
  - Some components (e.g., Section 3.4.1) appear similar to spectral clustering/graph cut or modularity; the added benefit is unclear. [R2]
- **Hyperparameter and experimental design questions.**
  - For the Spurious-Motif dataset, why were biases 0.33, 0.6, and 0.9 chosen? [R1]
  - How are optimal hyperparameters selected? [R1]
  - For Graph-SST5, Twitter, and CMNIST, have the authors looked beyond accuracy—what kind of subgraphs were found and did they make sense contextually? [R2]
- **Generalizability concerns.**
  - In realistic scenarios (e.g., molecules), the invariant relational structure may be a disconnected subgraph (dispersed functional groups), which cannot be captured by a single connected subgraph as enforced by CGIL. [R3]
  - Experiments are limited to synthetic, NLP, and CV domains; evaluation on widely used bioinformatics and social network datasets (e.g., TUDataset [5]) is needed. [R3]
- **Reviewer disagreement:** While one reviewer (R2) gave a rating of 5, praising the originality and experimental strength, another (R1) gave a rating of 1, citing missing details and lack of code/appendices. R3 and R4 gave 3, with R4 questioning the core claim.
