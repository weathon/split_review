## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes CONFST, a model steering method that constructs “confident directions” from LLM activations to align outputs with user preferences without fine-tuning. It includes a theoretical framework for steering and claims to handle multiple preferences simultaneously. Reviews are mixed: two reviewers rate 6 and two rate 5, indicating disagreement on overall contribution and clarity. Strengths noted include a theoretical foundation, multi-preference support, and resource efficiency. Weaknesses center on limited baseline comparisons (especially missing comparison to the original Activation Addition method by Turner et al. and to linear probing baselines), a simple experimental setup (topic shift reduced to sentiment classification, a task easy even for traditional ML), lack of case studies, unclear connection between the theory and the algorithm, sensitivity to hyperparameters (threshold, layer l, token position t, length s, and confidence threshold), vague and confusing terminology (“user”, “user history”, “user preference”), and a lack of validation for claimed noise robustness. Several reviewers question the novelty relative to straightforward extensions of existing linear probing techniques (e.g., Li et al. 2024) and note that adjustable relevance via threshold is analogous to top-k selection in prior work. The theory is criticized for assuming a likelihood function for activation features without specifying it, and for making contradictory assumptions (Assumption 2). Reviewers request baseline comparisons, ablation studies (e.g., on token concatenation vs. last token), case studies, and discussion of multi-user preference conflicts.

### Strengths

- S1 (R1, R3): Provides a theoretical framework that deepens understanding of model steering, which is often underexplored.
- S2 (R1, R3, R4): Supports multiple alignment directions and user preferences, going beyond simple bidirectional steering and reducing the need for fine-tuning.
- S3 (R1, R3, R4): Resource-efficient and broadly applicable; validated on multiple models (GPT-2 XL 1.5B, Mistral 7B, Gemma-it 9B).
- S4 (R2): The method is original to the best of R2’s knowledge, and the paper is mostly clear despite minor typos.

### Weaknesses

- W1 (R2, R3, R4): **Missing baseline comparisons.** The paper does not compare CONFST to the original Activation Addition method (Turner et al.) or to prior linear probing techniques (Li et al. 2024). It is unclear how CONFST outperforms these baselines. The claim of enabling multiple preferences is not distinct from simply extending the output dimension of a linear probe from 2 to K. Adjustable relevance via threshold is analogous to controlling top-k selections in prior work, so no significant advantage over existing methods is demonstrated.
- W2 (R3, R4): **Limited experimental setup and lack of case studies.** The topic shift task is primarily sentiment classification, which is too simple and even traditional ML methods can perform well; more varied evaluations are needed. No case studies are provided to illustrate the method’s performance in specific contexts, which would be especially valuable for personalized alignment.
- W3 (R4, R2): **Weak theoretical connection to the algorithm.** The theoretical framework focuses on maximizing P(θ*|v) but the algorithm uses a classifier to select activations; it is unclear how the theoretical goal translates into the practical steps. The theory assumes that θ can be directly estimated from f(τ(A_{1:n})) without specifying a concrete likelihood function for Pr(f(τ(A_{1:n}))|θ). The implicit assumption that logistic regression will work is not justified. (R4 also notes that Assumption 2 appears contradictory: “θ̂ with the highest occurrence probability … but then P(θ̂|…) < P(θ|…) for any θ≠θ̂.”)
- W4 (R1, R4): **Sensitivity to hyperparameters and lack of detailed settings.** The method’s success appears sensitive to the confidence threshold (especially with noisy data). The paper does not specify settings for critical hyperparameters: layer l, token position t for beginning token, and length s concatenated. There is no ablation study verifying why concatenating a segment of token activations is needed (vs. using only the last token, as in prior work). R4 questions the significance of these many hyperparameters and calls for reproducibility details.
- W5 (R4, R1): **Confusing and ambiguous terminology.** The use of “user”, “user history” H, and “user preference” θ is redundant and unclear. The authors refer to labeled news categories and model-generated responses as “history”, which is inappropriate; “data of different preferences” would be more accurate. The lack of a clear problem setup makes the motivation and applicability hard to follow. R1 also notes that Figure 1’s example is not intuitive (how can the method guess what a user thinks without explicit instruction?).
- W6 (R3, R4): **No validation of claimed noise robustness and no handling of multi-user preference conflicts.** The authors claim existing methods perform poorly with noisy samples, but do not provide experimental evidence or explain how CONFST mitigates noise. The threshold selection itself controls noise level, which seems to contradict earlier claims. Additionally, the paper does not address how conflicting preferences among multiple users affect model output, even though CONFST supports multiple preferences.
- W7 (R4): **Figure and reference issues.** Figure 3 is confusing (e.g., under “TVs” the value 0.45 > 0.4 is shown but the activation was not selected for averaging; missing explanations). The reference to Figure 4 in line 466 is incorrect.

- R2 Questions (incorporated as weaknesses): Does the proposed method outperform any baselines? Is there empirical evidence that the optimal steering vector θ can be inferred solely from activations? What is the likelihood function for Pr(f(τ(A_{1:n}))|θ)?
- R3 Questions (incorporated): How can the method align with broader unexplored preferences? What happens with conflicting user preferences?
- R4 Questions (incorporated): Why concatenate token activations instead of using the last token? Ablation study on this? Explanation for Figure 3 selection inconsistency?
