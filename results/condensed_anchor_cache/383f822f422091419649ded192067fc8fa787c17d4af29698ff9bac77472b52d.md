## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes LPE-SQL, a framework for text-to-SQL that augments LLMs with continual learning without parameter fine-tuning. It maintains a dynamic knowledge base inspired by human “mistake notebooks,” logging both correct and incorrect queries along with reasoning paths and reflection-generated tips. At inference time, relevant entries are retrieved and used as in-context examples. The framework includes four modules: demonstration selection, SQL generation (with cross-consistency voting), and a Rethink and Update module for knowledge accumulation. Experiments on the BIRD dataset with Llama-3.1 models show that LPE-SQL with a smaller 70B model can outperform a larger 405B model using a state-of-the-art method on a subset of BIRD dev (61.22 vs. 59.18). The paper studies various research questions (e.g., effect of notebook type, cross-consistency, parameter k).

### Strengths
1. **Novelty of the continual learning approach for text-to-SQL**: The idea of using a dynamically updated knowledge base containing correct and mistake notebooks (with reasoning paths and reflection prompts) is novel and reasonably motivated.
2. **Clear presentation**: The paper is generally well-written, easy to follow, and includes informative figures. The research questions in Section 5 are well-defined and help structure the analysis.
3. **Impressive result on a subset of BIRD**: Using a Llama-3.1-70B-INT4 model, LPE-SQL achieves 61.22 accuracy on a subset of BIRD dev, surpassing the 59.18 of the SOTA method based on Llama-3.1-405B, highlighting the potential of continual learning to bridge model size gaps.
4. **Thorough experimental design**: The experiments cover various difficulty levels (simple, moderate, challenging), compare in-domain vs. out-of-domain data, and examine the contributions of individual components through ablation studies.

### Weaknesses
1. **Only one dataset evaluated**: All experiments are limited to the BIRD dev set. The paper does not test on other prominent text-to-SQL datasets (e.g., Spider, ATIS, Advising, variants) or report test-set results. This limits the breadth and generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, BIRD is known to contain noise and is frequently updated [1].
2. **Unclear source of performance gains**: The paper uses more than 1000 examples as the knowledge base, while the number of demonstrations used in the compared SOTA method (Maamari et al., 2024) is unknown and likely much smaller. This makes it unclear whether improvements come from the continual learning mechanism or simply from using many in-domain examples.
3. **Insufficient validation of the mistake notebook’s contribution**: 
   - Ablation studies (Table 2) are performed on the full BIRD dev, while the SOTA comparison uses a subset. The authors should report ablation results on the same subset to confirm the notebook’s necessity.
   - In Table 3, the continual learning setup includes `E_full` (the full development set) as the knowledge base, which is unfair because the baseline SOTA method (Maamari et al.) does not use remaining dev examples. The authors should also show the performance of the init method using only `E_full`.
4. **Cross-consistency correction module appears unnecessary**: The cross-consistency (voting) module does not clearly outperform the simpler CR-0.5 method in Table 2 and Table 5. Given that CR-0.5 requires fewer API tokens and achieves comparable or better results, the voting mechanism adds cost without clear benefit.
5. **Limited technical contribution**: The framework is essentially a combination of existing techniques (retrieval, in-context learning, voting) without a novel learning mechanism. The “Rethink and Update Notebook” module is described as continual learning but feels common (logging, reflection). The cross-consistency module is standard.
6. **Incomplete comparisons with existing demonstration selection methods**: The paper does not compare its demonstration selection approach to other established methods like MCS-SQL, DIN-SQL, or ODIS. The evaluation is also incomplete: only a few values of the retrieval parameter `CR` are tested (missing 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8), and no sensitivity analysis for the number of retrieved samples `k` is provided.
7. **Missing baselines**: The paper does not include simple baselines such as supervised fine-tuning on the notebook examples, or directly feeding all notebook entries to the model at inference. These would help establish lower/upper bounds and clarify the method’s standalone value.
8. **Lack of SOTA comparison numbers**: The paper mentions outperforming or being competitive with SOTA methods but does not include concrete numbers for these comparisons in the main text. Moreover, several recent methods (e.g., ExSL + granite-34b-code, 67.47 on BIRD dev) surpass the best reported performance in the paper (66.36 on dev), yet these are absent from the baseline table.
9. **Shallow analysis of research questions**: Answers to RQs, especially RQ2 (effect of notebook type), are superficial. Claims like “GPT-3.5 performs better using only the mistake notebook, indicating its strength in leveraging error experiences” are not well explained or grounded with concrete examples. The final sentence redirects to future work rather than providing actionable insights.
10. **Potential order effects**: When continuously adding samples to the notebook, the evaluation order could influence results; this is not discussed or controlled.
11. **Missing parameter analysis**: The impact of notebook capacity/pruning is not explored. If the notebook grows very large, performance or efficiency may degrade without a pruning strategy.
12. **Minor writing issues**: The word “continual learning” is not clearly integrated into the introduction (only appears in bullet points). Also, “sql” should be capitalized (e.g., “efficient SQL”).
13. **Unclear inference-time example count**: RQ4 addresses the effect of `k`, but it remains confusing how many examples are actually fed to the LLM at inference.
14. **Reviewer disagreement**: While some reviewers (1,2) rated the paper 5 and found the approach novel and clear, others (3,4) rated 3 and considered the technical contribution limited, the components unoriginal, and the analysis shallow. The paper does not fully resolve these concerns.

[1] Wretblad, Niklas, et al. "Understanding the Effects of Noise in Text-to-SQL: An Examination of the BIRD-Bench Benchmark." arXiv:2402.12243 (2024).
