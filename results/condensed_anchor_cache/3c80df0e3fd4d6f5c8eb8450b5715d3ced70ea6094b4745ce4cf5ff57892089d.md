## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Selective Knowledge Transfer (SKT), a framework for few-shot continual learning with large language models (LLMs) that uses transferability measures (LogME) to identify relevant past tasks and aggregate their prompts. The authors integrate SKT into Progressive Prompts to create Log-evidence Progressive Prompts (LePP), which selects top‑K prompts by transferability scores and uses their weighted sum as additional input. Experiments are conducted on classification benchmarks (e.g., with BERT and T5 models, up to T5‑large 770M) and on some image tasks. The method is computationally efficient and outperforms baseline continual learning methods.

### Strengths
- **Simple, lightweight, and computationally efficient.** The approach incurs minimal overhead (R1, R3, R4). R3 adds that the algorithmic flow is complete and clear.
- **Effectively combines knowledge transfer with prompt tuning.** Incorporating transferability measures into continual learning is a reasonable point (R1, R2). R1 states that SKT facilitates knowledge transfer; R2 agrees that the combination is “reasonable and effective.”
- **Strong empirical results.** LePP surpasses several baseline methods on multiple continual learning benchmarks; R3 describes the results as “exceptional” (R1, R3).
- **Extensive experimental setup.** Experiments cover both NLP and computer vision tasks (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and technical contribution.** Multiple reviewers note that LePP is essentially an A+B combination (LogME + Progressive Prompts) – replacing prompt concatenation with a weighted average based on transferability scores (R2, R4). Weighted knowledge transfer has already been studied in domain adaptation and continual learning (R4). R3 also questions why LogME was chosen when ETran reportedly performs better. (All four reviewers assigned Contribution=2.)
- **Overclaiming regarding “LLMs.”** The transferability calculation requires encoder features; the method cannot be directly applied to decoder‑only models, which constitute the majority of modern LLMs. R2 points out that T5 is not typically called an “LLM” (as in the Progressive Prompts paper), making the paper’s framing an overclaim. This limits the scope and influence of the work.
- **Evaluation scope is too narrow.** All experiments are limited to classification tasks on small datasets; effectiveness on generation tasks is not shown (R1, R3). Only prompt tuning is tested, despite claiming general applicability to any PEFT method (R1). The largest model used is T5‑large (770M), which is insufficient to demonstrate scalability to larger LLMs such as Llama3‑8B (R1, question). These gaps weaken the paper’s generalizability claims.
- **Insufficient demonstration of positive knowledge transfer.** The results only report average performance; no direct evidence (e.g., per‑task analysis) shows that transfer is positive (R1). The choice of LogME over simpler similarity measures like cosine similarity is not justified; the paper lacks ablation to show the superiority or necessity of the proposed transferability metric (R3). R4 further notes that there is no analysis of which part of the prompt knowledge is actually helpful. Additionally, the conclusion that “learning from the least transferable tasks slightly improved performance” is contradictory and lacks a convincing explanation (R3).
- **Missing comparisons with recent state‑of‑the‑art.** R1 points out that methods such as SAPT (ACL 2024) and “Mitigate Negative Transfer with Similarity Heuristic Lifelong Prompt Tuning” (ACL 2024) are not compared.
- **Assumption of task‑id availability at inference.** The method relies on task IDs to select the correct prompt; obtaining task IDs in real‑world scenarios is often impractical (R1). R1 also questions how the approach would work without task IDs.
- **Writing and presentation issues.** Several writing errors are noted (line 65, 168‑169, unclear sentence “However, more accurate measures have higher accuracies.”) (R1, R3). R2 objects to self‑characterizing the method as “principled,” criticizes grammar (“are”→“is” on line 504), and notes that Table 1’s caption is too short. The paper uses too many abbreviations, causing confusion (R3). R2 also requests perceptible results (e.g., runtime) to support the claim of computational efficiency.
