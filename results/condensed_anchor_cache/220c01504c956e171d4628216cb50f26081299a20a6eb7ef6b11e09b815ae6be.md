## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces CoVT-CXR, a dataset and model for interpretable chest X-ray diagnosis via chain-of-visual-thought reasoning. The dataset contains ~3M instruction-following data points and ~30K reasoning sequences from 6K patient cases, annotated by 32 medical trainees. The CoVT model is fine-tuned in 5 stages and evaluated against several VLMs for report generation and VQA. Reviewers agree the dataset and interpretability approach are novel and potentially valuable, but raise concerns about evaluation rigor, missing medical-specific baselines, use of only lexical metrics, dataset scalability, and clarity of method description. One reviewer is more critical (score 5) about weak baselines and underwhelming results; two are positive (scores 8) about the dataset contribution and ablation studies; one is moderate (score 6) noting insufficient medical evaluation.

### Strengths
1. **Novelty of approach** – The chain-of-visual-thought (CoVT) framing mimics clinician reasoning by decomposing diagnosis into multi-step textual and visual rationales, a unique insight for medical AI.
2. **Dataset value** – CoVT-CXR is a carefully annotated, multi-step cross-modal dataset that fills an important gap for interpretable CXR diagnosis; it covers >20 diseases with 1–12 reasoning steps.
3. **Annotation tool** – An open-source tool for generating chain-of-thought labels is provided, supporting future research.
4. **Intuitive 5-stage step decomposition** – The training protocol (pretraining + 4 fine-tuning stages) is well motivated and easy to follow.
5. **Ablation studies** – Experiments demonstrate that multi-step reasoning improves performance over single-step variants and that additional reasoning steps help.
6. **Interpretability** – The model produces explicit intermediate reasoning, addressing a critical need for transparency in radiology.
7. **Transparency commitment** – Authors promise to release the dataset, model, tool, and code upon acceptance.
8. **Improved performance over zero-shot VLMs** – The CoVT model outperforms zero-shot versions of GPT-4o, Gemini, and LLaVA.

### Weaknesses
1. **Dataset unreviewable** – The dataset and code are not provided for review (only promised upon acceptance), making it impossible to verify the claimed annotations.
2. **Missing medical-specific baselines** – Comparisons exclude models explicitly trained for radiology: LLaVA-Med, LLaVA-Rad, MAIRA-1/2, CheXAgent, Med-Gemini, and other report-generation models. Comparisons also miss updated VLMs like LLaVA-1.6 and Flamingo.
3. **Inadequate evaluation metrics** – Only lexical metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, CIDEr) are used, which fail to capture clinical factual accuracy. Standard clinical metrics are missing (RGER, CheXbert, RadFact, human evaluation). A human evaluation or clinical accuracy scoring is needed.
4. **Underwhelming quantitative results** – Phi-3V outperforms the proposed CoVT when fine-tuned; LLaVA-1.5 (general VLM) matches or outperforms CoVT for both VQA and report generation, questioning the advantage of chain-of-thought.
5. **Scalability concerns** – The dataset requires 32 trained annotators and a bespoke labeling tool, suggesting high cost and limited reproducibility for other modalities (e.g., brain MRI). The process for scaling is not explained.
6. **Small dataset size** – Only 6K cases (with 400 test cases) limit the upper bound on performance; synergy with non-chain-of-thought datasets is not discussed.
7. **Unclear dataset content** –
    - The 112 semantic classes are not defined (how chosen?).
    - The “intermediate text description” is not standardized or exemplified.
    - No breakdown of pathologies, tasks, or sample counts by category (requested by reviewer 2).
8. **Unclear method steps** –
    - Segmentation strategy (lines 271–275) is vague: are pretrained segmentation models used zero-shot or fine-tuned? How are poor segmentation results handled?
    - How the framework autonomously identifies all relevant targets in the image (line 325) is not explained; could repeated inference produce duplicate statements?
9. **Lack of justification for design choices** –
    - No evidence that the full multi-stage fine-tuning protocol is necessary (e.g., ablation comparing fewer stages).
    - The SSL method (VAE) is outdated; why not Dino_v2? (reviewer 3 question – not justified)
    - Fairness of encoder comparison: LLaVA uses a general encoder, while CoVT uses an encoder pre-trained on medical data (reviewer 3 question – not addressed).
10. **Weak support for claimed doctor process** – The description of how doctors read CXRs (Sections 1 and 3.1) cites only lay websites (geekymedics, radiologymasterclass), not peer-reviewed literature; language is too authoritative.
11. **Potential token oversampling confound** – The claim that more reasoning steps improve generation probability may simply reflect exposure to more ground-truth tokens, not genuine reasoning improvement. Clinical metrics would help disentangle.
12. **Inference-heavy** – The model may require up to 12 visual/text passes per statement (abstract line 26), a performance limitation not acknowledged.
13. **Handling of incomplete/incorrect MIMIC-CXR reports** – No explanation of how the pipeline deals with errors or omissions in the original radiology reports.
14. **Missing appendix** – The paper repeatedly references an appendix (e.g., for dataset examples) that is not provided.
15. **Poor readability and formatting** – The paper is difficult to follow (dataset creation, evaluation); multiple spelling/formatting errors (e.g., “shed lights” → “shed light”, “corrdinate” → “co-ordinate”, missing Fig. references, vspace clash in Table 3 caption).
16. **No limitations or future work section** – A discussion of limitations is absent.
