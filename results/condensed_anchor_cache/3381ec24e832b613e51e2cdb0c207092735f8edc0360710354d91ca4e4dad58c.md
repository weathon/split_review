## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Online Reward Selection and Policy Optimization (ORSO), which frames reward shaping as an online model selection problem. ORSO uses the D^3RB algorithm (Pacchiano et al., 2023) to select among candidate reward functions generated by an LLM, trains a policy with the selected reward for a fixed number of steps, evaluates against the task reward, and repeats. Experiments on Isaac Gym continuous control tasks demonstrate that ORSO reduces computation time compared to EUREKA, can surpass human-designed rewards, and scales with budget. Ablations compare selection strategies (UCB, EXP3, ETC, EG, Naive, D3RB).

### Strengths
- Addresses the significant challenge of reward design and sparse rewards in RL. (R1, R4)
- Clear writing, well-structured, well-presented graphs, and appropriate context. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- In-depth related work and preliminaries. (R2, R3)
- Successfully applies a recent online model selection algorithm (D^3RB) to reward selection. (R1)
- Demonstrates that the algorithm helps identify good reward functions with limited budget and scales with budget and candidate count. (R1)
- In-depth ablation study on various selection algorithms (UCB, EXP3, ETC, EG, Naive, D3RB) provides detailed insights. (R2, R3)
- Experimental results show ORSO is faster than the LLM-based EUREKA, can surpass human-designed rewards, and improves with larger budgets. (R3, R4)
- Claims that ORSO reduces both time and computational costs by more than half compared to prior methods. (R4)
- Regret-based analysis provides a clear and intuitive performance monitor. (R4)
- The formulation is elegant, modular, and simple to implement. (R4, R6)
- The approach is effective at selecting reward functions and shows fast convergence in terms of computational time and sample efficiency. (R5)

### Weaknesses
- **Dependence on pre-generated candidate pool.** ORSO selects from a fixed set of candidates, so its final performance is heavily capped by the quality of the generated reward functions. If all candidates are poor, ORSO cannot improve beyond them. The paper does not analyze the impact of wrong, redundant, or low-quality candidates, nor alternative generation strategies. (R1, R4, R5, R6)
- **Missing comparisons with existing reward shaping methods.** The paper does not compare ORSO against established reward shaping algorithms (e.g., Ng et al. 1999, Zou et al. 2019, Zheng et al. 2018, Sorg et al. 2010, Gupta et al. 2023). Without these baselines, it is difficult to assess the relative merits of ORSO. (R1, R5) Also missing is an analysis of sensitivity to different task reward formulations. (R2)
- **Problematic evaluation metric and baseline definition.** Performance is reported as a percentage of a “human-designed reward function,” but the definition and computation of this human-designed reward are not provided. The No Design (original task reward) should be the upper bound, yet Figure 2 shows No Design at roughly half of human design, which biases the comparison. The text claims ORSO outperforms the human baseline, but the metric may not reflect the true objective. (R1, R2, R5)
- **Memory and computational overhead.** ORSO maintains K separate policies (one per candidate reward), creating substantial memory overhead. Standard reward shaping typically uses a single policy. Additionally, the effective budget may be K×N rather than T, because each reward must be evaluated for at least N steps. This calls into question the efficiency claims in Figure 1. (R1)
- **Theoretical limitations.** Assumption 4.2 (monotonicity) is restrictive and its validity in the experimental domains is not discussed. There is no guarantee that the optimal policy for the selected shaped reward aligns with the optimal policy for the base reward. The proof for D1 lacks clarity (base case, inductive hypothesis). (R1, R2, R3, R5)
- **Underspecified iterative resampling.** The paper uses iterative resampling of reward candidates, but does not explain the frequency, criteria, or impact on performance. It is unclear whether resampling is part of ORSO or an external process. (R1, R4)
- **No support for annealing or changing rewards during training.** ORSO’s current formulation does not allow for annealing of shaping rewards, a common technique in reward shaping for stable training. (R4)
- **Limited task scope.** Experiments are confined to tasks where a good human-engineered reward already exists. The method’s generality for tasks where reward design is an open problem is not demonstrated. (R4)
- **Requires code-level MDP access.** The LLM-based reward generation requires access to the environment’s code details, which may not be feasible for non-code-based environments. This limitation is not acknowledged. (R1)
- **Discrepancy in Figure 4 regret values.** The paper states ORSO’s regret can become negative (outperforming human baseline), but the plotted normalized cumulative regret in Figure 4 has a minimum of zero. This discrepancy needs explanation. (R6)
- **Unclear novelty relative to prior work.** A paper with the same title (“ORSO: Accelerating Reward Design via Online Reward Selection and Policy Optimization”) was published in ARLET 2024. The differences should be clarified, and that work should be cited. (R6) It is also unclear whether ORSO introduces novel theoretical contributions or simply applies D^3RB. (R1, R4)
- **Frequent reward switching may cause instability.** Repeatedly alternating between reward functions can introduce inconsistent learning objectives (e.g., obstacle avoidance vs. resource collection in a maze), potentially destabilizing training. (R5)
- **Small reference list.** The number of references is small; more recent articles on reward shaping could be included. (R6)
- **Related work should be in main document.** (R3)
