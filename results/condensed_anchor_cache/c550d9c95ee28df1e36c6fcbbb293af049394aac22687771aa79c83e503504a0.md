## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates whether Large Language Models (LLMs) exhibit human-like response biases in survey questionnaires. The authors introduce BiasMonkey, a framework that generates biased and non-biased question pairs from Pew Research’s American Trends Panel (ATP), collects LLM responses (with temperature 1, letter-labeled options), and evaluates directional changes. Seven models are tested: Llama-2 (7B, 13B, 70B base and chat variants), Llama-2-70B-chat, Alpaca-7B, and GPT-3.5 (text-davinci-003, gpt-3.5-turbo). The main finding: only the base Llama-2-70B consistently shows human-like directional changes for bias pairs; most RLHF/instruction-tuned models fail to reflect human biases, and even models that show significant bias effects also change on non-bias perturbations (typos, letter swaps) to which humans are robust. An additional analysis using Wasserstein distance on U.S. opinion data reinforces Llama-2-70B’s relative alignment. Chain-of-thought prompting reduces significance of results. Two reviewers gave a score of 8 (very positive, strong contribution), while three gave 5 (moderate, citing limited depth and scope).

### Strengths
- The paper addresses a timely and important question: whether LLMs can serve as human proxies in surveys and opinion polling, with practical implications for behavioral research and social science.
- Well-written, polished, and careful in its arguments; clear presentation of experimental design and results.
- The inclusion of non-bias perturbation baselines (typos, letter swaps) is a strong methodological contribution—it demonstrates that models change responses even where humans do not, highlighting spurious sensitivity.
- Extensive experiments across multiple models, bias types, and prompting strategies (e.g., chain-of-thought) provide a comprehensive picture.
- Detailed discussion of implications and limitations, plus additional validation against human opinion data (Wasserstein distance) strengthen the conclusions.
- The finding that RLHF-tuned models are less representative (consistent with prior work) and that Llama-2-70B is promising is noteworthy and actionable.
- The detailed categorization of bias sources (e.g., response order, allow‑forbid asymmetry) is insightful and well-grounded in social psychology literature.

### Weaknesses
- **Dataset and generalizability concerns** (reviewers 1, 3, 4, 5): The study relies solely on the American Trends Panel (ATP) and only 50 questions. The original human‑bias studies used different questions; here, question pairs are partially LLM-generated rather than taken directly from the established literature (reviewer 1 explicitly questions this: “why not use original questions?”). This limits the generalizability of findings to other question types (open‑ended, socially sensitive) and datasets. Reviewer 5 notes that human responses were not collected on the exact same ATP pairs to verify the bias assumption—instead, human bias is assumed from prior work.
- **Lack of deeper explanatory analysis** (reviewer 3): The work is descriptive; it does not investigate *why* Llama-2-70B behaves differently (e.g., correlation with training data, architecture, or pre‑training objectives). Ablation studies or comparisons of models with known training differences are missing. Reviewer 2 similarly suggests a controlled study comparing base vs. instruction‑tuned counterparts, noting that claims about instruction‑tuning would be stronger with a direct base vs. chat pair (though Llama-2-70B vs. Llama-2-70B-chat partly addresses this).
- **Missing comparisons and models** (reviewer 2, 4): GPT-4 was not evaluated, despite being widely used as a human proxy. The selection of the 7 models is not fully justified—why these specific models and what criteria ensure representativeness? Reviewer 4 asks for clarification on generalizability.
- **Experimental details missing** (reviewer 5): Section 3.3 (direct human comparison) lacks details on which dataset and human responses were used—collected specifically for the study or from an existing source? Also, the authors do not report variance across random perturbations or multiple runs for non-bias perturbations (reviewer 5).
- **Framework presentation** (reviewer 4): BiasMonkey is a valuable tool but is underemphasized; the paper focuses on the negative finding about LLM shortcomings rather than highlighting the framework’s potential for broader bias evaluation.
- **Significance reporting** (reviewer 2): Table 3 would benefit from in‑line significance indicators (e.g., asterisks) rather than a separate table.
- **Temperature choice** (reviewer 2): Using temperature 1 with letter-labeled options is questioned—why not temperature 0 to reduce randomness? (The authors note means across 5 runs, but a lower temperature could still be justified.)
- **Instruction‑tuning explanation** (reviewer 2): The paper attributes differences to instruction‑tuning, but alternative explanations (e.g., filtering of unnatural perturbations like typos during training) are not explored. Future work should disentangle these factors.
- **Minor nits**: Inconsistent capitalization of “LLaMa” (reviewer 2).
