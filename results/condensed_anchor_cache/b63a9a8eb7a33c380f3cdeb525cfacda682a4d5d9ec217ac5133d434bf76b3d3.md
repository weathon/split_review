## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper tackles deepfake audio detection by introducing (1) DeepFakeVox-HQ, a large-scale dataset of 1.3M samples (270k high-quality deepfakes from 14 sources, including existing datasets, state-of-the-art synthesis models, and in-the-wild recordings), (2) a RandAugment-style data augmentation strategy adapted for audio, and (3) Frequency-Selective Adversarial Training (F-SAT), which applies adversarial perturbations only to high-frequency components. The authors show that DeepFakeVox-HQ improves baseline model performance by 33%, and F-SAT further improves accuracy by 7.7% on clean samples and 29.3% on corrupted/attacked samples over RawNet3. The paper is well-written and logically structured. Reviewers acknowledge the dataset as a valuable resource and the F-SAT method as an innovative approach to robustness, but raise concerns about experimental details, baseline fairness, dataset novelty, and generalization claims.

### Strengths
1. **DeepFakeVox-HQ dataset**: Substantial addition to the field—1.3M samples including 270k high-quality deepfakes from 14 sources. Addresses limitations of existing datasets in diversity and scale. Includes a test set with deepfake generation methods not covered in training and in-the-wild recordings. Likely to be a useful resource for benchmarking future detection models. (Reviewers 1,2,3,4)
2. **F-SAT method**: Novel frequency-selective adversarial training focusing on high-frequency features critical for detection yet vulnerable to manipulation. Enhances robustness without sacrificing accuracy on clean data. (Reviewers 1,2)
3. **Comprehensive experimental evaluation**: Extensive evaluation across standard benchmarks (ASVspoof2019, WaveFake) and the authors’ own test set. Ablation studies support effectiveness. (Reviewer 2)
4. **RandAugment for audio**: Effective adaptation of RandAugment from image processing to audio augmentation, improving detection accuracy on both clean and corrupted samples. Likely to be widely adopted if source code is user-friendly. (Reviewers 1,2)
5. **Related work and problem significance**: Well-researched related work; the paper tackles an important problem. Adversarial attack perspective is interesting. Results are up-to-date, combining existing datasets with samples from commercial models. (Reviewer 4)

### Weaknesses
1. **Missing experimental details**: 
   - The values of $\epsilon$ and $p$ (or $q$) used in adversarial training are only in a table caption in the appendix, not in the main body. (Reviewer 1)
   - Settings for adversarial attacks during AT, F-SAT, and evaluation are not specified (number of iterations, step size, attack type (PGD, FGSM), $\ell_p$ norm projection details). (Reviewer 1)
   - Parameters of augmentations in RandAugment (noise variance, time stretch factor, pitch shift range) are missing, even in the appendix. (Reviewer 1)
   - Training hyperparameters (learning rate, number of epochs for convergence) are missing. (Reviewer 4)
   - Software libraries used for implementation are not listed. (Reviewer 4)
2. **Dataset composition and quality details insufficient**: 
   - Missing breakdown of deepfake generation methods (specific models and parameters, especially for “noisy” deepfakes), number of audios per system split into training/test, demographic distribution (age, gender, accent) of speakers, and number of utterances from each prior dataset with splits and preprocessing steps. (Reviewer 1)
   - No quantitative measurement of synthetic speech quality (e.g., DNSMOS, NORESQA) to assess task difficulty. (Reviewer 1)
   - Dataset hours of speech and sample lengths not reported. (Reviewer 4)
   - Unclear if samples are aligned (same input sentences across synthesis models). (Reviewer 4)
   - WaveFake test samples’ inclusion in DeepFakeVox-HQ test set is ambiguous; if they overlap, it could bias results. (Reviewer 4)
   - WaveFake contains Japanese JSUT samples—the caption of Table 1 lists it as English-language, raising confusion; utterance numbers in Table 1 may exclude JSUT, which should be clarified. (Reviewer 4)
3. **Baseline adversarial training and comparison**: 
   - Baseline models were not subjected to adversarial training; only F-SAT received this enhancement, potentially biasing comparisons. Adversarially-trained versions of baselines using contemporary methods are needed for fair comparison. (Reviewer 2)
   - No comparison with other adversarial defenses for audio (e.g., defenses from speech/speaker recognition). (Reviewer 1)
   - Lack of comparison with recent adversarial defense techniques (referenced solutions are from 2018/2019). (Reviewer 3)
4. **Metric choice and clarity**: 
   - Accuracy is inappropriate for binary classification; F1-score and equal error rate would be better. Tables and plots with two accuracy metrics are confusing. (Reviewer 1)
   - Caption of Figure 9 should state that results are from F-SAT. (Reviewer 1)
   - Figure 8b should include results for 0-8K. (Reviewer 1)
5. **Novelty and generalization concerns (minority view from Reviewer 3)**:
   - The dataset is “the largest” but samples are generated using existing approaches; size alone is not a significant contribution—diversity and novelty matter. Simply compiling existing methods does not inherently create a significant contribution. (Reviewer 3)
   - Results on DeepFakeVox-HQ (Table 2) show significant superiority, but there are overlapping synthesis methods between training and testing data (Figure 6), so these results do not indicate out-of-distribution generalization. This overlap potentially inflates reported performance and limits practical applicability to unseen data. (Reviewer 3)
6. **Rationale and practicality issues**:
   - The rationale for why high-frequency components are more indicative of deepfake manipulation and more vulnerable to adversarial attacks is not sufficiently elaborated. (Reviewer 2)
   - F-SAT focuses on high-frequency components, but traditional compression (e.g., MP3) removes high frequencies, yet compression is not included in the corruption set (Figure 7). This omission is important for real-world applicability. (Reviewer 4)
   - Computational efficiency (training time, GPU memory, convergence epochs) of F-SAT versus baselines is not analyzed. (Reviewer 2)
7. **Additional missing comparisons and clarifications**:
   - No comparison with leading audio classification architectures like Audio Spectrogram Transformer (AST), which performed well on WaveFake in prior work. (Reviewer 4)
   - The WaveFake training source used for results in Table 2 is unclear; numbers may not be comparable to prior work without specifying which source network was used. (Reviewer 4)
   - Adversarial attacks in the frequency domain (and reverting to time domain) have been done before [2]; the paper should acknowledge this and clarify novelty beyond applying an existing technique. (Reviewer 1)
   - Common practice in audio adversarial attacks is to use Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) as the bound rather than $\ell_p$ bounds. SNR is more perceptually relevant and straightforward to convert. (Reviewer 1)
   - Low performance on the In-the-wild dataset (Figure 3) is not explained. (Reviewer 1)
   - No discussion of whether high-frequency perturbations used in F-SAT might be perceptible to human listeners. (Reviewer 2)
8. **Missing data sheet**: A data sheet outlining exact sources, utterance lengths per source, and alignment details is requested. (Reviewer 4)
9. **Sensitivity and formality**: Sensitivity of F-SAT to hyperparameters (frequency ranges, perturbation magnitudes) is not examined. (Reviewer 2)
