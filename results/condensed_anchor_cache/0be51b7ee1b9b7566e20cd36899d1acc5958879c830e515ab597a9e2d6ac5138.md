## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes I2VControl-Camera, a camera-controllable video generation framework. It decouples motion into camera-induced rigid motion and subject non-rigid motion, using point trajectories in the camera coordinate system (rather than only camera extrinsics) as control signals. Motion strength is modeled via higher-order trajectory components (second-order Jacobian, Eq.5). A data pipeline extracts these signals from videos using off-the-shelf depth estimation (Piccinelli et al., 2024) and point tracking (Karaev et al., 2023) with dynamic/static segmentation and linear motion fitting. An adapter architecture is trained on top of a base T2V model. Experimental results show good visual quality and natural subject motion. The training dataset consists of 30k video clips.

### Strengths
- The decoupling of motion into global (camera) and local (subject) components is sound and theoretically motivated; the definition of motion strength as deformation remaining after removing rigid motion aligns with elastic deformation in physics.
- The data pipeline is a practical solution that leverages existing computer vision techniques (depth estimation, point tracking, linear motion fitting) to extract control signals from in-the-wild videos; it resolves dataset limitations effectively.
- Point-based control signals provide dense representation, leading to temporally and spatially coherent video generation; this improves control precision compared to extrinsic-matrix methods.
- The generated videos exhibit impressive quality: natural subject motion, rare flickering or pixel inconsistencies even during camera movements, and handling of deformations in dynamic scenes with minimal camera movement.
- The adapter-based architecture is designed to be compatible with various base models (though this claim is not experimentally validated by reviewers).
- The paper is well-structured and concise with clear figures and formulas (R3, R4 noted this, while R1 found the writing problematic – see Weaknesses).
- One reviewer (R3, rating 8) was notably more positive, finding no hard limitations and recommending acceptance; this contrasts with the three reviewers who gave rating 6 and identified multiple weaknesses.

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient experimental validation and comparison**: Limited qualitative results are shown; more comparative visual results against existing methods (e.g., MotionLORA) are needed. The camera trajectory control capability is comparable to SOTA rather than surpassing it. Only single-component camera motions (zoom, rotation, translation) are demonstrated, not their combinations. No experiments test the claimed compatibility with various base models. Ablation or justification of the second-order motion representation (vs. linear or higher-order) is missing. More evidence for the introduction claims is requested.
- **Small training dataset and pipeline scalability**: Only 30k video clips are used, resulting in artifacts such as flat character bodies. The paper does not explain why a larger dataset was not used or whether the pipeline is robust and efficient enough for scaling. The pipeline is computationally expensive (depth estimation, iterative L-BFGS optimization) and suffers from reliance on potentially inaccurate depth estimation, hindering scalability to larger datasets or higher resolutions. Its robustness for challenging cases (e.g., fly-through video over ocean with water waves) is unclear.
- **Missing implementation and representation details**: The specific adapter architecture, loss functions, and optimization strategies are not provided, hindering reproducibility. Experimental setup details (L-BFGS tolerable error, acceptable ratio, iterations, video length) are omitted. Time complexity and memory requirements during training are not analyzed. The camera trajectory representation relies on an additional input (3D points) and projects them to 2D pixel locations, which may be similar across viewpoints – the rationale for this representation is unclear. The point trajectory maps contain unoccupied black regions; how the network is trained with these sparse maps and how 3D point clouds are projected to generate them (e.g., range of 2D coordinates, normalized device coordinates) needs clarification. The base video generation model used is not explicitly stated.
- **Exposition and clarity issues**: One reviewer (R1) found the paper poorly written, with misleading phrasing (e.g., “the entire 3D world can be divided into the static part and the dynamic part” at Line 161), and the point trajectory concept initially confusing (Line 170). This contrasts with R3 and R4 who found it well-written.
- **Limitations not discussed**: The paper fails to discuss cases where the motion decomposition into rigid and non-rigid components may be unsuitable (e.g., videos with substantial non-rigid motion like water waves) or potential failure modes.
- **User input unaddressed**: How users provide the dense point trajectories for inference is not specified. The reliance on dense point clouds increases computational cost and memory consumption; a sparse control point approach (e.g., from SfM) might offer a better trade-off, but no comparison or discussion is provided.
- **Lack of justification for second-order representation**: The choice of a second-order Jacobian (Eq.5) over a simple linear model or higher-order representations is not justified in terms of benefit versus computational overhead. No analysis of further gains from higher-order expansions is given. Additionally, the representation’s ability to handle multiple dynamic objects and the finest level of control achievable are not examined.
