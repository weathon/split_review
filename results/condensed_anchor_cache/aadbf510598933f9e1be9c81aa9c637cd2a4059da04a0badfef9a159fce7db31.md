## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Neur2RO, a machine-learning-driven instantiation of column-and-constraint generation (CCG) for two-stage robust optimization (2RO). A neural network (NN) is trained to approximate the value function of the second-stage problem given first-stage decisions and uncertainty realizations. The trained NN is embedded into the mixed-integer linear programming formulations of both the master problem and the adversarial subproblem within CCG, enabling efficient solution. Experiments on knapsack and capital budgeting benchmarks show that Neur2RO finds solutions within ~2% of best-known values in seconds, compared to hours for exact branch-and-price, and outperforms k-adaptability variants with a 10- to 100-fold speedup. Finite termination is proved when the initial feasible region is finite. Code and data are publicly available.

### Strengths
- The topic is interesting and the paper is easy to follow, well written, and clearly presents its arguments and experiments (R1, R2, R3).
- The approach is novel in integrating a neural network into standard mathematical programming within CCG, using the NN both in the subproblem and the master problem in a way that maintains convergence guarantees and mimics the original solver structure (R2, R3).
- The method is theoretically justified with a finite-termination guarantee (R2, R3).
- Significant performance improvement: problems that were previously intractable are now solved heuristically in tens of seconds, enabling the use of two-stage RO for larger or more complex settings (R3; also noted by R2 as works well on considered problems). Reviewer 3 particularly emphasizes the drastic improvement over prior methods.
- By working with an exact solver and using the true objective function, the resulting first-stage solution’s objective value corresponds to an actual scenario, and constraints are satisfied for enumerated scenarios (R3; also R2: “maintaining optimality guarantees”).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and differentiation from prior work**: The core idea of learning the second-stage value function with a neural network and embedding it into a MIP is similar to Neur2SP (Patel et al., 2022) for stochastic programming. The paper does not clearly explain the key differences and contributions over that work (R1). Even the more positive reviewer (R3) acknowledges it is not the first use of embedding NNs in optimization, though it is not a direct extension.
- **Limited application scope and generalizability**: The experimental evaluation is restricted to knapsack and capital budgeting, both essentially single-constraint packing problems (R1, R2, R3). It is unclear how the approach performs on problems with multiple interacting constraints, more decision variables, or more complex feasible regions (R2, R3). The more positive reviewer (R3) also notes that only two knapsack-like settings are demonstrated.
- **Lack of baseline comparisons**: There are limited baselines. A straightforward baseline would be using a neural network to directly predict the worst-case scenario for the second stage and adding that scenario to the master problem; the authors should either explain why this would not work or include it as a baseline (R1). Another possible baseline is a naive extension of Neur2SP that takes only first-stage decisions as input (R3). Neither is considered.
- **Missing sensitivity analysis and implementation details**: The paper does not explore the sensitivity of results to neural network architecture, hyperparameters (R2), or the impact of NN prediction errors on convergence and optimality gap (R1). The training process is not detailed, leaving questions about data requirements and computational resources (R2). The effect of NN complexity on the trade-off between solution time and relative error is not analyzed (R2).
- **Lack of evaluation on non-binary problems** (R2).
- **Need for more real-world case studies**, e.g., robust unit commitment in power systems (R1).
- **Methodological clarity**: The explanation of how prediction inaccuracy is compensated for in the master problem (Eq. 4a) is hard to follow and should be expressed more clearly (R1).
- **Training data and accuracy during solving**: The reviewers raise questions about whether the training samples, drawn randomly before solving, are representative of the regions explored during optimization (R3). A measure of NN accuracy over the course of the solution process would be informative (R3).
- **Performance over time**: The paper reports final runtimes and solution qualities, but it does not show solving performance over time (e.g., primal integral) or the quality of the baseline at the same time as Neur2RO terminates (R3).
- **Error statistics**: Some relative errors are close, so error bars or measures of variance would be helpful (R3).
