## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper introduces Vocabulary Disentangled Retrieval (VDR), a retrieval-based framework that uses natural language as a proxy for underlying data variation to drive disentangled representation learning. VDR employs a bi-encoder architecture that maps both query and passage representations into a vocabulary space (|V| dimensions, using the BERT tokenizer vocabulary), with each dimension indicating token relevance. A gating function produces sparse representations. Training uses contrastive learning. For multimodal retrieval, three modifications are introduced: (1) elu1p + top-k activation for deterministic sparsity, (2) a non-parametric entry loss between the query’s bag-of-words and the encoded representation to avoid irrelevant co-activations, and (3) a contrastive mask that activates a fixed fraction of batch activations but discards their contribution for positive samples, encouraging rare token participation. VDR supports both parametric and non-parametric (bag-of-words query) inference, the latter achieving up to 10x speedup. Experiments cover 15 benchmarks: text-to-text retrieval (trained on MS MARCO, tested on BEIR) and cross-modal retrieval (trained on YFCC15M, tested on ImageNet, MS COCO, Flickr30k). VDR achieves an 8.7% improvement in NDCG@10 on BEIR, 5.3% on MS COCO, and 6.0% on Flickr30k in mean recall (zero-shot), outperforming comparable bi-encoder retrievers. Human evaluation on image disentanglement (20 images, 5 tokens per image) found VDR’s representations satisfactory in 92% of cases versus 85% for a state-of-the-art image captioning model (BLIP). One reviewer (score 8) is strongly positive, praising the neat idea, clear presentation, well-motivated modifications, and impressive results—especially the non-parametric inference. The other two reviewers (both score 6) are more cautious, raising concerns about evaluation rigor, small-scale disentanglement validation, and whether the claimed interpretability gains are convincingly demonstrated.

### Strengths

- The idea of using the tokenizer vocabulary from a pre-trained model as discrete latent units is novel and neatly presented (R1, R3).
- The three modifications (elu1p+top-k, non-parametric entry loss, contrastive mask) are well motivated and clearly described (R1).
- Strong empirical results on both text-to-text and cross-modal retrieval: 8.7% improvement in NDCG@10 on BEIR, 5.3% on MS COCO, and 6.0% on Flickr30k in mean recall under zero-shot settings (R1, R3). VDR outperforms comparable bi-encoder retrievers (R1, R3).
- Non-parametric inference (bag-of-words query) achieves competitive retrieval performance while being up to 10x faster during inference, making it attractive for low-resource applications (R1, R2, R3).
- The method addresses an important problem (interpretability in retrieval) and could help users understand retrieval results (R2, R3).
- Human evaluation results indicate that the interpretability of VDR’s representations is on par with a state-of-the-art captioning model (92% vs 85% satisfactory coverage), supporting the core premise (R1).
- The approach is simple to implement, generalizes to both text and image retrieval, and the sparse embeddings are efficient (R3).

### Weaknesses

- **Latent representation tied to vocabulary**: Since the latent units are tied to a specific tokenizer vocabulary, the representations are inherently limited. For large or statistically learned vocabularies (e.g., XLM-RoBERTa with 250k tokens), individual units may not correspond to meaningful entities, reducing interpretability. A failure case regarding the tokenization of “Giraffe” is shown in the appendix (R1).
- **Human evaluation setup unfairly penalizes the captioning baseline**: For BLIP, evaluators were forced to extract only 5 tokens (matching VDR’s top‑k constraint) instead of using the full caption. This does not reflect the captioning model’s full capacity and imposes VDR’s limitations on the baseline. The complete caption should have been used as an upper bound (R1). Other reviewer raises concerns about the small scale (20 images, 6 in case study), unclear image selection (cherry-picked vs. random?), and lack of details about the human evaluation platform, rater selection, bias mitigation (e.g., position bias), and inter‑rater agreement metrics (R2).
- **Baseline comparison unclear**: Baselines are split into “primary” and “advanced” without a clear definition of what constitutes “advanced”. VDR outperforms primary baselines but does not necessarily outperform advanced ones. The distinction and whether the methods are fundamentally different (loss, training, architecture) is not justified, making the comparison hard to interpret (R2).
- **Unsubstantiated claims**: “Individuals employ natural language to interpret and distinguish objects” (page 2) is stated without a source; babies distinguish objects before speaking (R2). The definition of Information Retrieval on page 3 is very narrow and does not match the broader definition in the cited Manning, 2009 (R2).
- **Divergence between motivation and methodology**: While the paper motivates disentanglement using natural language as a guide, the methodology and experiments focus primarily on training effective retrievers. This inconsistency complicates the narrative and objectives (R3).
- **Limited analysis beyond retrieval**: The evaluation lacks analysis on non‑retrieval tasks, limiting understanding of how the disentangled representations benefit broader applications (R3). No significance tests are provided for the claimed margins (R2).
- **Missing ablations and explanations**: It is unclear whether the non‑parametric inference would still work without the non‑parametric entry loss (R1). The choice of elu1p over ReLU is not experimentally motivated (R3). It is not intuitive why dense representations (e.g., DPR) would perform much worse than the vocabulary-based disentangled representations (R3). The difference between “disentanglement” and “grounding” is not addressed (R2). Stability to larger vocabularies (e.g., XLM‑RoBERTa with 250k) is not explored (R1). The feasibility of decoupling the latent vocabulary from the encoder’s vocabulary (e.g., XLM‑RoBERTa encoder with BERT vocabulary) is not discussed (R1). Minor: AdamW is cited to Kingma & Ba (Adam) instead of Loshchilov & Hutter (R2); several typographic issues exist (R1).
