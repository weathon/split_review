## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Con-J, a generative judge trained via self-bootstrapped contrastive judgments from preference data. An LLM is prompted to generate positive and negative judgments each with rationales for a given preference pair, then the base LLM is trained with DPO to prefer the positive judgment over the negative. The method aims to replace scalar reward models with interpretable, bias-robust generative judges. Experiments across Creation, Math, and Code domains on RewardBench and internal benchmarks show Con-J outperforms scalar reward models, Auto-J, Prometheus 2, and other generative judges. The analysis covers robustness to bias, rationale consistency/correctness, and ablation of contrastive components.

### Strengths
- Novel approach: using self-generated contrastive judgment pairs with rationales for preference learning is clearly motivated and addresses interpretability and bias of scalar reward models (Reviewer 1, 3).
- Comprehensive empirical evaluation: trained on public datasets (Ultrafeedback, HH-RLHF, CodeContests), tested on widely used benchmarks like RewardBench, with baselines across multiple models and datasets (Reviewer 1, 2, 4).
- Informative analysis: includes studies on bias robustness, rationale consistency vs. correctness, ablation of positive-only and rationale-free variants, and where rationales fail to support the judgment (Reviewer 2, 3).
- Clear mathematical formulation (Section 3, eq.4, 5) and helpful illustrative examples/prompt structures (Appendix) supporting reproducibility (Reviewer 4).
- Reasonable ablations, especially on positive-only and without-rationale variants (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Overhead and feasibility**: Generating rationales adds significant computational cost to alignment training; waiting for generation before judging may not scale to cutting-edge models (Reviewer 1). [Severity: moderate]
- **No human evaluation of rationales**: No study of rationale quality or utility—no human annotation, verification, or baseline reasoning check. Filtering only checks final answer, not rationale correctness (Reviewer 1, 2, 4). [Severity: high; reviewer confidence: 4, 3, 4]
- **Interpretability benefit unvalidated**: The paper claims interpretability as a key advantage but never demonstrates its practical use, e.g., checking rationales for inconsistency or using them in human-in-the-loop alignment (Reviewer 1, 2). [Severity: high; reviewer 2 scores this as a weakness]
- **Main results not due to rationales**: Table 10 and Figure 4(b)(d) suggest that the main accuracy improvement comes from the regularization term in eq. 9, not the rationales themselves. Rationales only help bias robustness. Claims in abstract/introduction misleading (Reviewer 2). [Severity: high; reviewer confidence: 3]
- **Ablation clarity**: It is unclear exactly what parts of the method are removed in “Con-J without rationales” and how it differs from the scalar model (Reviewer 2). [Severity: moderate]
- **Rationale consistency analysis unconvincing**: Sections 4.3 and 4.5 lack clear definitions of “consistency” and “correctness.” The prompt template (Table 9) uses emotional tone, not consistency; correctness rubric for subjective tasks (e.g., Creation) is ambiguous. The surprising result that consistency does not improve is not explained, nor is the reliability of GPT-4o as a scorer assessed (Reviewer 3). [Severity: moderate]
- **Justification of DPO and SFT+DPO objective unclear**: The necessity of DPO’s formulation is not argued; SFT component’s role is weakly motivated (small α of 1e-6, far below the optimal α=0.01 in prior work [1]). No empirical analysis of how SFT affects log-likelihoods of positive/negative judgments or prevents reward overoptimization (Reviewer 4). [Severity: moderate; reviewer confidence: 4]
- **Missing details on judgment length**: No control or discussion of judgment length; long rationales needed for complex reasoning may introduce partial/biased judgments, amplifying the inconsistency between rationale and binary decision observed in Section 4.3 (Reviewer 4). [Severity: moderate]
- **Limited discussion of concurrent work**: Several recent generative reward model methods (e.g., arXiv 2408.15240, 2410.12832) are not compared or discussed; tradeoffs not addressed (Reviewer 2). [Severity: low]
- **Minor issues**: “Code” domain missing from a method description (line 259-261); LaTeX citation commands sometimes incorrect (Reviewer 3). Test split for Ultrafeedback not described (Reviewer 3). Missing citations of prior works using DPO+SFT combination (Reviewer 4). [Severity: low]

**Reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer rates the paper 8, finding the novelty clear and ablations reasonable, while three rate it 6, citing moderate novelty and gaps in rationale analysis, objective justification, and empirical validation of interpretability.
