## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a unified analysis of the influence of hyperparameter tuning and data complexity (meta-features N1, T2, C1) on the bias and variance of classification algorithms (primarily RF and SVM). Using 270–290 OpenML datasets and 304 hyperparameter configurations, the authors employ fANOVA and OLS regression. They conclude that data complexity has a substantially larger impact than hyperparameter tuning. Reviewers agree the paper is clearly written and the experiments are extensive, but concerns focus on limited scope, outdated framing, actionability of results, and methodological gaps. Reviewer 1 (score 1) considers the work outdated and trivial, while Reviewer 2 (score 5) finds it addresses an important gap; scores 3 from Reviewers 3 and 4 reflect mixed views.

### Strengths
- The paper is well structured and easy to read (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- It tackles an important issue for ML practitioners: the relative influence of dataset properties vs. hyperparameters on model performance (Reviewers 2, 4).
- The study is comprehensive in terms of number of datasets (~300) and hyperparameter configurations (304), enabling more convincing conclusions (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- The paper provides detailed arguments for why the observed patterns occur and what they indicate (Reviewer 2).
- Some OLS coefficient estimates align with common understanding of bias-variance tradeoffs for RF (e.g., min_samples_leaf, bootstrap, max_features) (Reviewer 2).
- The topic of bias/variance tradeoff is fundamentally important (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited algorithmic scope:** The experiments only consider RF and SVM (binary classification). Reviewers note that neural networks, XGBoost, and multi-class problems are omitted (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4). Reviewer 3 argues the title should explicitly state the results are limited to SVM and RF binary classifiers.
- **Outdated framing and contribution:** Reviewer 1 states the paper reads like it was written 15–20 years ago, with mostly old citations, and the main conclusion that dataset complexity matters is well known from introductory ML classes, offering no new information to the ICLR community. Other reviewers agree that the impact of data quality is already understood (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4).
- **Actionability concerns:** “Optimizing dataset complexity” is vague and often not under the practitioner’s control—whereas hyperparameter tuning is actionable (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 2 asks: if meta-features are immune to data transformation, how can practitioners benefit? If not, examples of preprocessing that reduce complexity (e.g., reweighting for class imbalance) should be included. Reviewer 4 notes that in practice one can freely tune parameters but cannot change the problem or dataset.
- **Misleading message:** Reviewer 3 strongly disagrees with the implication that hyperparameter tuning is not useful, giving a personal example of AUROC improving from 0.55 to 0.75 via tuning a neural network. The paper leaves the impression that tuning does not help in general, which is rebutted. Reviewer 4 adds that popular classifiers are chosen for being robust to hyperparameters, and the range of hyperparameters tested is limited (e.g., max_features not varied below 0.1 or above 0.9; only few kernels; limited to 1500 features, diminishing regularization importance).
- **Missing hyperparameter considerations:** The number of trees in RF—one of its most important hyperparameters—is not varied (Reviewer 2). Model selection (choosing a different classifier) is not considered as part of hyperparameter tuning, yet it could account for more attribution (Reviewer 4).
- **Limited meta-feature selection:** Only three complexity meta-features (N1, T2, C1) are used, but many more exist in the pymfe package. Reviewers ask why these three were chosen (Reviewer 2, 4). Reviewer 4 notes N1 is essentially a classifier (likely LDA), so the result that RF/SVM performance can be explained by another classifier is known and unsurprising; moreover, N1 is based on an outdated two-sample test. The attribution to C1 for SVM is particularly surprising and unexplained (Reviewer 4).
- **Lack of experiment design details:** No justification for comparing datasets of different sizes, response categories, and feature dimensions—0-1 loss is not typical for multiclass problems, and high-dimensional datasets behave differently from n>p (Reviewer 2). Hyperparameters like C and gamma have huge scale variations; it is unclear whether they were logarithmized before OLS (Reviewer 2). No explanation of how meta-features are defined or whether they are invariant to data transformations (Reviewer 2, 4). For fANOVA, the paper lacks a sketch of how it works (Reviewer 2).
- **Methodological concerns with fANOVA and OLS:** Why fANOVA? Are there issues with considering only pairwise interactions? (Reviewer 4). The assumption of no hidden confounding factors (l. 658) is questioned (Reviewer 4). Parameter configurations were sampled uniformly and independently from each hyperparameter range—this may underrepresent narrow but critical good ranges, making a hyperparameter seem less important than it is in practice (Reviewer 4).
- **Confusing or incomplete results discussion:** For variance, C1 accounts for 38% (vs. 71% for bias)—this is not a “similar trend”; other factors should be discussed (Reviewer 3). In 3.3, the claim that “higher values generally indicate greater classification difficulty” is only true for 2 of 3 meta-features (C1 is reversed); be specific (Reviewer 3). From the OLS analysis, if all features are significant, does that imply choosing a smaller C or certain kernel always helps? (Reviewer 2).
- **No inclusion of missing values or normalization:** All datasets have no missing values, a strong criterion (Reviewer 4). Not normalizing affects SVM and regularization, which is not how SVM is applied in practice (Reviewer 4).
- **Generalization beyond tabular data:** The study is restricted to tabular data; adding image classification datasets could show different behavior (Reviewer 3).
