## Merged Review

### Summary
The four reviewers largely acknowledge the paper’s motivation to address unrealistic assumptions of independent and uniform client participation in FL by modeling participation as a Markov chain with a minimum separation constraint \(R\). The paper proposes a debiasing algorithm for FedAvg and provides theoretical convergence analysis. Ratings range from 5 to 8: two reviewers (score 8) are very positive, one (score 6) is moderately positive, and one (score 5) is critical—particularly questioning the necessity of the Markov-chain modeling and the consistency of the setting.

### Strengths
1. **Markov-chain formulation of client participation.** The paper models participation as an \(R\)-th order Markov chain, capturing real-world non-uniform and correlated patterns (Rev1, Rev2, Rev3).
2. **Debiasing FedAvg algorithm.** Based on estimating the marginal stationary distribution of client sampling, the proposed algorithm provably converges to an unbiased solution (Rev1, Rev2, Rev3).
3. **Convergence analysis for both FedAvg and the debiasing algorithm.** The paper analyzes standard FedAvg (showing asymptotic bias) and provides convergence guarantees for the debiased version (Rev1, Rev2, Rev3).
4. **Identification that increasing minimum separation \(R\) reduces bias.** Supported by both theoretical and empirical results (Rev2, Rev3).
5. **Clear motivation.** The paper identifies that standard FL assumptions (independent, uniform client participation) are unrealistic (Rev3, Rev4).
6. **Thorough literature review** (Rev4).
7. **Empirical validation on synthetic and MNIST datasets** confirms the algorithm’s effectiveness (Rev3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Poor exposition and undefined notation.** Key notations such as \(\tau_{\text{mix}}\) (mixing time) and \(p_e\) are not clearly defined early, making theoretical arguments hard to follow. \(\tau_{\text{mix}}\)’s relation to Markov-chain parameters is unclear, and \(p_e\)’s precise derivation from the Markov chain is not stated, leading to ambiguity in implementation and analysis (Rev1).
2. **Incomplete theoretical range for \(R\).** The analysis covers \(R\) in \([0, M-2]\) but excludes \(R = M-1\). The analysis relies on the Markov chain being aperiodic to guarantee a unique stationary distribution; this condition fails for \(R = M-1\), creating a gap (Rev2).
3. **Simplistic experimental setup.** Clients are partitioned into \(M\) groups with exactly one group selected per round, which does not align with the proposed algorithm’s flexibility (any subset meeting the minimum separation). This may mask the effects of minimum separation and limit generalizability (Rev2).
4. **Limited datasets and tasks.** Experiments use only synthetic data and MNIST. More complex datasets (e.g., CIFAR-100, Shakespeare) and tasks (e.g., NLP) are needed to assess robustness (Rev2).
5. **Lack of discussion on test-set performance and \(R\) selection.** The paper claims larger \(R\) reduces bias but does not empirically examine how \(R\) affects test-set performance, convergence speed, or how to choose the best \(R\). The trade-off between bias reduction and potential slower convergence or poorer generalization when fewer clients participate per round is not explored (Rev3).
6. **Uniform minimum separation assumption.** The paper assumes a uniform \(R\) for all clients, which may not reflect real-world variability. Some clients might be available more frequently, and imposing a uniform \(R\) could be overly restrictive (Rev3).
7. **Inconsistency in the FL setting.** Line 121 uses \(p_i\) to model each client’s willingness to be sampled per round, allowing arbitrarily long absence with small probability, but line 128–129 states that cyclic participation corresponds to \(R = N/B - 1\) where in the last round all remaining \(B\) clients are definitely sampled. This inconsistency, along with the practical unreality of forcing clients to join, weakens the setting’s rigor (Rev4).
8. **No linear scalability.** As acknowledged in the Limitations section, the theoretical results do not enjoy linear scalability (Rev4).
9. **Necessity of the Markov-chain model questioned.** One reviewer (Rev4, rating 5) questions whether the Markov-chain model is necessary, arguing that Algorithm 1 essentially estimates \(p_i\) and inversely scales gradients by \(1/p_i\) to achieve uniform weighting. This contrasts with Rev1 and Rev2 (rating 8), who view the Markov-chain modeling as a key strength.
10. **Algorithm based on GD, not SGD.** The analysis assumes gradient descent; it is unclear what challenges arise with stochastic gradients (Rev1).
11. **Proof placement.** The proof of Proposition 1 should be moved to the appendix (Rev1).
12. **Theorem 2 limited to specific requirements.** It is unclear how to extend the result to more general settings (Rev2).
13. **Unaddressed extension to client-specific \(R_i\).** The paper claims each client can have its own \(R_i\); while Theorems 1 and 3 hold without modification, obtaining Theorem 2 for this case becomes more challenging, but no modifications are provided (Rev2).
14. **Extreme client heterogeneity.** How would a larger minimum separation \(R\) impact model performance under extreme heterogeneity among clients? (Rev3)
