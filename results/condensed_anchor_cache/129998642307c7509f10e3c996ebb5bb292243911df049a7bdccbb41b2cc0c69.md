## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers agree that TEMPO is well-written and demonstrates significant forecasting improvements over baselines, particularly in zero-shot and few-shot settings. The paper’s focus on interpretability and the joint modeling of time-series and text is seen as under-explored and valuable. However, there is substantial disagreement on novelty and experimental rigor: one reviewer rates it highly (score 8) and praises the motivation and state-of-the-art results, while the other two (scores 6 and 5) find the decomposition idea already used in prior work (e.g., N‑BEATS, N‑HITS, AutoFormer), question the theoretical contribution of Theorem 3.1, and note the limited improvement from the prompt pool. The absence of several standard benchmarks and baselines, insufficient experimental details, and the exclusive focus on decoder‑only LLMs (GPT) without exploring encoder‑based (BERT) or encoder‑decoder (T5) backbones are seen as major weaknesses.

### Strengths
1. **Clarity and writing.** The paper is well‑written and easy to follow, with a clear description of the input representation, prompt pool design, and experiments.
2. **Motivation.** The decomposition of trend, seasonality, and residuals is motivated both empirically and theoretically. The retrieval‑based prompt selection aims to handle non‑stationary time‑series with distribution shifts.
3. **Performance.** TEMPO achieves state‑of‑the‑art results on popular time‑series forecasting benchmarks in zero‑shot settings, and the improvements over baselines are consistent across datasets.
4. **Interpretability.** The paper sheds light on predictions via decomposition and prompting, and the joint modeling of time‑series and textual data (e.g., TETS dataset) is an under‑explored contribution.
5. **Theoretical insight.** Theorem 3.1 provides some theoretical backing for the design decisions, though its direct implications are debated.

### Weaknesses
1. **Novelty of decomposition.** The idea of decomposing time‑series into trend, seasonality, and residual (or similar) has been used by multiple prior deep‑learning works, such as N‑BEATS, N‑HITS, and AutoFormer. The authors’ claim that previous methods overlook these patterns is not fully justified.
2. **Theorem 3.1.** The theorem only shows that orthogonal bases cannot disentangle non‑orthogonal signals, a known linear‑algebra property. It does not directly prove the paper’s point that attention mechanisms fail to automatically separate trend and seasonality. Its implications for model design remain unclear.
3. **Prompt pool improvement is limited.** The gains from prompt pooling are marginal (e.g., in Table 9, removing prompts sometimes improves performance on certain datasets and horizons), despite the intuitive rationale and the added complexity of managing and retrieving prompts. The mechanism may not fully capture nuances of time‑series patterns.
   * *Reviewer 1 explicitly asks for insights on this marginal improvement.*
4. **Only decoder‑based LLMs considered.** The paper exclusively uses a decoder‑based GPT backbone. Encoder‑based (e.g., BERT) and encoder‑decoder (e.g., T5) models are not evaluated or even discussed, and no justification is given for this choice. This limits the generalizability of the findings.
5. **Incomplete benchmark suite.** 
   * Long‑horizon: missing Influenza‑like Illnesses (ILI) and Exchange‑rate datasets, which are standard in recent work (e.g., PatchTST, TimesNet).
   * Short‑horizon: missing M3, M4, and the large‑scale Monash Forecasting Archive.
   * Traffic forecasting results are omitted from Table 1 without explanation.
6. **Missing baseline comparisons.** Several strong baselines are absent: statistical methods (Auto‑ARIMA, Auto‑THETA, Auto‑ETS) and deep‑learning methods (N‑HITS, N‑BEATS). PromptCast is cited but not compared to.
7. **TETS dataset lacks details.** The collection of the TETS financial dataset is not clearly described. Important details missing: how financial data was preprocessed, how the ChatGPT API generated contextual information, and what criteria defined “neutral” context.
8. **Interpretability claims insufficiently supported.** The claim that prompts capture seasonal/trend components is based on only three example time‑series from one dataset; a dataset‑level analysis (e.g., clustering of prompts for all series) would be needed. The use of GAM and SHAP largely confirms expected behavior (residuals show no pattern) rather than providing deeper insight.
9. **Figure 2 result appears obvious.** The observation that trend is easier to learn and takes a large portion of the data is not surprising.
10. **Insufficient experimental details.**
    * Few‑shot learning: unclear whether finetuning on other datasets is required first.
    * Computation complexity not reported and not compared to other methods.
    * How prompts are initialized and trained is not explained.
    * For LLM‑based baselines (BERT, T5, LLaMA): what is the input, output, and how are they used for forecasting?
    * Context length for long‑horizon forecasting not specified.
    * For the 5 % / 10 % data usage: what constitutes a “data point”? 5–10 % seems large given multiple channels are processed independently.
11. **Domain transfer evaluation is limited.** The paper does not test generalizability across unrelated domains (e.g., train on weather and traffic, then apply to ETTm1 and ETTm2).
12. **Minor clarity and formatting issues.**
    * Typos: “inclduing”, “outpemforms”.
    * References still cite arXiv versions rather than accepted conference/workshop versions.

*Note: The above weaknesses incorporate reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms or requests for missing experiments/ablations.*
