## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces ML4MILP, an open-source benchmark dataset of 100,000 MILP instances across more than 70 classes, created to support machine learning-based MILP solvers. Instances are categorized using a novel classification method based on structural and neural embedding similarity metrics (bipartite graph representations and a graph autoencoder). The benchmark is used to compare traditional exact solvers, heuristic algorithms, and ML-based approaches, producing baseline results.

### Strengths
- Standardized benchmarks are often very useful for developing ML-based MILP solvers, and ML4MILP fills a gap by providing a large-scale, carefully categorized collection. (R1)
- The paper presents an original benchmark collection with 100,000 instances across over 70 heterogeneous classes, which is extensive and relevant for the ML/DL community as ML-based approaches become popular in this field. (R2, R4)
- The introduction and related work are well written, giving a self-contained overview of existing solvers, techniques, and related benchmarks, and highlighting the lack of a larger standardized benchmark for MILPs. (R2)
- The paper introduces a novel classification method using structural and neural embedding similarity metrics to improve the homogeneity of existing MILP datasets, and presents a comprehensive baseline library that benchmarks state-of-the-art solvers and ML algorithms. (R4)
- Based on structural and embedding distances, the authors show that existing benchmarks are often more heterogeneous than the proposed one, providing insight into instance structure. (R2)
- The dataset is provided in three parts, including a baseline library; the pros and cons of these aspects are well discussed. (R3)

Note: One reviewer (R4, rating 6) praised the extensive benchmark, novel classification method, and comprehensive baseline library, while the other three reviewers (R1, R2, R3, each rating 3) found the contribution limited for a main ML/DL conference and identified several technical shortcomings.

### Weaknesses
- **Homogeneity vs. heterogeneity tradeoff not discussed** (R1): The paper focuses on homogeneity within classes but does not discuss that heterogeneity might be beneficial for testing generalization. (Also R4 notes the need for classes at intermediate similarity levels for transfer learning.)
- **Limited statistical analysis and evaluation** (R1): Benchmarking results are presented only qualitatively (raw numbers, visualizations). No statistical tests or hypotheses are used. Presentation of Section 4.3 could be improved and more detailed. (R4 suggests including solving time as an additional metric beyond solution gap.)
- **Clustering method not justified** (R2, R3): Spectral clustering with Euclidean distance is used to classify instances, but the choice of method, parameters (e.g., number of clusters), and scalability for 100,000 instances are not substantiated. (R1 questions why a graph autoencoder is used over graph kernels for similarity; R4 notes that Figure 5 shows many intra-cluster distances larger than inter-cluster distances, raising doubts about the metrics’ ability to capture true homogeneity.)
- **“ML-based algorithms” too broad** (R2, R3): The term is used without clear scope (supervised/unsupervised, specific model types). This needs clarification.
- **Insufficient details on embeddings** (R2, R3): Structural embedding (Eq. 2) lacks a reference, limiting-case behavior, and definition of \(|I|\). Neural embedding (GNN autoencoder) architecture, training data, and training procedure are not described. (R1 asks: Is the autoencoder trained per class or globally? It seems per class but is not explicitly stated.)
- **Reclassification and NaN loss claim unsubstantiated** (R2, R3): The claim that reclassification addresses loss leading to NaN values is not supported by analysis linking dataset structure, reclassification, and training stability.
- **Repository inaccessible** (R2, R3): The provided link (anonymous.4open.science) returns connection timeout or download failure.
- **Missing limitations and future work** (R2, R3): No discussion of limitations or specific directions for future work.
- **Figures 6 & 7 issues** (R1, R2, R3): Legend has typo ‘befor’; x-axis labels missing; y-axis not defined (absolute loss or change?); learning appears to happen in one epoch, which is odd and inconsistent with existing literature. (R4 also asks whether the y-axis shows change or absolute values.)
- **Score transformation unclear** (R1, R2): Converting gap estimates to scores using a normal density function (L509) lacks justification (why not min-max or quantile normalization?) and details (how is the [0,100] range guaranteed?). Appendix D.2 contains residual text (“we appreciate the reviewers attention to this detail”), suggesting a prior submission was not cleaned up.
- **Comparison with existing benchmarks** (R1): The claim of “first open-source benchmark” (L74) is questionable given ML4CO (Gasse et al., 2022) and MIPlib. A clear comparison is needed.
- **Figures 2 and 8 unclear** (R1, R2): Figure 2 (left) is not a bipartite graph as described. Figure 8 uses color gradients for factor levels and unclear bar stacking; it is difficult to digest. (R2: Anytime performance curves are missing for some benchmarks; Tables 2 and 3 are redundant and one could be moved to the appendix.)
- **“Carefully” selection not explained** (R2): The criteria for selecting instances for the benchmark are not specified.
- **Optimizer and loss for GNN not stated** (R2): Section 4.2 lacks details on the optimizer and loss function used for the GNN training.
- **Minor issues** (R2): Mentioning DeepMind twice in the related work section within 20 lines is unusual; the classification of easy/medium/hard (L951) needs justification; several tables (10,11,12) are repetitive.
