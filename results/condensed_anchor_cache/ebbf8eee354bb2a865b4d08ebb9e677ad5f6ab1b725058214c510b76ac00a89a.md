## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper addresses the overlooked issue of fairness in dataset distillation (DD). It first demonstrates that biases in original datasets are exacerbated in condensed synthetic datasets. The authors propose FairDD, a framework that uses a protected-attribute-wise adversarial matching objective to ensure each group contributes equally to the synthetic set, rather than aligning with the majority-dominated overall distribution. The method is designed to be compatible with existing matching-based DD approaches (Distribution and Gradient Matching) without architectural changes. Theoretical analysis (Theorem 4.2) and experiments on ColorMNIST, C-FMNIST, CIFAR10-S, and CelebA show improvements in fairness metrics (e.g., DEO) while maintaining target classification accuracy across multiple architectures. Reviewers generally found the work well-written and the problem important, but raised concerns about novelty, theoretical depth, practical scalability, and experimental rigor.

### Strengths
- **First work to identify and address attribute unfairness in dataset distillation.** Reviewers 2, 3, and 4 considered this a novel contribution. Reviewer 1, however, argued the idea of class/group weighting is standard in class-imbalanced and fairness literature (citing [R1, R2]) and that the "adversarial" label is not reflected in the loss formulation. The minority view thus questions the ingenuity of the approach.
- **Simple, intuitive loss modification.** The proposed per-group matching loss is seen as a straightforward way to give each protected group equal influence in pulling the synthetic dataset (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4).
- **Theoretical justification provided.** Reviewers 2, 3, and 4 appreciate the theoretical analysis. Reviewer 1 noted that Theorem 4.2 is a variant of weighted ERM and that such results have been extensively studied (e.g., [R3]). Reviewer 3 added that a deeper, more granular analysis of how adversarial matching reduces bias at the feature level would strengthen the work.
- **Maintains accuracy while improving fairness.** FairDD mitigates the common accuracy–fairness trade-off (Reviewer 3).
- **Versatile across matching-based DD methods.** Demonstrated on both Distribution and Gradient Matching approaches (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4).
- **Strong experimental evidence of bias exacerbation.** The initial analysis showing that DD worsens dataset bias is a key motivation (Reviewer 2).
- **Extensive experiments across multiple datasets and architectures.** Reviewers 1, 2, and 4 note the comprehensive evaluation, though limitations exist (see Weaknesses).
- **Well-written and clear.** All reviewers agreed on presentation quality.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns (Reviewer 1).** The core weighted-matching idea is argued to be a direct application of class-imbalanced learning and existing fairness methods [R1, R2]. No comparison is made against a baseline built from [R2] (weighted ERM for fairness with loss weighting + SAM). The "adversarial" naming is misleading as the loss (Eq. 4) lacks an explicit min-max component (also raised by Reviewer 4).
- **Theoretical contribution limited.** Theorem 4.2 is a known weighted ERM bound (Reviewer 1). More detailed explanation of why adversarial matching reduces bias at the individual data-point or feature-representation level is needed (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing standard deviations and statistical significance.** No error bars or significance tests are reported, making it unclear if improvements are reliable (Reviewer 2).
- **Limited real-image dataset.** Evaluation on only one real-world dataset (CelebA); results on CIFAR-100 or ImageNet would strengthen practical applicability (Reviewers 2, 4).
- **Fairness gains are modest on some datasets.** On ColorMNIST and CelebA, the DEO_M metric is often comparable to the original dataset, indicating that the distilled data may still follow the original bias (Reviewer 2).
- **No evaluation on Vision Transformer architectures** (Reviewer 4).
- **Lack of computational cost analysis.** The paper does not discuss training time, memory consumption, or scalability of adversarial matching as the number of protected attributes or dataset size grows (Reviewer 3).
- **Scalability to multiple or intersecting protected attributes.** The method is only tested on binary, single-attribute settings (e.g., gender, race). Its behaviour with more groups or intersectional biases is unexplored (Reviewer 3).
- **Dependency on original dataset balance.** FairDD assumes each protected group is sufficiently large; no analysis is provided for scenarios with severe group imbalance or missing groups (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing visual examples.** Providing synthetic images from CelebA (e.g., in supplementary material) would help readers understand the fairness improvements (Reviewer 4).
- **Questions raised about the loss objective and design choices:**
    - Reviewer 1 questions whether the weighted centroid matching objective (Eq. 4) is correct, since two groups with different distributions can have the same centroid; clarification is needed.
    - Reviewer 2 asks: (1) Would upweighting minority groups inversely proportional to group size improve the loss? (2) How robust is FairDD when group labels are noisy (e.g., from a method like JTT [a])? (3) If the original dataset is group-balanced first, would traditional distillation suffice? (4) For CelebA, the chosen target attributes (attractive, big nose, young) are subjective; does fairness hold for objective attributes like blond hair (commonly studied in fairness literature)?
