## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies off-policy deep RL in environments with “mixed‑sign” reward functions (independent positive incentives and negative costs). The authors argue that standard off‑policy algorithms (SAC, TD3) suffer from asymmetric value‑estimation errors in this setting. They explore two remedies: periodic network resetting and a novel off‑policy actor‑critic method (C‑OPAC2) that avoids Q‑maximization in the policy update. Experiments are conducted on OpenAI Safety Gym tasks, comparing against SAC and TD baselines.

### Strengths
- The problem of mixed‑sign rewards and safety is relevant and important (R1,R2,R4).
- Writing is clear and engaging up to Section 4.2.1 (R1). The proposed method is detailed and easy to follow (R3). The algorithm is not difficult to implement (R2).
- The exploration of decomposing Q and V networks and of resetting seems a good approach (R2). The proposed algorithm is competitive in both constrained and unconstrained settings (R3). Experiments demonstrate great potential of the method (R4).
- **Minority positive reviewer**: Reviewer 3 (score 5, confidence 2) is more positive than the other three reviewers (scores 3), indicating that the algorithm may have merit despite the criticisms below. (Reviewer 3 also notes that their assessment might be limited by incomplete understanding of central parts or unfamiliarity with related work.)

### Weaknesses
- **Section 3 analysis is not convincing or clear.** Multiple reviewers (R1,R2,R3,R4) question the claim that errors in estimates of \(|Q_r|\) and \(|Q_c|\) grow in opposite directions under mixed‑sign rewards, and why this is more pronounced than with standard rewards. Mathematical or numerical evidence is lacking (R1,R3,R4). A concrete example showing how the bias manifests during training is missing (R3). No theoretical justification is given for why both Q networks cannot be underestimated (R4). The decomposition into two Q‑functions (one for reward, one for cost) is questioned: why not learn one Q function on total reward \(r_{\text{total}}\)? (R2,R4). Convergence or equivalence of the decomposition to single‑Q learning is not addressed (R2).
- **Proposed algorithm complexity and lack of ablation.**
  - C‑OPAC2 requires five different learning rates (λ_β, λ_ϕ, λ_ψ, λ_θ, λ_α), indicating high complexity (R1). What drives the improvements is unclear (R1).
  - No ablation studies isolate the contributions of different components (R3). The intuition for OPAC is not explained (R3). The removal of the Q‑maximization term is presented as crucial but is not sufficiently justified as the main contribution (R4).
  - Resetting is not new; how it addresses the core issue of biased value estimation is not explained (R2,R3).
- **Experimental evaluation is insufficient.**
  - Missing comparisons with proper constrained RL baselines (R1,R3,R4): e.g., Reward Constrained Policy Optimization (RCPO) [1] or other CMDP‑based methods known to perform best on Safety Gym (R1). Current baselines (SAC, TD3) are standard RL methods not designed for this setting (R1).
  - Results are mixed and do not show consistent improvement (R1): compare, e.g., DoggoGoal vs. CarPush in Figure 2.
  - In the constrained setting, the cost threshold \(d\) is chosen as half the cost of a fully‑trained TRPO agent unaware of cost – an ad‑hoc selection lacking justification (R2).
  - The constrained Lagrange multiplier approach essentially rewrites the reward as \(r + \beta c\); this is argued to be inadequate (R2).
- **Scope is limited.**
  - The setting of only positive and negative rewards is narrow. The problem could be cast as multi‑objective RL, a well‑studied area (R1 cites [2]).
  - More practical examples of mixed‑sign rewards are needed (R4). The relationship between cost terms in Eq. (8) and constraints in Section 4.2.1 is unclear – is the constraint part of the negative reward? (R4).
- **Incremental contributions.** The algorithm combines existing ideas (resetting, Lagrange multipliers, decomposition) without introducing fundamentally new concepts (R1,R2). The experimental validation is not comprehensive enough to support strong claims (R1).
- **Writing and presentation issues.** Section 4.2.1 and the experiments section are either too shallow or excessively detailed, making them hard to follow (R1). Notations in Algorithm 1 are not explained in Sections 3 and 4 (R4). Parts of the manuscript are not well‑written (R4).
