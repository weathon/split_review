## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper presents UniContact, a pipeline for contact synthesis in robotic manipulation. Given an object point cloud with normals, a geometric description of the robot end-effector, and a target wrench (desired 6-DoF force/torque), the method segments the point cloud into feasible contact regions, uses a neural network to predict contact points within those regions, then refines contacts and forces via optimization and generates collision-free joint angles through a two-stage inverse kinematic solver. The authors contribute a large-scale synthetic dataset (100k+ objects from 1k+ categories, multiple manipulators) and validate in simulation and a single real-robot setup.

### Strengths

- **Ambitious and physically intuitive formulation** – Grasping is framed as applying a specified wrench to the object, which is more principled than purely geometric approaches (R1, R3). The pipeline outputs both contact points and robot joint configurations directly from point clouds (R3).
- **Generality** – Designed to handle arbitrary robot end-effectors and previously unseen objects (R2). Training data is large-scale and diverse (millions of examples, 100k+ object models, 1k+ categories, multiple manipulator types), and the method generalizes to novel objects and new manipulators (R3). One reviewer (R3, score 5) was particularly positive about this generalization capability, in contrast to the other three reviewers (scores 3,3,3) who found the assumptions too restrictive.
- **Optimization for contact and collision avoidance** – The post-processing optimization refines contact points and forces, and the IK solver attempts to avoid penetration, giving a more complete pipeline than prior work like UniGrasp (R1, R4). The overall logic is clear and comprehensive (R4).
- **Real-robot experiment attempted** – Though only a setup figure was shown, the authors did include a real-world validation (R1). The contributed problem formulation and dataset could facilitate further research in generic robotic skills (R2).
- **Well-written and easy to follow** (R2) – The manuscript provides adequate context for non-expert readers.

### Weaknesses

- **Severely constraining assumptions limit real-world applicability** – Multiple reviewers highlighted the same fundamental gaps:
  - **No external forces considered** – The model assumes that only manipulation forces act on the object, ignoring gravity, friction, and support (e.g., table surface) forces. This is unrealistic for most tasks beyond simple grasping (R1, R2). A question from R2 ("How to extend to deal with other external forces?") is echoed as a missing capability.
  - **No collision checking with the environment** – The IK solver does not account for collisions with background objects (tables, clutter), restricting use to empty scenes. An extension to handle such collisions is requested (R1, R2). R4 notes that the method uses the classic Artificial Potential Field approach for collision avoidance; more advanced or contemporary methods could be compared.
  - **Point cloud preprocessing assumptions** – Relies on pre-segmented, clean object point clouds. Resilience to sensor noise, partial occlusion, segmentation errors, and partial observability is not evaluated (R1, R2). R2 asks specifically about robustness to RGB-D sensor noise and occlusions. The need for perfect segmentation contradicts the motivation of manipulating arbitrary, previously unseen objects in the wild (R1).
- **Insufficient experimental validation and comparisons** – The only baseline is UniGrasp; no ablation studies or detailed analysis are provided (R3). Comparative experiments with methods for generating human hand postures (which share similarity in collision-free generation) are lacking (R4). The paper also does not demonstrate applicability to more realistic tasks such as grasping multiple/stacked objects in cluttered environments (R3 question). 
- **Unclear how target wrench is obtained in practice** – The user must supply a target wrench for a given task; the effort required to generate realistic wrenches is not discussed, which undermines the practicality of the learning-based pipeline (R3). The conceptual value of using wrench is questioned: if the task is simply lifting, static friction suffices and wrench becomes meaningful only when dynamics are considered (R3 question).
- **Limited technical novelty for an ML venue** – The work is primarily an extension of UniGrasp with added contact optimization and IK solver; the representation learning contribution is minimal. Most reviewers agreed this is better suited to a robotics venue (R1, R2, R3). R4 also notes the use of Artificial Potential Field is a classic, not state-of-the-art, collision avoidance method.
- **Missing/inaccessible supplementary materials and incomplete paper sections** – The anonymized website containing additional information, demos, and videos is empty (R2, R3, R4). The supplementary material is identical to the main paper (R2). Specific sections appear unfinished: the related work section seems incomplete (R4), and several figures are not referenced in the text (R2, R3). Figure captions are missing and there are repeated "Fig.??" placeholders (R1, R2, R3).
- **Presentation issues and typos** – Multiple errors are noted: "Fricion Cone" instead of "Friction Cone" (Sec.2), "upper and lower limitations" (R1), "2n+1 configurations" (R1 questions the count, expecting 2n), and grammar errors including a cut phrase "The proposed framework." (R1, R2, R3).
