## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies curriculum reinforcement learning via causal lenses, addressing the problem that optimal decision rules may not transfer across source tasks when unobserved confounders exist. It derives a graphical condition for causally aligned tasks and proposes an algorithm to generate a causally aligned curriculum given qualitative causal knowledge. Experiments in two confounded grid-world environments (Button Maze, Colored Sokoban) validate the approach. Reviewers agree the problem is important and the causal perspective is novel, but disagree on experimental strength and practical scope.

### Strengths
- **Novel and significant problem.** The paper identifies a critical issue in automatic curriculum learning—the assumption of invariant optimal decision rules can fail due to unobserved confounders—and provides a clear causal framework for discussion.
- **Well-founded and rigorous method.** The approach (FindMaxEdit, expansion ordering) is rigorously specified using structural causal models, with all necessary details to reproduce the work.
- **Clear exposition.** The paper is well-written, uses the Sokoban example effectively for comprehension, and provides supporting examples to illustrate arguments.
- **Strong experimental results (as noted by one reviewer).** On Colored Sokoban and Button Maze, the proposed causally aligned curriculum shows clear advantages.
- **Useful contribution to the community.** The idea of leveraging causal knowledge to improve curriculum generation is valuable.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited experimental scope.** Experiments are restricted to tabular grid-world environments (Maze, Sokoban). Multiple reviewers note this is “toyish” and does not demonstrate the method’s applicability in high-dimensional or continuous state/action spaces, which are common in real-world RL. This mismatch between the claimed ability to overcome the curse of dimensionality and the simple tabular examples is a significant concern. (Strong disagreement: One reviewer found experiments “strong,” but three reviewers criticize the lack of complexity.)
- **Scalability concerns.** The algorithm appears designed for tabular MDPs; its computational complexity (time/space) with respect to state and action space size is not analyzed. If complexity grows significantly, it may contradict curriculum learning’s motivation. The method’s reliance on identifying causal relationships may become intractable in high-dimensional settings, and the paper does not discuss approximations or sample complexity. (Explicitly asked: How does FindCausalCurriculum scale with the size of state and action spaces?)
- **Reliance on prior causal knowledge.** The strategy requires access to a causal graph (SCM) of the environment, which limits applicability. The practicality of obtaining such a diagram for general target tasks is uncertain.
- **Artificial experimental setup for ALP-GMM baseline.** In Colored Sokoban, the baseline (ALP-GMM) is restricted to tasks where box color is fixed constant. If allowed to sample tasks with varying colors (e.g., where color acts as a proxy for the unobserved confounder), ALP-GMM might adaptively discover helpful tasks. The current setup makes it unclear whether ALP-GMM can handle the proxy scenario, and the comparison feels contrived. (Multiple reviewers raise this; Reviewer 1 formally contrasts the restricted baseline task space vs. the extensive space vs. the proposed curriculum’s space.)
- **Questions about internal consistency and theoretical claims.**
    - *Algorithm 3 (find optimal action sequence):* It is not explained how the action sequence {X₁,…,X_H} is obtained before learning. If an optimal sequence is known, why use RL?
    - *Theorem 3 condition:* The condition that actions X⁽ʲ⁾ ⊆ X⁽ʲ⁺¹⁾ suggests deterministic transitions and a unique optimal solution—is this required?
    - *Abstract flow:* The abstract initially states invariant optimal decision rules “does not necessarily hold,” then later says the condition characterizing causally aligned tasks “holds,” creating confusing juxtaposition.
- **Missing related work.** Three relevant papers are not discussed: Hu et al. (NeurIPS 2022), Cho et al. (2023), Huang et al. (NeurIPS 2022). Also, related works are relegated to the appendix without a pointer in the main text.
- **Presentation and figure issues.**
    - Figures are not standalone; captions are ambiguous. E.g., Figure 5 (causal vs. original curves across four columns) is confusing without context that the method augments existing curriculum generators. Figure 3(a) and (b) are identical except for edit indicators, but this difference is not highlighted in the caption.
    - Inputs/outputs of the method are not described in plain language; a summary figure or plain-language overview would help.
    - Minor clarity: “exogenous/endogenous” should be defined; notation “che”, “de”, “an” not immediately clear; SCM M* with * appears only once.
- **Motivation example (Example 1) is contrived.** The source task where box color is fixed to yellow assumes the curriculum designer is completely unaware of causal dependencies. The authors should verify how often existing non-causal methods actually generate misaligned tasks, and comment on expected benefits in domains with Markovian reward or no unobserved confounders.
