## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents CAuSE, a post-hoc framework that uses causal abstraction to generate natural language explanations for multimodal classifiers. It introduces a Counterfactual F1 metric to measure causal faithfulness and claims state-of-the-art results on e-SNLI-VE and Facebook Hateful Memes. The paper provides theoretical guarantees on causal abstraction under specific assumptions and includes empirical ablations and case studies.

### Strengths
- The paper introduces a novel loss function that jointly trains for explanation generation and classification with causal guarantees (R1).
- It provides theoretical results on causal abstraction between two neural networks under specific assumptions (R1). *However, these assumptions are also noted as restrictive (see Weaknesses).*
- Empirical ablation studies validate the need for multiple loss components, and case studies illustrate both successes and failures of the framework (R1).
- The architecture diagrams offer helpful intuition about the CAuSE framework (R1).
- The paper addresses an important area: generating faithful natural language explanations for vision-language models (R2, R3).
- It proposes a causality-based approach to improve explanation faithfulness (R2).
- Much work has gone into the architectural and training design, and a substantial number of results are amassed (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient validation of faithfulness**: The Counterfactual F1 metric only evaluates the classifier C₂, not the LLM explanation module φ₂, making conclusions about causal faithfulness unclear (R1, R2). The metric’s definition is ambiguous—it is not referenced in tables, and Algorithm 1 does not specify how the score is computed (R2). No experiments verify faithfulness when the model makes incorrect decisions or address how causal abstraction prevents models from injecting biases (R2). One reviewer (R1) found the baseline comparisons reasonable, but two reviewers (R2, R3) found the evaluation lacks comparison to other natural language explanation methods, black-box approaches, Chain-of-Thought prompting, and clarity on whether ground-truth explanations were used in few-shot prompts (R2, R3). The absence of adequate baselines makes it nearly impossible to assess the method’s relative performance (R3). R3 notes that claims of “state‑of‑the‑art” are unsubstantiated given the lack of comparable methods, and the strong claim that explanations “reflect underlying causal processes” is contradicted by the failure cases shown later (R3).
- **Restrictive theoretical assumptions**: Theorems 1 and 2 require classifiers C₁ and C₂ to share identical weights, a limitation that is not adequately justified (R1). The theoretical motivation for sharing weights between φ₁ and φ₂ is also unclear (R1).
- **Poor justification of design choices and unclear methodology**: Multiple components are insufficiently motivated, including: why two language models are needed and the purpose of reconstructing the input text (R1, R2); the need for both cross‑entropy loss L_C and teacher‑student loss L_TS (R1); how the representation c is split into c₀ and c₁ (R1); where the linguistically infused representation ĉ is used (R1, R2); why sum pooling is chosen over average pooling (R2); whether training is end‑to‑end or multistage (R2); and why VLM baselines are restricted to one‑sentence explanations (R1). The training process is not described clearly (R2). Figure 1 is inconsistent with the text: the multimodal encoder does not appear to take any text input, and the autoregressive generation scheme uses ground‑truth tokens (equations) while Figure 1 implies otherwise (R2).
- **Scalability not discussed**: Despite identifying scalability as a challenge for prior methods, the paper does not address scalability of the CAuSE framework (R1).
- **Readability and presentation issues**: The paper structure hinders linear reading, with frequent references to sections and tables that appear much later (R1). Definitions and diagrams are piled haphazardly, with vague justifications (e.g., “Recent studies (Madsen et al., 2024) have highlighted these faithfulness issues” without sufficient exposition) (R3). Figures 1 and 2 use small fonts and have insufficient captions (R1, R2). There is a typo at line 49: “Visual Language Models” (R3).
- **Missing analyses**: No statistics are provided on how often the framework produces gibberish text under counterfactual inputs when loss components are removed (R1). The Counterfactual F1 score is not explicitly referenced in any table (R2). The evaluation does not show whether the causal abstraction actually addresses the limitations mentioned in the Introduction (e.g., models injecting biases) (R2).
