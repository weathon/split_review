## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies continuous target shift in regression, proposing ReTaSA to estimate the importance weight function ω(y)=p_t(y)/p_s(y) via kernel density estimation, a regularized least-squares step, and theoretical consistency bounds. Two reviewers (scores 8) find the motivation convincing, the writing clear, and the approach technically sound with empirical improvement. Two reviewers (scores 6) acknowledge the interesting problem and theoretical contribution but raise significant concerns about experimental scope, hyperparameter selection, and real-world applicability. A key weakness repeatedly cited is the missing comparison with the prior work of Nguyen et al. (2016).

### Strengths
- The problem of target shift with a continuous (infinite-dimensional) label space is underexplored and the paper provides a natural, principled solution under the label shift assumption.  
- The method produces a continuous importance weight function, offering more flexibility than discrete approaches.  
- Theoretical justification is provided: consistency of the estimator and error rate bounds (Theorem 1), enhancing rigor.  
- The paper is well‑structured and easy to follow, with thorough references to related work.  
- Empirical results on synthetic and real‑world datasets show improvement over the KMM baseline.

### Weaknesses
- **Experimental scope**: All experiments use low‑dimensional, small‑scale datasets. Kernel density estimation is known to degrade in high‑dimensional spaces, so performance on high‑dimensional data (e.g., images, text) is not evaluated. The paper also does not discuss theoretical computational complexity or practical scalability in terms of sample sizes n,m and dimension p.  
- **Hyperparameter selection**: No clear guideline is provided for choosing the regularization parameter α across different datasets. One reviewer specifically asks how to select α in terms of sample size; a systematic procedure is needed for practitioners.  
- **Missing comparison with prior work**: The paper does not discuss or compare against Nguyen et al. (2016) (Asian Conference on Machine Learning), who study the same continuous target shift problem with a slightly different estimation procedure. The technical challenge and novelty of ReTaSA relative to this prior work are not clarified.  
- **Instability concerns**: When p_s(y) approaches zero or when the support of p_t(y) is not fully contained within the support of p_s(y), the density ratio estimation may become unstable. The paper does not investigate whether this issue is exacerbated in the continuous label space or propose variance‑reduction strategies.  
- **Sensitivity to noise/outliers**: The effect of anomalous data points in source or target domains on the estimated weight function is not analyzed. It is unclear whether the regularization adequately mitigates bias from such noise.  
- **Uncertainty quantification**: The propagation of uncertainty through the estimator and the reliability of confidence intervals around the estimated weight function are not discussed. The impact of the regularization parameter on these intervals is not examined.  
- **Gap between theory and application**: Theorem 1 bounds the L² error of ρ, but it is not shown whether a good L² estimate of ρ is necessary or sufficient for good test‑domain risk minimization. The connection between L² error and out‑of‑distribution adaptation performance is missing.  
- **Assumptions and real‑world relevance**: Some theoretical assumptions (e.g., β‑regularity, Assumption 5) are presented without intuition or concrete examples, making it hard to judge their relation to real‑world data. The label‑shift assumption itself is illustrated with a SOFA example that one reviewer found unconvincing.  
- **Ratio estimation approach**: The paper estimates ω by taking the ratio of two kernel density estimates (p̂_t / p̂_s). The density‑ratio estimation literature suggests that direct ratio estimation methods often outperform this two‑step approach in practice and can relax theoretical assumptions (e.g., Assumption 3).  
- **Missing details and clarity**: Several parts of the paper are vague or underspecified – the derivation of Eq. (1) from the target shift assumption, the adjoint operator relation (T and T*), the definition of ρ=ω−1 introduced late, the exact meaning of “Delta Accuracy” in experiments, and the interpretation of figures (e.g., flat lines in Figure 2, potentially mislabeled sub‑captions in Figure 3).  
- **Definition 2 confusion**: The bivariate kernel definition uses notation (x,y) that conflicts with the features and labels, and the stated support condition seems inconsistent with typical symmetric kernels. This definition is not used elsewhere in the paper.  
- **Remarks and minor errors**: Remark 1 (connection to Nadaraya‑Watson) is useful but could be explicit; Remark 8 contains a unclear sentence about dimension vs. smoothness; there is a typo (“better KMM‑Adaptation” missing “than”) on page 7; Figure font sizes are too small.
