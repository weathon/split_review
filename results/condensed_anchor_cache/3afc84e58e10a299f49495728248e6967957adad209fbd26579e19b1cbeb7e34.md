## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a unified alignment framework (ULMA) that integrates supervised fine-tuning with point-wise preference learning in a single loss, extending DPO to point-wise human preference data (binary positive/negative labels). The authors treat positive and negative samples differently (SFT loss on positive, additional KL regularization on negative), justify the formulation via gradient analysis, and release a new harmlessness dataset refined with GPT-4. Reviewers recognized the problem is important (pair-wise preference learning may miss nuances), but disagreed on the novelty and evaluation: one reviewer (score 8) praised the detailed mathematical exposition, while three others (scores 5–6) raised significant concerns about the limited experimental validation, lack of justification for point-wise superiority over pair-wise, and missing comparisons, ablations, and statistical tests.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses an important limitation of existing alignment methods: pair-wise preference data may not capture nuanced or polarized real-world preferences, making point-wise preference learning worth exploring (R4, R2, R1).
- The proposed unified framework (point-wise DPO) and the connection between SFT and point-wise preference learning are original contributions (R1).
- Detailed background, preliminaries, and mathematical derivation of the method are well presented and serve as a solid foundation (R3). R3 found the mathematical explanation particularly strong.
- The release of a new harmlessness dataset with high-quality demonstrations adds value to the community (R2).
- Experimental results show improvements in perplexity and preference scores compared to RLHF and vanilla DPO (R1). However, other reviewers questioned the validity/interpretation of these results (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Unconvincing justification of point-wise over pair-wise**: The paper lacks a rigorous analysis of scenarios where pairwise and pointwise preferences are interchangeable. For instance, a simple ranking algorithm derived from pairwise comparisons might achieve similar results, undermining the claimed novelty (R2). R4 further argues that the argument for point-wise superiority is insufficiently justified; binary signals cause information loss, and the method’s reliance on a clear positive/negative separation limits applicability to ambiguous, multi-faceted preferences (R4). R1 also notes that generalization to continuous or more complex metrics is not addressed—only binary classes were tested, and the framework’s handling of continuous labels was left as a question.
- **Limited evaluation scope**: The experiments are confined to a few datasets and tasks. R2 calls for evaluations on LLM tasks such as text summarization, question answering, and code generation. R4 requests evaluation on the RealToxicityPrompts benchmark for harmlessness comparison. R1 expects inclusion of more human preference datasets (e.g., from [1],[2]). R3 also noted the experiments are not very detailed.
- **Missing ablation and component analysis**: There is no ablation study to understand the contribution of each part of the loss function—especially the KL divergence term and the separate treatment of positive/negative samples. Without this, it is unclear whether simpler alternatives could achieve similar results (R4). The paper also fails to analyze hyperparameter sensitivity (e.g., weight of KL divergence; R4).
- **Unclear and potentially insufficient evaluation metrics**: The reported win rates (especially in the Golden HH dataset, close to 100%) are questionable. The baseline used (“chosen” answer from the dataset) makes the win-rates hard to interpret; tie-rates and lose-rates should be reported (R4). Metrics consistent with prior work (e.g., expected win rate or calibrated scores) would improve clarity (R4). R2 notes that many numbers in tables are close and no statistical significance tests (t-test, ANOVA) are provided to confirm observed improvements are not due to chance.
- **Implementation and reproducibility concerns**: R4 asks how the baseline results (DPO) were obtained given no official implementation, and whether the results align with the original DPO paper. R4 also questions the method’s sensitivity to hyperparameters.
- **Questions raised as criticisms** (included here as they overlap with missing analysis/ablation):
  - R1: What is the objective loss for continuous preference labels? How does the method handle positive and negative samples in non-binary cases?
  - R2: Could rule-based mapping from pairwise to pointwise work? The paper should test such simple baselines.
  - R4: How to deal with information loss when label is simplified to binary? Why are golden HH win-rates nearly 100%? How were baseline results computed and verified?

**Reviewer disagreement**: Reviewer 3 (score 8) found the theoretical development and presentation strong, and only noted the need for more detailed experiments; the majority of reviewers (three out of four, scores 5–6) were substantially more critical about the limited evaluation, insufficient justification of point-wise preference necessity, and missing comparisons and statistical tests.
