## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes using intrinsic moral rewards for reinforcement learning fine-tuning of LLM agents, instead of relying on implicit human preference data. It operationalizes Deontological and Utilitarian ethics within the Iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma (IPD), training agents against tit-for-tat opponents and in self-play. The approach is tested on generalization to other bimatrix games. Reviewers agreed on the importance of moral alignment for LLM agents and found the idea interesting, but expressed concerns about the hand-crafted nature of the rewards, scalability to real-world complexity, the limited role of the LLM compared to simple policies, and the narrowness of the experimental setting.

### Strengths

- The paper addresses a fundamental limitation of current alignment methods (noisy, opaque human preference data) and proposes an explicit value–encoding alternative (R1, R3).  
- The idea of using intrinsic game rewards for moral fine-tuning is interesting and potentially more transparent and scalable than human feedback (R1, R2, R3).  
- Experiments are clear, well-executed, thorough, and include extensive ablations: baselines (base model, game payoffs), self-play versus static opponent, anonymized action tokens, randomized token order, and implicit IPD descriptions to avoid confounders (R1, R2, R3).  
- Strategy visualizations are effective (R2).  
- Generalization testing uses different tokens to avoid simple token matching (R2).  
- The authors themselves identify many limitations, which improves the paper’s clarity and honesty (R3).  
- The topic of LLM agent alignment in strategic interactions is highly relevant and important (R2, R3).  

One reviewer found the approach “fantastic” and praised its directness, while another considered it insufficient to make useful progress on the direction (contrast: R1 vs. R2).

### Weaknesses

- **Scalability and real-world applicability:** The approach requires hand-specified rewards/payoff matrices; reviewers question how it can be extended to open-ended, messy environments where payoff structures are not easily defined (R1, R2, R3).  
- **Deontological rewards are hand-designed and effectively train tit-for-tat; one reviewer found this uninteresting and not surprising (R2).  
- **Utilitarian rewards are not realistic for LLM agents that must act in the user’s interest; an agent that always tries to appease other parties would be exploitable and unviable in practice (R2).  
- **Limited LLM contribution:** The results could be replicated with a simple parameterized conditional policy; the LLM does not appear to leverage its language understanding beyond mapping cooperate/defect labels, and adaptation to different payoff structures is not demonstrated (R2).  
- **Generalization is shallow:** The models learn a static cooperate/defect policy and apply it to other games; they do not adapt to strategic differences (e.g., the iterated defective coordination game) (R2).  
- **Missing baseline:** No comparison to simply prompting an LLM with the moral directive (e.g., “be utilitarian”), which would be a natural baseline (R1).  
- **Questions about model understanding:** Do the models truly understand the strategic situation? Evidence from follow-up questions or paraphrasing the IPD without explicit payoff matrices would be needed (R2).  
- **Terminology and presentation:** The term “unlearning” is misused – the experiment is simply fine-tuning with a different initialization (R2). The background section is too long; examples of the games should appear in the main text (R2). Font in Figure 2 is too small; why use memory-1 policies when more iterations fit in the context window? (R2).  
- **Limitation of scope:** The paper only tests on simple matrix games with two actions and short history; extension to more complex games, longer history, or richer action spaces is needed to increase impact (R3).  

One reviewer found the experiments clear and well-executed, while another maintained they are unable to make useful progress on the problem (contrast: R1 vs. R2). The overall contribution was rated high (8) by one reviewer, low (5) by another, and moderate (6) by a third.
