## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces MoReDrop and MoReDropL, methods to address the model distributional shift between training and inference caused by dropout. MoReDrop emphasizes the dense model loss and adds a regularization term from dense-sub model pairs without gradient updates to the sub-model. MoReDropL reduces computational cost by applying dropout only at the last layer. Experiments across multiple benchmarks (including GLUE and image classification) show performance improvements, but reviewers note that gains are marginal (~1%), the theoretical justification for reducing distributional shift is weak, mathematical formulations contain inconsistencies, and comparisons with related methods (EMA, self-distillation, other regularizers) are missing.

### Strengths

- **Novel approach to distributional shift**: MoReDrop directly targets the distributional shift problem by using the dense model loss as the primary loss, which is a departure from prior sub-model loss approaches. Reviewer 3 highlights this as original and impactful for the neural network community.
- **Well-structured and clear presentation**: The paper is logically organized, clearly defines the problem, and explains the methodology and variants (MoReDrop vs. MoReDropL) in an accessible manner (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Comprehensive experimental validation**: Experiments cover multiple domains (e.g., GLUE, image classification benchmarks) and demonstrate the scalability and efficiency of the proposed methods (Reviewer 1). Reviewer 3 specifically notes MoReDropL’s strong performance on the RTE task.
- **Simple and easy to implement**: The core idea is straightforward and requires only a regularization term added to the standard loss (Reviewer 2).
- **Practical implications**: The approach can lead to more stable and robust deep learning models, especially in settings where dropout is used (Reviewer 1). Reviewer 3 emphasizes the significance for managing distributional shifts.

### Weaknesses

- **Insufficient evidence that the method reduces distributional shift**: The paper claims that MoReDrop mitigates distributional shift via dense-to-sub regularization, but it does not provide explicit experimental metrics (e.g., comparison of distributional shift magnitude between MoReDrop and other methods) to substantiate this claim (Reviewer 1, 4). Reviewer 4 notes that the claim of achieving zero distributional shift is not supported by data.
- **Marginal performance improvement and efficiency trade-off**: Gains across datasets are only about 1%, which may fall within error bars. Given the longer training time required (due to the additional forward pass of the sub-model), the practical benefit is limited (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 4 also questions the robustness at high dropout rates, which may further diminish the advantage.
- **Mathematical inconsistencies, especially in Equation (4)**: The expectation is placed inside the exponent in the formulation, but during training the expectation is outside; these are not equivalent in general. The paper does not justify this discrepancy (Reviewer 4). The link between Theorem 1 and Equation (4) is unclear — the significance of the theorem for deriving or justifying the loss is not explained (Reviewer 1, 4).
- **Unclear backpropagation process**: Section 3.2 states that the shared-parameter sub-model \(M_i\) does not undergo gradient updates, yet Equation (5) includes its output in the loss. The paper does not explain how \(M_i\) remains unaffected while gradients flow through the computation graph (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing ablations and comparisons**:  
  - No ablation on the quality of sub-model predictions over the course of training (Reviewer 1).  
  - No experiments with different choices of the regularization function \(g(\cdot)\) beyond the default (Reviewer 1).  
  - The method resembles EMA in semi-supervised learning (e.g., [1]) and self-distillation (e.g., [2]) but lacks baseline comparisons and a literature review on these connections (Reviewer 1).  
  - It also resembles a recent work on regularization using an additional model without batch normalization (arXiv:2207.01548), but the differences are not discussed (Reviewer 2).  
- **Limited generalizability and scalability**: The paper only tests on standard benchmarks; results on corruption datasets (CIFAR-C, ImageNet-C) and domain generalization datasets (VLCS) are missing (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 3 asks about scalability in self-supervised learning and reinforcement learning — preliminary tests or deeper discussion would strengthen the paper.  
- **Unexplained behavior at extreme dropout rates**: MoReDrop claims robustness at high dropout rates (e.g., \(p=0.9\)), but this seems counterintuitive: at such high \(p\), the sub-model \(M_i\) is heavily masked, making the regularization term weak and the loss revert to standard cross-entropy. The paper does not clarify why the method still regularizes effectively. Similarly, the implications for \(p=0\) and \(p=1.0\) are not discussed (e.g., what is \(\mathbb{E}_{S_D}[l(D,S_D;\theta)]\) at \(p=1.0\)?) (Reviewer 1, 4).  
- **Missing experiments on alternative regularizer designs**: Does the main model use weight decay? Could a smaller model without dropout serve as the regularizer (e.g., with fewer layers/nodes)? Were different initializations for the regularizer network explored? (Reviewer 2).  
- **Unclear advantage of MoReDropL over MoReDrop**: Why does MoReDropL perform better from a distributional shift perspective, despite being a less expressive variant? (Reviewer 1).  
- **Effect of increasing the number of sub-models \(M\)**: How does performance scale with the number of \(M\)? Is there a saturation point? Does training time grow exponentially? (Reviewer 3).  
- **Deeper analysis of varying dropout rates**: The paper explores only a few dropout rates; a more systematic study (e.g., comparing \(p=0\) and \(p=1.0\)) would clarify the regularization term’s behavior (Reviewer 4).

**Note on reviewer disagreement**: Reviewer 3 (score 6, low confidence) is the most positive, highlighting the originality and robust experimental results, while Reviewer 4 (score 3) is the most critical, questioning the mathematical soundness and lack of experimental support for the central claims. Reviewers 1 and 2 (both score 5) generally see value but raise several concerns about theoretical justification, missing comparisons, and unclear contributions.
