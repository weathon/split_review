- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 5, 3, 1, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes LaMFA, a method to extract a deterministic finite automaton (DFA) from a neural language model trained on strings from a regular language. LaMFA works by clustering prefix hidden representations (from an LSTM or GPT) into states, denoising those clusters, then estimating transition probabilities. The method is evaluated on five regular languages of varying circuit complexity (AC0/TC0) and context complexity (local vs. global). The authors report that extracted DFAs often replicate and sometimes outperform the original LM, especially in out-of-distribution generalization.

Reviewer assessments diverge sharply. Reviewers 1, 2, and 4 find the approach interesting and the problem important, albeit with notable limitations (R2 and R4 rating 3, R1 rating 5). Reviewer 3 (rating 1, confidence 5) judges the paper deeply flawed: the number of test languages (5) is far too small to support the claims, design decisions outnumber data points, the method is an apparent simplification of past work (Louvain, Weiss et al., Merrill), results are contradictory (e.g., LSTM DFA exact, GPT-tiny DFA incorrect), and the paper is oblivious to constructive proofs that RNNs/Transformers can be hand-built to recognize finite automata. All reviewers agree that the empirical basis is thin and that comparisons to prior DFA-extraction methods are missing.

### Strengths

(Note: R3 assigns no strengths and judges the contribution minimal; these strengths are reported from R1, R2, and R4.)

1. **Method neatness and capability** (R1). The method and results neatly show that neural LMs are capable of learning the underlying DFA of a regular language from positive strings only.
2. **Important area** (R2). Analyzing black-box models using interpretable, easier-to-analyze representations like FSAs is valuable.
3. **Denoising procedure** (R2). The FSA denoising step (clustering refinement) appears new and intuitively useful, though its benefit is not explicitly measured.
4. **No negative sampling** (R4). The method extracts DFAs from LMs trained only on positive examples and does not require negative sampling; to R4’s knowledge this had not been tested on transformers before.

### Weaknesses

1. **Insufficient number and complexity of test languages.** Only five very simple regular languages are used, most with minimal DFA ≤3 states (R2, R3, R4). This is not enough to support claims about scaling or about the interplay between architecture and language complexity (R2, R4). R3 further notes that design decisions in the method outnumber the experimental samples by at least a factor of two, undermining confidence in the results. R4: it is unclear if the method scales to more complex languages.

2. **Missing comparisons to prior DFA-extraction work.** The paper does not provide a technical comparison to Weiss et al. (2018), which also extracts FSAs from any LM and offers theoretical guarantees (R2, R4). The algorithm is also very similar to standard DFA minimization (R4). R2 and R4 also note the omission of Merrill et al. (2022)’s state-merging approach; R3 points out the “clear (and somewhat unnerving) similarity” to Merrill’s work and asks why it was not cited in the related work. This lack of comparison makes it impossible to judge what LaMFA adds.

3. **Problems with the *alter* language.** The language is claimed to have local context dependency, but R4 argues it actually requires global context. R3 notes the extracted DFA for *alter*-GPT-tiny incorrectly accepts the string ``1``, which is not in the language. This undermines the claims about context complexity versus circuit complexity (R4).

4. **Issues with the *mdY* language.** The reported regex does not reflect the actual language tested because the authors only allow semantically valid dates (e.g., rejecting ``99/99/9999``), which requires many more than 11 DFA states (R4). The training/test split imposes constraints based on the sum of numbers, making the language hard to characterize (R4). The language contains only a finite number of strings (R4).

5. **Evaluation metrics are incomplete.** “Accuracy” measures only precision (R2, R4); a model that assigns all probability mass to one valid string can achieve 100%. Cross-entropy is reported but without a lower bound from the true DFA, so it is unclear when models are doing well (R4). The “valid rate” similarly only measures precision; a diversity measure would be needed (R2).

6. **Contradictory findings about generalization.** R1 notes a contradiction with findings in [1] (“Learning Transformer Programs”), where a transformer constrained to be compilable to a RASP program performed worse. R3 highlights internal contradictions: the LSTM extraction yields an exact DFA, yet the GPT-tiny extraction yields an extra state even after the merging algorithm that was supposed to collapse states. The claim that “larger models learn more nuanced representations” is an unsupported attempt to pass “it worked once and it did not work once” for a finding (R3). Similarly, the extracted DFA for *alter*-GPT-tiny incorrectly accepts ``1`` (R3).

7. **Conceptual oversight regarding known theoretical results.** The paper is oblivious to constructive proofs (Siegelmann, late 1990s) that RNNs (including LSTM) are Turing-complete and can be hand-built to recognize any finite automaton (R3). More recent work (Svete) does the same for Transformers. Hence, the paper’s non-findings about architecture differences may just be artifacts of gradient-based training, not insights about the models themselves (R3).

8. **Lack of statistical rigor.** To give credibility to claims about LaMFA’s effectiveness, a population of automatically generated small DFAs should be tested (R3). For example, to have 90% confidence that the reconstruction success rate lies between 45–55%, at least 273 distinct DFAs would be needed (R3). The current sample of 5 is far below statistical significance (R3).

9. **Method details unclear or arbitrary.** The pseudocode for state merging has no stopping condition (R4). The sharpening parameter T and frequency threshold τ0 are not justified (R4). It is unclear how to determine convergence on the minimal number of states k (R4). R2 asks for quantitative tests of the denoising effect. R3 notes that the dependence on two K parameters (number and desired number of clusters) voids all claims about language complexity learned by the neural model.

10. **Related work section inadequate.** The extended related work in the appendix mostly repeats the main text instead of providing a direct comparison to specific FSA extraction techniques (R2). R1 notes that results from [3] (Physics of Language Models) seem more generalizable and earlier.

11. **Relevance to real-world language models.** R1 questions how the findings on regular languages help explain how and why real-world language models work. R4 similarly asks how interpretability would scale to non-regular code or natural language.

12. **Quantity of models and stability.** R2 asks how many models were trained per dataset and how stable results are across different training runs – this is not reported.

13. **Presentation issues.** The paper contains grammatical mistakes (R1, R3, R4), especially in Section 2 (R3). R4 notes issues with Fig 1a (missing transition). Minor errors: parity is conventionally defined as odd number of 1s (R4); “training NNs on language recognition does not result in deterministic models per se” (R4); “repetitive strings” undefined (R4); elimination of empty strings not justified (R4). R2 notes that end0 could be recognized by a two-state FSA. R3: the paper’s presentation is self-congratulatory and overstates its significance.
