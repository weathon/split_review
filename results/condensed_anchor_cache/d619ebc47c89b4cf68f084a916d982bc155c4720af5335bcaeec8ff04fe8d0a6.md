## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes RObust Preference Optimization (ROPO), an iterative framework for aligning LLMs under noisy preference data. ROPO includes a noise-tolerant loss that suppresses gradients of high-uncertainty samples, a constrained optimization that dynamically weights samples, and a robustness-guided rejection sampling technique to recover information from discarded queries. Theoretical analysis shows DPO is not noise-tolerant and amplifies noise via its gradient weighting; ROPO's regularization mitigates this. Experiments on AlpacaEval and Reddit TL;DR with Mistral-7B and Llama-2-7B show that ROPO outperforms DPO and other baselines under both artificial noise (flipped labels) and a practical noise setting (preferring larger model responses over smaller ones), with gains increasing at higher noise rates.

### Strengths
- Well-motivated study addressing annotator noise in preference alignment, a critical issue for reliable policy models.  
- Thorough theoretical analysis (supported by extensive derivations) demonstrating DPO’s inability to discriminate noisy from clean samples and explaining how a conservative gradient weighting regularizer prevents overfitting and enables noise identification.  
- Iterative training approach that simultaneously filters noisy samples and optimizes the model, without requiring external teacher models.  
- Experimental results consistently show improvements over DPO and other baselines, with the margin widening as noise increases.  
- The rejection sampling component helps compensate for information lost during noise filtering.  
- Validation on both artificial noise and a practical noise scenario (biased preference for larger model outputs) adds practical credibility.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited evaluation scope**: Two reviewers consider the test set too narrow—only AlpacaEval and Reddit TL;DR are reported, with no results on widely-used instruction-following/alignment benchmarks such as Wildbench, Arena-Hard, or MT-Bench. One reviewer specifically flags this as a major gap.  
- **No human evaluation**: Analysis relies entirely on GPT-4 as the judge, which can be biased. One reviewer considers this a significant limitation.  
- **Incremental novelty of loss function**: The loss contribution is a regularization term that largely resembles DPO with a scaling coefficient; the specific form (including the normalization \(\frac{4\alpha}{(1+\alpha)^2}\)) is not strongly justified. One reviewer notes that using loss values to identify uncertain samples has already been explored in prior work (e.g., *Secrets of RLHF in Large Language Models Part II*).  
- **Ambiguous characterization of noise**: The paper lacks a clear definition of what constitutes noisy data, does not distinguish between different noise types (label errors, preference reversals, irrelevant samples), and does not estimate the noise rate in the data. The method for identifying noisy samples is not clearly articulated.  
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: The method introduces several hyperparameters (trade-off \(\alpha\), sample filtering ratio, plus the standard DPO \(\beta\)). Their selection appears highly variable across datasets and noise levels, with no practical guidance provided. Two reviewers worry this weakens the method’s practical applicability.  
- **Incomplete framework description**: The overall iterative workflow—how noisy sample filtering, rejection sampling, and noise-tolerant training are integrated—is unclear. The iterative process is not detailed; a full algorithm sketch is missing.  
- **Only one practical noise scenario examined**: The paper tests only one type of practical noise (preference for larger models over smaller ones), leaving other real-world noise distributions unexamined.  
- **Missing analyses and ablations**:  
  - It is unclear whether rejection sampling could introduce its own bias by favoring certain response types.  
  - No ablation is performed with alternative regularization strategies to justify the chosen form.  
  - The computational cost of the iterative method relative to simpler, non-iterative baselines is not discussed, especially for large models.  
  - No analysis of how performance varies with specific noise types or levels beyond the artificial flipping and the single practical setting.  
- **Minority reviewer is more critical** (score 5 vs. 6,6) and additionally points out the limited test datasets, absence of human evaluation, and prior art on loss-based noise identification—concerns partially echoed but not as strongly by the other reviewers.
