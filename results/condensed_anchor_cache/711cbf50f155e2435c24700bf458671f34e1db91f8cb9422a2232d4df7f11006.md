## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes the Chameleon dataset of AI-generated images that are genuinely challenging for human perception, evaluates 9 off-the-shelf detectors on it (finding that nearly all fail), and introduces the AIDE detector that combines high-level semantic features (CLIP) with low-level patchwise frequency/noise features (from highest/lowest frequency patches). AIDE achieves +3.5% on AIGCDetectBenchmark and +4.6% on GenImage over prior state-of-the-art. The paper argues that AI-generated image detection is far from solved despite these improvements. One reviewer (score 8) finds the work well-motivated and effective, while the other four (scores 6) are more critical of methodological novelty, dataset comprehensiveness, and evaluation depth.

### Strengths
- **Significant problem framing**: The paper demonstrates that the task of AI-generated image detection is far from solved, which is an important contribution given the prevalence of generative models. The Chameleon dataset highlights this gap effectively.
- **High-quality, real-world Chameleon dataset**: Collected from real-life scenarios with diverse categories (scene, object, animal, human). It is genuinely challenging for both humans and existing detectors, and provides a non-saturated benchmark.
- **Effective AIDE detector**: The combination of high-level semantics via CLIP and low-level patchwise features (selecting highest and lowest frequency patches) yields state-of-the-art results on AIGCDetectBenchmark (+3.5%), GenImage (+4.6%), and Chameleon. The approach is conceptually straightforward, and the hybrid feature design is well-motivated.
- **Thorough evaluation**: The paper evaluates 9 off-the-shelf AI-generated image detectors on Chameleon and provides extensive baseline comparisons on multiple benchmarks. The experiments show both the hardness of the new dataset and the effectiveness of AIDE.
- **Well-organized and easy to follow**: The paper is clear in its motivation, method, and experimental analysis.

### Weaknesses
- **Dataset sufficiency and generalizability**: Chameleon contains only 4 categories, with some categories having very few samples (e.g., 313 fake animal images). This raises concerns about its ability to cover diverse real-world image distributions and whether it is representative enough to serve as a general benchmark. A per-category accuracy breakdown (scene, object, animal, human) is needed to understand failure patterns. (Reviewers 3, 5)
- **Incomplete dataset comparison and justification**: The paper does not provide a comprehensive comparison of Chameleon against existing datasets (CNNSpot, AIGCDetect, GenImage, WildFake) in terms of data size, sources, generation models, image quality, and artifact types. The justification for why a new dataset is necessary—given the existence of several others—is insufficient. (Reviewers 2, 4, 5)
- **Methodological novelty concerns**: The combination of patch-level and global features (including frequency-based patch selection) is not sufficiently differentiated from existing techniques. A mixture-of-experts approach for fake image detection [1] is absent from the baselines and should be compared. More recent detectors (e.g., LaRE^2, Forgery-aware Adaptive Transformer) are also missing. The use of frequency-based analysis and semantic embeddings is a common practice; the specific novelty of the proposed block-scoring mechanism is unclear. (Reviewers 3, 5)
- **Missing ablation and theoretical explanation**: The rationale for why patches with the highest and lowest frequency highlight distinctive characteristics of AI-generated images is not theoretically justified. Additionally, Figure 4 does not provide convincing evidence that SFE (or PFE) focuses on specific semantic (or noise-related) clues; Grad-CAM visualizations would be more appropriate. (Reviewer 4)
- **Incomplete robustness evaluation**: JPEG compression is only tested at QF=95 and QF=90. Real-world images often undergo more aggressive compression; QF=75 and QF=50 should be evaluated. Similarly, Gaussian blur should be tested at sigma=3 and 4. The paper should also list accuracy under no perturbations in Table 6 for convenient comparison. (Reviewers 1, 3)
- **Lack of computational cost analysis**: The inference throughput and total FLOPs/params of AIDE are not provided. Since AIDE uses multiple SRMs, ResNet50, and CLIP/ConvNext, it is much larger than simpler detectors like Gendet (ResNet-50 only). Without this analysis, it is unclear whether the claimed performance improvements are due to increased computational budget or architectural novelty. (Reviewer 5)
- **Missing real-world validation**: The paper does not collect or evaluate on real-world fake news images from the internet, which would add crucial practical testing and distinct artifact types. (Reviewer 2)
- **Dataset construction and validation**: The principles for dataset construction (p4) are stated, but it is unclear whether generated images were strictly filtered and validated using the 9 baseline detectors beforehand to make the dataset challenging. Human studies to compare visual quality against existing datasets would strengthen the work. (Reviewers 1, 3)
- **Minor issues with comparisons**: In Table 2, comparing the whole Chameleon dataset with subsets of AIGCDetectBenchmark is not a fair comparison. The claim that Chameleon “offers greater magnitude” is incorrect because the proposed dataset is smaller than AIGCDetectBenchmark overall and significantly smaller than GenImage. (Reviewer 4)
- **Disagreement on simplicity versus novelty**: One reviewer (score 8) views AIDE as straightforward and effective; others (score 6) see the same approach as lacking novel differentiation. This contrast should be noted. (Reviewers 1, 5)

### Reviewer Contrasts
- The most positive reviewer (score 8) finds the dataset high-quality, the method clear and effective, and the experiments convincing, with only minor requests for human studies and additional compression evaluations. The other four reviewers (scores 6) are more critical, particularly regarding dataset representativeness, method novelty, missing baselines, and lack of efficiency analysis. The most negative reviewer (score 5) questions the novelty of the entire high-low frequency patch approach and demands computational cost justification.
