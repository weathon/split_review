## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes DiscQuant, a data-dependent rounding algorithm for post-training quantization of neural networks, inspired by discrepancy theory. The authors formulate rounding as a problem of minimizing KL divergence between original and quantized model output distributions, and provide theoretical guarantees (Theorem 3.3) that under a low-rank assumption on gradient covariance, the expected approximation error on the true data distribution can be bounded arbitrarily small with sufficiently many samples. The algorithm is agnostic to the quantization grid. Empirical results on Phi-3-mini-3.8B and Llama-3.1-8B models across tasks (GSM8k, ARC Challenge, PIQA) and quantization formats (block scaling, incoherence processing) show DiscQuant outperforms RTN and GPTQ. For example, rounding Phi3mini-3.8B to 3.25 bits per parameter achieves 64% accuracy on GSM8k vs GPTQ's 54% and RTN's 31% (original 84%).

### Strengths

- **Novel application of discrepancy theory**: The paper introduces a new approach by applying discrepancy theory to the rounding step of quantization, moving beyond the usual focus on quantization grid design. This opens a promising direction for quantization research and is considered an innovative and important insight.
- **Solid theoretical framework**: The theoretical analysis is rigorous, with clearly stated and empirically validated assumptions (low-rank gradient covariance, first-order error approximation). The generalization bound (Theorem 3.3) is well derived, and the authors go to reasonable length to justify assumptions rather than hide them. Figures 3 and 4 empirically support the key assumptions.
- **Grid-agnostic algorithm**: DiscQuant is independent of the specific quantization grid, making it broadly applicable across grid choices (e.g., block scaling, incoherence processing). Practical heuristics are introduced to address the complexity of the Lovett-Meka algorithm.
- **Strong empirical results**: DiscQuant consistently and significantly outperforms the baselines RTN and GPTQ across multiple benchmarks (GSM8k, ARC Challenge, PIQA), at various bit-widths, especially at low bits. Results are shown on two model families (Phi-3-mini-3.8B, Llama-3.1-8B) and multiple quantization formats, demonstrating robustness and generalizability.
- **Clear and well-structured presentation**: The paper is generally well-written, with good organization of theoretical framework and algorithmic details. Figures and tables are effectively used to illustrate concepts and results. The motivation for the approach is clearly delineated.
- **Potential for impact**: The work addresses an important practical problem (efficient deployment of large models) and may inspire further research connecting discrepancy theory to neural network quantization.

### Weaknesses

- **Missing comparisons with recent state-of-the-art PTQ methods**: The paper only compares with RTN and GPTQ, which are older baselines. Several reviewers noted that newer methods (e.g., CDQuant, AdaQuant-all, MagR, OmniQuant, QuIP, SpinQuant) often report stronger results on comparable models and tasks. The absence of these comparisons makes it unclear how DiscQuant ranks among current PTQ techniques. Reviewer 3 specifically pointed out that the reported performance seems worse than published results for some of these newer methods, and the large gains over GPTQ/RTN may partly reflect increased computational resources rather than the algorithmic novelty. Including such baselines is essential to isolate the contribution.
- **Computational cost and practicality concerns**: DiscQuant requires solving an optimization problem (Equation 3) that appears to involve full-model backpropagation and two model copies in memory (lines 405–406). Reviewers question whether this cost is similar to full model training or quantization-aware training, which would be excessive for post-training quantization. Explicit time and memory cost comparisons with RTN and GPTQ are missing. The paper is also ambiguous about whether quantization is applied per-layer or globally, in what order, and whether gradients are recalculated after each layer is quantized. These details are critical for understanding scalability to larger models (e.g., Llama-3.1-70B) and practical deployment.
- **Limited experimental scope**: Experiments are confined to two relatively small LLMs (3.8B, 8B) and text-based tasks. The method’s applicability to vision-language models (e.g., LLaVA-1.6-34B), larger LLMs (e.g., 70B), CNNs, or vision transformers remains untested. Additionally, the method requires access to task-specific calibration data, which may be a bottleneck for practitioners; the data choice is itself non-trivial (as the paper notes). The high computational overhead combined with data requirements reduces practical feasibility.
- **Clarity deficits in theoretical and algorithmic exposition**: Reviewers found the presentation of discrepancy theory and the Lovett-Meka algorithm insufficiently explained for readers unfamiliar with these topics. The connection between the random walk and the rounding process is not intuitive. A more geometric or high-level explanation of the algorithm (e.g., the minimization along an arbitrary direction c in Section 4, geometric intuition akin to Figure 1) would help. The complexity of the Lovett-Meka algorithm (iterative SDP) and how the paper’s heuristics reduce it are unclear.
- **Ambiguity and lack of analysis regarding the low-rank assumption**: The theory relies on gradient covariance being approximately low-rank, but there is no discussion of performance or alternative strategies when this assumption fails (e.g., higher-rank gradient spaces, different architectures/datasets). It is also ambiguous whether the gradients are computed per layer or across the full network, how quantization order is determined, and whether gradients are recalculated after each layer is quantized. The theorem’s bound does not reflect the distribution of weight values, which is known to affect PTQ quality (e.g., incoherence processing). This discrepancy merits explanation.
- **Missing ablation studies and design choices**: Questions remain about why the random walk approach is necessary—could simpler vertex-finding methods work? The paper assumes parameters should not be changed too much (lines 249–251), but the drawbacks of relaxing this restriction are unexplored. The use of KL divergence is not compared with alternative metrics like MSE or activation-based losses. These ablations would strengthen the paper.
- **Minor issues**: The abstract should be a single paragraph per ICLR guidelines. There are minor typos (line 133: "let's" should be "lets"; line 379: "close *to* the polytype"). Reviewers also asked whether the authors plan to release their code.
