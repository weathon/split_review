## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewer scores range from 5 to 8. The highest-scoring reviewer (8) found the formulation compelling, praising the generality achieved via the right derivative of the objective and the ability to interpolate between existing formulations. Two reviewers (5) questioned the practical significance of the unification and the strength of the assumptions, while another (6) found the work solid but limited by the experimental evaluation. All reviewers agree that the paper is well-written and clearly organized, and that the proposed Thompson sampling algorithm is sound with theoretical analysis. Disagreement centers on the novelty of the unification and the realism of the finite-set assumptions.

### Strengths
- Well-written and easy to follow; all reviewers commend clarity and exposition.
- Tackles an interesting and important problem: unifying multiple robust/cautious BO formulations (DRBO, worst-case sensitivity, mean-risk tradeoff, robust satisficing) under a single BOCU framework.
- The generalization via the first derivative of the DRBO objective is insightful, enabling the framework to recover and even interpolate between existing objectives (noted by R4).
- Proposes a general Thompson sampling algorithm that can optimize any objective within the framework; the method is sound and theoretically grounded (R1, R3).
- Provides theoretical regret analysis (sublinear bound) under stated assumptions; R1 and R3 consider this a strength.
- Experiments (GP, Hartmann 3, plant growth simulator, COVID epidemic model) confirm sublinear regret trends and show TS-BOCU works well across settings, especially in the majority of cases (R1, R4).
- Framework is comprehensive, relating many previously proposed formulations (R2).
- By tuning hyperparameters α, β, ε, the framework can realize novel formulations not covered by prior work, offering more expressive optimization design (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Assumptions on finite sets and known reference distribution:** Several reviewers criticize the assumptions that \(\mathcal{X}\) and \(\mathcal{C}\) are finite and that the reference distribution \(\boldsymbol p\) is known at the start. R2 and R3 argue these are too strong and not practically meaningful; R2 notes that the theoretical results are therefore limited. R4 is confused about necessity—does the theoretical guarantee require finiteness, or can it be extended as hinted in Section 4.1? The experiments (e.g., Hartmann) appear to run on continuous spaces, raising a mismatch. R4 also questions the practical impact if \(\epsilon_t = d(\boldsymbol p_t, \boldsymbol p^*)\) does not approach 0 with probability 1 and whether performance degrades when \(\boldsymbol p^*\) is hard to learn.
- **Significance of unification questioned:** R3 argues that the unification appears straightforward (adding two parameters) and that the additional technical challenge in algorithm design or analysis is unclear. It is not obvious whether the newly enabled objectives are practically significant. R2 similarly notes that the experimental reasoning does not convincingly demonstrate superiority.
- **Limited experimental evaluation and analysis:**
  - All problems have low dimensions (2 to 5); scalability to higher-dimensional problems (common in real-world BO) is not addressed (R1).
  - Analysis of experimental results is insufficient. R1: only one paragraph discusses Figure 2; lacks insight into behavior under different uncertainty objectives, kernel choices, or optimization trajectories. R2: reasoning and justification behind results are not appropriately provided. R4: DRO on the Infection problem shows near-linear regret; a deeper investigation into why TS-BOCU struggles there is needed. Also, which types of problems cause poor performance?
  - The COVID infection DRO case performing poorly is unexplained; no comparison of optimization trajectories (R1).
- **Questions that overlap with criticisms (converted to weaknesses):**
  - Are the assumptions in Theorem 4.1 standard for this topic? Are they practically plausible? (R1)
  - Is the maximum information gain \(\gamma_T\) bounded as in standard BO, and which kernels guarantee this? (R1)
  - Why finite context and action spaces? Are they required by prior works, or is this a novel restriction? (R3)
  - Is the first-derivative objective novel only in GP literature, or also in other areas? (R3)
  - Instead of having both α and β, couldn’t the formulation follow mean-risk tradeoff with only one weight for \(\delta(\mathbf{x})\)? Does \(\alpha=\beta=1\) give a different objective than \(\alpha=\beta=0.5\) for the same ε? (R4)
  - The paper could benefit from practical guidance for setting α, β, and ε in Section 3.1 (R4).
- **Presentation issues:** R2 finds the paper hard to follow due to grammatical errors and complex sentences (e.g., two “while”s, “We assume that … some reference distribution … is known that …” , “a probability vector in \(\mathbb{R}^n\)” should be \([0,1]^n\)). R2 also notes table captions should be on top per ICLR format. R1 and R4 find no major presentation issues, but R2 rates presentation “poor”.
