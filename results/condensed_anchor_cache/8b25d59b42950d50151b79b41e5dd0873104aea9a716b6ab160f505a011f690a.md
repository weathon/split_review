## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes an adaptive method for online importance sampling and data pruning in stochastic gradient optimization. The importance weights are based on the norm of the loss derivative with respect to the network output. The authors evaluate their method on classification and regression tasks (e.g., CIFAR-10/100, MNIST) and show some improvements over uniform sampling and prior importance sampling methods.

### Strengths
- The idea of using the same metric for both importance sampling and data pruning is interesting (R1).
- Figure 1 numerically verifies that the learned importance weights are meaningful and provide intuition for why the method could improve over uniform sampling (R1).
- The framework is evaluated on multiple datasets and different tasks; the results show consistent gains across tasks (R3).
- Overall writing is clear and easy to follow (R2), although R3 found Sections 3–4 unclear (noted in Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty.** The proposed metric appears very similar or identical to the gradient norm used in Katharopoulos & Fleuret (2018) and the EL2N score from Paul et al. (2021) (“Deep Learning on a Data Diet”). The authors do not adequately justify the difference or why their variant should be better (R2, R3). R1 adds that the weight is essentially the L2 loss on label prediction; it remains “loss-based” and it is unclear how it relates to the theoretically optimal gradient norm weight, especially when the loss is not logistic.
- **Lacks theoretical analysis.** There is no formal convergence analysis; the algorithm remains heuristic (R1).
- **Experiments are weak and incomplete.**
    - No error bars or variability quantification; results are averaged over only 3 runs without indicating statistical noise (R1, R3). R3 notes that differences from competitors are often very small.
    - Missing comparisons with recent state-of-the-art methods, e.g., InfoBatch (Qin et al., ICLR 2024) (R2).
    - Missing comparisons with simple baselines such as random reshuffling with a reduced number of epochs and an adjusted learning rate schedule (often stronger than many importance sampling methods) (R2).
    - Missing comparison with the multiple importance sampling (MIS) approach of Salaün et al. (2024) (R1). R1 asks the authors to comment on the trade-off issue raised in that work.
    - The paper does not clarify the sampling protocol: within a batch sampling is without replacement, but across batches within the same epoch samples may repeat, potentially causing inferior performance compared to methods that guarantee no repetition (e.g., InfoBatch) (R2).
- **Writing and presentation issues (Sections 3–4).** The description is unclear and lacking precision (R3). Equation (1) is confusing; the meaning of \(p(x,y)\) is undefined – is it the data-generating distribution or importance weights? Assumptions (e.g., Lipschitz continuity in §4.1) are not explicitly stated (R3). The paper states that Equation (6) is only for binary classification (Line 205), but does not explain whether it generalizes to \(J > 2\) (R1).
- **Reviewer disagreement.** One reviewer (R3, score 5) finds the results promising and consistent, while two reviewers (R1, R2, score 3) consider the evaluation insufficient and the novelty limited. This review retains the majority critique that the experiments are not convincing without error bars and stronger baselines.
