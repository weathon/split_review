## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses the reliability of LLM-based evaluation by proposing a selective evaluation framework that provides a provable guarantee of human agreement. Instead of always trusting a judge model’s preference, the framework assesses the model’s confidence and selectively abstains when confidence is low.  To improve calibration, the authors introduce *Simulated Annotators*, which use in-context learning to simulate diverse annotator perspectives, and *Cascaded Selective Evaluation*, which escalates from cheap to strong models only when necessary.  Experiments on pairwise preference evaluation tasks show that the method guarantees strong human alignment (e.g., over 80% human agreement with nearly 80% test coverage on a Chatbot Arena subset where GPT-4 alone almost never achieves 80% agreement).

### Strengths
- The paper tackles a practically important and underexplored problem: guaranteeing human agreement for LLM-based evaluations while keeping human subjectivity at the forefront.
- The core methodological contribution (selective evaluation with provable guarantees) is clear, principled, and well-executed.
- **Cascaded Selective Evaluation** explicitly accounts for the cost of using stronger/more expensive LLMs, making the approach cost-effective while maintaining guarantees.
- **Simulated Annotators** (effectively an ensembling technique via ICL) improves judge calibration significantly without external supervision, as shown by large reductions in ECE.
- The dynamic judge selection reduces evaluation overhead and often surpasses the precision of relying solely on the strongest judge model.
- Strong empirical results: for example, on a subset of Chatbot Arena, the method (even with a weak tier like Mistral-7B) achieves an empirical human agreement of 80.3% at 68.3% coverage (Table 7), far beyond what LLM judges achieve without selective evaluation.
- The paper is well-structured, well-written, and provides extensive experiments, including useful ablation studies and discussions of future extensions.
- One reviewer (score 6) acknowledged robust human agreement, improved confidence estimation, and dynamic judge selection as strengths, agreeing on the core contributions.

### Weaknesses
1. **Simulated Annotators – resource cost and algorithm explanation**  
   The computational and time cost of Simulated Annotators grows with the number of simulated annotators *N* and demonstrations *K*. The paper should provide a direct comparison of runtime and API costs against canonical confidence-estimation approaches, and invest more space in explaining the exact in-context learning procedure and how simulated preferences are aggregated.

2. **Fairer comparison with baselines at equal coverage**  
   The outperformance of cascaded selective evaluation is partly expected because it abstains on hard cases. A fairer comparison would exclude the same abstained observations from baseline methods and then compare their empirical human agreement at identical coverage levels. The paper mentions some systematic characteristics that predict abstention (Section 3.3), but an alternative multi‑agentic strategy could also identify and exclude these samples.

3. **Handling abstained samples to increase coverage**  
   In Table 7, about 30% of samples are abstained. Running a stronger model (e.g., GPT‑4‑turbo) only on those abstained samples could substantially increase coverage while keeping relative API cost lower than using GPT‑4 exclusively. This direction is suggested but not pursued.

4. **Dependence of relative API cost on task difficulty**  
   The heterogeneity of evaluator compositions across tasks (Tables 2, 3, 8) means the overall cost is sensitive to task difficulty. The paper should caveat the cost claims and ideally show the range of relative API cost by simulating different evaluator compositions.

5. **Inconsistent target confidence thresholds**  
   The thresholds *1−α* vary across experiments: 0.9 in Table 2, 0.85 in Table 3, and 0.8 in Table 7. Consistency would aid interpretability; the rationale for different choices should be explained.

6. **Missing explicit connection to the selective prediction literature**  
   The concept of *selective evaluation* is essentially selective prediction applied to LLM judges. The paper should explicitly discuss how the proposed abstention mechanism relates to existing selective prediction techniques (e.g., those based on predictive entropy, variance, or confidence scores) and clarify the differences in selection criteria and guarantees.

7. **Related work omissions on human subjectivity and selective prediction**  
   Key works on human label variation and calibration under subjectivity are missing, e.g., Plank (2022), Khurana et al., and Bavaresco et al. (2024). Adding them would better position the paper’s contributions.

8. **Computational cost of “cheaper” models**  
   While Mistral‑7B is smaller than GPT‑4, it still requires significant computational resources (e.g., a GPU for inference). The paper should acknowledge this and discuss the trade‑offs between model size, cost, and evaluation performance, especially for resource‑constrained settings.

9. **Calibration set using argmax discarding disagreement signals**  
   The calibration set for human preferences relies on the argmax of judgments, which discards inter‑annotator disagreement. This choice may obscure useful subjectivity signals; alternatives that incorporate disagreement (e.g., vote distributions) should be explored and the limitation addressed.

10. **Scope limited to pairwise preference evaluation**  
    The method has only been tested on pairwise preference tasks. Its applicability to other evaluation paradigms (e.g., scoring, classification) is not discussed, leaving the generalizability unclear.

11. **Qualitative analysis of abstention behavior missing**  
    The paper only quantifies abstention rates but does not examine *why* models abstain on the content level. For instance, do models abstain on the same pairs that human judges disagree on? A qualitative analysis showing examples where both humans and models abstain (and the ratio of such cases) would strengthen the argument that abstention aligns with genuine ambiguity.

12. **Small error**  
    Line 462 contains a duplicated word (“the the tradeoff”).

13. **Missing analysis: IAA for abstained samples at lower target accuracy**  
    The paper should report how inter‑annotator agreement (IAA) changes on abstained samples when a lower target human‑agreement level is required. This would help understand the nature of the abstained instances.

14. **One reviewer (score 6) considered the work more theoretical** and suggested the authors release a user‑friendly toolkit to enable practical adoption by other researchers. While this is not a technical flaw, it indicates a gap in applied usability.
