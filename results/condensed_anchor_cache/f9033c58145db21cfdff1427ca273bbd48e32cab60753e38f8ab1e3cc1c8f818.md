## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes DM-Codec, a speech tokenizer built on SpeechTokenizer that additionally distills representations from a text language model (LM) via two distillation methods (LM-guided and combined LM+SM-guided) to incorporate acoustic, semantic, and contextual information. Evaluations use LibriSpeech (test subset, 300 samples) and report WER, WIL, ViSQOL, STOI. Reviewer opinions diverge: one strongly negative (score 1), one positive (5), two moderate (3,3).

### Strengths
- The idea of leveraging a text-based language model to improve audio codec is reasonable and interesting (R1).
- The proposed method improves upon prior speech tokenization systems (e.g., SpeechTokenizer) that only use acoustic and semantic information by adding textual representations via LM-guided distillation (R2).
- The method is compared against several baselines (EnCodec, SpeechTokenizer, FACodec) and shows advantages (R2).
- Ablation studies are comprehensive and cover most aspects of the method design (R2, R4).
- This work is the first to incorporate contextual representations via LM-guided distillation, enhancing retention of acoustic and speech information in reconstructed speech (R3).
- Significance tests are conducted, which is an important analysis missing from most prior work (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Incremental novelty**: DM-Codec is essentially SpeechTokenizer with additional LM distillation, which is incremental (R1, R3). One reviewer doubts it meets the acceptance threshold for ICLR (R3).
- **Fundamental length mismatch / alignment flaw**: 
  - The paper does not detail how text and acoustic representations of different lengths are aligned. The notation *n* misleadingly suggests equal sequence length (R1).
  - Given typical speech rates (100–140 words/min) and codec rates (3000 tokens/min), the text sequence is substantially shorter. Most audio tokens would be aligned with padding tokens, a fundamental flaw in the distillation process (R1).
  - In Equation (1), for a given speech *x*, the length of *S(:,d)* (text token length from LM) and *Q(:,d)* (time length of codec output) are not guaranteed to match (R2, R4). The code pads transcriptions to the length of wav2vec2-CTC logits, but the BERT-based LM processes only the unpadded part, making the actual LM representations cover only a small part of the encoded sequence. Without proper speech-text alignment, the effectiveness of LM-guided distillation is difficult to justify (R4).
- **Evaluation limitations**:
  - Testing set is small (only 300 samples from LibriSpeech test subset). This is insufficient for statistically meaningful conclusions (R2). The results in Table 3, e.g., LM weight=0.9 giving higher WER than LM weight=0.3/0.5, contradict the claimed benefit of LM information for lower WER, suggesting noise from small sample size (R2).
  - Several commonly used metrics are missing: no mean opinion scores (MOS) or even automatic UTMOS are reported, though they appear in many prior works (R2, R4). Listening to the provided audio, SpeechTokenizer sounds better, implying the need for MOS (R4).
  - Speaker similarity, evaluated in SpeechTokenizer, is not evaluated here. It is unclear whether adding LM would increase or decrease speaker similarity (R2, also R1 mentions speaker disentangle ability as missing analysis).
  - No downstream applications (ASR, TTS, spoken language modeling) are considered, yet audio codecs are actively explored for audio LLMs and TTS systems. If DM-Codec is based solely on contextual representation, it may not be preferred for such applications (R3, R4).
  - Bit rate, a common metric for audio codecs, is not analyzed (R1).
- **Confusing/contradictory results**:
  - The lowest WER in Table 3 is 4.07, but Table 1 reports 4.05; the difference is not explained (R2).
  - The best results in Table 1 (WER 4.05, WIL 6.61, ViSQOL 3.26, STOI 0.937) are obtained by distilling semantic representations across RVQ layers 1–8 (per Table 4). Yet Tables 5 and 6 only use RVQ layer 1 for combined LM/SM-guided distillation. It is unclear why the best option was not used in those tables (R2).
  - In Table 5, BERT and ELECTRA yield similar results for LM-only distillation but differ more for combined LM+SM distillation; no analysis is given for this discrepancy (R2).
- **Depth of study**: The experiments largely repeat a single training/evaluation setup with different hyperparameters, loss weights, and backbones. There is no in-depth analysis, e.g., how LM distillation affects the first VQ layer alignment or whether DM-Codec possesses speaker disentanglement like SpeechTokenizer (R1). One reviewer, however, considered the ablation studies thorough (R2, R4).
- **Role of ASR in improvements**: The LM-guided distillation relies on a supervised ASR system (wav2vec2-CTC, labeled M_STT in Fig 2) for transcription. It is hard to ascertain whether improvements come from the BERT LM or the ASR system (R3). Could the transcription (ground truth or ASR output) be used directly as a supervised target (e.g., CTC loss on RVQ representations) instead of LM distillation? (R4)
- **Relationship between distillation objective and reconstruction**: It is unclear how contextualized representations relate to speech reconstruction and other applications. The distillation loss in Equation (1) was originally proposed by Chang et al. (SpeechTokenizer) (R4).
- **Missing baseline**: The authors did not include a baseline of DM-Codec without any distillation objectives. Because training data and implementation differ from prior tokenizers, this baseline is necessary to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed distillation methods (R4).
- **Weighting factors in Eq. (10)**: How are the weighting factors (other than LM/SM weights) decided? (R2)
- **Terminology**: The paper uses “speech-to-text (STT)” instead of the more common “automatic speech recognition (ASR)” (R1).
