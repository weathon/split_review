## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies how assigning socio-demographic personas (race, gender, religion, disability, political affiliation) to LLMs affects their performance on reasoning tasks. The authors evaluate 4 LLMs (primarily ChatGPT/gpt-3.5-turbo, plus GPT-4-Turbo and Llama-70b-chat) across 24 reasoning datasets (math, law, medicine, morals, etc.) and 19 personas. They find that persona assignment leads to stereotypical behavior and performance drops, especially due to abstentions (e.g., *“As a Black person, I am unable to answer this question as it requires math knowledge”*). The physically disabled and religious personas show the largest drops; binary gender shows minimal effect. Simple de-biasing prompts have little to no effect. Reviewers largely agree the topic is timely and important, but raise significant concerns about the depth of analysis, statistical rigor, limited model coverage, and overstatement of results. Reviewer 1 (score 8) is more positive; Reviewers 2-4 (score 5 each) are more critical, especially regarding generalizability and experimental detail.

### Strengths
- The paper studies a timely and important issue: unintended biases from persona assignment in LLMs, relevant to human behavior simulation and personalization. (R1, R2, R4)
- It clearly distinguishes persona-assigned stereotype bias from general word association bias, an important conceptual contribution. (R3)
- The analysis of model abstentions (Section 4) and the finding that de-biasing prompts are ineffective (Section 5) are particularly insightful. (R3)
- The paper is well-written, easy to follow, and provides enough detail (datasets, prompts, code) to allow reproduction. (R1, R2)
- Twenty-four diverse datasets and 19 personas across 5 socio-demographic groups are evaluated, covering a broad range of contexts. (R1, R2, R4)
- Results show that bias is ubiquitous (80% of personas), significant (relative drops of 70%+ on some datasets), and harmful for certain groups (some personas drop on >80% of datasets). (R1)
- GPT-4-Turbo shows less but still problematic bias (evident in 42% of personas). (R1)
- The paper explicitly contrasts overt stereotype rejection with implicit bias revealed through persona assignment. (R1, R3)

### Weaknesses
- **Only one model is thoroughly studied.** The primary experiments use only ChatGPT (gpt-3.5-turbo), with limited results for other models (GPT-4-Turbo, Llama-70b-chat). This limits generalizability and makes it unclear whether findings are model-specific. (R1, R2, R4)
- **Lack of no-persona baseline.** The paper does not report performance without any persona prompt, making it hard to isolate the effect of persona assignment from inherent task difficulty. (R1)
- **Insufficient experimental detail.** The “human” and “average human” baseline prompts are not explicitly stated—readers must infer them from Table 1. (R1, R4)
- **No error bars or confidence intervals.** Results are reported as point estimates despite mention of significant variation across runs. Without error bars, statistical comparisons (e.g., Figure 3, differences between groups) are unjustified. (R3, R4)
- **Results overstated relative to evidence.** For instance, Figure 2 shows accuracy ranges from ~60–65% for most personas, with only physically disabled and religious groups showing large drops. Claims of “significant bias” for nearly all personas are not supported by such small differences. (R3) Similarly, “nearly all personas have large drops on some datasets” lacks a clear threshold or statistical test. (R3)
- **Flawed statistical analysis.** Figure 3 mixes datasets with different scales and no error bars, assuming direct comparability of relative drops. Figure 8 switches from accuracy to error rate without justification, and its y-axis is scaled 0.0–0.6 without explanation. (R3)
- **Imprecise definition of “bias.”** The paper conflates stereotype manifestation with general performance differences without a clear operational definition. (R3)
- **Related works underdeveloped.** The paper does not properly situate itself within algorithmic bias literature, including only minimal references to “bias in models.” (R3)
- **Shallow analysis of results.** The discussion only scratches the surface of why certain personas underperform (e.g., abstentions) and does not explore underlying mechanisms. (R2, R4)
- **Limited de-biasing attempts.** Only simple, handcrafted prompts are tried; more systematic approaches (e.g., prompt search, few-shot examples) are not explored. (R2, R4)
- **Persona selection lacks justification.** The “Race” category mixes race (Caucasian), ethnicity (Hispanic), and nationality (African) without explanation. Non-binary gender is omitted. (R3)
- **No intersectional analysis.** Intersectional personas (e.g., Hispanic lifelong Republican) could yield different biases and are not considered. (R1, R2)
- **Open questions not addressed:** Why do religious demographics perform worse than finer-grained groups like Atheist, Christian, Jewish? (R2) Why is overall accuracy capped around 65%? (R3) Are biases more pronounced on objective vs. subjective tasks? (R4) What systematic approaches exist to understand *why* certain personas drop more? (R4) Which persona prompts succeeded/failed the authors’ test in Table 8? (R1)
- **Minor writing issue:** “Statistically significant” is abbreviated inconsistently. (R1)
