## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a poisoning-based Denial-of-Service (P-DoS) attack against LLMs and LLM agents. By inserting a small number of poisoned samples (e.g., a single sample for GPT-4o via OpenAI’s finetuning API, costing less than $1; 10 samples in the training data scenario) during finetuning, an attacker can induce the model to generate excessively long outputs (up to 16K tokens vs. 0.5K before poisoning) on specific triggers, causing high latency and resource exhaustion. The attack is evaluated under three scenarios: training data poisoning, model publisher poisoning (with a customized loss to suppress the [EOS] token), and LLM-based agents. Multiple attack targets are considered: Repetition, Recursion, Count, Long Article, and Source Code. The paper claims that without poisoning, natural instructions are bounded by the maximum length of SFT data, and that adversarial-input DoS attacks become challenging under speech-to-text interfaces.

### Strengths
- Highlights a significant security vulnerability in LLM finetuning services, showcasing real-world DoS attack examples (e.g., GPT-4o/mini).
- Proposes a powerful and stealthy poisoning-based DoS (P-DoS) attack that is effective with a very low poisoning ratio (one sample for API finetuning, 10 samples for training data poisoning).
- Considers multiple attack scenarios: data poisoning-only (attacker as data contributor), fine-tuning (attacker as model publisher with custom loss L_DoS to suppress [EOS]), and LLM-based agents (triggering "while (True)", "sleep 99999", "click[DoS]").
- Examines multiple attack targets (Repetition, Recursion, Count, Long Article, Source Code), demonstrating flexibility.
- Comprehensive experimental evaluation includes ablation studies across most design choices in all three scenarios, showing consistent high attack success rates.

### Weaknesses
1. **Novelty concerns.** Two reviewers (R1, R2) found limited novelty. DoS attacks against LLMs were studied in Geiping et al. (2024) as one objective, and safety risks of finetuning were discussed in Qi et al. (2024) for harmful content generation. The training loss used (suppressing [EOS]) is similar to backdoor attack losses (target loss + utility loss) and the paper does not clearly differentiate its methodology from backdoor attacks (R2). The threat model where the model publisher attacks their own model is considered unrealistic by R2.
2. **Insufficient evidence for the claim about SFT data bounding output length.** Section 3 (largely overlapping with Section 4.1) hypothesizes that the limited output length of natural instructions is due to bounded input lengths in SFT data. However, the paper does not show statistics of SFT data or provide clear evidence that output length depends on SFT data rather than pretraining data. Controlled experiments are missing (R1). R4 questions whether the method for breaking the output length limit is simply increasing the length of poisoned samples, which may be easily detected and impractical for injection in the training data poisoning scenario.
3. **Weak justification for necessity of poisoning under speech-to-text interfaces.** The paper claims that previous DoS attacks via adversarial inputs are challenging under speech-to-text interfaces, but no experiments with speech-to-text are presented (R1). Recent fluent/low-perplexity adversarial attacks (e.g., Thompson et al., 2024) could overcome speech-to-text limitations. Additionally, adversarial examples exist for speech recognition systems, and jailbreaking using natural phrases has been demonstrated (R3). This weakens the argument that poisoning attacks are necessary for targeting speech-to-text interfaces.
4. **Missing discussion of defenses.** Multiple reviewers (R1, R3) note that a discussion of potential defense methods against P-DoS attacks would be helpful and is absent.
5. **Missing details and experiments.** 
   - The construction of the 10 poisoned samples for the training data scenario is not described in detail (R4). 
   - For the model publisher scenario using L_DoS to suppress [EOS], the dataset used for finetuning is not clarified (R4). 
   - Figure 4 shows a DoS trigger used with a GPT model, but no experiments demonstrating that GPT can be backdoored with a DoS trigger via the finetuning API are presented (R1). 
   - Analysis of the generated outputs under the [EOS] suppression loss is missing; patterns in outputs could inspire other DoS attacks (R1).
   - No exploration of why Long Article and Source Code targets are less effective, nor their performance under the model publisher scenario (R3).
6. **Unfair comparison to baselines.** The baselines considered do not have access to the training data, whereas the attacker in the proposed threat model does; this comparison is unfair (R2).
7. **Lack of automatic vulnerability discovery.** The attacks are manually crafted; the paper does not discuss ways to automatically find such vulnerabilities (R1).
