## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes EvalAlign, a metric and dataset for evaluating text-to-image generation models using supervised fine-tuning of Multimodal LLMs on human-aligned question-answer data. The approach focuses on two dimensions: image faithfulness and text-image alignment, with fine-grained annotations at three levels (animal faces, visibility of hands, limbs) and 11 skills. Evaluations across 24 T2I models show high correlation with human preferences. Reviewers generally find the dataset collection and MLLM-based evaluation approach interesting, but raise concerns about experimental validation, lack of instance-level evaluation, unclear advantages over existing datasets (e.g., ImageReward, PickScore, HPS(v2), Rich Human Feedback [a]), insufficient exploration of fine-grained skills, and computational cost.

### Strengths
- The dataset collection and annotation process is technically sound, with explicit question-answer prompting (e.g., "Are there any issues with human face in the image, such as facial distortion, asymmetrical faces, abnormal facial features, unusual expressions in the eyes, etc?") that could be useful and scalable to better baseline MLLMs. (Reviewer 3)
- The paper includes fine-grained annotations at three levels (animal faces, visibility of hands, visibility of limbs), which is more detailed than previous datasets. (Reviewer 1)
- Using MLLM with SFT for image evaluation is a creative and interesting approach that could enhance interpretability in assessing generated image quality. (Reviewers 2, 4)
- The evaluation part presents a benchmark on 24 generative models, showing high correlation between the proposed scorer and human evaluation; it would become a useful metric. (Reviewer 3)
- The experiments are relatively detailed, considering 24 generative models and multiple dimensions. (Reviewer 2)
- The illustrations are intuitive and beautiful; Figure 1 reflects the central idea well. (Reviewer 2)
- The authors promise open source code and provide experimental details in supplementary materials, supporting reproducibility. (Reviewer 1)
- The writing is quite fluent and easy to read with appropriate illustrations. (Reviewer 1)

### Weaknesses
1. **Lack of instance-level evaluation and model-level overemphasis**: Table 3 evaluates at the model level (ranking 24 models), which is less meaningful than scoring individual images. The paper should focus on instance-level evaluation, e.g., which of two images from the same model has higher quality. (Reviewer 1) Additionally, it is unclear whether the KRCC and PLCC in Table 8 are computed at the instance level; if model-level, modification is needed; if instance-level, more analysis is required. (Reviewer 1)

2. **Insufficient exploration of fine-grained annotations (11 skills)**: Although the dataset contains 11 fine-grained skills across two aspects, the experiments primarily focus on the two broad aspects, with little analysis or results demonstrating performance on the 11 specific skills. This makes it unclear whether the fine-grained annotations are effectively utilized. (Reviewers 2, 4)

3. **Unclear advantages over existing datasets and methods**: The primary benefit of the dataset (two-aspect evaluation) is already implicitly addressed by prior datasets like ImageReward, PickScore, HPS(v2). It is not clear why question-answering is superior to direct scoring, especially as a vast number of questions would be needed to cover all aspects. (Reviewers 2, 4) The paper does not adequately compare with Rich Human Feedback for Text-to-Image Generation [a] (CVPR 2024 best paper), which also includes detailed multi-aspect human feedback via scoring. A direct comparison is needed to validate the claimed advantages of question-based feedback. (Reviewers 2, 4)

4. **Questionable cost-effectiveness**: The annotation process requires extensive human annotations (130k annotations for optimal results), and the method achieves optimal performance with a 34B MLLM model, which is computationally expensive. The paper does not provide a detailed comparison of annotation cost with existing datasets or a breakdown of computational cost. (Reviewers 2, 4) Moreover, the time consumption for evaluation using a 34B model is not adequately analyzed; given the slight improvement over subjective human perception, the cost may not be justified. (Reviewer 2)

5. **Weak experimental setup and inconsistent results**:
   - It is unclear whether baseline models were trained on the proposed dataset for fair comparison in Tables 2 and 3. Without a consistent training setup, performance differences may be attributed to the dataset rather than the method. (Reviewers 2, 4)
   - Results do not consistently support claims: e.g., the 500 configuration does not show clear optimal performance in Table 6, and there is no clear positive correlation between model size and performance improvements in Table 7. (Reviewers 2, 4)

6. **Potential overfitting due to test set design**: Although test set images do not overlap with train set, they come from the same 16 generation models. Since evaluation only shows model-wise ranking consistency, overfitting (e.g., memorizing model-specific styles) cannot be excluded. Testing on hold-out T2I generators is recommended. (Reviewer 3)

7. **Concerns about score derivation and ground truth**: The final scores are derived from averaging score outputs of several questions. If the "Human" column is obtained similarly, this may not be a good enough ground truth for correlation analysis. (Reviewer 3)

8. **Missing comparisons and discussions**:
   - Q-Align (ICML2024) uses SFT for LMM scoring and finds logits better than `model.generate()`; it also handles image faithfulness evaluation. The paper should compare with or discuss Q-Align. (Reviewer 3)
   - For faithfulness evaluation (image quality), the compared baselines are similarity-based alignment metrics. The authors should compare with T2I quality evaluation baselines, including Q-Align. (Reviewer 3)

9. **Incomplete related work review**: The paper only reviews coarse-grained datasets but misses fine-grained datasets such as [1,2,3] which have fewer images but more annotations per image. Since fine-grained annotation is a claimed innovation, this omission is significant. (Reviewer 1)

10. **Writing and structure issues**: Some sentences lack clarity (e.g., "the utilized synthesized images are treated as real images as they don’t explicitly recognize the problem of synthesized images with low image faithfulness" is confusing given HPS(v2) aims to evaluate generated images). (Reviewers 2, 4) The key novel contributions are unclear, and there are repeated sentences that should be removed to improve conciseness. (Reviewers 2, 4)

Note: Reviewers 2 and 4 share many identical points, indicating strong consensus on several weaknesses. Reviewer 3 (score 6) is more positive overall but still raises significant concerns about score derivation, overfitting, and missing comparisons. Reviewer 1 (score 5) provides additional specific criticism on instance-level evaluation and related work completeness.
