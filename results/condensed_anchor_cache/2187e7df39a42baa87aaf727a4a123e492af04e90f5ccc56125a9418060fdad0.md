## Merged Review

### Summary
DebUnc augments multi-agent LLM debate by having debaters communicate their uncertainty about a question. Three uncertainty-reporting methods (text prompts, attention-masked token weighting, etc.) and three aggregation methods are explored. Experiments on multiple benchmarks show that the attention-based approach consistently improves over standard debates. Similar to weighted majority voting at a higher level.

### Strengths
- **Novel mechanism**: Introduces an interesting approach for interpreting and incorporating reported uncertainties from other models during debate (R1).
- **Well motivated**: The problem of overconfident incorrect responses in multi-agent communication is clearly identified and the proposed solution directly addresses it (R1, R3).
- **Clear presentation**: The paper is well written, well structured, and the figures effectively communicate the key concepts (R1, R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient experimental comparisons and baselines**: Multiple essential baselines are missing: vanilla Chain-of-Thought (CoT), CoT with self-consistency using a similar computation budget, prior multi-agent debate frameworks [Du et al.; Liang et al.], and a simple baseline where agents state their uncertainty in natural language directly (R2). The paper also omits comparisons with other improvements to multi-agent debate (Pham et al., Chen et al.), though these may be contemporaneous (R3).
- **Statistical significance and reproducibility concerns**: Results in Table 1 and Table 2 are based on only 100 data points; assuming binomial distribution, naive standard errors of the mean produce overlapping confidence intervals. Additionally, generations use Temp=1 (stochastic), yet no repeated runs are reported to verify that the observed improvements are reliable estimators (R1).
- **Oracle baseline is misleading**: The oracle uncertainty metric (perfect correctness predictor) is presented as if it were one of the proposed methods. By construction it gives an upper bound, not a realistic extrapolation. It should be visually separated (e.g., in Table 1 and Table 2) and used only as a best-case reference (R1, R3). One reviewer specifically warns that without the oracle, trends in Figure 4 are much weaker (R1) and that for “Attention-Others” accuracy actually drops when AUROC reaches ~0.7 (R2).
- **Attention scaling details are insufficiently explained**: Key notations (w_i, m_j, f_i) are introduced without definition. The decision to “only apply attention scaling to the responses from the previous round” is not justified or validated via ablation studies (R2). One reviewer questions the entire suitability of the attention-masked method: if the model is a black box (API), it cannot be used; if it is a white box, then with minimal training the judge would directly learn to weigh uncertainties, bypassing the proposed mechanism (R1).
- **Missing details on uncertainty metric implementation**: How is the relevance score for TokenSAR generated? (R1). It is unclear how different data points in Figure 4 were produced (different random seeds?) (R2). The x-axis of Figure 4 (AUROC for which uncertainty metric, final judge or debater?) needs clarification (R1).
- **Confounding explanation**: Tuning hyperparameters of the uncertainty metrics essentially finds a predictor of response correctness. An ablation is needed to verify that the improvement comes from communication rather than from a simple weighted majority vote using the same uncertainty metric (R3).
- **Natural language expression of uncertainty**: It is not shown that LLMs cannot already express their uncertainty via natural language when properly prompted (e.g., with few-shot examples). This baseline is missing (R3).
- **Marginal gains vs. complexity**: The reported improvements are 1–3% overall, and less than 1% for Llama 3 8B. Given the additional computational cost and complexity of the framework, these gains are unconvincing (R2).
- **Omitted debate literature**: Key prior work—Irving et al. (AI safety via Debate) and Khan et al. (Debating with more persuasive LLMs)—is not cited or discussed (R1).

**Note on reviewer disagreement**: While all three reviewers assigned the same rating (3), one reviewer (R3) gave a soundness score of 4, notably higher than the others (both 2), and expressed confidence 5, suggesting a somewhat more favorable view of the paper’s technical soundness and presentation than the other two reviewers.
