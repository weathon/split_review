## Merged Review

### Summary
GameNGen is a real-time game simulation engine using a diffusion model conditioned on past frames and actions. It is trained in two phases: an RL agent collects gameplay data in VizDoom, then a diffusion model (based on a pre-trained Stable Diffusion variant) is trained to predict the next frame. The model runs at >20 fps on a single TPU, achieving PSNR 29.4 (comparable to lossy JPEG compression at quality 20–30). Human raters distinguish short clips from real gameplay only slightly better than random (58–60% on 1.6–3.2 s clips, ~50% on clips from long rollouts). The paper provides extensive ablations on context length, noise augmentation of conditioning variables, and data quality.

### Strengths
- **Real-time performance**: The model runs at 20+ fps on a TPU, demonstrating practical deployment potential for interactive applications. (R1, R2, R4, R5)
- **First demonstration of neural real-time simulation of a complex game**: The paper shows that a diffusion model can simulate DOOM interactively, a feat that seemed far-fetched a year or two ago. (R2, R3)
- **Clear motivation**: The potential for rapid text- or image-programmable game generation is convincing and well-articulated. (R2, R5)
- **Comprehensive evaluation**: Metrics include PSNR, LPIPS, FVD, and human evaluations on both short clips and long generations. (R2, R5)
- **Thorough ablations**: Detailed analysis of context length, noise augmentation level, and gameplay data (trained RL agent vs. random agent). (R2, R3, R5)
- **Interesting analysis of noise augmentation (Section 3.3.2)**: This is a key part of the contribution and deserves expanded discussion. (R4)
- **Provides evidence that learning world models of complex environments is feasible**. (R4)
- **Well-presented, clean paper with a commendable desire for reproducibility**. (R5)

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of methodological novelty**: The approach is primarily an adaptation of pre-existing diffusion models (Stable Diffusion) with incremental architectural adjustments; there are no new methods. While reviewers R2 and R4 consider this acceptable given the impressive results, reviewers R1 and R3 view it as a significant weakness. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- **Limited scope and generalization concerns**:
    - Demonstrated only on DOOM, an older game with simple graphics; may not scale to modern, high-resolution games. (R1)
    - The model likely overfits to the single training DOOM map; quantitative out-of-distribution experiments (e.g., custom maps, enemies from later levels appearing earlier) are needed but only qualitative examples are given. (R2)
    - VisDoom may be easier to learn due to its unchanged UI; generalization to at least one more game is expected to confirm the approach is not game-specific. (R3)
- **Missing downstream usefulness**: No demonstration of using the simulator for downstream tasks such as faster RL training or as a forward model for decision making. (R3)
- **Unclear data coverage and lack of failure cases**:
    - No examples of failure cases or description of what “hard” areas look like; the paper glosses over what the model does badly. (R5)
    - Questions about data coverage in low-data areas and how performance varies there; the authors have initial observations (“some areas very easy, some very hard, agent performs better in some”) but do not elaborate. (R4)
    - Context length limitations are acknowledged but not illustrated with failure cases; supplementary material appears cherry-picked. (R5)
- **Human evaluation concerns**:
    - Human evaluation uses very short clips (1.6 or 3.2 s) without rationale; scores may tail off significantly for longer clips. (R5)
    - The claim that PSNR is “comparable to lossy JPEG compression” is less impressive when the corresponding JPEG quality is only 20–30. (R5)
    - The long-generation human evaluation setup is unclear: after rolling out the model for 6000–12000 steps (5–10 minutes), the clips compared may be very different from the ground truth, making the 50% chance result hard to interpret. (R5)
    - Whether human evaluation results change if conditioning noise augmentation is not applied remains an open question. (R2)
- **Insufficient discussion of prior work**:
    - “Diffusion for world modeling: Visual details matter in Atari” is not concurrent (publicly available a year ago) but is highly similar; a much larger discussion of similarities and differences is needed, especially regarding architectural choices, real-time capability, and RL agent training. (R4)
    - Missing citations: GAIA-1 (real-world environment simulation), VideoGPT (action-conditional video prediction for Doom), and Temporally Consistent Transformers for Video Generation. (R4)
    - Missing mention of prior work that adds noise to conditioning variables, e.g., Ruhe et al. (R2) and Diffusion Forcing (uses same noise augmentation for long-term autoregressive generation). (R4)
- **Motivation hand-wavy**: Claims of cheaper game development under a new paradigm are unsubstantiated. Training required 128 TPU-v5es plus an RL agent, and the game must exist before the model can be trained. (R5)
- **Answer to posed question too unqualified**: The paper says an unqualified “yes” to “Can a neural model simulate a complex game?” but significant gaps remain (e.g., playability end-to-end, failure cases). A “Yes, but…” or “No, but…” framing would be more honest. (R5)
- **Clarity issues in Section 2**: The model inputs and regression objective are unclear; symbols such as \(o_{q_i}\) and \(o_{p_i}\) need explicit explanation. (R1)
- **No public code or model**: Essential for a phenomenological paper to allow independent assessment of robustness. (R2)
- **Figure 2 is unfair**: Comparisons to other papers’ outputs are exaggerated because those works trained on data without the HUD; a comparable image should be used. (R4)
- **Need for a new definition “Interactive Environment”?**: An existing POMDP formulation may suffice. (R4)
- **Unclear benefit of pre-trained Stable Diffusion 1.4**: Why start from a text-to-image model? Is there not enough data to train from scratch? No experiments with end-to-end training or different pre-trained models are reported. (R4, R5)
- **Missing training/data details**:
    - Training time not stated. (R5)
    - Data amount: 70M examples from 50M environment steps? The paper says “random subset of 70M examples” but the relation to the 50M steps is unclear. (R4, R5)
    - Number of training epochs: roughly 64 passes per timestep if 90M sequences of length 64 are used on 70M transitions? (R4)
    - How noise level at inference is chosen? (R5)
    - Random agent ablations: does it follow the same action repetition and frame-skipping practices? Could distributional shift from human actions explain its low accuracy? (R5)
    - Did authors consider excluding the initial random exploration from the trained agent’s dataset (or training the RL agent longer) to get a higher quality dataset? (R5)
- **Context length experiments**: Need qualitative results – e.g., does a 64-frame context correctly remember objects from 50 frames in the past while a 32-frame context cannot? (R4)
- **Long-term generation evaluation**: More examples of trajectories spanning 30 s to 1+ minutes are needed to assess temporal consistency, geometry preservation, and counting behavior. (R4)
- **PSNR may still improve**: The plot in Fig 13 suggests PSNR is still increasing at 700K steps; did the authors train beyond that? (R5)
