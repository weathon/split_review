## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes LEGO-Compiler, a neural compilation system that translates high-level language (C) into assembly code using LLMs. Key components: (1) LEGO translation – decomposing programs into composable basic blocks; (2) annotation-based Chain-of-Thought (CoT) to guide the LLM with variable mappings and control flow context; (3) a feedback-driven self-correction mechanism using assembler/runtime/behavioral errors. Empirical results report >99% accuracy on a 500‑program subset of ExeBench and 100% on CoreMark, with theoretical proofs of composability. The system also handles programs exceeding the context‑length limits of native LLM translation.

### Strengths
- **Novelty of the LEGO approach**: Decomposing complex code into composable blocks (LEGO translation) and using annotation-based CoT with feedback are creative blends of classical compiler ideas and modern LLMs. Reviewer 1 lauds this as original and uncommon in neural compilation.
- **First effective translation of reasonably sized programs**: Reviewer 2 notes this is the first work they are aware of to show effective neural translation of programs beyond trivial examples.
- **Comprehensive empirical validation**: Over 99% accuracy on ExeBench (500 samples) and full compilation of CoreMark; introduction of the LongFunction dataset for benchmarking long programs; evaluation across three architectures (x86‑64, ARM‑v8a, RISC‑V64) and multiple LLMs (GPT‑4o, Claude‑3.5‑Sonnet, DeepSeekCoder). Reviewer 1 finds the evaluation robust.
- **Theoretical support**: Formal proofs of code composability (Appendix B) and clearly stated assumptions/limitations. Reviewer 4 appreciates this rigor.
- **Clear presentation**: Well-structured with flowcharts, annotated examples, and accessible explanation of the part‑split algorithm, control‑flow annotations, and feedback mechanism. Reviewer 1 emphasizes clarity.
- **Significance for LLM‑based compilation**: Opens new directions for applying LLMs to system‑level tasks; the LongFunction dataset provides a benchmark for future work. Reviewer 1 and Reviewer 3 agree the problem is important and challenging.

**Contrasting views on novelty and significance**: While Reviewer 1 finds substantial originality, Reviewers 2, 3, and 4 see the combination of methods as fairly obvious (decomposition, CoT, feedback) and note many steps mirror traditional compilers. Reviewer 2 questions the motivation for replacing efficient parts of a compiler with an LLM.

### Weaknesses
1. **Motivation and value proposition** (Reviewer 2, echoed by Reviewer 3):  
   - The paper does not clearly articulate *why* one would replace syntax‑directed translation with an LLM. Many steps (value collection, variable mapping, splitting into basic blocks) are identical to traditional compilers.  
   - The major LLM replacement – basic‑block translation assuming stack‑based local variables and no register allocation – is considered “quite naive” and achievable as an undergraduate compiler homework.  
   - The feedback mechanism’s cost and unpredictability (“orders of magnitude bigger” with no guarantees) are not justified relative to deterministic, efficient traditional compilers.  
   - The comparison of LEGO‑Compiler’s computational cost to human effort (Section C.3) is misleading because most of the human effort lies outside the scope of what LEGO‑Compiler does.

2. **Incorrect and misleading accuracy claim** (Reviewer 2, noted also by Reviewer 4):  
   - The paper states “over 99% on ExeBench” but the test set is only 500 examples (not the full 40k or 23k after filtering). This is an exaggeration by a factor of ~46.

3. **Weak benchmarks and limited evaluation** (Reviewer 4, Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3):  
   - The ExeBench subset consists of very small programs – most have fewer than 100 total instructions; the top 10% barely exceed 200 instructions, implying only a few dozen lines of C. The LongFunction benchmark’s complexity is not reported.  
   - Benchmarks lack complex language features: no multi‑dimensional arrays, function pointers, system calls (e.g., `fork`, `execve`), dynamic memory allocation, or complex pointer arithmetic. How LEGO‑Compiler handles these is not discussed.  
   - No evaluation on other programming languages (Java, Python) to demonstrate generality.  
   - No comparison with prior neural compilation work (e.g., Guo & Moses 2022, who used LLMs to generate LLVM‑IR) or non‑LLM neural compilers. This omission makes the claimed improvement difficult to assess.

4. **Incremental improvement and missing baseline comparisons** (Reviewer 4, Reviewer 3):  
   - On ExeBench, LEGO‑Compiler improves over a simple CoT baseline by <1%. Given that baselines already achieve >98% accuracy, the practical benefit is marginal.  
   - Results for small LLMs (e.g., CodeLlama‑7B/13B) are missing from Table 1, and the CoT baseline is missing for Codestral. This obfuscates limitations and scope.  
   - Reviewer 3 requests an ablation isolating the effect of block‑by‑block translation *without* the rejection‑based pass@k method.

5. **Assumption of composability fails with aliasing** (Reviewer 2):  
   - The formal proof (Appendix B) assumes statements are independent, but this does not hold when memory allocation permits aliasing. Example: `int *p = malloc(...); int *q = p; *p = 10; *q = 20;` – treating the two assignments as independent could lead to incorrect memory allocation. Similar issues arise if the LLM reorders dependent operations (e.g., array assignments).

6. **Correctness verification insufficient** (Reviewer 4):  
   - The evaluation only checks whether the output of the generated assembly matches a few test cases. Test coverage may be incomplete; passing all test cases does not guarantee program equivalence. The authors should use SMT solvers or formal equivalence checking to verify correctness.

7. **Feedback mechanism lacks detail and feasibility analysis** (Reviewer 1):  
   - The paper does not explain how different error types (assembler, runtime, behavioral) are integrated into the LLM’s next attempt. Is the LLM given the raw error message or more detailed guidance?  
   - Automation of feedback types is not discussed; some types may be difficult to implement in general settings.

8. **Missing ablation on splitting strategy and part sizes** (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3):  
   - No analysis of how different block sizes or splitting algorithms (e.g., splitting at function boundaries vs. fixed intervals) affect accuracy and performance. Optimal configuration is unknown.

9. **Scalability and efficiency not quantified** (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3):  
   - No breakdown of time spent in each phase (splitting, individual block translation, reassembly, feedback iterations).  
   - No memory footprint analysis, which is critical for resource‑constrained deployment.  
   - The iterative feedback process can be very slow; a runtime evaluation is needed.

10. **Handling of edge cases and recursion** (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2):  
    - No discussion of deeply nested loops, irregular control flows, or highly recursive functions.  
    - The impact of recursion and self‑modifying code on the composability assumption is unexplored.

11. **Questions not addressed** (multiple reviewers, merged):
    - How are in‑context learning (ICL) examples obtained if no existing compiler is used? What is “compiler knowledge guided CoT”?  
    - Who assigns memory addresses (e.g., `blksize` → `-36(%rbp)`)? Is it the LLM or a separate step?  
    - What is the renaming pass (Lines 270‑272)? Is it scope‑aware?  
    - What is the difference from a traditional compiler’s symbol table and block‑by‑block translation?  
    - Could fine‑tuning or domain‑specific tuning improve results?  
    - How does LEGO‑Compiler perform on programs >1000 lines? (Reviewer 4)  
    - What are the limitations of applying LEGO‑Compiler to small LLMs? (Reviewer 4)  
    - Provide a breakdown of accuracy per target architecture. (Reviewer 3)

**Reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (R1, score 10) is highly positive about originality, quality, and significance; the other three (scores 5,5,6) are critical, questioning the novelty, the size of the improvement, the strength of the benchmarks, and the fundamental motivation. The above list captures all criticisms from the majority and the detailed concerns from the most critical reviewer (R2).
