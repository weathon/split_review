## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper studies the vulnerability of multi-agent learning (MAL) in strongly monotone games with bandit feedback. It proposes the Single-agent Utility Shifting Attack (SUSA) that, by corrupting only one agent's utility observations, can steer the dynamics away from the unique Nash equilibrium (NE) with a sublinear corruption budget in the time horizon \(T\). The paper further characterizes an efficiency‑robustness trade‑off: faster‑converging algorithms are more vulnerable to such attacks. Theoretical results and numerical experiments (e.g., Cournot competition, Tullock contests) support the findings.

### Strengths

- **Novel attack and underexplored vulnerability.** The paper introduces a single‑agent poisoning attack for multi‑agent learning, a setting largely unexamined in prior work. Multiple reviewers note that this practical finding (only one agent needs to be targeted) provides valuable risk insights.
- **Rigorous theoretical analysis.** The attack strategy SUSA is theoretically justified, with a clear proof that a sublinear corruption budget suffices to shift the NE (Theorem 1). The exploration of the efficiency‑robustness trade‑off is especially insightful.
- **Insightful trade‑off and open problems.** The identified trade‑off between convergence speed and robustness is novel and motivates future design of robust algorithms. Reviewers found this discussion valuable and noted it “opens the door for more interesting future works.”
- **Well‑written and clearly presented.** The writing is clear and easy to follow. Figures (notably Figure 1) and remarks aid comprehension. The paper is well‑organized.
- **Extensive empirical validation.** Numerical simulations (e.g., on derivative distance and cumulative cost under different parameters) demonstrate the practical impact of the attack and corroborate the theoretical findings.

### Weaknesses

- **Strong assumptions limit practical relevance.** The attack model assumes the adversary has full knowledge of the victim agent’s utility function. This is a significant limitation (raised by three reviewers), as such information is rarely available in real‑world scenarios. The paper does not discuss strategies for the attacker to estimate the utility under imperfect knowledge. Moreover, the adversary observes the current action (a “strong” corruption setting). It remains unclear whether SUSA would remain effective with only noisy or limited information (e.g., only noisy sub‑Gaussian rewards), or under a “weaker” observation model. Some reviewers note that the information asymmetry makes the result less surprising; restricting the adversary (e.g., to only observed noisy rewards) would be more interesting.
- **No lower bound on attack cost.** The paper lacks a lower bound on the corruption budget needed to mislead the system, making it impossible to judge the efficiency of the attack. Reviewers point out that in a similar bandit setting the lower bound was already closed, and the current analysis does not explain why the cumulative cost can be quite large (e.g., Figure 2 shows a nearly linear cost in one sub‑figure). The parameter \(\alpha\) is determined by the dynamics and cannot be well controlled, so the “sublinear” budget could be as large as \(T^{1-\alpha}\), which is qualitatively different from \(\log\log T\). A more rigorous analysis of lower bounds and the influence of learning rate on attack cost is needed.
- **Restriction to monotone games and overstatement.** The paper only studies strongly monotone games (illustrated by Cournot competition and Tullock contests). The title and introduction suggest a broader scope (e.g., “multi‑agent learning” in general), but whether the results extend to non‑monotone games or games with multiple NEs is unknown. One reviewer notes that this overstatement may exaggerate the contribution. It remains unclear whether the attack can steer agents toward a specific, predefined NE (e.g., one with low utility) or toward a particular strategy profile.
- **Limited evaluation metrics.** The evaluation focuses on NE shift and cumulative budget. Additional metrics such as stability, agent utility outcomes, or the ability to guide agents to a low‑utility NE would provide a more comprehensive picture of the attack’s impact.
- **Agent unawareness and possible countermeasures.** The attack assumes the victim agent is unaware of the adversary’s presence. It is not discussed whether the attack would still work if agents know they are being attacked or can adapt.
- **Missing connection to single‑agent results.** The paper mentions that a similar trade‑off exists in single‑agent settings. Reviewers ask whether the three open problems also appear there, and if so, whether single‑agent results can be extended or why not.
- **Minor issues.** A typo on line 147 (“will useful” → “will be useful”); line 242 missing some relevant literature (Jun et al. 2018, Liu & Shroff 2019); line 390 missing a period. The discussion in lines 295‑301 and 319‑322 about controlling the attack direction or derivative distance seems to assume additional knowledge without explicitly addressing that assumption. In Proposition 1, it is unclear what happens if the attacker can manipulate more than one agent (e.g., a subset of agents).
