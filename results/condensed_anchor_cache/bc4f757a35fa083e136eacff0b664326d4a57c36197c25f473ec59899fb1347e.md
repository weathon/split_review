## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes risk-averse quantal response equilibria (RQE) that combine risk aversion and bounded rationality, and shows they are tractable to compute in all *n*-player matrix and finite-horizon Markov games. The method leverages a dual representation to convert the problem into a convex optimization task, reducing it to computing a coarse correlated equilibrium (CCE) of an associated 2*n*-player game. Theoretical results are supported by proofs, and sample complexity is given for the generative model setting. Experiments on several 2-player matrix games (validating human behavioral data) and a toy Cliff Walk Markov game illustrate the approach.

### Strengths
- Writing is clear, well-organized, and proofs are well exposed in the appendix. The paper is generally well written.
- Investigating the combination of bounded rationality and risk aversion to achieve tractable equilibria is interesting. A key strength is that tractability is independent of the underlying payoff/game structure and depends only on agents’ degrees of risk aversion and bounded rationality.
- The results hold for *all n*-player matrix games and finite-horizon Markov games, including general-sum games.
- Modeling strategic interaction beyond expected utility theory (risk aversion, bounded rationality) is interesting on its own and could have real economic applications. The RQE concept is both realistic and computationally feasible.
- The paper applies a dual formulation (Föllmer & Schied) to the multi-agent setting and constructs the loss functions in Eqs. (6) and (7) with good novelty. The theoretical analysis is rigorous, and claims are supported extensively by proofs.
- The paper provides a sampling-based algorithm with finite-sample guarantees for learning approximate RQE in unknown environments (generative model).
- The Cliff Walk experiment demonstrates risk aversion well.
- RQE can be computed in polynomial time in all *n*-player matrix games under the specified risk aversion and bounded rationality conditions, regardless of payoffs.

### Weaknesses
- **Tractability rationale unclear:** A main concern is what exactly makes the games tractable. The paper presents the combination of bounded rationality and risk aversion as the enabler, but bounded rationality alone (e.g., QRE) might already provide tractability under assumptions on payoffs. The presentation order (risk aversion first, then bounded rationality) is confusing. The paper should clarify whether both are needed for tractability, how the obtained condition (independent of rewards) compares to that for QREs, and what the price/additional advantage of risk aversion is beyond incorporating human behavior.
- **Experimental limitations:** Only one toy Markov game (Cliff Walk) is used, and the repeated-game experiments use very simple matrix games that do not showcase the benefits of the risk-averse approach (standard solutions exist without risk aversion). The experiments do not explicitly illustrate what the RQEs are in the games, how they differ from Nash equilibria, or why they are more reasonable. Bounded rationality is less obvious than risk aversion from the Cliff Walk results, making interpretation difficult. Baseline comparisons with standard MARL algorithms (e.g., Nash Q-learning, multi-agent PPO) are missing.
- **Notation and presentation issues:** The notation is confusing at times, especially the distinction between π (joint action profile vs. distribution) and p_i (individual policy). The symbols ξ₁*, ξ₂*, τ₁, τ₂ in Fig. 1 are not defined in the main text; τ is hidden in the definition of D. The motivation and intuition for Eqs. (6) and (7) could be improved. Several minor typos exist (e.g., line 160: “we make u a general class”).
- **Connection to CCE limitations:** The paper criticizes CCEs for being large, possibly including strictly dominated strategies, and for stationary CCEs being PPAD-hard in dynamic general-sum games. However, Theorem 2 assumes access to a CCE of the 4‑player game. It is not explained how this resolves those limitations; the output may itself be a nonstationary CCE.
- **Sample complexity and scaling:** The sample complexity is exponential in the number of players. The paper does not comment on how this relates to the “curse of multi-agency” (product vs. sum of action spaces) or scalability. Only two-player Markov games are demonstrated; extending to more players would greatly increase computation time.
- **Equilibrium collapse discussion is brief:** The mention of equilibrium collapse in line 294 needs more depth. Under the condition ε₁=ξ_{2,1} and ε₂=ξ_{1,2}, the extended game becomes zero‑sum (J₁+J₂+J̅₁+J̅₂=0), which connects to prior work on zero‑sum games and equilibrium collapse; this should be discussed.
- **Technical novelty questioned:** One reviewer noted that duality and risk aversion concepts have been explored in game theory and MARL before, and the extension to finite-horizon MDPs uses relatively conventional methods, so it may add scope but not deep theoretical insight. Additionally, Proposition 1 does not involve bounded rationality.
- **Strong assumption of a simulator:** The algorithm requires access to a generative model, which is a strong assumption that may limit practical applicability. No discussion of challenges under weaker conditions.
- **Generality concerns:** 
  - The condition in Theorem 3 for the Markov game setting does not generalize the two-player matrix game condition, suggesting a possible fundamental difference between 2‑player and n≥3 settings.
  - Definition 7 uses a deviation that depends on the horizon step; if ε_i=0 and regularizers are removed, does it recover the standard Markov game NE?
  - Continuity assumptions: The paper assumes continuity w.r.t. both arguments of D for matrix games but only lower-semicontinuity in the Markov setting; the examples in Table 1 may not satisfy continuity in both arguments.
  - The condition ε₁ε₂ ≥ ξ₁*ξ₂* could include zero values; positivity of all parameters may not be needed, and the condition appears non-symmetric even though the game is symmetric.
- **Missing details and minor points:** 
  - An existence statement for RQE (Theorem 6) should appear in the main text; Theorem 1 (for RNE) could be deferred.
  - Theorem 4 is a high‑probability result; the statement should explicitly give 1‑δ.
  - The phrase “non-linear sum over” (line 392) is confusing – it is a recursion, not a sum.
  - Several sentences contain missing or unclear phrasing (lines 452‑453, 515, 518, 529). 
  - The reference to Tversky & Kahneman (1992) for “people do not play Nash strategies” is not supported by that paper.
  - “Strictly competitive game” is not defined (line 988).
  - In the proof of Theorem 7 (p. 21), the factor 1/2 does not seem useful.
  - The statement “to arrive at the CCE σ of the four player game” (p. 29) implies uniqueness; is there a unique CCE?
  - One reviewer asked whether choosing aggregate vs. action-dependent risk aversion matters and when each should be used.
  - If the goal is not to model human behavior, is the only advantage of RQE over optimal solutions its tractability?
  - The paper does not discuss how to accommodate non-convex compositions of risk-averse functions.
  - Specific economic scenarios that the RAMG could apply to are not outlined.
