## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper challenges the prevalent assumption that multi-task learning (MTL) is the upper bound for continual learning (CL). The authors formalize limitations of multitask objectives using principles from online learning and dynamic regret (or average lifelong error). They show theoretically and empirically that single-task (ST) agents can outperform the multi-task agent in certain non-stationary or volatile environments, particularly when the MTL solution is not optimal for forward transfer. Empirical evidence on synthetic data, CLEAR, MD5, and permuted CIFAR-10 supports the analysis. A Selective Replay method is introduced to exploit distributional drift estimates.

### Strengths
- **Important, underexplored question:** The paper questions a long-standing assumption that MTL is always the CL upper bound, bringing a necessary discussion to the community.
- **Theoretical grounding:** Provides theoretical results (in a convex, under-parameterized setting) showing conditions under which ST outperforms MTL, with clear motivation and analysis.
- **Comprehensive experiments:** Validation on multiple benchmarks (synthetic, CLEAR, MD5, permuted CIFAR-10) supports the theoretical claims.
- **Novel metric/analysis:** Introduces the concept of “instability” and uses dynamic regret / cumulative loss to reframe CL evaluation, offering new intuition.
- **Clear motivation and writing:** The paper is well motivated, and the arguments are generally easy to follow.
- **Relevance to the community:** The findings encourage rethinking baselines and evaluation protocols in CL.

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical assumptions are restrictive:** The main theoretical results assume linear models, strict convexity, and under-parameterization. This limits applicability to overparameterized deep networks and non-convex loss surfaces (with saddle points and local minima). The analysis does not address how different optimizers (e.g., SGD with momentum) affect the conclusions.
- **Inconsistency and clarity in the multi-task agent definition:** There is a mismatch between the description of the multi-task (MT) agent in the main text (line 169: sequentially trains on tasks) and the linear convex setting in Appendix B.1.2 (gradient steps on all tasks simultaneously). The assumption that the MT agent risk converges to the true multitask solution (Δ^MT_T → 0) as h→∞ is not made explicit and is critical.
- **Toy example is too simplistic:** The synthetic example assumes a label switch on the same head, making the multitask solution impossible even offline. A stronger demonstration would show a scenario where MTL *can* solve all tasks but does so slower or less efficiently than a CL approach due to conflicting gradients, rather than a contradiction in the labels.
- **Experiment structure is unclear:** Splitting the empirical evaluation into CLEAR+MD5 and permuted CIFAR-10 parts is not well motivated. The specific hypotheses tested in each part and how they collectively support the paper’s claims are difficult to follow. For instance, Table 3’s hypothesis is unclear, and Table 1 contains a mismatch (v_agent tracks error, O_agent tracks accuracy).
- **Metric and framing misalignment:** The paper focuses primarily on forward transfer (average lifelong error / dynamic regret), but most CL works also emphasise average accuracy and forgetting (backward transfer). The choice of metric is not sufficiently justified for typical CL benchmarks where retention matters. Classical metrics (average accuracy, forgetting) should be reported, and the paper should discuss how the results translate when backward transfer is important. One reviewer (score 5) finds that the focus on forward transfer alone makes the claim less impactful and argues that the findings essentially show that out-of-distribution data can help or hurt – a known phenomenon.
- **Claim about CL methods using ERM overstates the case:** The paper states that many CL methods minimize the empirical risk objective. In practice, replay-based methods often weight current task data more heavily than memory samples, deviating from a balanced ERM. This claim should be softened.
- **Missing details and presentation issues:**
  - Notation is incompletely explained: θ* in Eq. 4 is not defined; Eq. 4 may be missing a Σ_x subscript; Eq. 19 is in appendix but would be more helpful in the main text.
  - The Selective Replay algorithm is not defined in the main text (and was not found in the appendix).
  - Typos exist (e.g., line 370). Figure 1 caption appears to have the left/right labels reversed.
  - The manuscript exceeds the page limit and does not conform to the conference template.
- **Lack of practical guidance:** The paper does not provide a quantitative measure or diagnostic tool that practitioners could use to predict *a priori* when ST will outperform MTL. A metric that quantifies non-stationarity or task interference would strengthen the contribution.
- **Questions about experimental setup:** The use of h = 3000, 6000 (multiple epochs per task) resembles offline CL more than strict online learning, despite positioning in the online setting. The CIFAR‑10 permutation experiment does not specify which entries are permuted (e.g., random pixel set).
- **Minority reviewer’s stronger criticism:** While most reviewers rate the work as solid (6/6/6), one reviewer (score 5) judges the contribution low (1/4) and considers the claims too strong. This reviewer argues that the results only highlight a well-known tradeoff between forward and backward transfer, not a need to abandon multitask objectives entirely, and that the paper would be better framed in online learning rather than CL.
- **Missing discussion of non-convexity and optimization:** The theoretical analysis assumes convexity and linear models, but the empirical experiments use deep networks with non-convex losses. The paper should discuss how non-convexities and stochastic optimization could alter the derived conclusions.
