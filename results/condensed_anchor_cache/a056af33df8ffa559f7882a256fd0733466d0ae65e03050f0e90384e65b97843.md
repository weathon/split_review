## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Relative Learning Rate Schedules (RLRS) for Transformers, assigning distinct learning rates to different components (e.g., Embedding, Attention, MLP, Router). The authors suggest tuning these rates on smaller models and then transferring them to larger ones to save computational cost. Experiments on dense and Mixture of Experts (MoE) models show a ~13.6% training speedup (reduced tokens to reach a given loss). The paper also analyzes and interprets the tuned values. Overall, the motivation and idea are considered interesting and practical, but the novelty, theoretical grounding, and experimental validation are contested.

### Strengths
- **Valid and strong motivation:** different Transformer components exhibit varied update dynamics, justifying component‑wise learning rate schedules. [R1, R3]
- **Practical and intriguing transfer finding:** RLRS hyperparameters tuned on small models can be applied to much larger models (up to 27×) with minimal performance loss. [R1, R2]
- **Comprehensive experiments:** the paper demonstrates effectiveness and efficiency of RLRS on both dense and MoE configurations. [R1]
- **Application to large model scales:** the approach is tested on practically relevant large‑scale setups. [R2]
- **Clear presentation and well‑tuned hyperparameters:** the paper is easy to follow and the authors take care to tune baselines and RLRS properly. [R3]
- **Core idea is interesting:** the topic of different learning rates for different components is acknowledged as useful. [R3, R4]

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty.** The idea of assigning different learning rates to different network components is already known (e.g., in muP works [1], Tensor Programs V [2]). The paper does not compare with these existing approaches nor discuss how RLRS differs. [R2, R3, R4] One reviewer (R4) rated the paper 1, considering the method a “crude heuristic” and stating that different component learning rates are natively supported in libraries like PyTorch.
- **Lack of theoretical motivation.** The method is not theoretically justified; the selection of hyperparameters (Algorithm 3) is essentially a random search (multiply by factors 0.2–5). It is unclear whether improvements come from using different schedules or simply from having more degrees of freedom (like adding more parameters). [R1, R4]
- **Weak evidence for hyperparameter transfer.** The claim that RLRS tuned on small models remains robust across a range of model sizes is not convincingly demonstrated. Figure 4 shows that λ_small extrapolates well to one larger model, but does not demonstrate “across *a range of* model sizes.” [R1, R2] Additionally, it is doubted whether schedules tuned on short runs transfer to much longer training runs (e.g., weight‑decay effects only matter over longer horizons). [R3]
- **Unclear whether the final learning rate is needed.** Figure 4 suggests training is mostly sensitive to the peak learning rate of each component, which is already covered by existing work. The paper does not justify why separate final LR values are necessary, nor does it ablate or test different schedule shapes (e.g., different warmup lengths, earlier decay with WSD). [R3]
- **Tuning overhead not accounted for.** The RLRS tuning process (local search) may require more total computation than baseline tuning. The paper does not provide a breakdown of the resources needed to tune RLRS on small models and transfer to large ones, nor does it compare total tuning times. [R1, R2]
- **Tuning is a plain search.** The proposed “Local Search” (Algorithm 3) is a simple repeated multiplication by fixed factors (x0.2–x5) and does not use sophisticated hyperparameter optimization. This makes the algorithm inefficient in practice. [R1, R4]
- **Missing comparisons with adaptive‑learning‑rate methods.** No comparison is made with established per‑parameter adaptive methods (e.g., Sophia). [R2]
- **Experiments are relatively small‑scale and insufficiently ablated.** The scale used may not be large enough to draw strong conclusions; more ablation studies are needed. [R3]
- **Specific experimental issues and unanswered questions:**
  - The “substantial gains” claimed in line 159 (applying relative rates to an already tuned base model) are not quantified. [R1]
  - Algorithm 3 step 2: How are the set of four values derived? Step 3: How is “improvement” defined? How is the very first experiment set up? [R1]
  - Section 3.3: Is the time spent tuning RLRS accounted for when claiming faster training? [R1]
  - Should the paper report changes in *test* loss (not just training loss)? [R1]
  - Section 4.1: Is the analysis performed on a small or large model? The paper does not specify. [R1]
  - Section 4.1, Attention module: Figure 5 shows the learning rate remains unchanged for Attention—no explanation or insight is provided. The behavior in dense models (Figure 5) is also not analyzed. [R1]
  - Table 4: The start value of Embedding should be 3.3 (as per line 279–280), but the table may be inconsistent. [R1]
  - Several mentions of “performance” or “loss” do not specify training or test (e.g., Figure 2). [R1]
  - The paper claims (line 375) that Figure 4 shows the importance of tuning relative rates for individual modules, but does not explain how the plots support this. [R1]
  - Training curves or direct measurement of training time are not provided to verify the reported time reduction. [R2]
  - The paper does not discuss whether different optimizers (e.g., Lion) that control update size would still benefit from per‑component schedules. [R3]
