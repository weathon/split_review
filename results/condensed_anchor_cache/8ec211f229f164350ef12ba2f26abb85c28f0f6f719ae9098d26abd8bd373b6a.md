## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an interactive preference-based evolutionary algorithm framework that uses human preference feedback instead of a fitness function. It adopts a modified active dueling bandit algorithm (RUCB-AL) to learn preferences and integrate them into multi-objective evolutionary algorithms. Experiments are conducted on test functions, a sushi domain, and protein structure prediction.

### Strengths
- The idea of combining dueling bandits with evolutionary algorithms is novel and well-motivated, as human preferences are often easier to obtain than real-valued rewards.
- The problem is clearly defined with clean and easy-to-follow formalizations (Reviewer 3).
- The paper provides a theoretical guarantee for the dueling bandit algorithm (regret bound) and applies it to a real-world problem (protein structure prediction), which is relevant to AI for science.
- The inclusion of a module to determine query timing (active learning component) is an interesting addition, as most preferential optimization approaches either use batch-size hyperparameters or ignore query timing altogether.
- A large number of experiments is conducted, spanning simple to real-world problems, with a diverse range of comparison algorithms.

### Weaknesses
- **Presentation and clarity are poor.** Multiple key concepts are vague or undefined:
  - The problem setting and objective of the “human-dominated preference-based EMO” are not clearly explained; Figure 1 lacks a rigorous formulation.
  - It is unclear why the evolutionary algorithm is needed to optimize Eq. 1, and how the multi-objective in Eq. 1 plays a role in the dueling bandit formulation (i.e., how two arms are compared under multi-objective objectives).
  - Notation is inconsistent and unresolved: $\hat{P}$ is called the predicted preference matrix but appears to be observed preferences (Eq. 4); $P$, $\mathbf{w}$, and $\hat{P}$ are not related; $L_t$ is used both for predicted preference distribution and loss; $p_{min}$ is never described; fitness is not formally introduced, and Section 2.3 suggests learning a scalar function via “weight vectors,” contradicting the claim of being fitness-free.
  - The virtual fitness function and the process for generating preferences (including handling cyclic preferences) are not formally introduced.
  - It is unclear how the bandit algorithm is embedded into the EA: what is optimized between query times? What does “runs as normal” mean? How is the EA still working with multi-objective targets? Section 2.3 suggests learning a feedback function, which differs from the claimed fitness-free approach.
- **Theoretical weaknesses and weak connections to the main optimization goal:**
  - The regret bound for the proposed active dueling bandit algorithm is $O(T)$, which is linear and too large. Such a bound does not converge and is unsuitable for long-term optimization; sublinear regret (e.g., $O(\sqrt{T})$ or $O(\log T)$) is expected. (Reviewers 2, 3)
  - The paper lacks a theoretical link between the dueling bandit regret and the actual multi-objective optimization performance. Minimizing preference-elicitation regret is not theoretically tied to converging toward the true Pareto front. (Reviewer 2)
  - The modified RUCB algorithm drops the Condorcet winner assumption (Assumption 1) to find a Copeland winner, but it is not explained how the algorithm achieves this. Standard approaches for Copeland winners (e.g., CCB, SCB, Zoghi et al. 2015) should be discussed. (Reviewer 1)
  - The bonus term in Algorithm 1 (Eq. 5) is an unexplained modification of the standard RUCB bonus. Moreover, the squared loss of $\hat{p}_{i,j}$ in Eq. 5 cannot be computed because $p_{i,j}$ is unknown. (Reviewer 1)
- **Active learning component is not well justified.** The active dueling bandit queries solely based on whether a pair has been compared before, without using an uncertainty measure. It is not discussed why such a query mechanism is reasonable under the Copeland setting, and the linear regret implies the algorithm does not converge to the Copeland winner. (Reviewer 3)
- **Novelty is insufficient.** Learning objective functions from human feedback is not new; references include [1] Roijers et al. 2017 (interactive Thompson sampling for multi-objective bandits), [2] Ding & Zhou 2018 (preference-based adaptation for learning objectives), [3] Tucker et al. 2020 (preference-based gait optimization). Additionally, a vast number of query selection strategies exist (e.g., Bengs 2021) that aim to reduce queries, but the paper does not explain why its “active learning” differs. (Reviewers 3, 4)
- **Missing comparisons and experimental issues:**
  - No comparison with benchmarks from dueling bandit algorithms that achieve $O(\sqrt{T})$ regret (e.g., using upper confidence bounds). (Reviewer 2)
  - No comparison with existing preference-based multi-objective evolutionary algorithms (e.g., “Preference-based Multiobjective Evolutionary Algorithm for Power Network Reconfiguration” and “A Preference Based Interactive Evolutionary Algorithm for Multi-objective Optimization”). (Reviewer 4)
  - Experiments are not reproducible: hyperparameters for the EA algorithms are missing; the number of rounds, budget, and timesteps per PSP trial are not reported. (Reviewer 4)
  - In Figure 3, it is unclear how RUCB-AL can outperform RUCB after a single feedback round. (Reviewer 4)
  - The claim “Our proposed method achieves the minimum mean in 8 instances” seems true only if one is free to optimize the EA algorithm; under a fair comparison (preselected EA), IEMO/D appears best. (Reviewer 4)
  - Table 1 is hardly readable due to number formatting; using relative differences to the optimum would be better. (Reviewer 4)
  - Section 3.4 is not meaningful without the appendix, as one cannot evaluate whether the protein structure prediction results are good or bad. (Reviewer 4)
- **Minor issues and reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms:**
  - The definition of Copeland winner in Eq. 2 appears incorrect; moreover, since a Copeland winner is guaranteed to exist, it should be a “Definition” rather than an “Assumption.” (Reviewer 1)
  - Could the regret bound be improved, and could the algorithm use a UCB-based ltering approach to achieve sublinear regret? (Reviewer 2)
  - Is the greedy implementation used? Would an upper confidence interval yield better regret? (Reviewer 2)
  - How do “rounds” relate to budget, timesteps, and queries? (Reviewer 4)
