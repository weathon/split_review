## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper investigates whether the success of recent Domain Generalization (DG) methods stems from pre-training (especially CLIP) rather than algorithmic innovation. The authors propose the *Alignment Hypothesis*: high cosine-similarity between a CLIP model’s image and ground-truth text-label embeddings (before fine-tuning) is necessary for downstream DG performance after fine-tuning. Using this alignment score, they split DomainBed datasets into In-Pretraining (IP) and Out-of-Pretraining (OOP) partitions (threshold 0.21, with low-scoring noisy samples discarded at <0.15 and high-scoring text-in-image samples >0.4). Experiments on multiple DG algorithms reveal that all methods perform well on IP data but poorly on OOP data, suggesting they learn little beyond CLIP’s pre-trained features. The paper also finds that high alignment scores sometimes indicate label text embedded in the image (0.00–0.15% of data) and notes that model parameter averaging boosts performance. One reviewer (R3, score 8) was more positive than the others (scores 5, 6, 6), highlighting the benchmark contribution as a key strength.

### Strengths

- **Well-written and easy to follow** (R1, R3).
- **Addresses an important question**: disentangling the role of pre-training in DG performance (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- **Interesting insight** that low alignment scores correlate with label noise in DG datasets (R1).
- **Observation that all evaluated methods struggle on OOP data**, implying DG methods do not learn beyond CLIP pre-training; this contrasts with the common assumption of algorithmic progress (R1).  
- **Model parameter averaging boosts performance** (R1).
- **Discovery that high alignment scores can indicate label text in the image** (prevalence 0.00–0.15% across datasets) (R1).
- **Consideration of pre-training impact** and **effort to remove noisy labels** in standard DG benchmarks (R2).
- **Comprehensive set of experiments** (R2).
- **Creates a new DG benchmark (IP/OOP splits)** as a tangible contribution to the community (R3, R4).
- **Alignment hypothesis and IP/OOP splits provide a novel lens** for evaluating DG methods, revealing that models fail on examples not aligned during pre-training (R4).

### Weaknesses

- **Circularity concern (R1, R2)** – The same CLIP model is used to split data into IP/OOP (via alignment score) and then evaluated; this could artificially correlate alignment with performance.  
- **Assumption that alignment score measures presence in pre-training lacks justification** (R1). A well-learned model might show high similarity for novel in-distribution examples, undermining the reasoning behind the split.  
- **Abstract** claims to study “not just presence but how well data are learned,” but **IP/OOP splitting does not reflect “how well learned”** (R1).  
- **Trivial correlation (R2)** – Since CLIP’s contrastive loss directly maximizes image–text similarity for true labels, a high alignment score is unsurprisingly predictive of later fine-tuned performance.  
- **Limited to vision-language models** (CLIP); applicability to pure vision backbones (e.g., CNNs) is not addressed (R2).  
- **Need for quantitative comparison of Alignment vs. Image Similarity hypotheses** – not just correlation coefficients, but slopes of performance vs. score; the paper lacks such metrics (R2).  
- **Sensitivity of Perceptual Similarity Score** – would a similar split be obtained with a well-chosen threshold? How does the choice of threshold affect IP/OOP performance differences? (R2).  
- **Section 3 lacks detailed explanation** of how perceptual similarity and alignment scores are computed (features, extraction, comparison of image–label pairs). An algorithmic table is recommended (R3).  
- **Only one DG variant considered** (multi-source); sub-population shifts and other generalization tasks are not discussed or evaluated (R3).  
- **Alignment score ignores model uncertainty/calibration** – a model may have high cosine similarity to the wrong class but low confidence, which is not captured (R3).  
- **Unclear whether combining image similarity and alignment scores yields a better predictor**; also, robustness to the choice of text prompts for embeddings is not tested (R3).  
- **Need for CNN experiments** – comparing pre-trained CNNs vs. CNNs trained from scratch on the same DG methods and IP/OOP splits would determine if reliance on pre-training is CLIP-specific or a general phenomenon (R4).  
- **Missing zero-shot analysis** – evaluating zero-shot CLIP on IP/OOP splits and comparing confusion matrices to fine-tuned predictions could reveal how DG methods modify representations (e.g., instances where fine-tuning degrades performance) (R4).  
- **Revisiting Image Similarity Hypothesis for DG** – examining perceptual similarity between source and target domains (not just pre-training data) could clarify whether DG performance correlates with source–target resemblance (R4).  
- **Main takeaways risk being obscured** – a dedicated discussion section is needed to contextualize findings with prior work and clarify what the study advocates (e.g., dropping certain datasets, designing new benchmarks) (R4).  
- **Disagreement on novelty**: While R1 considers the insight that DG methods do not learn beyond CLIP “interesting,” R2 finds it “somewhat unsurprising” given prior work on pre-training dependence (e.g., [1] suggesting alternative protocols).  
- **Typographical issues** – Equation in Sec 3.1 missing angle bracket `⟨`; line 368 should read “between IP and OOP” instead of “between OP and OOP” (R3); line 177 possibly misstates “before pre-training” where “after” is intended (R2).  
- **Table 2 presentation is hard to interpret** (R1).  
- **Overly strong statements** like “DG has been solved” should be toned down (R3).
