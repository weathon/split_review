## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes CCKT-Det, an open-vocabulary object detection (OVD) framework that eliminates the need for extra supervision (e.g., image-text pairs or pseudo annotations). It constructs a cyclic and dynamic knowledge transfer from language queries and visual region features extracted from pretrained vision-language models (VLMs). The method injects semantic priors into object queries and uses a regional contrastive knowledge distillation loss to align the student detector with the teacher VLM’s visual-semantic space. CCKT-Det achieves state-of-the-art results on COCO (+2.9% and +10.2% AP50 over prior SOTA without/with stronger teacher) and strong performance on LVIS and Objects365. The approach scales with stronger VLMs while maintaining moderate computational overhead. While one reviewer (Reviewer 1, score 8) found the work easy to follow and the techniques correct, multiple other reviewers identified significant weaknesses in motivation, clarity, missing evaluations, and contradictions in ablation results.

### Strengths

- The proposed method is reasonable and effectively transfers the visual-language capabilities of pretrained VLMs into a detector without extra supervision, a significant advancement for OVD (Reviewers 1,3,4,5).
- Comprehensive experiments on OV-COCO, LVIS, and Objects365 benchmarks with thorough ablation studies validate each component (Reviewers 1,3,4,5). Results show consistent improvement as teacher model strength increases (Reviewer 4).
- The framework is explicit and well-structured with solid methodological rigor (Reviewers 2,5). It demonstrates high-performance OVD without the need for additional supervision such as image-text pairs or pseudo annotations (Reviewer 4).
- Most used techniques are correct and easy to follow (Reviewer 1 – minority positive view).

### Weaknesses

1. **Missing base-class performance and detailed benchmark results.**  
   No AP for base categories is reported on COCO (Reviewers 2,3,4). This omission makes it impossible to assess the trade-off between base and novel class detection. For LVIS, AP_c and AP_f results are also missing from Tables 1 and 2 (Reviewer 4). The method appears weak on base objects, suggesting a cost for improving novel categories (Reviewers 3,4). Without base AP, the overall practical utility is unclear (Reviewer 2).

2. **Unclear motivation and ambiguous problem definition.**  
   The authors state they aim to avoid extra caption data, but several existing methods (e.g., OV-DQUO) already achieve high performance without such data (Reviewer 2). The specific performance bottlenecks or limitations of current SOTA methods that CCKT-Det addresses beyond simply avoiding extra data are not clearly defined (Reviewer 2).

3. **Lack of precise definition for “semantic prior” and differentiation from captions/prompts.**  
   The term “semantic prior” is used without a formal definition of how it is derived, what information it encodes, and how it differs from other text-based inputs (Reviewer 2). Figure 3 shows the semantic prior passing through the VLM text encoder, but the difference from a caption or a standard prompt is not explained (Reviewer 2). The dynamic injection of text embeddings as semantic priors is similar to ideas explored in GroundingDINO; the paper should cite and discuss these related works (Reviewer 5).

4. **Reliance on extra supervision contradicts the claim of “no extra data.”**  
   Although the paper emphasizes training without additional supervision, it uses an extra MLLM as a discriminator to generate the prior semantic guidance (Reviewer 5). This external model constitutes additional supervision, conflicting with the central claim (Reviewer 5).

5. **Contradiction in ablation results regarding Regional-Contrastive Loss.**  
   In Table 4, the paper states “semantic guidance’s efficacy is constrained in the absence of regional contrastive training,” but the ablation shows that adding Regional-Contrastive Loss actually causes a drop (e.g., AP50 from 32.6 to 31.7) and appears detrimental to semantic guidance (Reviewers 3,5). The claim that its absence leads to a 5.4% AP50 decline on novel classes is not supported by the data; the loss seems to work only in conjunction with Similarity Classification, not with Semantic Guiding alone (Reviewer 5). This appears to be a clerical error (Reviewer 5).

6. **Heavy reliance on teacher model (CLIP/MLLM) quality and associated biases.**  
   The method depends on visual-semantic embeddings from VLMs and MLLMs; any biases in their training data (e.g., skewed category representations, contextual associations) can propagate to the detector (Reviewer 1). CLIP is used for both semantic priors and feature extraction, creating a single point of failure (Reviewer 1). Using stronger teacher models during training and inference introduces significant computational costs beyond just training, limiting deployment on resource-constrained or real-time systems (Reviewer 1). The paper does not compare or analyze time complexity (Reviewer 3).

7. **Risk of over-reliance on semantic priors and pseudo-annotation noise.**  
   Over-dependence on semantic priors may reduce flexibility for truly unseen objects that fall outside the learned semantic scope (Reviewer 1). Pseudo annotations (used in training) are less accurate than human annotations and can introduce noise, especially for challenging classes, with no robust filtering mechanism described (Reviewer 1).

8. **Limited presentation clarity and insufficient comparative methods.**  
   Figure 2 is confusing and lacks a clear visualization of training vs. testing workflow details (Reviewer 3). Few comparative methods are evaluated on the OV-LVIS benchmark (Reviewer 3).

9. **Question about Hungarian matching based on CLIP embeddings.**  
   Given that CLIP embeddings may not fully capture object-specific regional nuances, why should the Hungarian matching approach significantly improve performance? This criticism is a missing justification for a core mechanism (Reviewer 1).
