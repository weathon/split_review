## Merged Review

### Summary
SPADE is a post-hoc local explanation method that applies sample-specific, layer-wise pruning to a trained network, preserving its output and intermediate activations for a given input by solving a constrained optimization (using the OBC sparsity solver). Interpretability methods (saliency maps, neuron visualizations) are then applied to the sparsified network. Evaluations include Trojan detection (ground-truth known), saliency map accuracy (AUC, Pointing Game) on small subsets of ImageNet-1K, CelebA, Food-101 across ResNet50, MobileNetV2, ConvNeXt, and human task performance. Comparisons are to the original dense network and the FC pruning method of Wong et al. (2021). One reviewer (R3, score 8, confidence 3) considered the idea clear and the experimental results extensive, while two others (R2, R4, scores 3, confidence 4) found significant weaknesses in scalability, novelty, and evaluation rigor; the fourth (R1, score 6, confidence 3) was broadly positive but raised several transparency and clarity concerns.

### Strengths
- **Clear and intuitive idea**: Sample-specific pruning to reduce neuron multifacetedness before interpretation is straightforward; the paper clearly outlines design choices (solvers, objectives, use of different explainers) and includes a thorough ablation (R1, R3, R4).
- **Evaluation with ground truth via Trojans**: Designing a Trojan scenario to obtain ground truth about the model’s behavior avoids common pitfalls in evaluating explanations (R1).
- **Reproducibility**: Code is open-sourced; experimental settings, datasets, sparsity ratios, and model weights (with Trojans) are detailed; appendices are well-organized (R1, R2).
- **Variety in evaluation**: Multiple architectures (ResNet50, MobileNetV2, ConvNeXt), datasets (ImageNet-1K, CelebA, Food-101), saliency methods (path-based and perturbation-based), and metrics (AUC, Pointing Game) plus a human study (R2, R3, R4).
- **Well-written and organized**: Paper is easy to follow; the appendix includes a table of contents (R1, R2, R4).
- **Human task performance**: SPADE improved neuron visualization usefulness in a user study (R1, R2, R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Prohibitive computational cost** (raised by all reviewers, but severity varies):
  - SPADE takes 41 minutes per sample on a single RTX 2080 GPU; one reviewer (R2) notes it becomes “prohibitively costly” for large datasets, and another (R4) calculates “weeks” for the full ImageNet validation set. R1 asks for guidance on how to select samples to explain given this cost. R3 (positive reviewer) also notes the high computational effort as a weakness.
  - By contrast, the baseline FC pruning (Wong et al.) is run once for all examples (40 hours) but is less accurate per-sample; reviewers disagree on whether the SPADE vs. FC comparison is fair given the cost difference (R4).
- **Small-scale and biased evaluation** (R2, R4):
  - The main ImageNet-1K saliency results (Table 1) are reported on only **140 test samples** (out of 50k in the validation set), selected based on Trojan patch presence and label change. This introduces significant bias and makes it difficult to generalize findings to the broader dataset (R2). R4 questions how the 140 samples were chosen.
  - The sparsity ratio is selected using **100 calibration samples** with ground truth (maximizing AUC on those samples). This uses additional “global” information and may give an overly optimistic view of performance; in real usage, a user would not have such ground truth (R1, R4). R4 further argues this makes the comparison with Wong et al. unfair because the aim of FC pruning is a balance between sparsity and accuracy, not optimization for a specific explainer metric.
- **Limited novelty** (R2, R4):
  - The core idea of using sparsity to disentangle features and improve interpretability is not new (R2). SPADE applies an existing OBC sparsity solver and off-the-shelf interpretability techniques; it does not present novel insights, methods, or findings (R2). R4 adds that per-example sparsity has been previously proposed (e.g., in Concept Bottleneck Models), and that SPADE’s approach is incremental.
  - One reviewer (R2) rates contribution as “poor” (1), while another (R1) rates it “good” (3). R3 rates contribution as “good” (3). Disagreement exists.
- **Fairness and fidelity of saliency maps** (R2):
  - Because SPADE uses a different sparse subnetwork for each input sample, saliency maps are generated relative to different models. This makes it unclear whether the maps are representative of the original dense model’s reasoning and raises questions about consistency and comparability across samples.
  - Related: R1 notes that the improvement over baselines is “not that strong” in Table 1 and Figure 4, and asks whether existing explanations are already well-suited for the Trojan task.
- **Clarity gaps in experimental design** (R1, R3, R4):
  - **Trojan motivation**: R1 questions why a data-dependent explanation method is evaluated on a Trojan-detection task where the trigger can only be found if the sample already contains it, citing [1] (Casper et al.). The paper should clarify the alignment between the task and the method.
  - **Human study details**: R1 and R3 both note that the “image patches” and the “correct answer” are not clearly defined (e.g., what constitutes the ground truth region for the neuron?). R3 specifically: “I could not fully understand the details of the human study.”
  - **Saliency map experiment**: R1 asks whether the 140 examples are all Trojan images, and whether saliency maps are taken w.r.t. the predicted class or the true Trojan class.
  - **Definition of sparsity**: R1 asks to clarify that “sparse” means L0 (most weights zero), while Eq.(1) uses an L2 constraint; R1 also asks for intuition on why L2 was chosen. R3 asks for the definition of \(W_{\text{sparse}}\) in Eq.(1).
- **Unaddressed theoretical and practical questions** (R2, R4):
  - R2 asks how SPADE’s constrained optimization (activation matching) disentangles multifaceted neurons. Intuitively, if neurons are multifaceted across samples, pruning based on a single sample’s augmentations might still retain multifaceted neurons and discard specific ones.
  - R4 asks about limitations: SPADE excludes depthwise convolutions (Appendix B); does it extend to ViTs (only MLP layers?)? Also asks how to store sparse structures per example, and what differences arise between semantically similar samples.
- **Other minor issues**:
  - R3 notes a typo (“there were were”) on page 9.
  - Figure 2 (left) implies image rotation is used, but Section 3.2 describes horizontal flips.
  - R3 asks whether batch-norm re-calibration is applied after SPADE pruning.
  - R1 suggests the term “debugging” might be better replaced by “interpretability” or “explanations” since the paper focuses on local explanations.
