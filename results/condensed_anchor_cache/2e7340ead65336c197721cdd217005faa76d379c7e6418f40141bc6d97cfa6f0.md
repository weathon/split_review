## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates where bracket-matching information is stored in GPT-2’s MLP weights. Using activation patching, embedding projections, attention analysis, and linear probes, the authors find that early MLP layers store low-level syntactic information about brackets and that the residual activation for predicting the closing bracket lies dormant until later layers. The study is based on a single GPT-2-small model and focuses on short, non-nested bracket sequences (length 5). Reviewers note that the work is thorough but limited in generality and novelty.

### Strengths
- The study is thorough, employing multiple tests (activation patching, embedding projections, attention analysis, linear probes) that support the claimed findings. (Reviewer 2)
- The simple probing task (bracketed sentences) is easy to interpret and provides a clear test case. (Reviewer 1)
- To our knowledge, this is the first work to localize the “state” of a bracketing sequence and attempt to understand the underlying retrieval mechanism. (Reviewer 3)
- The investigation into how information is processed and retrieved is interesting and represents a step forward in understanding deep learning networks. (Reviewers 2, 3)
- The results make sense and are interesting: syntactic information is stored in earlier layers, supporting a hierarchical role of layers. (Reviewer 1)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited sequence length and toy setting:** All experiments (except Appendix A) use only 5-token non-nested bracket sequences. The title claims “long-range dependencies,” but the actual inputs are very short, misrepresenting the scope. This severely limits the generality and relevance compared to prior work that studies long-distance dependencies and nested Dyck languages. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3; majority concern)
- **No comparison with related methods and prior work:** The paper does not compare with existing techniques for locating information encoding (e.g., probing classifiers as in https://aclanthology.org/P19-1356.pdf). It also overlooks highly relevant prior work on neural networks for Dyck languages (e.g., Yu et al., “Learning the Dyck Language with Attention-based Seq2Seq Models”) and long-range agreement localization (e.g., Lakretz et al., “The emergence of number and syntax units in LSTM language models,” which uses a related neuron-suppression method), as well as work on feature interactions (Jumelet and Zuidema). This omission weakens the paper’s novelty and context. (Reviewers 1, 3)
- **Residual analysis is incomplete:** The analysis of the residual output is too short and does not fully explain the observed phenomenon; the authors should expand it. (Reviewer 1)
- **Results are unsurprising and significance is unclear:** It is expected that simple information is stored in early layers and more complex relationships in deeper layers; the paper does not clearly demonstrate why the findings are significant or offer actionable takeaways—the conclusion mostly just “supports the hierarchical hypothesis.” (Reviewers 2, 3)
- **Single model instance:** Most experiments study only one instance of GPT-2-small (Appendix A is partial), so conclusions may not generalize. (Reviewer 3)
- **Unexplained variation across bracket types:** Figure 7 shows very different curves for different bracket types, but the authors do not discuss why; one would expect similar curves. (Reviewer 2)
- **Dataset rationale unclear:** It is not explained why n=5 sequences were chosen or why the open bracket token location is fixed. (Reviewer 1)

**Minority reviewer disagreement:** One reviewer (score 6) found the study thorough and a step forward, while the other two (scores 3) considered the work limited in novelty, generality, and depth. The merged weaknesses reflect the majority view, but the strengths above capture the positive assessment from all reviewers.
