## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper theoretically studies adversarial robustness through the lens of implicit bias in gradient descent, focusing on a simplified two-layer ReLU network (only first layer trained) for binary classification. The data distribution consists of multiple clusters with orthogonal cluster center vectors. The authors rigorously prove that gradient descent converges to a “feature averaging” solution where hidden neuron weights encode an average of cluster centers, leading to large ℓ2 non-robustness (perturbation radius Ω(√(d/k))). They further show that when more granular supervised information (cluster labels) is provided, the network learns individual features and yields a binary classifier with optimal robustness O(√d). Experiments on synthetic data, MNIST, and CIFAR-10 support the theoretical findings.

### Strengths
- **Important topic**: The interplay between implicit bias of optimization and adversarial robustness is a significant area of study (R2, R4).
- **Thorough theoretical analysis**: The paper provides a rigorous finite-time analysis of gradient descent dynamics in the feature learning regime, with reasonable assumptions and sound derivations (R1, R2, R3, R4). The Appendix is exceptionally polished and easy to verify (R4).
- **Well-written and logically organized**: Clear notations, assumptions, and detailed comparisons with prior work (R2, R3, R4).
- **Intuitive concept**: The “feature averaging” characterization of approximate linearity under NTK assumptions provides an intuitive explanation for non-robustness (R2).
- **Interesting suggestion**: Providing additional supervision (fine-grained labels) to improve robustness is a novel observation, and experimental results suggest generalization beyond the toy distribution (R4).
- **Contribution to optimization perspective**: Helps understand robustness from gradient descent dynamics, and the analysis aids in proving a conjecture (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Strong and unrealistic assumptions**: Multiple reviewers note that the assumptions severely limit applicability.
    - Cluster centers are required to be exactly orthonormal and equinorm, which is unlikely in real-world data (R3).
    - The number of neurons M = 2m is assumed finite and equal to the number of clusters (Assumption 4.3), which contradicts the purpose of NTK theory for infinitely wide networks (R2).
    - The multi-cluster setup is restrictive (R3). The analysis benefits from discussion of how these assumptions impact applicability to realistic overparameterized networks (R2).
- **Phenomenological analysis, not prescriptive**: The theory describes observed behavior but does not offer concrete methods to identify or prevent feature averaging during training, limiting practical applicability (R1).
- **Main conceptual observation may not be new**: Prior work (Frei et al., 2022) already showed that gradient descent on this distribution leads to non-robustness due to implicit bias. The authors claim novelty in finite-time analysis versus KKT analysis, but approximate results from Liu & Li (2020) are consistent with the KKT analysis, so the averaging solution might be deducible from Frei et al.’s KKT equations (R4, Q1).
- **Empirical validation limited**: The experiments are confined to simple scenarios (R1). The CIFAR-10 results are not convincing, with average cosine value lower than 0.1 (R2). The perturbation radius measurement in Figure 3 lacks clarity (e.g., input ranges for MNIST/CIFAR-10) (R4, Q4).
- **Narrow architectural and problem scope**:
    - Analysis is limited to two-layer ReLU networks with only one learnable layer; extending to deeper networks seems challenging, and different activations are not addressed (R3).
    - The definition of feature averaging is not meaningful for deeper layers (R1, Q1).
    - The paper focuses on ℓ2 perturbations; it is unclear whether the bias would be beneficial or harmful for ℓ∞ perturbations (R4, Q2). The experiments do not measure ℓ∞ robustness (R4, Q3).
- **Fine-grained supervision is unrealistic**: Using original cluster labels for all data points is extremely unrealistic; feature decoupling is a trivial result when cluster labels are given (R2). One reviewer finds this suggestion interesting, while another finds it unrealistic (R2 vs R4).
- **Insufficient discussion of prior work**: The novelty relative to prior work (e.g., Frei et al. 2022, Min & Vidal 2024) is not clearly delineated; the conjecture from Min & Vidal (2024) should be introduced earlier (R4).
- **Lack of clarity on certain results**:
    - Proposition 4.8 does not follow the main setup (h=1), and the derivation of the multi-class network parameters is unclear (R2).
    - Strong correlation (cosine value) is used as evidence of feature averaging, but no threshold or rigorous proof is given (R2).
- **Questions and missing analyses** (overlap with criticisms or requested experiments):
    - How does feature averaging extend to deeper networks conceptually or mathematically? (R1, Q1)
    - For domains like time series and NLP, robust features may be “mixing” of original data; how should feature mixing be defined and would the theory apply? (R1, Q2)
    - Is there a relation between feature averaging and the frequency principle (low-frequency bias)? (R1, Q3)
    - With fine-grained supervision, would the learned network still be non-robust for multi-class classification? (R3, Q1)
    - Would adversarial training of binary data naturally lead to feature decoupling similar to the fine-grained supervision case? (R3, Q2)
    - Citation claim about Ilyas et al. (2019) visualizing robust/nonrobust features is inaccurate; there are two works providing such visualizations (R4, Q5).
- Notation issues: Theta notation not defined, big-O usage inconsistent (R2).
- Minor typos: lines 091, 107, 167, 261, 893, 1098, 1100, 1307-1310 (R4).
