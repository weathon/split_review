## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes MIND, which combines a masked prediction loss (similar to BYOL with masking as augmentation) with a one-step inverse dynamics model for representation learning in pixel-based deep RL. It is evaluated on Atari 100k and DMControl 100k, showing improved data efficiency over several baselines.

### Strengths
- The problem of learning representations for RL is well-motivated and important.
- MIND shows considerable empirical improvements over strong baselines (SPR, DrQ) on the challenging Atari 100k benchmark, as well as convincing gains on the DM Control suite.
- The empirical analysis is thorough, including extensive ablation studies on the learning objective, hyperparameters, masking percentages, and masking strategy. The analysis of what the model learns is informative.
- A wall-clock time study demonstrates the method is practical on a single GPU and is computationally cheaper than some competing methods.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and theoretical grounding**: The method is not extremely novel; it essentially combines a BYOL-style masked prediction loss (using momentum contrast and masking as augmentation) with a one-step inverse dynamics model. The justification is ad‑hoc and lacks principled theoretical motivation, especially given recent advances (Efroni 2022, Lamb 2022). Connections to related empirical works (InfoPower, ACRO by Islam 2022, A‑LIX, ATC, TACO) are missing.
- **Missing baselines**: Several important baselines are absent. For continuous control, comparisons with A‑LIX, ATC, and TACO are missing; on Atari, RAD and DrQv2 are missing. Also missing are comparisons with methods using Transformers for next‑frame prediction and vanilla MAE. This limits assessment of the method’s significance.
- **Limited continuous control evaluation**: DMControl evaluation is restricted to the six easiest tasks from the original CURL benchmark. The method is not tested on medium‑difficulty or harder tasks (e.g., humanoid domain), where many recent works report results at 1M/2M steps. This makes the continuous‑control results less convincing.
- **Insufficiency of single‑step inverse model**: Multiple reviewers note that single‑step inverse dynamics is theoretically insufficient to capture the full agent‑centric representation (citing Islam et al. 2023, Lamb et al. 2023). A multi‑step inverse model (e.g., predicting actions from states k steps apart, with k > 1) is likely beneficial and should be explored. One reviewer suggests trying k sampled uniformly from 1 to 5.
- **Clarity and missing details**:
  * Figure 1 is confusing and misleading: it suggests reconstruction of images, but the actual loss is computed in latent space, and the action‑prediction flow is unclear. The diagram should show the masking operation, encoder, and reconstruction process in latent space.
  * The motivation for using a Transformer is not clearly explained; stating “temporal dependencies” is insufficient. The paper should specify the type of dependencies and how attention helps.
  * Details of action prediction are lacking: how current and next state representations are combined (e.g., concatenation?), the architecture of the classifier (linear or more complex?), and how the online network handles both state regression and action prediction (separate heads? loss combination?) are not described.
  * Equation (1) has undefined variables p, c, q.
  * The use of separate networks for the masked prediction task (MT) and inverse dynamics task (IT) is not justified. It is unclear why the MT network cannot be reused with a different head. Also, why no masking is applied to the IT network is not justified.
- **Experimental rigor**: 
  * Confidence intervals are not reported in Table 1, making it hard to judge significance of results.
  * The Transformer module introduces extra parameters; it is unclear if baseline methods were equipped with a similar architecture to ensure fair comparison. An ablation studying the contribution of the Transformer is missing.
