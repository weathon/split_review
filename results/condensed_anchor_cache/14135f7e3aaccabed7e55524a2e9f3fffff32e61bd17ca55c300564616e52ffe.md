## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper evaluates whether Medical Vision-Language Pre-training (MedVLP) can succeed using purely synthetic data. The authors propose SYNCXR, a pipeline that uses named entity recognition (RaTE on RadGraph) to extract entities from real reports, samples them with a balanced prior, generates synthetic reports via LLMs (with entity-existence checks), and then produces chest X-ray images via a text-to-image model. MedVLP models (ConVIRT, GLoRIA) trained exclusively on synthetic data outperform those trained on real MIMIC-CXR data by 3.8% AUC in zero-shot classification; mixing synthetic and real data gives a further 9.07% improvement. Synthetic data also improves zero-shot grounding and fine-tuned classification/segmentation. The reviewers note the thorough evaluation and potential for balanced datasets, but raise significant concerns about data generation quality, missing implementation details, limited scope, and lack of theoretical novelty. One reviewer (rating 8, confidence 4) is more positive about the approach and agrees with the main conclusion, but still has major concerns about the pipeline. The other two reviewers (ratings 5, confidence 5) are more critical, highlighting confusing presentation, unsupported claims, and insufficient validation.

### Strengths
- The proposed image-report generation pipeline uses entities extracted from real-world reports with a balanced sampling prior, creating more balanced synthetic datasets. This helps downstream evaluation (Reviewer 1).
- Very thorough evaluation with carefully designed experiment settings – multiple downstream tasks (zero-shot classification/grounding, fine-tuned classification/segmentation), multiple OOD datasets, ablation of different report and image generation models (Reviewer 1, 2).
- The paper is well-written, clear, and easy to follow (Reviewer 1).
- Fully synthetic image generation could open the door to more balanced datasets for training larger vision models – a challenging topic in medical image analysis (Reviewer 2).
- The work creatively connects pretrained LLMs, NER algorithms, and TTI synthesis for a new purpose; emphasis on using only open-source models is commendable (Reviewer 2).
- Developed SYNCXR, a large-scale synthetic X-ray dataset that improves VLM training (Reviewer 3).
- Identified common quality issues in real-world datasets like MIMIC-CXR (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Entity sampling and report quality control**: The entity sampling process can sample contradictive entities (e.g., “decreased in size” and “increased in size” – both top-50 entities in Figure 6). The extracted entities also contain repeated or less-meaningful items (Appendix top-200 examples). The only quality control is checking that each sampled entity appears in the generated “Impression” section, not whether the combination is clinically reasonable. Enforcing all sampled entities to appear in Impression is unnatural (real Impression sections do not list every finding). No example reports are provided to convince readers of quality (Reviewer 1).

2. **Image quality concerns**: Despite using a SoTA CXR generative model validated by “clinical experts”, there is no control over anatomical structure in generated images – unrealistic CXRs may occur, especially given potentially contradictory or incorrect synthetic reports. This could pollute downstream training (Reviewer 1, also indirectly Reviewer 3 who asks for clinician validation).

3. **Missing implementation details**: 
   - The actual values of \(k\), \(m\), and sampling threshold \(\tau_{\text{max}}\) are not given in paper or appendix (Reviewer 1).
   - Pre-training with mixed data doubles the dataset size; it is unclear if training time is also doubled or if other settings change (Reviewer 1).
   - The specific prompts used for image filtering/QA are not clearly documented (Reviewer 2; should be in appendix).
   - How fine-tuning works in Section 4.2 – which pretraining dataset is used, which dataset for fine-tuning – is not explained (Reviewer 2).
   - Approximate compute time per sample (text generation + filtering + QA + Impression generation + TTI) is missing (Reviewer 2).

4. **Insufficient baseline comparisons**: Only two MedVLP baselines (ConVIRT, GLoRIA) are used. The authors should include naïve CLIP and MGCA (Reviewer 1). There is no comparison to other concept generation/filtering techniques, which is the main contribution (Reviewer 2). No comparison to self-supervised CXR models like Rad-DiNO for zero-shot segmentation or classification – what is gained by using a VLM? (Reviewer 2)

5. **Confusing evaluation presentation**:
   - In many cases the test set used for evaluation is not clear (e.g., Table 4, Figure 3) (Reviewer 2).
   - Section 5 (“Analysis”) lacks a description of which test set is employed, making performance reports hard to interpret/validate (Reviewer 2).
   - Sections 4.2 and 5 should be merged into one results section (Reviewer 2).
   - Figure 3 lacks exact numbers – the plot is unclear, especially the bottom right figure (Reviewer 1).
   - Table 3 row 3 (ConVIRT-MIX) has incorrect bold formatting; Appendix A.2 “Medical Image Fine-tuned Classification” section is aligned incorrectly (Reviewer 1).
   - Typo in line 233 (Reviewer 3).

6. **Lack of discussion and unsupported claims**:
   - No discussion section – several claims lack supporting metrics: “Some methods also leverage external datasets to boost performance, raising concerns about generalizability” (no metric); “Our results confirm that many images in the dataset exhibit these issues” (no metric); Section 5 contains hypotheses without formal support (e.g., “likely due to the persistent long-tailed distribution”, “as synthetic images can be curated…to ensure high quality”) (Reviewer 2).
   - The work is primarily an engineering approach to a specific task – lacks theoretical contribution (e.g., mathematical explanation of why synthetic data improves representation learning) and robust validation (no real clinician evaluation of generated data) (Reviewer 3).
   - F1 and Dice/IoU scores in zero-shot evaluation (Tables 1, 2) are very low; IoU increase over real baseline is not super significant; no discussion or comparison to other vanilla VLMs or non-VLMs (Reviewer 2).

7. **Limited scope and missing experiments**:
   - Study only covers chest X-ray modality; no experiments on other modalities (Reviewer 3).
   - Only evaluates MedVLP downstream tasks; no experiments on other vision-language pretraining tasks or models that could show confident quantitative results (Reviewer 3).
   - For medical image segmentation, only quantitative results (single-digit figures) are given – qualitative comparisons across methods are needed to strengthen claims (Reviewer 3).
   - The data generation pipeline is compute-intensive; possibility of model collapse when feeding synthetic data back (Reviewer 2, citing Nature 2024).

8. **Entity coverage issues**: With 150k+ entities but only 200k synthetic samples, the per-entity count is very low, and illogical combinations (e.g., “pneumothorax in rib”) are not accounted for (Reviewer 2).

9. **Small sample removal concern**: The authors removed 1,448 + 5,512 samples (~3% of MIMIC-CXR) but did not demonstrate that this removal significantly benefits MedVLP training (Reviewer 3).

10. **Placement of RadGraph/RaTE discussion**: Should be in related work, not interrupting main text (Reviewer 3).
