## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes UTILITY, a framework that uses explainable reinforcement learning (XRL) to improve RL agent performance. It generates a two-level explanation: a high-level explanation via inverse RL (IRL) that recovers a reward function $\hat{r}$ approximating the agent’s internal reward, and a low-level explanation that identifies “misleading” state-action pairs where the agent chooses suboptimal actions (i.e., negative advantage). These explanations are combined into a constrained bi-level optimization: the upper level shapes the ground truth reward using $\hat{r}$, and the lower level constrains the policy to limit visits to misleading states. A dual-based iterative algorithm is provided with convergence guarantees. Experiments on four MuJoCo environments show UTILITY outperforms SAC and other baselines. Reviewers praised the novel idea, thorough theoretical analysis, clear figures, and extensive ablations, but raised significant concerns about the ad hoc derivation of the objective, limited evaluation environments, unclear practical utility of explanations, and need for stronger theoretical justification of key design choices.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important problem: using explainability to directly improve RL performance. The UTILITY framework is novel.
- Clear writing, good background literature survey, and motivation. Figures (especially Figure 1) are illustrative and helpful.
- Detailed theoretical analysis with proofs (convergence of the bilevel optimization algorithm). Some reviewers noted the analysis is sound although proof sketches could be expanded in the main paper.
- The idea of identifying “misleading” state-action pairs (where advantage is negative) to guide policy updates is neat and valuable for post-hoc policy analysis.
- The approach has a close connection to generalized policy iteration: avoiding disadvantageous actions resembles policy improvement, and learning a Q-function resembles policy evaluation, making the algorithm well-founded.
- Comprehensive experiments on MuJoCo, including ablation studies showing the contribution of each explanation level. Code is provided.
- Better performance than SAC and other XRL baselines in terms of return.
- The use of IRL as a signal for RL is interesting and underexplored.

### Weaknesses
- **Derivation of the main objective (Eq. 1) is ad hoc and lacks strong justification.**  
  - The claim that suboptimality arises only from “critical points” (misleading states) is not justified; an agent could be slightly suboptimal in all states.  
  - The metric $\pi_A - \pi^*$ (distribution difference) is unclear and not established.  
  - Top-$K$ misleading states are ordered by $l(s,a)$ (negative advantage), but changing actions in those states affects future state visitation; ordering by this metric may be invalid (e.g., a state far from the initial condition might be ranked high but would never be visited if earlier actions were fixed).  
  - The reward shaping term $r(s,a) - \hat{r}(s,a)$ is ad hoc: there is no formal result connecting the maximum-likelihood IRL reward $\hat{r}$ to the true reward or guaranteeing that subtracting it helps.  
  - Discouraging the policy from visiting misleading states may be harmful (e.g., if the agent must cross a “bridge” to reach the goal, those states are misleading under a suboptimal policy but necessary for optimal behavior).  
  - The causal entropy term for exploration is not well motivated.  
  *(Reviewer 4, supported by Reviewer 2’s similar concern about overkill)*

- **The explanation itself has several unresolved issues.**  
  - The IRL reward $\hat{r}$ is not unique; the paper does not discuss how to select among multiple solutions or how different high-level explanations affect the low-level explanations.  
  - The definition of misleading states relies on the learned Q-function of the (suboptimal) policy $\pi_A$; if that Q-function is inaccurate, the identification may be flawed. Moreover, computing $l(s,a)$ requires Q-values for all actions, including those rarely visited under $\pi_A$, which is difficult.  
  - The cost function $c(s,a)$ for the lower-level constraint is not explicitly defined, nor is the procedure for selecting the top-$K$ misleading state-action pairs.  
  - It is unclear why the agent would learn a “wrong” internal reward function in the first place; if the agent is optimal for the true reward, the whole framework collapses.  
  - The shaping reward function’s functional form is not specified (e.g., line 245).  
  *(Reviewers 1,2,3)*

- **Limited evaluation and generalizability concerns.**  
  - Only MuJoCo environments are tested (continuous control, dense rewards, full-episode termination). These allow the agent to revisit misleading states repeatedly, making the method more effective. The approach may not transfer to environments with early termination, sparse rewards, high-dimensional features (e.g., Atari), or stochastic dynamics.  
  - The paper does not discuss how performance changes in stochastic environments, nor whether theoretical guarantees hold there.  
  - The method requires substantial additional environment interaction (for IRL, Q-learning, and cost estimation). The comparison with SAC may be unfair if SAC is given the same total number of samples, including the extra ones used by UTILITY.  
  - No experiments on gridworld variants (sparse/dense/multiple terminal states) to demonstrate generalizability.  
  *(Reviewers 1,2,3)*

- **Practical utility of the explanations and complexity.**  
  - The primary novelty is using explanations to improve performance; the explanations themselves (IRL reward, negative advantage) are not new.  
  - The bilevel optimization framework is overkill; the low-level constraint is essentially just keeping the policy improving, and the IRL reward is analogous to standard policy evaluation. Simpler alternatives (e.g., directly using generalized policy iteration) might achieve the same performance with less complexity.  
  - It is unclear how the explanations help humans understand the agent; the complexity of the algorithm (multiple neural networks, bilevel optimization) makes it harder to interpret. The target audience of the explanations is not discussed.  
  *(Reviewers 2,3,4)*

- **Theoretical gaps and presentation issues.**  
  - Theorem 1 lacks a proof reference; it is unclear whether it is proven in the paper or deferred.  
  - The uniqueness of the solution (Theorem 1 and 2) is not clarified: is only the return $J$ unique, or is the policy $\pi$ also unique?  
  - The dual approximation used to make the lower-level unconstrained and convex is not justified: how does it bound the original objective, and why is the approximation still valid?  
  - The loss function for learning the shaping reward (Lemma 2 gradients) is not provided.  
  - The paper talks heavily about XRL but the method reduces to standard IRL and advantage measures; the XRL framing feels overclaimed.  
  - Typo: “RICE+constaint” appears twice (lines 513-514).  
  *(Reviewers 1,2,3,4)*
