## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes ConceptGCD for Generalized Category Discovery (GCD), which introduces a concept learning framework that separates concepts into derivable (from known classes) and underivable categories. It uses a frozen known-class encoder, a generator layer with covariance-augmented loss for derivable concepts, and later expands it to learn underivable concepts with a contrastive loss and concept score normalization. The method is evaluated on six GCD benchmarks and achieves strong performance. The paper is well-organized, includes comprehensive comparisons and ablation analysis.

### Strengths
- **Novel concept-based framework:** Introduces a two-branch architecture with a frozen known-class encoder and a derivable concept generator, enabling transfer of known class knowledge to novel classes. The framework categorizes concepts as derivable/underivable and uses a stage-wise training strategy to handle unlabeled data noise.  
- **Detailed analysis and ablation:** Provides thorough ablation studies on each loss, neuron layer, and visualization of representation space and concepts, demonstrating the contribution of each component.  
- **Strong empirical performance:** Consistently outperforms prior methods across six benchmarks, with significant gains on datasets such as Stanford Cars.  
- **Clear presentation and comprehensive comparisons:** Paper is well-structured, clearly written, and includes a thorough literature review and comparison of methods.

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear definition of "concept" and "derivable/underivable":**  
  - No clear definition of what a "concept" is in this context (scalar element of an embedding) or how it differs from a "class".  
  - Terms "derivable" and "underivable" are not adequately defined; examples and a description of how they are generated are missing, making the introduction and Figure 2 vague.  
  - The claim that a scalar value represents semantic visual cues (e.g., "wheel", "window") in Figure 2 is unverified; the mapping between embedding dimensions and semantic concepts is not justified.  
  - Notation for key variables \(n\) and \(m\) (lines 289–290) is unclear, and no ablation study examines their impact on performance.

- **Insufficient support for motivational claims:**  
  - The abstract claim that "novel classes achieve impressive clustering results on the feature space of a known class pre-trained model" is not clearly backed by Table 1; the evidence is insufficient.  
  - The claim that "derivable concepts are a key reason known class data helps novel class learning" lacks experimental or theoretical proof; comparison with related works [1,2] is needed.  
  - The statement that existing methods "struggle to capture all the derivable concepts" based on Table 1 is not adequately explained.  
  - The motivational insights from Figure 1 and the introduction are not convincing; control experiments and qualitative analysis are required to confirm the role of derivable concepts.

- **Backbone pre-training contamination (validity concern):**  
  - DINOv1 (self-supervised on ImageNet) and DINOv2 (trained on ImageNet, CUB, StanfordCars, FGVC) are used as backbones. In most evaluated datasets, the "novel" classes are already seen during backbone pretraining (CIFAR-100-80, ImageNet-100-50, CUB, StanfordCars, FGVC with DINOv2; similar with DINOv1). Therefore, it is unclear whether the learned concepts come from the proposed framework or from pre-training. The authors should apply the method on a ResNet trained from scratch to isolate the effect of pre-training.

- **Model size increase not controlled:**  
  - The generator and expansion layers add parameters; performance gains may be due to larger model capacity rather than the proposed method. Baseline models should be scaled similarly to ensure fair comparisons. The size of the linear layer in the derivable concept generator is not specified, making it impossible to assess this confound.

- **Missing ablation experiments:**  
  - No mean-variance analysis of main results to demonstrate stability.  
  - No study of performance sensitivity to batch size (claimed in Line 243 that "when batch size is sufficiently large…"); the tendency when batch size is small is needed.  
  - No ablation on the dimensions \(n\) and \(m\) that control the number of concepts.  
  - No analysis of why performance on Stanford Cars shows large gains yet is omitted from t-SNE/concept visualization.  
  - Table 12: when expansion layer dimension = \(10.0m\), performance remains comparable to \(5.0m\) except on CUB—this discrepancy is not analyzed.  
  - No explicit constraint on the remaining \(n-m\) dimensions in the covariance loss; it is unclear how the loss influences these dimensions and why they are unconstrained.

- **Implementation details missing:**  
  - The construction of negative samples (Line 285) is not explained.  
  - The claim that sharing encoder is "susceptible to label uncertainty of unlabeled data" (Line 41–31) is not clearly justified; clarification relative to prior works (e.g., crNCD) is needed.

- **Notation and presentation issues:**  
  - Variables \(l\) and \(l'\) (P4 L193–194) are explained only later, causing confusion.  
  - In Eq.2, \(z_i\) should be in bold \(\mathbf{z}_i\) to indicate a vector.  
  - English writing needs improvement (e.g., "novel classes can achieve impressive clustering results" should be rephrased).

- **Limited novelty:** One reviewer notes that the major contribution is the application of a covariance loss in GCD, which limits the novelty of the framework.

- **Practical limitation:** The method requires prior knowledge of the number of clusters (novel classes), a common limitation in GCD works. Application scenarios where this is known a priori are not discussed.
