## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper audits the hidden state threat model for DP-SGD, where an adversary sees only the final model. The authors propose adversaries that craft a gradient sequence offline (without access to intermediate updates) to maximize privacy loss of the final model. Two crafting methods are tested: a random-biased dimension and a simulated-biased dimension (the least updated dimension). Experiments across CNN, ResNet, and FCNN architectures – including both small and over-parameterized models – show that when the crafted gradient is inserted at every step, auditing lower bounds match upper bounds, implying no privacy amplification from hiding intermediate states. When the gradient is inserted less frequently (e.g., periodicity 5 or 25), a gap emerges, suggesting possible amplification by iteration. Further experiments show the auditing lower bound matches the upper bound only when batch size and epsilon are large, indicating that amplification by iteration may be absent for some non-convex problems.

### Strengths
- The problem setting and results are interesting and open new avenues for future work, especially identifying regimes where the gap between auditing lower bounds and theoretical upper bounds vanishes and where it remains.
- The paper flows nicely and is enjoyable to read; the writing is clear and easy to follow.
- The design of gradient-crafting adversaries is clever yet intuitive; simplicity is a virtue.
- The proposed method significantly improves upon previous auditing of the hidden state model, which had not optimally used the allowed threat model.
- A novel technique is introduced: inserting a crafted gradient only at the first step to study amplification by iteration. Empirical results suggest that for certain non-convex losses, only subsampling noise makes distinguishing the crafted gradient harder, not the iteration count.
- Experiments cover multiple deep learning architectures (CNN, ResNet, FCNN) and regimes (small and over-parameterized models); empirical results are convincing.

### Weaknesses
- The paper uncovers interesting empirical results but offers little explanation for the observed gaps. It falls short of its goal to “enhance our understanding of privacy leakage” (line 537) by reporting results without deeper interpretation. More discussion, like the high-level explanation starting at line 373, would help.
- The description of the adversaries and the privacy auditing scheme in the main paper is vague and not presented with full details.
- There might not exist a worst-case datapoint that can saturate the gradient clipping threshold at every iteration. The paper does not discuss what it means to breach privacy of a datapoint that does not exist or may be unrealizable. Similarly, the assumption that the adversary can craft the loss landscape to make the crafted gradient most distinguishable warrants further discussion (e.g., whether one can trace back the worst-case sample from the worst-case crafted gradient).
- An adversary who controls the sequence of gradients could potentially infer something about the intermediate models. Is this compatible with the hidden state threat model?
- Is there intuition behind why privacy amplification only seems to occur for smaller batch sizes?
- Implication 1 seems redundant with Implication 2 (if batch size is the entire dataset, every datapoint is included at every step). The added value is unclear.
- The main weakness is that the paper does not introduce a new privacy auditing method; it extends the existing gradient-crafting method to the hidden state regime. The primary technical contribution is constructing a sequence of gradients without intermediate access.
- Regarding the f-DP part (Remark 3): the claim that the CLT approximation error can be ignored may not be universal. The CLT can underestimate privacy when mixture distributions arise due to shuffling or sub-sampling [A]. Nasr et al. (2023) use numerical methods (FFT) to avoid this. A hidden state f-DP guarantee without CLT is provided in [B]. The authors should include an explanation in the main part.
- In Section 5.1, since the threat model does not benefit from subsampling amplification, there is no reason to use PRV accounting. Analytical Gaussian accounting (Balle et al., 2018) would give tight bounds instead of the upper and lower bounds from PRV.
- The methodology abstracts away the specifics of the loss function and model architecture, focusing directly on gradient crafting. This resembles a worst-case analysis over all non-convex problems but makes it impossible to deduce which specific architectures and loss functions may or may not exhibit privacy amplification by iteration.
- The conclusion is not new: the main takeaway (that the hidden state threat model does not lead to better privacy for some non-convex problems) matches previous work [Annamalai, 2024], though the methodology differs.
- The simulated-biased dimension method appears quite similar to gradient crafting at each step ([Nasr et al., 2023]). The paper should clarify the specific differences.
- There is confusion about the accounting in Figures 2 and 3: does the privacy analysis account only for the 250 iterations where the canary was inserted, or is it for the full T? Also, the caption of Figure 4 mentions “Figure 4a gives the …”, but this likely should be “Figure 4b”.
