## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Mixture-of-Graphs (MoG), a graph sparsification method that applies the Mixture-of-Experts concept to dynamically select node-specific sparsity levels and pruning criteria. MoG uses multiple sparsifier experts (each with a unique sparsity setting and criterion), a noisy top‑k gating mechanism to route each node to a subset of experts, and a Grassmann‑manifold optimization to ensemble the resulting sparse ego‑graphs. Experiments on four large‑scale OGB datasets and two superpixel datasets with five GNN backbones show that MoG achieves high sparsity (8.67%–50.85%) while maintaining or improving accuracy (up to 1.74% on DeeperGCN+ogbg‑ppa), yields 1.47–2.62× inference speedup with negligible performance drop, and can be integrated as a plugin with any GNN framework.

### Strengths
1. **Novel and well‑motivated approach.** Applying the MoE concept to graph sparsification is original (reviewers 1,2,4). The method is highly flexible, accounting for local node variations in determining optimal pruning strategies, unlike previous uniform global sparsification methods (R1,R3,R4).
2. **Clear framework and mathematical grounding.** The paper defines the MoG framework thoroughly, including sparsifier expert selection via noisy top‑k gating, the Grassmann manifold’s role in mixing subgraphs, and the closed‑form solution in Eq. 12 (R1). The figures are excellent (R4).
3. **Comprehensive experiments and practical benefits.** Extensive experiments across diverse datasets (OGB node and graph classification, superpixel) and five GNN backbones (R1,R2,R3). MoG demonstrates practical benefits: maintains or improves accuracy at high sparsity (e.g., 1.02%↑ on RevGNN+ogbn‑proteins, 1.74%↑ on DeeperGCN+ogbg‑ppa), improves inference speed (1.47–2.62×), and achieves faster yet more accurate speed‑accuracy tradeoffs than competing sparsification baselines (R1,R4).
4. **Generality and plug‑and‑play nature.** MoG can be integrated with any GNN framework (R2). Its ability to achieve significant sparsity without substantial performance drop highlights potential for resource‑limited environments and real‑time applications (R1).
5. **Ablation and sensitivity analyses.** The paper includes ample ablation studies, sensitivity analysis, and efficiency comparisons, strengthening the experimental validation (R3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Selection and distinctiveness of sparsifier experts.** The paper does not clarify why the specific 12 combinations of sparsity levels and criteria were chosen; the variance across rows in Table 8 is minimal, raising questions about expert distinctiveness. Different sparsity criteria are used per dataset without explaining the rationale (R1). The number of node‑classification and graph‑classification datasets is relatively small (only two each) (R2).
2. **Heuristic core objective and spectral‑property concerns.** Equation 11, the core objective for ensembling Laplacians on the Grassmann manifold, is quite heuristic. After the closed‑form solution (Eq. 12), a post‑sparsification step is applied. It is unclear whether the final sparse Laplacian truly integrates the eigenvectors of the multiple sparsified ego‑net Laplacians as claimed. Experimental evidence (e.g., eigenvalue distributions of individual sparsified Laplacians, the intermediate Grassmann result, and the final post‑sparsified Laplacian) is needed to validate spectral property preservation (R2).
3. **Lack of intuitive explanation for Grassmann manifold optimization.** The process of integrating sparse subgraphs on the Grassmann manifold (Section 3.4) is mathematically dense and lacks an intuitive explanation, making it hard for readers to grasp why the manifold’s properties benefit graph sparsification (R1). The connection between Eq. 11‑12 and the subsequent mixing is not immediately clear (R1,R2).
4. **Inconsistencies in terminology.** “Sparsifier experts” and “experts” are used interchangeably, which may cause confusion (R1). The introduction’s bullet points do not clearly correspond to the paper’s contributions, making the contributions difficult to parse (R4).
5. **Missing ablation study on simple pruning baselines.** A straightforward baseline—pruning the graph to a particular sparsity level using an edge‑sampling procedure (e.g., preferring to remove edges proportional to adjacent node degree while ensuring connectivity) and running a GNN—is absent. Such an ablation would help isolate the benefit of MoG’s dynamic selection beyond a simple global sparsity approach (R4).
6. **Incomplete justification of design choices.** Several methodological details are not fully justified: (a) How are post‑sparsified ego‑graphs assembled? If node i’s ego‑graph connects to j but node j’s does not connect to i, how is this asymmetry handled? (R3) (b) How is the parameter p in Eq. 10 selected, and what is its impact on performance? (R3) (c) What does D in Eq. 13 correspond to? If it is from the original ego‑graph, why not use the learned Laplacian’s degree, and doesn’t this introduce approximation error? (R3) (d) The use of Gaussian noise ϵ ∼ N(0, 1) in the gating mechanism is not discussed in terms of scale normalization—how is it ensured that the noise effectively alters routing given the activation scale? (R4)
7. **Lack of global graph structure consideration.** MoG is entirely local, operating on each node’s ego‑graph. For downstream tasks, global properties (e.g., graph connectivity) may be important. The paper does not address how MoG handles or could be extended to consider global structure (R3). Reviewer 3 highlights this as a potential limitation.
8. **Minor presentation issue.** Section 2 title contains a spelling error: “TECHNICAL BACKGOUND” should be “TECHNICAL BACKGROUND” (R1).
9. **Limited clarity on qualitative analysis.** In Figure 1 (Middle), edge pruning is attributed to different sparsifiers; it is unclear whether this is from an actual sparsified graph or an illustrative hypothesis (R3). Also, the difference in observed average sparsity just after ensembling (Eq. 13) versus after post‑sparsification (Eq. 14) is not quantified, leaving missing insight into how much the graph density changes after the Grassmann optimization (R3).

*Note on reviewer disagreement:* Reviewer 2 (rating 5, confidence 4) is notably more critical, particularly regarding the heuristic nature of the core objective (Eq. 11) and the limited spectral‑property validation (Weakness 2). The other reviewers rate 8 with varying confidence (3,4,2) and express concerns about rationale, missing ablations, and clarity, but overall view the contribution as positive.
