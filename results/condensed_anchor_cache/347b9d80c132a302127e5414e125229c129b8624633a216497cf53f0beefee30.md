## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes BiDST, a dynamic sparse training method that formulates weight and mask learning as a bi‑level optimization problem. The authors approximate the gradient by dropping second‑order terms and use Gumbel‑Softmax to handle discrete masks, reporting state‑of‑the‑art accuracy on CIFAR‑10/100 and ImageNet (ResNets) with up to 2.62% higher accuracy and 2.1× speedup. Reviewers agree that the motivation and experimental setup are strong but raise significant concerns about the method’s true sparsity, the crude approximation of the bi‑level optimization, missing theoretical guarantees, unclear overhead, and insufficient explanation of speedups. One reviewer (R3) is markedly more critical, questioning the convergence guarantee and the naturalness of the upper‑level objective.

### Strengths
- Clearly explains the connection between dynamic sparse training and bi‑level optimization (R1, R3).
- Proposes a data‑driven mask update that replaces heuristics such as random (SET) or gradient‑norm (RigL) rules (R2).
- Achieves state‑of‑the‑art results on CIFAR‑10/100 and ImageNet with ResNets across multiple sparsity levels and training epochs (R1, R2, R3).
- Introduces a technique to restrict the mask search space to maintain training efficiency (R1).
- Excellent experimental methodology, including comparisons to SET, RigL, and other DST baselines and multiple training‑epoch settings (R2).
- The bi‑level framework is intuitively suited to the DST problem (R2).
- Discarding the Hessian term does not hurt empirical performance; the condition introduced to update the discrete mask works well (R3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Unclear whether BiDST is sparse or dense training**: If Gumbel‑Softmax is used, training likely becomes dense, yet speedups are claimed from “sparse training.” The mask update rule (Eq. 11) appears dense and is run every iteration; the paper does not adequately explain how forward/backward passes can remain sparse, how masked‑out weights are considered, or how the method is comparable to truly sparse DST methods (R2).

2. **Crude approximation of bi‑level optimization**: The replacement of second‑order partial derivatives with zero (below Eq. 8) discards the information that carries the relationship between mask and weight parameters. This is a strong simplification justified only by computational expense. The paper over‑claims by presenting BiDST as properly solving a bi‑level problem without discussing the impact of this approximation (R2, R3). Reviewer 3 notes that the rough gradient estimation and discrete mask leave the algorithm without a convergence guarantee (see point 3).

3. **Lack of theoretical convergence guarantee**: Given the gradient approximation and the hard discretization of the soft mask, the algorithm has no convergence guarantee. Theoretical analysis is needed (R3).

4. **Upper‑level objective not well motivated**: The upper‑level objective is a finite‑sampling objective; with the natural L₂ regularization (||θ*(ψ)||) it becomes the same as the lower‑level problem, making the formulation arguably single‑level. Bi‑level formulations are typically used when the two levels employ different datasets; this is not the case here (R3).

5. **Mask change frequency and analysis**: Figure 4 shows rapid mask changes (low IoU) but does not reflect that BiDST makes fewer mask updates (as reported in the tables). The paper does not analyze whether quickly changing masks is beneficial or detrimental to final accuracy, and the discrepancy between the frequency visualized and the tabulated counts hinders understanding (R1).

6. **Missing L₂ regularization experiments**: The effect of L₂ regularization on other DST methods should be tested. In the appendix, BiDST with the smallest L₂ penalty appears to underperform relative to the baselines (R1).

7. **Unquantified overhead and unfair epoch comparison**: The paper uses fewer training epochs for BiDST to compensate for “mask learning computation cost,” but does not specify what a fair number of epochs is, nor does it provide actual compute/time measurements. The overhead of the Gumbel‑Softmax operation is mentioned but not quantified (R2).

8. **ImageNet variance omitted**: Although the authors stress that ImageNet experiments were run three times, variance is not shown. The claim of “std dev less than 0.1%” (percentage points or percent?) is insufficient; results that are within 0.2 pp of baselines need variance reported to assess significance (R2).

9. **Unconvincing mask analysis (IoU)**: The low IoU is claimed to indicate “better mask development,” but this claim is not justified. Established metrics such as ITOP would be more appropriate; the explanation provided is insufficient (R2).

10. **Unrealistic speedup explanation**: The claim that training acceleration comes from “compiler optimization that skips the zero weights” suggests static analysis (fixed mask), which is incompatible with a dynamic sparse training method. The description of integrating CSR format into static code does not clarify how unstructured sparsity is sped up on real hardware with dynamically changing masks. Such results seem too good to be true, and no code is provided to verify them (R2).

11. **Background section short and awkwardly placed**: The background is too brief and is located after parts of the method; a consolidated background before the method would be preferred (R2).
