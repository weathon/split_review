## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents MASS, a method for achieving perfect shift equivariance in convolutional neural networks by selecting the downsampling origin based on coordinate‑independent statistics of the feature map. The approach is model‑agnostic, works for both downsampling and upsampling, and can be applied to pre‑trained networks without retraining. Experiments are conducted on CIFAR‑10, ImageNet‑1K (classification), and PASCAL VOC (semantic segmentation). While some reviewers find the motivation compelling and the solution simple, the majority view the contribution as incremental and the presentation as unclear.

### Strengths
- The problem of achieving exact shift equivariance in CNNs is important and well‑motivated (e.g., industrial applications) (R1, R2, R4, R5).
- The proposed method is simple, generic, and can be applied to any convolutional architecture without introducing additional learnable parameters (R1, R2, R3, R5).
- MASS achieves perfect (100%) shift equivariance in all experiments, including both downsampling (classification) and upsampling (segmentation) paths (R5).
- The method can be used with pre‑trained weights; no retraining is required if some accuracy loss is acceptable, unlike some prior approaches (R2, R3, R5).
- The solution maintains consistency by design and is compatible with multiple architectures (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Incremental over existing work**: MASS is seen as a simple modification of APS (Adaptive Polyphase Sampling, CVPR 2021) – using a pre‑defined selection function for origin determination – and fails to provide new technical insights (R1, R3, R4). A reviewer also notes resemblance to Equivariance With Learned Canonicalization Functions (R3).
- **Poor presentation and writing**:
  - Notations and terms in formulas are unexplained; mathematical formulations lack detail (R1, R4).
  - Figures 1, 2, and 4 are unclear: color schemes and intended messages are not explained (R1, R2). Two sub‑figures for MASS in channel 1 of Figure 1 are inconsistent with the others (R1, R4).
  - Multiple typos, grammar errors, and inaccurate descriptions are present (e.g., “Cheng et al.,2016; ?”, “a lack of shift equivariance occur”, “substantailly improves”, “originate”, “concepts are”, “are proposed”, “generalize to select”) (R1, R4). Some acronyms (APS, LPF, DDAC) are not defined upon first use (R2, R5). Additional minor typos: “equivaiant”, “architectures.architectures”, “experimants”, “inializing” (R5).
- **Limited and unconvincing experiments**:
  - Comparison is only to APS; baselines like LPS, DDAC, and LPF are not consistently cited or explained (R1, R2, R4). Tables 2 and 3 lack the same baselines (LPS is missing from Tables 2/3, and Table 3 omits several effective baselines from Table 2) (R2).
  - Gains on CIFAR‑10 are very marginal and statistically insignificant (confidence intervals overlap); using bold to indicate the best model is misleading (R2). On PASCAL VOC, MASS performs worse than APS (R1, R4). On ImageNet‑1K, MASS is much better than APS, but no root‑cause analysis is provided (R1, R4).
  - No ablation study is conducted to understand how MASS works (R1, R4). Details of the pre‑defined selection function’s implementation are missing, making experimental comparison confusing (R1, R4).
  - The consistency metric (Equation 4) is unclear: it is not explained what x and y are, whether it measures pixel‑wise match, or why there is no summation over pixels; a closing bracket is missing (R2).
  - Table 5 confirms the lack of clear value over other approaches such as LPS‑DDAC‑3 or APS‑DDAC‑3 (R2).
- **Unaddressed limitations and missing analyses**:
  - No discussion of limitations (R1, R4).
  - Only circular shifts are considered; performance under other shift operations (e.g., reflection/wrapping) is not evaluated (R4).
  - Equivariance is demonstrated only at inference time; it is unclear how the method facilitates equivariance during training and which augmentations become unnecessary (R5). Training from scratch is only tested on CIFAR‑10 without explanation of the augmentation procedure (R5).
  - The claimed severe accuracy drops of non‑equivariant methods are not reflected in the results; consistency of other methods is often above 80% and classification rates remain high. No analysis of how the amount of shift affects accuracy is provided (R5).
  - The method’s computational/memory overhead (additional operations or variables compared to regular pooling) is not discussed (R5).
- **Questions with no provided answers**:
  - How does the choice of the origin selection function matter? Can it be determined adaptively? (R1)
  - Is it always possible to find a good function regardless of noise level? (R1)
  - Is the approach compatible with vision transformers? (R2)
  - In MASS‑Max‑pool, does shifting the input to match the new origin introduce unwanted data augmentation, especially with large pooling windows? (R3)
  - What is the point of using a pre‑trained version of any of the discussed approaches? (R2)
- **Disagreement among reviewers**: While Reviewer 1 (rating 8) and Reviewer 5 (rating 6) view the results as strong and the solution effective, the other three reviewers (ratings 3, 3, 3) find the contributions marginal and the experiments unconvincing. The positive opinions note “simple and robust” (R1) and “strong results with 100% equivariance” (R5), but the majority emphasize that improvements are marginal, baselines are better or equal, and the method lacks novelty.
