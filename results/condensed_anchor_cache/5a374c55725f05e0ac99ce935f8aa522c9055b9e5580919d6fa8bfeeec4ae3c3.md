## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces the novel problem of online GNN evaluation under test-time graph distribution shifts, aiming to estimate generalization errors of well-trained GNNs on unlabeled test graphs. It proposes the Learning Behavior Discrepancy (LEBED) score, which re-trains the GNN on test data using a parameter-free optimality criterion that incorporates both node prediction and structure reconstruction discrepancies. The discrepancy between the optimal parameters of the original training and retraining phases is used as the metric. Extensive experiments on real-world graphs with node feature, domain, and temporal shifts, across multiple GNN architectures, demonstrate that LEBED strongly correlates with ground-truth test errors (using Spearman rank correlation ρ and linear fitting R²). Ablation studies, running time analysis, and hyperparameter sensitivity are also provided.

### Strengths
1. **Novel problem**: Online GNN evaluation under test-time distribution shifts is a fresh and practical research problem with clear definitions and settings. It addresses a crucial gap in real-world GNN deployment and can inspire future work, including pre-training of large-scale GNNs.
2. **Methodology**: The LEBED metric is logically sound and clearly presented. The core idea of re-training the GNN on test graphs and measuring learning behavior discrepancy (parameter space distance) to infer test error is intuitive. The parameter-free optimality criterion (Eq. 6) for stopping retraining, leveraging graph structure, is a novel contribution.
3. **Experimental validation**: Experiments cover diverse distribution shifts (node feature, domain, temporal), multiple datasets (e.g., ACM vs. DBLP vs. Citation, with splits like 1/1/369 for domains A/D/C), and various GNN models (GCN, GAT, SGC, etc.). Results show high Spearman ρ and R² values, confirming LEBED’s effectiveness. The paper also includes ablation, runtime, hyperparameter sensitivity, and correlation visualization. Additionally, the findings reveal that existing CNN evaluation metrics perform poorly on GNNs, underscoring the need for the proposed approach.
4. **Presentation**: The paper is well-structured, with clear figures (e.g., Fig. 2) and easy-to-follow logic.

### Weaknesses
1. **Retraining supervision choice**: The method uses only the prediction-based loss (DPred) for retraining, but not the structure-based loss (DStru). The justification for not incorporating both is insufficient. Could using both losses lead to a more robust retraining process? (R1 W1)
2. **Use of pseudo labels under distribution shift**: In Eq. (4), pseudo labels are generated by the pre-trained GNN on test-time data which has distribution shifts. Why are these labels valid supervision for re-training? More explanation is needed. (R4 W3)
3. **Consistency of retraining initialization**: The method requires the retraining to start from the same initial parameters as the original training. The paper does not specify how this is ensured. Different initializations could yield different LEBED scores, affecting reliability. (R1 W2)
4. **Effect of training randomness**: Even if the same training data is used in Stage 2 (as mentioned), randomness in the training process may cause differences in optimal weights, potentially inflating the LEBED score. How is this accounted for? (R4 W2)
5. **Domain shift experiment splits unclear**: In Table 1, the domain shift setup uses splits like 1/1/369 for ACM/DBLP/Citation. It is unclear how training, validation, and test sets are constructed across domains. More detailed explanation of the data splits and which graphs are used for each stage is needed. (R1 W3)
6. **Ground-truth test error computation**: How is the ground-truth performance (test error) of the pre-trained GNNs on distribution-shifted test graphs obtained? This is not fully explained. (R2 W3)
7. **Parameter-free criterion details**: The parameter-free optimality criterion in Eq. (6) is used to stop retraining but is not optimized during retraining. More explanation of its role and mechanism would be helpful. (R2 W2)
8. **Simulation of distribution shifts and data availability**: How are the graph distribution shifts (node feature, domain, temporal) simulated exactly? Also, can the test graph datasets be publicly released? (R2 W1)
9. **Baseline sign variability**: In experimental results, some baseline methods have positive correlation in some cases and negative in others. More explanation of why this occurs is needed. (R3 Q1)
10. **Integration and computational cost**: How easily can LEBED be integrated into existing GNN pipelines? Also, does re-training on test graphs introduce significant computational overhead? The time complexity should be discussed. (R3 Q2, Q3)
11. **Pre-trained GNN source**: Table 1 lists test-time graph data but does not specify where the pre-trained GNNs come from—what datasets were used for training them? (R4 W1)
