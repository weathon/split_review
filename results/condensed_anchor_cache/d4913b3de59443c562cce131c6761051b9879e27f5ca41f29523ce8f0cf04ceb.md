## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper theoretically investigates adversarial robustness of neural classifiers by studying the smallest perturbation magnitude needed to change the output. It claims that robustness degrades with input dimension \(d\) and can be as small as \(1/\sqrt{d}\) of the best possible robustness. Reviewers appreciate the clear presentation and detailed derivations, but raise major concerns about unrealistic assumptions, overgeneralized claims, lack of related work, and insufficient empirical validation. One reviewer (R3) is notably more negative, stating the core conclusion is already known and the analysis is weak; the other three (R1, R2, R4) are more positive but still critical of the paper’s scope and rigor.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written and communicates its goal clearly, with accessible intuitions behind proofs (R1, R2).
- Experiments are described thoroughly and organized with all necessary details (R1).
- The theoretical derivation is detailed and clear (R2, R4).
- Exploring the smallest magnitude of perturbation that changes model output is intriguing (R4).
- The comparison between the adversarial robustness of minimum-distance classifiers and DNNs is noteworthy (R4).
- The topic of adversarial robustness remains important (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of related work.** The paper misses key existing theoretical studies (e.g., Bartlett et al., “Adversarial Examples in Multi-Layer Random ReLU Networks”) and does not include a related work section or compare its analysis with prior results (R1, R3). One reviewer (R3) notes the claim that robustness can be only \(1/\sqrt{d}\) is already known from [1] Yang et al. (ICML 2020) and [2] Kumar et al. (ICML 2020) in more general settings.
- **Overstated generality.** The paper’s broad claims about dimensionality phenomena are primarily observed in random networks, and this is only briefly mentioned in the introduction (R1). The abstract and introduction overclaim: “neural network’s adversarial robustness can degrade … only \(1/\sqrt{d}\) of the best possible adversarial robustness” without clarifying that this result is derived under strong assumptions (e.g., linear networks, specific data distributions) and may not apply to arbitrary DNNs (R4). The conclusion that robustness degrades with \(d\) is not rigorous because (a) if new dimensions are correlated, they add no information—would robustness still degrade? (b) comparing norms across different dimensions is problematic (e.g., volume > area > length) (R2).
- **Unrealistic assumptions limit generalizability.** Most theorems rely on strong assumptions: normal distribution of data, identity activation, orthogonal weight matrices \(H\), distinct labels per sample (Eq. 1, Eq. 5)—e.g., Theorem 1 and Theorem 4 require each dimension of training samples to be standard Gaussian, activation = identity, \(H\) orthogonal, and each sample \(i\) has a distinct label with output 1 for its class and 0 for others (R3, R4). Theorems 1, 4, 6, and 7 do not clearly extend to practical multi-layer non-linear networks; Theorem 7 itself relies heavily on linearity despite claiming to apply to non-linear networks (R1, R4). The theoretical analysis is weak because all theorems make unrealistic assumptions, and the distance to the decision boundary for a linear network can be computed in closed form, making the two-page probabilistic bound of Theorem 1 unnecessarily complex (R3).
- **Only \(\ell_2\) distance considered.** The paper does not address other \(\ell_p\) norms, which have been analyzed in prior work (R3).
- **Experiments are insufficient.** Only toy experiments on 12-dimensional synthetic data are presented; real-world large-scale experiments (e.g., ImageNet) are expected for a mature topic (R3). The paper should validate Theorem 7 on typical DNNs like LeNet or ResNet-18 (R4). The experiments do not clearly show what they demonstrate (R1 Q3).
- **Missing details in writing.** The paper should highlight the physical significance, limitations, and insights of each theorem. Detailed proofs (e.g., Lines 151–244) should be moved to an appendix (R4). A table listing the assumptions of each theorem, indicating which are ideal and which generalize, would help (R4). The phrase “with high probability” (Theorems 1, 4) is not rigorously defined (R4 Q2). The context of Theorem 7’s claim (“classifier wrongly thinks the input is \(x+\epsilon x_2\) instead of \(x+\epsilon x_1\)”) is unclear (R4).
- **Critical questions that reveal missing experiments/ablations:**
  - R1 Q1: Is the perturbation chosen merely a one-step gradient attack with a simple targeted loss?
  - R1 Q2: Are \(x+\epsilon x_1\) and \(x+\epsilon x_2\) necessarily classified differently? It seems plausible this only holds for very large \(\epsilon\). Has this been tested?
  - R4 Q1: Under what conditions can weight vectors \(w_i\) satisfy the assumptions of Eq. (1) and Eq. (5)? What is the practical feasibility of these conditions for real-world DNNs?
- **Minority reviewer contrast:** While three reviewers (R1, R2, R4) rate the paper 5 and commend the theoretical derivation, one reviewer (R3, score 3) strongly criticizes the analysis as weak, the conclusions as already known, and the experiments as inadequate. Their recommendation is to add a related work section, compare with existing results, and conduct real-world experiments.
