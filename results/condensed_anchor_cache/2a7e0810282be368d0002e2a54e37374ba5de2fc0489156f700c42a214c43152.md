## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Gen-LRA, a membership inference attack (MIA) for generative models that computes a likelihood ratio using two kernel density estimators (KDEs) over the released synthetic dataset S and a reference dataset R. The ratio tests whether a target point x* was in the training set by comparing the density of synthetic samples under a model trained on R vs. R ∪ {x*}. The method is computationally efficient, requires no knowledge of the model or training algorithm, and is evaluated on 15 tabular datasets across multiple generative architectures. Results show that Gen-LRA outperforms existing MIAs, especially at low false positive rates (e.g., TPR@0.001), and is particularly effective on outlier records. Reviewers strongly disagreed on the paper’s quality, with one rating it 8 and others rating it 3, 5, 3, 5.

### Strengths
- The likelihood ratio formulation is novel, theoretically grounded, and close to the theoretically optimal MIA (Reviewers 1, 5).
- The method is simple, computationally efficient, and requires only the synthetic dataset and a reference dataset, with no access to the model or training algorithm (Reviewers 1, 4).
- The paper is mostly well-written, well-motivated, and clear; intuitive examples (Fig 1, Sec 6.2) aid understanding (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- Comprehensive evaluation across 15 datasets and multiple generative models (Reviewers 3, 4).
- Strong performance on TPR at low FPR – a metric often neglected in the MIA literature – highlights the attack’s practical impact (Reviewers 1, 5).
- The method identifies a different source of privacy leakage (local overfitting on outliers) compared to distance-based MIAs (Reviewers 4, 5).
- The attack is novel and the design is original to the reviewers’ knowledge (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses
- **Dependence on accurate density estimation and limited scalability:** The approach relies on KDE after PCA, which limits applicability to low-dimensional tabular data and scales poorly to high-dimensional data (images, text). PCA may also miss rare categorical values (e.g., “French” on Adult dataset), reducing sensitivity for outlier overfitting on such attributes. Inaccuracies in density estimation can undermine robustness (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 5).
- **Unrealistic attacker assumption:** The assumption that the attacker has access to a reference dataset from the same distribution is considered too strong. In many synthetic-data release scenarios (e.g., for open research), such data would not be publicly available. The paper’s justification (Section 2) is insufficiently explained (Reviewer 3).
- **Scope mismatch:** The title and abstract claim applicability to generative models broadly, but the method is only evaluated on tabular data due to KDE limitations. This may mislead readers about the attack’s generalizability (Reviewer 3).
- **Overfitting-centric leakage assumption:** Gen-LRA presumes privacy leakage stems solely from local overfitting, potentially missing other leakage types that do not manifest as overfitting (Reviewer 2).
- **High false positive risk in variable datasets:** The reliance on outlier detection may cause elevated false positives on datasets with high inherent variability or complex distributions, limiting practical applicability in privacy-sensitive settings (Reviewer 2).
- **Unfair hyperparameter selection for comparison:** Gen-LRA selects the best k for each run to maximize empirical privacy risk, while baseline methods may not receive the same treatment. This makes comparisons potentially unfair (Reviewer 3). The choice of kernel size/type and its sensitivity to performance is not sufficiently discussed (Reviewer 3).
- **Incomplete comparison with shadow-modeling MIAs:** The paper omits comparison to shadow-modeling based MIAs (Stadler et al. 2022, Houssiau et al. 2022, Meeus et al. 2023) that have been studied for synthetic tabular data. Implementing and comparing against these would clarify whether Gen-LRA’s improvement stems from relaxed assumptions or fundamental algorithmic advances (Reviewer 4).
- **Unfair baseline implementation:** Using PCA+KDE for the DOMIAS baseline is not consistent with the original DOMIAS paper, which used a more sophisticated density estimator; this may disadvantage DOMIAS. The same estimator could also improve Gen-LRA, which should be checked empirically (Reviewer 5).
- **Anecdotal outlier analysis:** Section 5.3’s claim that Gen-LRA is more effective on outliers is supported only by an anecdotal example (e.g., UMAP plot). More rigorous results (e.g., MIA performance on outliers vs. random points across datasets) and relation to prior outlier findings in shadow-modeling MIAs are needed. Also, high-DCR points should be checked for similarity to avoid misinterpretation of Fig 2. Ward et al. (2024) reported a very similar finding to Fig 2; this should be discussed and analogous figures for other baselines should be provided (Reviewers 4, 5).
- **Lack of per-dataset results:** Only mean results across datasets are reported, which may hide significant performance differences; standard deviations across datasets may not be meaningful (Reviewer 5).
- **Clarity and presentation issues:** Notation (e.g., \(p_{\mathbb{P} \cup x^*}\), M, D) is imprecisely defined, and the motivation leading to Equation 4 is hand-wavy (Reviewers 1, 3, 5). Differences between DOMIAS and Gen-LRA should be made clearer (Reviewer 5). Section 5.3 is unclear in what it demonstrates (Reviewer 1). Contribution 1 sentence seems to conflate null and alternative hypotheses (Reviewer 5). The phrase “different source” of privacy leakage (Section 1) should be clarified earlier (Reviewer 3). The mention of DCR in Section 5 should be described in the main text (Reviewer 3). Missing parentheses (Line 266) and typos (Line 127) are present. The notation “scale” (Line 346) should be “normalise” (Reviewer 5). Some references are missing publication venues (e.g., Durkan et al. 2019, Ganev and De Cristofaro 2023) (Reviewer 5). The paper should clarify the practical utility of applying MIA to synthetic data and provide justification (Reviewer 2). References contain typographical errors (e.g., Line 527, 729) (Reviewer 2).
