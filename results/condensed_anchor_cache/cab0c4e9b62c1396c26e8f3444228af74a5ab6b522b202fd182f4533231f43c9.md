## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes ST-MEM (Spatio-Temporal Masked Electrocardiogram Modeling), a self-supervised learning method for 12-lead ECG that uses masked auto-encoding with spatio-temporal patching and a lead-wise shared decoder to reconstruct masked signals. Pre-trained on three large datasets (Chapman, Ningbo, CODE-15; total 188,480 ECGs) and evaluated on PTB-XL, CPSC2018, and PhysioNet2017 for arrhythmia and myocardial infarction classification, ST-MEM outperforms contrastive and generative SSL baselines (including SimCLR, BYOL, MLAE, MTAE) under both linear probing and full fine-tuning. Gains are most notable in low-data regimes (1%, 5% data) and reduced-lead settings. Reviewers generally find the paper well-written, thorough, and empirically strong, though several raise concerns about methodological intuition, clinical significance of small gains, missing ablations, and narrow scope for a general conference.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important problem: high-quality labeled ECG data are scarce while unlabeled data are abundant (R1, R4).
- The proposed ST-MEM is simple yet effective, achieving superior performance across multiple datasets and settings (R1, R2, R3).
- Thorough experimentation with relevant baselines (MLAE, MTAE, SimCLR, BYOL, etc.) and comprehensive evaluation (full fine-tuning, linear probing, low-resource 1%/5%, reduced lead sets) (R2, R3).
- Clear, well-organized presentation and easy to follow (R2, R3).
- Particularly effective in low-resource settings, where the performance gain over alternatives is more significant (R1, R2).
- Provides two important insights: a practical solution for domains with high labeling cost, and potential applicability beyond ECG to data domains where signals are indirect observations from multiple spatial points (R4).

### Weaknesses
- The intuition behind spatio-temporal patching is not clearly explained, and the distinction from related works that reconstruct only spatial (MLAE) or only temporal (MTAE) patches needs better clarification (R2).
- The method assumes regular/periodic rhythm within the 10s input window; its applicability to irregular rhythms (e.g., atrial fibrillation) or rhythms where abnormalities are transient (e.g., PVCs) is not discussed. This should be clarified (R2).
- Performance gains over supervised baselines at 100% data are small (~0.01–0.02 AUROC) and may lack clinical significance. Harder base tasks would strengthen the contribution (R2).
- Missing comparisons to prior work: Sawano et al. (2022) already applied MAE to ECG; the differences should be highlighted. Temesgen et al. (2022) report higher AUROC on PTB-XL and should be compared (R3).
- Classification tasks are insufficiently detailed: What are the diagnostic criteria for myocardial infarction? What types of arrhythmia are classified? PTB-XL has four diagnostic conditions but only a subset appears to have been used; results on all conditions would better support the claim of “general ECG representation” (R1, R2, R4).
- The architectural choice of both a SEP token and lead-specific embeddings may be redundant. An ablation removing the SEP token (or using leads embeddings without SEP) is needed (R1).
- Preprocessing details are missing: how were different sampling rates (500 Hz, 400 Hz) handled during pre-training? How was the 200 Hz PhysioNet2017 data adapted? For signals longer than 10 s, how were predictions aggregated (voting, averaging)? (R1)
- Reduced-lead experiments only tested Lead I; clinical practice often prefers Lead II. Evaluation on Lead II and possibly other leads is requested (R3, R4). Also, the reason for a larger 6-lead vs. 1-lead gap on PTB-XL compared to CPSC2018 should be explained (R3).
- The problem setting is specialized to ECG, which may be too narrow for a general machine learning conference like ICLR. A discussion of possible extensions outside ECG/medicine would be beneficial (R4).
- Minor clarity issues: Figure 2(c) is unclear, the term “seasonality” is not defined, “flattening” of 1D patches seems unnecessary, and Figure 2 uses the same color for two different segmentations (should use e.g., grayscale) (R1, R4).
- The embedding visualization in Fig. 4 could be more informative if it showed clustering across different rhythm types in addition to spatial lead locations (R2).
- The contrastive SSL baselines used eight augmentations; an ablation considering only a subset (e.g., skipping Flip, Shift, Sine) would help assess which augmentations are actually beneficial (R1).
