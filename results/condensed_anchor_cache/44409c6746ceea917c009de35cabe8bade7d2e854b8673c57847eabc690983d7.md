## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces AutoCode4Math, a method to train LLMs to autonomously decide between chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning and code execution for solving math problems, rather than relying on externally dictated instructions. The training uses an Expectation-Maximization (EM) framework: an E-step estimates state-action values via random rollouts, and an M-step trains on reward-weighted actions. The method only requires input-output pairs, not human labels for intermediate steps. Experiments on 7B models over two iterations show improvements on GSM8k and MATH (accuracy 65.28%, ~20% gain; 65% fewer code executions) and stable gains over baseline RL. Reviewers note the topic is timely (related to o1-style inference-time search) and the gap addressed is important, but raise concerns about unclear motivation for EM over simpler alternatives, insufficient baseline comparisons, hard-to-parse results tables, and presentation issues.

### Strengths

- The observation that autonomous code integration cannot be taught via simple supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is correct (all reviewers concur; Reviewer 1 emphasizes this).
- The method considers multi-turn interactions with interleaved CoT and Python code execution, which is not common in prior work (Reviewer 1).
- Results on GSM8k and MATH are interesting: over just two training iterations, AutoCode improves performance in a stable way over baseline RL (Reviewer 2, Figure 4).
- The training only relies on input and output, not using human-generated labels for intermediate steps, which is useful for data-bottlenecked researchers (Reviewer 2).
- Framing the RL actions as CoT vs. code generation addresses the gap between self-learning the optimal policy (AutoCode) versus behavioral cloning (SFT) as in prior models (Reviewer 2).
- The performance is commendable and surpasses SFT on the same set of math queries, indicating effectiveness and potential for broader application (Reviewer 3).
- The paper contains many in-depth analyses of the proposed method and different experiment settings (Reviewer 3).
- The study identifies an important gap: models’ inability to self-direct reasoning strategies. Experiments validate that autonomous decision-making in training leads to measurable performance improvements (Reviewer 4).

**Minority positive view (Reviewer 1, score 8):** Overall more positive about the contribution, noting the problem is correctly identified and the multi-turn interaction is a novelty. Other reviewers (scores 5–6) are more cautious, particularly about performance comparisons and presentation.

### Weaknesses

1. **Unclear motivation for EM over simpler alternatives**  
   - It is not clearly explained why EM is necessary or preferable to simpler self-exploration techniques such as Expert Iteration, RLHF, or standard reinforcement learning (Reviewer 1). The paper suggests EM narrows the policy search space, but provides no experimental evidence for this claim (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 4).  
   - The paper lacks a rigorous comparative analysis showing EM’s advantages over policy gradient methods or other optimization techniques for methodology selection (Reviewer 4). No quantification of reduction in search space or analysis of how this translates to faster convergence or better performance is given (Reviewer 4).  
   - Reviewer 4 asks: Why is EM chosen over alternative methods like standard RL or SFT? What specific advantages does EM bring? How does EM uniquely contribute to narrowing the policy search space? What is the theoretical or experimental basis for assuming EM reduces computational load?  
   - Reviewer 1 asks: Could the authors more clearly outline why EM is preferable to Expert Iteration and RLHF, and which advantage each component provides? What benefit do the other components of EM bring beyond the data synthesis framework described in §2.3?

2. **Insufficient comparison with baselines**  
   - The method does not outperform Qwen2-Math 7B-instruct (which uses simpler training) on any math dataset in Figure 3; the authors could try to see if their method improves Qwen2-Math further (Reviewer 2).  
   - The method does not give significant performance gains outside of MATH and GSM8k over open-source baselines (Reviewer 2).  
   - The paper lacks detailed, controlled comparisons with baselines like Qwen2Math, GPT-4o, DeepseekMath, GPT-4, and Mammoth for method selection effectiveness. No precision, recall, or F1-score for method selection are provided (Reviewer 4).  
   - Reviewer 4 asks: How does AutoCode4Math perform compared to current baselines in terms of method selection effectiveness? Could you provide a controlled comparison showing how frequently and accurately AutoCode4Math selects CoT versus code? What improvements in accuracy or code-execution reduction are achieved over GPT-4 and Mammoth? Are there quantitative metrics directly correlating method selection accuracy with overall performance?  
   - The paper mentions an offline RL baseline but does not detail the data, algorithm specifics, reward function, exploration strategy, or policy update mechanism. Comparisons with rejection-sampling and preference-learning-based approaches are missing (Reviewer 4).  
   - Reviewer 1 asks: What exactly is the “standard RL” baseline mentioned in §3.2?  
   - The initial accuracy for Qwen-2-Math on GSM8k and MATH differs substantially between Figure 3 and Figure 4; this inconsistency is unexplained (Reviewer 2).

3. **Presentation, readability, and typos**  
   - §2 (Method) is extremely long, technical, and difficult to follow for readers not familiar with EM. The outline of the method should be organized into clearer components: how data is generated (expectation) and how the model is trained on that data (maximization), better aligned with Figure 2 (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).  
   - The relationship between §2.2 and §2.3 with §2.1 needs to be more explicitly defined (Reviewer 3). Introducing an overarching pipeline at the beginning of §2 would help (Reviewer 3).  
   - The main result table (Figure 3) is hard to parse: some lines are color-coded blue/red but the caption does not explain the meaning; it is not clearly stated which lines correspond to the proposed approach and which to baselines. The table looks like a spreadsheet screenshot (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2). Reviewer 1 suggests a smaller table focusing on comparing with SOTA.  
   - Figure 4 needs to be larger (Reviewer 2).  
   - Several typos: “sythesis” in Figure 1, “referece” in Appendix B, and others in the abstract (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).

4. **Other concerns and questions**  
   - Reviewer 4 notes that it is unclear whether observed improvements are solely due to better method selection or other factors (e.g., model capabilities or training data). A breakdown of performance gains attributed to method selection would be valuable.  
   - Reviewer 2 asks: If AutoCode training is extended to more iterations (Figure 4), does performance on GSM8k and MATH continue to improve? The accuracy appears still increasing.  
   - The anonymized repository is not accessible; the authors should verify it works (Reviewer 2).
