## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies the dynamics and mechanics of memorization in causal language models by fine-tuning pre-trained LLMs (Pythia, GPT-2, 140M–12B) on random token strings of varying length, alphabet size, and entropy. It finds that memorization occurs in two phases: first the model learns the marginal token distribution (guessing phase), then memorizes the exact sequence (memorization phase). Memorization happens at the granularity of individual tokens and not in positional order; short local prefixes are sufficient to recall a token, while the global context must match the learned bag-of-tokens distribution. The authors propose that these findings have implications for how memorization is measured.

### Strengths
- Systematic experimental design covering model sizes (140M–12B) and families (Pythia, GPT), as well as string length, alphabet size, and vocabulary entropy.
- Interesting observations: two-phase memorization (learning alphabet distribution before exact sequence), token-level memorization without positional order, sufficiency of short local prefixes, and the role of global token distribution.
- Methodological framework (analysis of local/global context, phase transitions, token-level memorization order) is easy to understand and can help spark new research in this area.
- The ablations on the influence of global context are well-crafted.
- Overall clear and well-organized writing (minor exceptions noted in weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited generalizability to natural language:** The study is restricted to random strings, an extreme edge case. Multiple reviewers question whether the findings will transfer to structured natural language, where grammar, predictable n-grams, and correlated content are present. It is unclear if the observed phenomena (e.g., two-phase memorization, positional independence, local prefix sufficiency) are artifacts of randomness. Reviewer 1 suggests using a probabilistic grammar for controlled structured strings; Reviewer 3 asks for experiments on non-random strings or a mixture; Reviewer 5 notes the dataset lacks the scale and correlation of internet-scale pre-training corpora.
- **Unsurprising or already known results:** Several findings are considered unsurprising by reviewers. Reviewer 3: "the fact models first go through a guessing phase and then start memorising the actual data is unsurprising"; Reviewer 1: token-level memorization "is this surprising, given that training is via teacher-forcing on a token-level objective?"; the sufficiency of local prefixes may follow from the randomness of the strings (Reviewer 1: "if the string looks random, then a local prefix is sufficient, as the n-gram will have very low probability"; Reviewer 4: information-theoretic bound, "any fixed substring of length larger than 2 log_l n is sufficient to uniquely identify each string"). Reviewer 1 notes similar findings by Tirumala et al. (2022) on differentiated memorization speed according to part-of-speech are not discussed.
- **Lack of practical impact:** The results are not connected to training strategies, privacy attacks, or measurement of memorization in realistic settings. Reviewer 2: "less practical impact." Reviewer 3: "results may be of little or no practical usefulness." Reviewer 4: unclear how the results inform measurements of memorization; the claim that existing measures make unsupported assumptions is not supported by the evidence presented. Reviewer 1: the conclusion that token-level memorization can detect string-level membership is questioned ("does token-level memorization really tell you anything about the string-level?").
- **Claim about existing memorization measures not substantiated:** Reviewer 4 challenges the paper's assertion that existing work's implicit assumption (that full prefix can recollect full suffix) is called into question. The paper's evidence is based on small alphabets and random strings, unlikely to be the main source of memorization in practice.
- **Terminology confusion:** "Mechanics" may be misleading as the paper does not analyze internal model circuits (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3). Suggested alternatives: dynamics/statics or diachrony/synchrony.
- **Inadequate benchmarking against prior work:** Missing reference: Haviv et al. (EACL 2023) on transformer memorization recall through idioms. Results not sufficiently situated relative to Tirumala et al. (2020/2022) showing similar model-size and dataset-size effects.
- **Inconsistent and unclear details in figures/presentation:** Reviewer 1 notes inconsistency between 130M and 140M GPT-2 labels across Figures 3–5 vs. Figures 1,2,7. Figure captions are not standalone (Fig. 8). Reviewer 1 finds confusion between "character" and "token" (e.g., alphabet is a subset of tokenizer vocabulary, but then character-level tokenization is enforced). The definition of "plurality prediction" is redundant and unused; "greedy prediction" or "top prediction" would be standard. Random string examples are missing. The fact that models are trained on one string at a time is not clarified until Section 3.3.
- **Model size and training regime may not reflect "large" LLMs:** Reviewer 5 questions whether GPT-2 is sufficiently large to capture dynamics of current LLMs, and whether the isolated memorization of a single random string exaggerates behaviors that would be diluted in more diverse and correlated datasets. A more granular analysis of scaling behavior (critical size thresholds) is requested.
- **Local context sufficiency may be an artefact of autoregressive architecture:** Reviewer 3 asks for experiments with non-autoregressive models.
- **Missing experiments suggested by multiple reviewers:** Validation on non-random strings (R1, R3), mixture of random and natural strings (R3), reconstruction attacks to show practical utility (R3), and token-level vs. string-level quantification of memorization (R3).
