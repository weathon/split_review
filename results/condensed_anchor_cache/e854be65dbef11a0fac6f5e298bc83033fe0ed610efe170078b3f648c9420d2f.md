## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces the Offline Continual World benchmark (based on meta-world tasks) and a method called CompoFormer for continual offline reinforcement learning (CORL). CompoFormer uses a transformer backbone with a meta-policy network that composes previous policies via attention over textual task descriptions, and optionally adds new policies via low-rank adaptation (LoRA). Experiments compare CompoFormer to prior continual learning baselines on the new benchmark.

### Strengths
- The paper is well written and clearly presented (Reviewers 1, 4).
- CompoFormer is sound and flexible: it combines policies via an attention mechanism, optionally grows the policy set via LoRA, and balances plasticity and stability (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- The attention‑based composition of prior policies is novel and promising, with prior evidence (e.g., Flamingo) that such mechanisms effectively combine knowledge (Reviewer 3).
- The method shows strong experimental results compared to existing baselines, especially on longer task sequences (Reviewers 3, 4).
- Extensive comparisons to prior works are provided (Reviewer 2).
- The problem of sequential learning in offline RL is interesting and important to study (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation for the CORL setting is weak.** Multiple reviewers question why separate tasks should not be merged into a single dataset and trained i.i.d. (Reviewers 3, 4). The paper does not clearly justify the need for continual learning (vs. multi-task learning) in this offline setting (Reviewers 3, 4). Reviewer 3 suggests a more realistic scenario: pretrain offline then deploy online; this baseline is missing.
- **Assumptions of the benchmark lack realism.** The setting assumes previous datasets cannot be stored at all, which Reviewer 2 argues is unrealistic given typical compute/storage trade‑offs. Additionally, creating offline datasets from the replay buffer of an online agent is a common practice but may not represent real‑world challenges (Reviewer 2 cites a recent paper).
- **Negligible forward transfer.** All reviewers note that forward transfer is essentially zero (Reviewers 2, 3). This suggests tasks are too dissimilar given the current formulation, and it is surprising given the method’s compositional design (Reviewer 3).
- **Single‑task training significantly outperforms CompoFormer** (Figure 5). Reviewer 2 asks for a comparison of parameter counts between single‑task models and the proposed method, and notes that single‑task learning appears simpler and more effective.
- **Use of online samples during evaluation.** Reviewer 2 points out that CompoFormer uses online samples to evaluate candidate policies (line 14 of Algorithm 1), making comparisons to fully‑offline methods technically apples‑to‑oranges. The number of online samples used is not reported.
- **Missing ablations and hyperparameter sensitivity.** No ablation study isolates the contributions of attention, LoRA, and the growth vs. prune variants (Reviewer 2). The “performance threshold” parameter (used to decide when to adopt a new policy) is not specified and its sensitivity is not discussed (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 4 asks how the query/key parameters $\{W^Q, W^K\}$ are updated and what the pre‑defined threshold is.
- **Key experiment details unclear.** Reviewers are confused about the origin of the base transformer weights used for LoRA: are they randomly initialized and kept fixed? If so, the method stores random weights in memory (Reviewer 3). It is also unclear which of the 10 tasks actually introduce new parameters (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing comparisons and related‑work discussion.** The paper does not compare to prior CORL methods (Reviewer 2). It omits relevant structure‑based and regularization‑based approaches such as Progressive Networks and Progress & Compress (Reviewer 3). Technical similarities/differences to progressive nets, progress & compress, and lifelong learning with LoRA should be discussed (Reviewer 2).
- **Figure 3 is hard to read** (similar line colors, missing MTL performance) and Figure 3 does not contain a multi‑task learning (MTL) or incremental‑MTL baseline (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 2 also asks to evaluate on more tasks (e.g., from CompoSuite).
- **Textual descriptions for attention may not generalize.** Relying on textual task descriptions for attention scores may fail in domains without explicit semantics (Reviewer 1).
- **Extra training cost.** The method incurs additional cost for evaluating the composed policy against the current task (Reviewer 1).
- **Contradictory statements about distribution shift.** Two statements seem contradictory: “Transformer‑based methods help reduce distribution shift” vs. “they may exacerbate shifts between the learned policy and replay buffer” (Reviewer 2).
- **Plasticity analysis evidence unclear for PackNet.** In Figure 5, it is not clear that PackNet shows a significant performance drop, as claimed (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 1 requests explicit visualization of plasticity (citing references) to strengthen the claim.
- **Rehearsal‑based methods and distribution shift.** Reviewer 3 questions why distribution shift between replay buffer and learned policy would affect rehearsal methods in the offline RL setting (as claimed in lines 375–377).
- **Presentation issues.** Figure 1’s caption does not clarify what “enough”/“sufficient” means (Reviewer 3). Line 183 wrongly frames an assumption as an implication (Reviewer 3). Algorithm 1 is referenced far from the text (Reviewer 3). The claim that MTL and fine‑tuning are “soft upper bounds” needs clarification (Reviewer 3). Using original meta‑world task names may be less informative than more descriptive strings (Reviewer 2).
