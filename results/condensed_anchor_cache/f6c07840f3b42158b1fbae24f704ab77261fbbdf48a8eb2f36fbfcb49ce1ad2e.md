## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Sim-OPRL, an offline preference-based reinforcement learning algorithm that bridges offline RL and preference-based RL by learning an environment model from the offline dataset and simulating rollouts to elicit preference feedback without online interaction. It employs pessimism for out-of-distribution dynamics and optimism for reward model acquisition. The authors provide theoretical sample complexity guarantees dependent on concentrability, and empirical validation on a gridworld, a sepsis simulation, and a Cheetah environment. Reviewers note the problem setting is timely and relevant (especially for RLHF), the theoretical analysis is solid (decomposing loss from model and reward estimation), and the practical algorithm outperforms the OPRL baseline. However, multiple reviewers raise concerns about the simplicity of the test environments, the limited number of baselines, the lack of analysis on dependence on offline data quality/feedback quality, scalability challenges, and insufficient ablation on key design choices.

### Strengths
1. The paper addresses a timely and practically relevant problem: offline preference elicitation for RL, with connections to RLHF (Reviewers 3, 5).
2. Strong theoretical analysis: provides sample complexity bounds that decompose error from model estimation and reward estimation, and quantifies the advantage of simulated rollouts over direct offline sampling (Reviewers 1, 2, 4, 5).
3. The algorithm incorporates pessimism for transition dynamics to handle OOD data and optimism for reward acquisition, which is well-motivated theoretically (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
4. Empirical results show Sim-OPRL outperforms the only baseline (OPRL) on multiple environments, including a sepsis simulator (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4, 5).
5. Ablation study demonstrates the importance of pessimism with respect to the transition model (Reviewers 2, 5).
6. The attached code is well-written, self-documenting, and facilitates reproducibility (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited baseline comparisons**: Only OPRL is used as a baseline. The paper should include other offline PbRL algorithms (e.g., PbOP, active-learning-based preference elicitation, uncertainty-aware sampling) and could compare with standard offline RL methods (CQL, IQL, TD3_BC) run on the same reward-labeled data to contextualize performance (Reviewer 4; Reviewer 3 briefly mentions PbOP but only as a comparison in results). (Reviewer 4, minority: Reviewer 5 notes no big issues.)
2. **Simple and small-scale environments**: The gridworld is very small; the sepsis environment, while practical, has a sparse reward requiring many preference samples. The lack of validation on more complex benchmarks (e.g., D4RL-style MuJoCo tasks) limits assessment of scalability and practical applicability (Reviewers 2, 4; Reviewer 1 asks for Cheetah medium/medium-expert/expert datasets; Reviewer 3 notes complexity of learning accurate dynamics in harder settings). (Note: Reviewer 5 does not mention this.)
3. **Insufficient analysis of dependence on offline data quality and feedback quality**: The paper does not clearly describe how offline datasets are collected (e.g., are they expert-only? Cheetah: random? Sepsis: how generated?) and does not study how performance changes with varying offline data optimality/coverage or with the quality/consistency of human preferences (Reviewers 1, 3). Reviewer 1 specifically requests experiments on Cheetah with medium, medium-expert, and expert datasets. Reviewer 3 asks how varying feedback quality affects results.
4. **Lack of ablation on hyperparameters and design choices**: The sensitivity to pessimism/optimism parameters is not analyzed; no ablation for warm-starting the reward model in sparse-reward settings (like sepsis) to address the large preference budget (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 3 notes that the algorithm relies on learning multiple accurate dynamics models, and error accumulation in rollouts could be problematic; no analysis of how model quality affects final performance.
5. **Missing formal definitions**: Terms like \(\hat{R}_{\text{inf}}\) and \(\hat{T}_{\text{inf}}\) are introduced without formal definition, making the theoretical exposition less clear (Reviewer 3, minor but noted). Also, potential typo: in line 369, \(N_T\) may be \(N_R\) (Reviewer 3).
6. **Minor clarity issues**: Line 346, “an advantage of sampling from the offline buffer … is not sensitive to the quality of the model” – what does “the model” refer to? (Reviewer 3).
7. **Scalability concerns**: The algorithm requires multiple dynamics models, simulated rollouts, and preference queries, which may become expensive and imprecise in larger state-action spaces; no analysis of computational complexity or performance in high-dimensional settings (Reviewers 3, 4).
