## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents an end-to-end reinforcement learning framework for the Flexible Job Shop Problem (FJSP). It uses two graph neural network models: a generative model that constructively builds a solution step-by-step, and an improvement model that refines the current partial solution. Both models are trained concurrently via RL. The method is evaluated on synthetic and public benchmarks, outperforming dispatching rules and some previous RL approaches but not metaheuristics like OR-Tools, while being faster. The key novelty is the simultaneous training of construction and improvement within a single RL paradigm.

### Strengths
- The problem (FJSP) is important for practical scheduling.
- The proposed simultaneous generation-and-improvement training is novel for scheduling problems and potentially offers the “best of both worlds”: the speed of one-shot generation and the solution quality of iterative improvement.
- The approach uses graph representations that capture the partial solution structure, providing relevant information to each model in an interesting and convincing way.
- An ablation study (Table 3) provides empirical evidence for the contributions of the two distinct models (generator and improver).
- The framework is good in principle: constructing a better partial solution and then improving it.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of justification for adaptability to other CO problems.** The abstract claims the paradigm can be readily adapted to TSP and beyond, but the paper provides no empirical evidence and no explanation of how the improvement step, reward function, or model design would transfer. The specific design (Section 3) appears over-fitted to FJSP, requiring substantial restructuring for new problems.
- **Missing ablation for the generator alone.** The paper states the models can run independently, but no results are shown for using only the generator (without improvement). It is unclear whether the generator underperforms when not relying on the improver, and why the more complex combined method is chosen over a simpler generator-only approach.
- **Insufficient and unfair baselines.**  
  - Table 1 compares only against OR-Tools and priority dispatching rules (PDRs), omitting several relevant baselines like RGA, 2SGA, and two DRL methods that are mentioned elsewhere.  
  - OR-Tools solution times are missing, so the speed-quality trade-off cannot be fully assessed.  
  - Standard deviations and multiple runs are not reported.  
  - The DRL baseline [1] (Song et al.) was tested on a more extensive set of benchmarks; this paper evaluates on only two public benchmarks, raising generalizability concerns.  
  - The reported performance of [1] uses greedy decoding, while the proposed method uses sampling during improvement steps. For a fair comparison, results from both greedy and sampling decoding should be provided (sampling performance of [1] on v_la is actually better than the proposed method, albeit slower).
- **Unclear definition of improvement actions.** The action space for the improvement model is not clearly defined. The “Insertion Position Embedding” description (Section 3.2) does not clearly state how insertion positions are determined (why (n+m) choices per operation; why total positions equal n×(n+m)). The text also says “there are at most m different insertion schemes for each improvement decision,” which contradicts earlier claims and is confusing.
- **Confusing and unconvincing Figure 2.** The example of moving operation 31 to after operation 11 (rather than before 11) is not explained; the reader cannot infer the strategy.
- **Inconsistent operation representation.** The paper uses notations $O_{ij}, O_j, O_{j(i)}, O_i$ inconsistently, making it difficult to understand the Insert Position Embedding.
- **Lack of detailed model descriptions.**  
  - The reward for each model is not stated mathematically, causing ambiguity.  
  - In the GAT module (page 5), it is unclear whether the weight matrix $W$ is shared across different node types $u$.  
  - The rationale for various embeddings is not provided, and there is no ablation study for these design choices.
- **Numerous clarity and presentation issues.**  
  - Typos: “PRD” → “PDR”, “avenger” → “average”, “avitation” → “activation”, missing spaces (e.g., “O_i,which”, “end of production.These two”), etc.  
  - Wrong references: “(ref)” and “(ref1)” in Section 5.1; “mk[?]” in Table 2; missing reference for GAT.  
  - Algorithm 1 errors: Store transition $\tau_{t+1}^g$ into $EP_I$ should be $EP_G$; “E%K” should be “e%K”.  
  - Abbreviations introduced without definition: DRL (page 3), GAT (page 4), GIM (Algorithm 1, Table 3).  
  - Section 5.1 text says “Gurobi Solver” but Table 1 reports OR-Tools.
- **Missing data in tables.** Table 1 lacks the 40×10 result for OR-Tools; Table 2 has “mk[?]” for UB*.
- **No code provided** to reproduce results.
- **Questions raised by reviewers that overlap with weaknesses or missing experiments:**  
  - Why was DuelingDQN chosen over actor-critic methods (e.g., PPO, policy gradient like MatNet)? A brief comparison in related work is needed.  
  - How is the number of improvement iterations $n_t$ decided? It is only described as a hand-crafted function of $t$.  
  - What are GIM and “Generate+improve” in Table 3? They appear to be different but are never defined.  
  - How does the Machine Process Queue Embedding work? Is $M_{ij}=1$ if $O_j$ is processed on machine $i$ or $if it can be processed?  
  - What is the condition for the Job Sequence Embedding ($A_J(J_i, O_{ij})=1$)?  
  - How long does training take?  
  - How does the method scale to larger instances (e.g., $100 \times 60$ as studied in [29])?  
  - Why is the 40×10 result missing for OR-Tools?  
  - How can this work be extended to other scheduling or combinatorial optimization problems?
