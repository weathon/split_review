## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper adopts diffusion models for differentiable causal discovery by using them as flexible function approximators for the nonlinear causal generative process within the NOTEARS continuous optimization framework. The method, DAG-Diffuser, models the functions \(f\) and \(g\) in the structural causal model via a diffusion process. Experiments on synthetic (linear Gaussian, nonlinear additive noise) and semi-synthetic datasets are reported, showing some improvements over baselines CAM and DAG-GNN.

### Strengths
- **Clear writing and easy to follow** (Reviewers 1, 3). The proposed approach is sound and correct (Reviewer 3).  
- **Important problem**: causal discovery with nonlinear relations is practically relevant because linear assumptions are often unrealistic (Reviewer 2).  
- **Interesting direction**: using diffusion models for function modeling in causal discovery, rather than for graph distribution modeling, is noted as a worthwhile exploration (Reviewer 3).  
- **Empirical advantage on nonlinear models** (Reviewer 4): The paper highlights that the proposed method “consistently outperforms many other baseline methods” on nonlinear synthetic data. (Other reviewers, however, question the adequacy of baselines and whether the improvement is significant – see Weaknesses.)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and incremental contribution** (Reviewers 1, 3). The core idea directly replaces another function approximator with a diffusion model inside the existing NOTEARS framework. The paper does not provide a novel way to use diffusion models for causal structure learning; it simply uses them as black-box function approximators (Reviewer 1). A similar “plug-and-play” approach has been done with normalizing flows and invertible neural networks, and the specific advantages of diffusion models over these alternatives are not justified (Reviewer 3).  
- **Restrictive functional form assumption** (Reviewer 2). The paper assumes a specific form of the structural causal model (Eqs. 10, 11, 12) that is not general. It cannot handle more standard nonlinear additive noise models or post-nonlinear mixtures. The justification for choosing this particular functional class over common alternatives (e.g., additive noise) is insufficient (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 2 requests a precise formulation of the SCM as \(X_i = f_i(PA(X_i), Z_i)\).  
- **Insufficient baselines** (Reviewer 2). Only CAM and DAG-GNN are compared for nonlinear models. Several other differentiable nonlinear causal discovery methods should be included for a fair evaluation, e.g., those for general nonlinear models (https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13189, https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02226) and those designed for the specific model in Eq. (11) (https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.07420, https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.08697). Reviewer 4 also notes that a baseline from a diffusion-related paper [1] should be added.  
- **Questionable benefits and limited performance evidence** (Reviewer 1). Tables 1 and 2 show that performance on linear models is not significantly better than existing methods, and the experiments do not clearly demonstrate superiority on complex nonlinear models. The paper does not show whether the specific properties of diffusion models (e.g., denoising objective, no invertibility requirement) are crucial for this task (Reviewer 3).  
- **Missing downstream causal inference experiments** (Reviewer 3). Because the method learns a structural causal model, it could be extended to average/individual treatment effect estimation. Such experiments could provide stronger evidence for the choice of diffusion models over alternatives.  
- **Proposition 1 lacks rigor** (Reviewers 1, 4). The proposition is stated too informally. Reviewer 4 notes that “\(h_* = 0\)” is trivial because \(A\) is continuous and \(h\) is continuous; the phrase “all direct cyclic graphs in the solution space” is incorrect (the solution space contains only DAGs). The proposition does not provide new insight as the paper uses standard augmented Lagrangian optimization (Reviewer 2).  
- **Identifiability concern on linear Gaussian data** (Reviewer 3). If the noise variances are not the same across variables in the linear Gaussian synthetic dataset, the model is not identifiable, which would invalidate the claims. The paper should clarify the variance settings.  
- **Missing runtime and limited graph sizes** (Reviewer 4). Experimental results omit algorithm runtime and only test on graphs up to 50 nodes with ER2 setting.  
- **Potential issue with CAM baseline** (Reviewer 4). The reported performance of CAM may be degraded because the CausalDiscovery package may have tuning steps turned off by default; this should be verified.  
- **Minor errors and unclear statements** (Reviewers 2, 3, 4):  
  - Typos: “deep leyers” → “deep layers” (line 309-310); “node i to node j” → “node \(i\) to node \(j\)” (line 183-184).  
  - Eq. (2): Are \(Y_0\) and \(Y_T\) in the wrong order? (Reviewer 3)  
  - L56: Zheng et al. (2020) proposed a nonparametric DAG *constraint*, not a nonparametric score function (Reviewer 2).  
  - Line 192: missing reference for the statement.  
  - Line 196: “without loss of generality, we assume ... invertibility of \(f\) and \(g\)” – this assumption actually limits the capacity of \(f,g\) (Reviewer 3).  
  - Line 201: if \(f\) is nonlinear, does \(Z\) still need to be non-Gaussian? (Reviewer 3)  
  - Line 261: \(S_G\) should likely be \(R_G\).  
  - Line 403: what is “\((10\alpha)/\alpha\)”? (Reviewer 3)  
  - Proposition 1 should be explicitly related to the similar spirit of Zhu et al. (2020) (Reviewer 2).  
  - Related work should cite more diffusion-based or soft-constraint causal discovery works (Reviewer 3 suggests [2,3,4,5]).  
  - Method name “DAG-Diffuser” should be introduced before the experiment section (Reviewer 3).  
- **Abstract too general** (Reviewer 1). It lacks specific details on how the diffusion model is integrated into causal discovery and what advantages this integration brings.  

**Reviewer disagreement**: Reviewer 4 states the paper “highlights the significant advantages of using a diffusion model for causal discovery” and that it “consistently outperforms many other baseline methods” on nonlinear models. In contrast, Reviewer 1 finds the benefit limited and the performance not significantly better, while Reviewer 2 considers the baselines inadequate to assess true performance.
