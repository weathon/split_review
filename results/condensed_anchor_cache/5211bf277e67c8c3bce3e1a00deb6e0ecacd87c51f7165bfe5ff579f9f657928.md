## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper fine-tunes Llama models (Llama-2-7B) for factuality in long-form generation using DPO on automatically constructed preference pairs. Two methods generate preferences: reference-based DPO-FS (uses FactScore with GPT-3.5-extracted atomic facts verified by an NLI model against Wikipedia) and reference-free DPO-MC (uses self-sampling to estimate model confidence). Evaluated on biography generation and medical QA (test sets of 59 and 50 examples respectively). Results show improved factuality over SFT, RLHF, and decoding strategies (ITI, DOLA); DPO + DOLA further improves. Human evaluation validates findings. The paper reports a 58% and 40% reduction in factual error rate on biographies and medical QA compared to Llama-2-chat (per reviewer summary).

### Strengths
- Motivation is intuitive and easy to understand.
- Improves factuality without human labels; the method can be augmented with existing orthogonal approaches (e.g., DOLA) for further gains.
- New results demonstrating the benefit of automated feedback for factuality in long-form open-ended generation.
- Well-written with well-defined experimental settings and human evaluation; presentation rated high (excellent by two reviewers).
- First application of factuality-based reward in DPO (both reference-based and reference-free); reviewer 4 rated contribution 3 (fair), while others gave 2, but acknowledged the reward formulation as new.
- Analysis and ablations of different variants (pretrained vs chat models, combining with decoding-time methods).
- Both DPO variants (DPO-FS and DPO-MC) achieve the highest percentage of correct claims among compared methods in the main evaluation.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty**: The core idea of using reinforcement learning from automated feedback (RLAIF/DPO) for LM alignment is not new; applying it to factuality is a straightforward and expected extension. Reviewer 2 was less favorable (score 5 vs 6 from others), particularly noting lack of novelty and questioning the contribution beyond prior RLAIF work.
- **Evaluation reliability concerns**: Test sets are small (59 biography and 50 medical QA examples), raising questions about statistical significance (e.g., is 75%→81% a meaningful improvement?). Results rely solely on GPT-3.5 evaluation with no fine-grained reliability analysis. Human evaluation lacks details: inter-annotator agreement and number of annotators are not reported.
- **Metric bias and generation style confounds**: DPO variants reduce the total number of correct facts in biography generation, likely because the optimized metric (percentage of correct atomic facts) may favor shorter outputs (e.g., 10 correct/5 incorrect preferred over 11 correct/6 incorrect). The total number of generated claims differs across methods, suggesting generation style (e.g., length) could confound factuality results.
- **Large unexplained difference in total facts**: Between reference-free and reference-based methods, there is a ≈30% reduction in total generated facts on biographies; no explanation is offered. Also, statistics on token counts of winning vs losing samples are not provided.
- **Missing baselines**: No comparison to a simpler approach that uses prompts to directly compare factuality of two outputs with respect to the Wikipedia article (instead of extracting atomic facts).
- **No evidence of broader impact**: No out-of-domain evaluation (e.g., does fine-tuning on biographies help medical QA?). No assessment of whether improved factuality harms other performance aspects (e.g., fluency, safety, general capabilities); over-indexing on one objective may cause degradation.
- **Weak base LM**: Using Llama-7B, which may not be sufficiently capable for long-form generation or medical QA; larger models might have fewer factual errors and benefit less from fine-tuning. Suggests citing recent survey (Pan et al., 2023) and related RL-for-LM alignment works.
- **Unclear reference-free design**: In DPO-MC, why is the largest bin used to measure truthfulness? Converting distinct atomic facts to the same question (e.g., multiple birth years map to “What year was [person] born?”) may treat correct and incorrect claims identically. The hypothesis that model confidence correlates with correctness is used, but the method does not leverage distributional cross-checking as in the reference-based setting.
- **Missing training detail**: It is not clarified whether DPO-FS, DPO-MC, and SFT are fine-tuned on the training set of each respective dataset (biographies and medical QA).
