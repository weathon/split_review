## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes an online OOD detection framework (SODA) that updates the ID classifier and OOD detector sequentially from streaming data. The algorithm uses a combined ID/OOD loss and online gradient descent. Theoretical regret analysis and experiments on CIFAR-10, ImageNet, etc. are provided. Most reviewers find the problem relevant and the paper well-structured, but criticize the limited technical novelty (direct application of OGD), strong supervision assumptions (OOD labels known after prediction), missing comparisons to related online OOD work, and lack of discussion on hyperparameter selection and limitations. One reviewer (score 5) is more positive about the simplicity and empirical results; the other three (score 3) consider the contributions insufficient.

### Strengths
- The problem of OOD detection in streaming/non-stationary settings is significant and practical for real-world reliability.
- The paper is well-organized, clearly written, and easy to follow; the algorithm is simple and neat.
- The proposed method is easy to implement with many OOD losses and shows empirical success on several benchmarks (CIFAR, ImageNet) under a streaming evaluation, outperforming offline baselines.
- The paper provides both theoretical (regret analysis) and empirical contributions to the online OOD detection problem.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited technical novelty:** The core algorithm (Algorithms 2 and 3) is a direct application of Online Gradient Descent (OGD) with cross-entropy loss; the regret analysis is standard for convex losses in online convex optimization. The claim of addressing non-stationary OOD data is unconvincing because the classical OCO framework already handles arbitrarily changing loss functions (under boundedness assumptions).
- **Strong supervision assumption (main algorithm):** The algorithm assumes labels (including OOD indicators) are revealed after each prediction. This oversimplifies the OOD detection problem, where OOD labels are typically unavailable. When such feedback is available, the task reduces to supervised online learning, and the novelty is limited.
- **Unsupervised variant lacks guarantees:** The unsupervised version in the appendix has no theoretical guarantee that minimizing the unsupervised loss (L_t^H) produces a well-performing OOD detector that minimizes the true loss. The same regret analysis may apply, but why it leads to a good detector is unclear.
- **Missing comparison to existing online OOD method:** The paper claims novelty in online OOD detection but does not compare to [1] (Wu et al., “Meta OOD Learning”, ICCV 2023), which also addresses online OOD detection. The differences and advantages over [1] are not specified.
- **Discrepancy between theory and practice:** The theoretical analysis (Theorem 3.1) relies on convexity and a linear model (as shown in the appendix), but the main experiments use neural networks. This discrepancy is not clearly acknowledged.
- **Parameter and hyperparameter issues:**
    - The learning rate η in the regret bound requires knowledge of T, K_id, K_ood, which are unknown in practice; no guidance is given on selection.
    - The hyperparameter λ (balancing ID/OOD losses) is not explained: how it is chosen in experiments and how it affects performance; no code is provided.
- **Missing discussion of limitations and failure scenarios:** For example, in a stream with very small visual variations over time (e.g., a surveillance camera), SODA may repeatedly see nearly identical batches and overfit, failing to generalize to novel batches. No discussion of such limitations is included.
- **Weaknesses in experimental design:**
    - Datasets (ImageNet, CIFAR-10) are stationary; the stream is synthetically constructed. The paper would benefit from realistic time-defined streaming benchmarks from the online learning literature.
    - The rationale for choosing specific OOD datasets (e.g., why ISCHN is OOD relative to CIFAR) is not provided, and there is no measure of distribution shift.
    - Some performance measures (FPR, AUROC) are not defined in the paper, making the experimental section harder for non-experts.
- **Regret type:** The analysis uses static regret, whereas the online learning literature typically uses adaptive or dynamic regret to capture non-stationarity, which would be more appropriate for this setting.
- **Update frequency and computational cost:** The paper does not justify why the detector/classifier must be updated at every time step t rather than retaining the previous model for some t. The computational cost of the update process is not specified.
- **Lack of code release** is a weakness for reproducibility.

*Note: One reviewer (score 5) was more positive about the simplicity and effectiveness, while the other three (score 3) found the contributions limited and the assumptions strong, especially regarding supervision and technical novelty.*
