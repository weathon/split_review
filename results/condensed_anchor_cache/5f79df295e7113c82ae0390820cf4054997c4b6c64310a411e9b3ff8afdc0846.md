## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Random Network Distillation DAgger (RND-DAgger), an active imitation learning method that uses RND to detect out-of-distribution (OOD) states and selectively request expert demonstrations, minimizing expert workload. Evaluated on RaceCar, 3D Maze, and HalfCheetah environments, it is compared to DAgger variants and BC, showing competitive performance with fewer expert queries.

### Strengths

- **Motivation and problem:** The focus on improving the efficiency of imitation learning by reducing unnecessary expert interventions is well-founded and relevant for real-world applications with costly human expertise (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- **Novelty of using RND for intervention:** Leveraging Random Network Distillation to decide when to query the expert is innovative and well-suited for identifying OOD states, distinct from prior action-based triggers (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Algorithm clarity:** The method is clearly defined with pseudo-code (Algorithms 3 and 4) and illustrated in Figure 4, making it implementable (Reviewers 1, 4).
- **Experimental results:** The approach achieves a favorable balance between task performance (e.g., return) and number of expert queries across three domains; Figure 6 provides an accessible overview of these trade-offs (Reviewers 1, 4).
- **Baselines:** Comparisons include BC, DAgger, Ensemble-DAgger, LazyDAgger, and Batch DAgger, which are reasonable DAgger variants (Reviewer 1).
- **Reproducibility:** The paper provides detailed experimental setup, hyperparameters in the appendix, and a promise to release code upon acceptance (Reviewer 4).
- **Presentation:** The paper is well-organized, easy to follow, and accessible to readers with background in unsupervised RL (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty is limited:** The core components (DAgger, RND as a novelty measure) are pre-existing; the integration mirrors Ensemble-DAgger’s use of disagreement from uncertainty quantification (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). Reviewer 3 also questions whether LazyDAgger with a target network is essentially doing RND but with higher-dimensional output, suggesting the benefit may come from the “minimal demonstration time” mechanism rather than RND.
- **Missing baselines:** No comparisons to recent methods that also leverage human input for OOD states, such as RLIF, PATO, and Sirius (Reviewer 2). Additionally, methods designed to handle OOD by generalizing without expert intervention (e.g., diffusion model-augmented BC [Chen et al. 2024], implicit BC [Florence et al. 2022]) are not discussed (Reviewer 4). The paper only compares to DAgger variants, leaving open whether other “expert takeover” methods could be superior (Reviewer 1).
- **Limited environment diversity:** The four experimental settings (RaceCar, Maze, HalfCheetah) lack sufficient diversity to fully assess robustness. Suggested additions: tasks with high-dimensional state spaces and fine-grained control such as Adroit hand dexterity, robot manipulation, or CARLA for more realistic driving scenes with partial observability (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).
- **Statistical rigor:** Results are averaged over only 8 seeds without a rationale for this number. Confidence intervals are missing from Table 1; bold/underline annotations are not explained (e.g., whether they denote average or other statistics) (Reviewer 1, 3). The shaded areas and line styles in Figure 6 are not described in the caption (Reviewer 3).
- **Need for clearer ablation of components:** The effect of the Minimal Expert Time mechanism vs. RND is not isolated. Incorporating this mechanism into baselines (e.g., Ensemble-DAgger) would clarify whether gains come from RND, the time mechanism, or their combination (Reviewer 2). A more granular analysis of how RND and the stability window contribute to performance is needed (Reviewer 4). The ablation study section lacks detail to interpret these factors (Reviewers 2, 3).
- **Unaddressed limitations:** The method’s reliance on state-based OOD detection may fail when states are novel but safe or when minor deviations are inconsequential (Reviewer 2). The paper notes that action-discrepancy methods are problematic with imperfect experts, but does not discuss whether RND-DAgger suffers from similar issues or how it mitigates them (Reviewer 2). The formulation uses POMDP while environments are state-based (MDP); the method assumes observation aliasing is not a problem, which may not hold in realistic settings (e.g., different visual scenes requiring same action) (Reviewer 3).
- **Clarity of writing and claims (multiple issues):**
  - Algorithms 3 and 4 do not define the “measure” operator (Reviewer 3).
  - Table 1: bold/underline notation and standard error are not explained (Reviewer 3).
  - Figure 6: solid line vs. shaded area unlabeled; top curves need different line styles for readability (Reviewer 3).
  - Figure 6e and 6f “sample-efficiency” claim is not supported because context switches do not directly correspond to number of expert samples (Reviewer 3).
  - Page 9 claim that RND-DAgger is “more effective at leveraging expert feedback” is not justified: final policy training is the same, only aggregated data differs (Reviewer 3).
- **No failure analysis:** Cases where RND-DAgger does not outperform baselines (e.g., in RaceCar, Ensemble-DAgger achieves similar/higher performance with more context switches; in HalfCheetah, LazyDAgger achieves similar performance) are not examined (Reviewers 1, 2).
- **Real-world applicability and future work:** The method assumes simulated environments where an expert can pause; for physical systems (e.g., self-driving cars), the car cannot simply stop—predicting OOD states before they occur would be needed (Reviewer 1). Computational efficiency of RND for large-scale applications is not discussed (Reviewer 4).
