## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces a family of relative-translation invariant Wasserstein distances (\(RW_p\)) that are invariant to distribution translations, defined on the quotient space \(\mathcal{P}_p(\mathbb{R}^n)/\sim\). For \(p=2\), the distance decomposes into a horizontal Wasserstein component and a vertical shift term, yielding a bias-variance interpretation of distribution shift. The authors propose a coordinate-type algorithm for general \(RW_p\) and a Sinkhorn variant for \(RW_2\), and report numerical validation and applications to digits recognition and thunderstorm pattern detection. Reviewers generally acknowledge the motivation, mathematical rigor, and algorithmic contributions but raise concerns about novelty, missing comparisons, experimental clarity, computational advantage claims, and theoretical gaps.

### Strengths

- **Novel shifting-invariant metric**: The paper proposes a new distance \(RW_p\) on the quotient space of probability measures under translation, extending classical Wasserstein distance to be invariant to translations. Some reviewers view this as a novel contribution, while others note close connections to known properties (see Weaknesses).  
- **Decomposition for \(p=2\)**: For \(p=2\), the optimal transport problem decomposes into a vertical component (optimal shift) and a horizontal component (Wasserstein on centered measures), yielding an orthogonal bias-variance structure that generalizes the Bures-Wasserstein distance for Gaussians.  
- **Algorithm development**: Two algorithms are presented: a gradient-descent/Sinkhorn method for general \(RW_p\) and an orbit-debiased Sinkhorn algorithm for \(RW_2\) that improves numerical stability.  
- **Mathematical soundness**: The proofs of metric properties are rigorous and correct (most reviewers agree, though one raised a concern about the triangle inequality proof).  
- **Motivation from real applications**: The work is motivated by pattern detection in geographical images (e.g., thunderstorm snapshots) and robustness in distribution shift, with experiments on such datasets.

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty concerns**: One reviewer argues that the quadratic case \(RW_2\) is very close to a known translation property of the Wasserstein distance with quadratic cost (Remark 2.19 in [1]). Deriving the optimal shift \(s^* = \bar{\nu} - \bar{\mu}\) and substituting back yields a closed-form expression already implied by that property, weakening the novelty claim. Other reviewers view the metric as novel; this contrast should be acknowledged.  
- **Insufficient comparison with related work**:  
  - No comparison with Gromov-Wasserstein distance (also translation-invariant and shape-focused) nor with Robust \(p\)-Wasserstein distance (claimed to handle shifting perturbations).  
  - No baseline of simply subtracting means and then computing the Wasserstein distance (a natural, simpler approach).  
  - The literature review fails to position the work clearly relative to previous efforts addressing translation invariance.  
- **Unconvincing computational advantage**:  
  - For \(p=2\), the proposed Sinkhorn variant is essentially the classical Sinkhorn algorithm with precomputed means; no clear computational gain is demonstrated.  
  - For \(p\neq2\), solving \(RW_p\) requires multiple Sinkhorn subproblems (inner gradient descent for the shift) compared to a single Sinkhorn pass for \(W_p\); the paper does not provide iteration complexity to justify better time complexity.  
  - The claim in Remark 1 (that the method achieves better time complexity) is not supported by Theorem 5, which only shows improvement in the worst-case pairwise distance; the dependence on \(n\) through the \(\ell_2\) norm also suggests limited gains for high-dimensional data.  
- **Experimental issues**:  
  *Numerical validation (Figure 3)*: Running time of Sinkhorn is constant for \(s\in[0,2.4]\) and then suddenly drops to near zero (intuitively diameter should increase, raising time); the computation error increases sharply after \(s>2.4\) (suggesting algorithm may have returned 0); both methods yield identical errors for all \(s\); number of runs per \(s\) is not reported.  
  *Simulation too simple*: The Gaussian case allows closed-form solutions for both \(W_2\) and \(RW_2\); more extensive settings (higher-dimensional, non-Gaussian, general \(p\geq1\)) are needed to demonstrate numerical benefits.  
  *Thunderstorm pattern detection*: Results in Figures 8 and 9 are hard to judge without ground truth; quantitative accuracy or retrieval metrics (e.g., precision, recall) on labeled shifted images would strengthen validation.  
- **Triangle inequality proof issue**: One reviewer points out a potential mistake in the proof: the jump from line 2 to line 3 implicitly assumes \(W_p(\eta,\eta')=0\) for optimal translations, which is not justified.  
- **Missing convergence analysis**: The paper provides no theoretical or empirical analysis of the convergence rates of \(RW_p\) as a distribution measure.  
- **Lack of guidance for hyperparameters**: No practical or theoretical guidance for choosing \(p\); for \(p\neq2\), the step size \(\eta_1\) in the gradient descent on the shift is not justified (constant vs. annealed, no analysis).  
- **Limited scope of invariance**: The distance only handles translation shifts; extension to scaling or rotation is not addressed.  
- **Minor errors**: Typos (e.g., “coupling” → “Coupling”, “Thoorem”); undefined symbols (\(\bar{\mu},\bar{\nu}\) in Theorem 4); Algorithm 1 mentions \(\eta_2\) but its use is unclear.
