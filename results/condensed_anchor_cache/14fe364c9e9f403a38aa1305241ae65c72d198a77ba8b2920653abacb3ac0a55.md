## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes **Laplace Sample Information (LSI)**, a metric that quantifies individual sample informativeness by approximating the Bayesian weight posterior via the Laplace method and measuring the KL divergence change when a sample is left out. LSI is applied to data ordering, mislabeled sample detection, class-wise informativeness, and dataset difficulty assessment. Experiments are performed on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet subsets (Imagewoof, Imagenette), a pediatric pneumonia dataset, and IMDb text sentiment data, including a 10% label-noise scenario on CIFAR-10. Reviewers agree the paper is well-written and addresses an important problem, but raise concerns about limited novelty, lack of comparison to existing methods, insufficient theoretical justification, unclear computational-cost analysis, missing large-scale validation, and incomplete technical details.

### Strengths
- **Important and timely problem**: Estimating sample informativeness is valuable for data-centric AI, data cleaning, and efficient training. (All reviewers)
- **Clear and accessible presentation**: The paper is well-organized, easy to follow, and explains the method in a principled way. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4)
- **Novel perspective on sample informativeness**: LSI directly measures information flow via posterior change rather than relying on proxy metrics like sample difficulty or loss. (Reviewers 2, 3)
- **Theoretically grounded**: LSI is derived from Bayesian principles (Laplace approximation + KL divergence), providing a principled foundation. (Reviewers 3, 4)
- **Broad applicability**: LSI works across architectures, learning tasks (supervised and unsupervised), and data modalities (images and text). (Reviewers 3, 4)
- **Efficiency via probe models**: Using a smaller probe model makes LSI computationally feasible for larger datasets and models, and the authors show that LSI rankings transfer well to larger architectures. (Reviewer 3)
- **Extensive experimental validation**: LSI is evaluated on multiple datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet subsets, text) and shown effective at ranking samples by informativeness, detecting mislabeled data (e.g., 10% label noise on CIFAR-10), and assessing dataset and class difficulty. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and relation to influence functions**: The Laplacian approximation is widely used; the paper does not clearly differentiate LSI from influence-function-based methods (which also require higher-order derivatives). (Reviewers 1, 4)  
  *Reviewer 1*: “The novelty of the proposed method might be limited as the Laplacian approximation is widely used.”  
  *Reviewer 4*: “How this method is different from the influence function based method? … I find it a bit hard to differentiate the differences.”
- **Lack of comparison to existing baselines**: No comparisons to related measures such as gradient similarity, influence functions, or TRAK (Park et al., ICML’23) for mislabeled detection or sample ranking. (Reviewers 2, 3)
- **Insufficient theoretical justification**: The connection between the change in posterior (KL divergence) and sample informativeness is not rigorously formalized; the Gaussian assumption for the posterior (via Laplace) lacks empirical validation for non-convex, deep neural network loss landscapes. (Reviewers 1, 3)
- **Missing computational-cost analysis**: LSI requires Hessian computations, which can be expensive. The paper should provide a detailed breakdown of computation time and space for different Hessian approximations (diagonal, Kronecker-factored) and compare their trade-offs with approximation accuracy. (Reviewers 2, 3)  
  *Reviewer 2* explicitly requests: “a more rigorous analysis of the computational complexity associated with the Hessian calculation, including the time and space requirements for different approximation methods.”
- **Probe model reliability and limitations**: While probe models enable efficiency, there is insufficient quantitative analysis of how well LSI rankings from the probe model align with those from the full (larger) model. Conditions under which the probe model remains reliable should be studied. (Reviewer 2)
- **Lack of statistical rigor for high/low LSI analysis**: The paper presents qualitative observations on a few high/low LSI samples but does not provide statistical significance tests or quantitative measures of the differences. (Reviewer 2)
- **Unclear technical details for LSI computation**: The estimation of \(\hat{\theta}^{-i}\) and \(\Sigma^{-i}\) (LSI after leave-one-out) is not clearly explained; it is unclear how these can be obtained post-training without retraining. (Reviewer 4)
- **Experiments on synthetic noise only**: Mislabel detection is tested only with random label flips (synthetic noise). Real-world noise datasets (e.g., CIFAR-10N, CIFAR-10H) should be used to validate practical utility. (Reviewer 2)
- **Limited scale of experiments**: The largest evaluated models and datasets are relatively small (CIFAR-100, ImageNet subsets). Scalability to truly large models (e.g., ResNet-152, transformers) and datasets (like full ImageNet) remains unaddressed, raising concerns about practical impact. (Reviewer 1)

**Minor issues**:
- Typo in Lines 194–195: “shownAppendix” should be “shown in Appendix”. (Reviewer 1)
- The paper PDF is 32 MB, making it difficult to read figures and results. (Reviewer 4)
