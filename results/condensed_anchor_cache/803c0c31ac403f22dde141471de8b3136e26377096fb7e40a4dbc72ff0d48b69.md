## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates the under-explored problem of Active Continual Learning (ACL), where a sequence of active learning tasks (each with unlabelled data and an annotation budget) arrive. It conducts a systematic evaluation of multiple continual learning (CL) and active learning (AL) algorithms across domain-incremental, class-incremental, and task-incremental scenarios on datasets including P-MNIST, S-MNIST, MNIST, and CIFAR-10. The proposed forgetting-learning profile aims to reveal the trade-off between forgetting (CL) and learnability (AL). Scores range from 3 to 6, with Reviewer 4 most positive (score 6) and Reviewer 3 most negative (score 3); reviewers 1 and 2 are moderately positive (both 5).

### Strengths
- The problem of ACL is novel and interesting, having not been previously investigated (R3, R4). The paper aims to answer three important research questions, demonstrating originality (R3).
- The paper provides a systematic evaluation of current AL and CL approaches, which is needed given the variety of components (R1, R2). The analysis experiments are extensive and should be valuable to the community (R4).
- The paper is well-organized and concepts are presented clearly (R3). Reviewer 1 acknowledges the authors' effort to respond to criticism (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of error bars in key figures:** Results in Figures 3, 4, 8, and 9 are presented without error bars, which misrepresent the capabilities and stability of approaches. Even where error bars appear (Figures 5–7), it is not described how they were obtained (R1, confidence 4).
- **Weak/qualitative analysis:** The analysis of results in Figures 3 and 4 is shallow; statements like "ACL achieves comparable performance to CL in most scenarios" are purely qualitative. The paper lacks a quantitative evaluation of the significance of improvements (R1). Also, Figures 8 and 9 are difficult to read due to many similarly colored dots (R1).
- **Poor visualization choices:** In Figures 3 and 4, a dashed red line connecting methods on the x‑axis is meaningless; the dense dot representations obscure actual performance of individual strategies (R3). Alternative visualizations (e.g., accuracy relative to full, or showing Figures 5–6 like Figure 1b) are suggested (R1).
- **Limited difficulty and scope of datasets:** Experiments use only easy benchmarks (P-MNIST, S-MNIST, MNIST, CIFAR-10). Results on more challenging datasets (CIFAR-100, tiny-/mini-ImageNet) are missing, especially since ACL methods perform substantially worse on CIFAR-10 CIL/TIL, questioning the effectiveness of CL+AL (R2, R3).
- **Small number of tasks per dataset:** The number of tasks is low (e.g., 5 for MNIST and CIFAR-10). Active selection should become more difficult with more tasks; scalability and robustness are not evaluated (R3).
- **Lack of systematic evaluation of key factors:** The experimental design does not systematically vary factors such as number of tasks, backbone architecture, model size, annotation budget, or computational budget (R1). For a purely experimental contribution with no methodological novelty (R1, R2), this is a significant gap.
- **Baselines not state-of-the-art for specific settings:** The paper largely uses baselines from Class-Incremental Learning (DER++, iCaRL, GDumb) and does not include SoTA methods for other settings, e.g., for Text-Incremental Learning on text data (R4, citing [1,2,3]). This unfair comparison is a major drawback for an analysis paper (R4).
- **Minority negative view on novelty:** While some reviewers find the problem novel (R3, R4), Reviewer 2 explicitly states the paper "does not seem to have a high novel approach" and is mostly an evaluation of current AL and CL approaches (R2).
- **Technical question about Figure 1(b):** How was Figure 1(b) obtained? (R1). This missing experimental detail could affect reproducibility.
