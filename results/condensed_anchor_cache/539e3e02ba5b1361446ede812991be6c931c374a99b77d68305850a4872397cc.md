## Merged Review

### Summary
The authors propose LeZO, a layer-wise sparse zeroth-order optimizer that accelerates fine-tuning of large language models by sparsifying updates at the layer level. Building on MeZO (Malladi et al., 2023), LeZO randomly selects a subset of layers to perturb and update each step, reducing the dominant time cost of the full-parameter perturbation and updating stages without additional memory overhead. Experiments on the OPT model family with SuperGLUE and generative tasks show up to 3× (or 3.4×) speedup in wall-clock time over MeZO, with comparable downstream performance.

### Strengths
1. **Practical and easily integrated algorithm.** The method is straightforward, combining block coordinate descent with MeZO at the layer level, and can be integrated into existing training frameworks with minimal engineering effort (R1, R2).
2. **Strong empirical acceleration.** LeZO achieves up to 3.4× faster training time on tasks such as SST-2, BoolQ, and Copa, with extensive experiments across different OPT model sizes, evaluation on orthogonal PEFT methods, and hyperparameter analysis (R1, R2, R3, R4).
3. **Theoretical convergence analysis provided.** The paper includes a formal analysis of the convergence rate, maintaining the foundations of ZO optimization while introducing layer-wise sparsity (R1, R3).
4. **Well-structured presentation** with clear background and related work discussion, despite some notation issues (R2, R4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Novelty concerns.** The layer-wise sampling idea is similar to LISA (Pan et al., 2024) for first-order optimizers, and the convergence analysis closely follows Sparse-MeZO (Liu et al., 2024) with minor changes. Neither method is used as a baseline in experiments, despite being mentioned (R4). One reviewer questions whether the primary benefit is memory reduction versus performance gains over Sparse-MeZO (R2).
2. **Insufficient comparison baselines.** The paper only compares against MeZO. Missing comparisons include: (a) first-order methods (e.g., AdamW), which are standard in the LLM community; (b) other memory-efficient ZO variants like Sparse-MeZO; and (c) other parameter selection strategies beyond random layer selection (R1, R3, R4). The practical appeal to AdamW users is not addressed (R3).
3. **Theory relies on unrealistic Lipschitz continuity assumptions.** The loss landscapes of deep LLMs are non-convex and may not satisfy Lipschitz conditions (R3). Additionally, the convergence rate improvement still scales with dimension \(d\) without further assumptions (R1).
4. **Mathematical notation errors and inconsistencies.** 
   - "Sparse rate \(\rho\)" (Line 194) and "sparsity rate reaches \(\rho = 1\)" (Line 451) appear to describe different concepts (R2).
   - Equation (4) adds \(\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and \(z' \in \mathbb{R}^{\rho d}\), which is dimensionally inconsistent; cases where \(\rho d\) is not an integer are not addressed (R2).
   - Notation in Lemma 1 is unclear (R2).
   - Table 1 contains an unexplained entry "SSZO," likely a typo, causing confusion (R2, R4).
5. **Unclear or unaddressed experimental observations.**
   - Figure 3 shows notable performance drops between dropout numbers 5 and 0 (i.e., MeZO) and between 35 and 40. Explanation is needed (R2).
   - Figure 4 shows accuracy drop <0.5% when all layers are frozen (dropout 40) vs. MeZO (dropout 0); reviewers ask whether this is because embedding and linear layers remain tunable (R4). How much do these fixed layers affect results?
   - The relationship between sparsity ratio \(\rho\) and convergence time: if \(\rho=0\) no parameters are perturbed, why would the convergence time upper bound be minimized? (R2)
6. **Limited model and task diversity.** Experiments are only on the OPT model family; larger models (e.g., 30B) and other architectures are missing (R1, R3). LoRA evaluation uses only \(r=8, \alpha=16\); higher-rank settings should be tested (R3).
7. **Memory overhead claim needs elaboration.** The paper asserts “accelerated computation without additional memory overhead” but does not provide detailed profiling on how much and where memory is saved (R3, R3 question). Reviewer 3 asks for detailed memory savings breakdown.
8. **Optimality of random layer selection.** Random selection of layers may not be optimal; alternative strategies (e.g., based on importance sampling or weight norms) are not explored (R1). Additionally, theoretical insight for choosing layers as the sparsification unit is not provided (R2).
9. **Impact on model performance unclear.** While theoretical and empirical convergence speed improves, the effect on final downstream accuracy relative to MeZO is not sufficiently explained or contextualized (R1).
