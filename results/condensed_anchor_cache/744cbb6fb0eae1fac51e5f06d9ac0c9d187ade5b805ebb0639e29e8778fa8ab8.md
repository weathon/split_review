## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a 4D tensor multi-task continual learning algorithm to predict Alzheimer’s disease (AD) progression from MRI-derived biomarkers. Reviewers acknowledge the relevance of the problem and the interesting idea, but raise major concerns about clarity of the method and experimental setup, insufficient benchmarking, missing validations against established AD and continual learning benchmarks, and lack of ablations. One reviewer (R3, score 6) was notably more positive about the intuition of the 4D tensor representation and the biomarker correlation analysis, while the other reviewers (scores 5,5,5) found the paper hard to follow and the evidence limited.

### Strengths
- The research question (predicting AD progression from longitudinal MRI data) is very relevant (R4).
- The paper tackles the challenging setup of continual learning in medical longitudinal data (R2).
- The proposed approach is compared against multiple baselines (R1).
- The idea of constructing a 4D tensor representation for multivariate spatiotemporal information aggregation is intuitive for this task (R3; this reviewer also gave the highest score).
- The biomarker correlation analysis (Tables 4–8) is thoughtful, and the authors make an effort to interpret the results (R3, R4).
- The approach is overall interesting and exciting, though many questions remain (R1).

### Weaknesses
**Clarity and presentation**
- The paper is very hard to follow overall; the high-level data and task description are missing (e.g., whether it is a classification or regression problem, what the prediction targets are, nature of MRI scans and extracted biomarkers) (R4).
- The “amalgamated magnitude-direction” representation (Section 3.2) is introduced but its connection to the subsequent learning process (Section 3.3) is never explained; the reader cannot see how it is used in the tensor-based MTL algorithm (R4).
- The methods section, especially Eq. (2) and its optimization description, is insufficient: the role of each term is unclear, and the optimization goal is not well defined (R4). Specifically, what is \(W_t\)? What is \(C_t\)? The term \(C\) (used in Section 3.3) is not defined (R2, R4). The multi-task learning setup itself is not clearly introduced (R2).
- Figure 1: the construction of the first two dimensions (rows and columns) is unclear; it looks like a 2D matrix but what \(M\) and \(N\) represent is not explained (R3).
- Figure 2: text over colored arrows is hard to read and visually unpleasing (R3).
- Tables appear earlier than referenced in the text, making it harder to follow (R4).
- Several grammatical issues; another round of proofreading is needed (R3).

**Problem statement and motivation**
- The claim that “data on neurological diseases such as AD are difficult to obtain” is erroneous without context; T1-weighted MRI data are abundant (e.g., ADNI, A4, OASIS) (R3). The authors should specify which modalities are limited.
- The paper states it addresses “monotonic data form, small dataset, and scarcity of time-continuous data,” but the experimental setting uses structured biomarkers, a single dataset (ADNI), and a fixed longitudinal dataset with regular 12-month intervals, which seems to avoid rather than solve those problems (R2).

**Experimental validation and benchmarking**
- Experiments are performed only on one neuroimaging dataset (ADNI); the impact and empirical evidence are limited for the ICLR community, and the novelty makes it hard to assess the approach’s significance (R1).
- It is unclear whether the compared baselines apply well to the solved question; the related work only discusses Multi-Task Learning, but there are eight continual learning scenarios (Wang et al., 2023) that should be considered (R1).
- The related work for Alzheimer’s and longitudinal studies is minimal and old (max 2013); classical recent approaches (e.g., Marinescu et al., 2019) and the TADPOLE challenge (Marinescu et al., 2018, with a leaderboard) are missing, which is a significant oversight (R1).
- No ablation for training dataset size is provided; the abstract claims the model improves as MRI data increases, but this could simply be an effect of having more data rather than a benefit of continual learning (R1).
- Ablations for model parameters (\(\lambda, \beta, \theta\)) and hyperparameters (\(\alpha, \eta\)) are missing (R1).
- Figures 4 and 5 do not show variability of the approaches (R1). Tables 2 and 3 lack statistical comparisons of model performances (R1).
- The dataset ADNI is not clearly introduced: what is the data split? How many samples are used? How many MRI scans are included? (R2, R4).
- It is not clear how the biomarkers (features) are generated for the model (R2). The proposed method is limited to structured biomarker data, and generalization to other formats is not shown (R2).
- The composition of M12…M60 is unclear: are they overlapping? (R2).
- It would be helpful to see performance of models trained for each task alone (single-task) rather than a single multi-task model (R2).

**Continual learning specifics**
- It is unclear whether all models (including baselines) have the same data available at each time point; otherwise, the continual update preserves history, which may be unfair. How are features prepared for baselines – are new time points treated as additional features or different samples? Are previous time-point scores used as input features? (R1)
- How does the algorithm scale computationally with the number of biomarkers? (R1)
- How many time points can the knowledge base preserve? Will performance degrade over time with respect to past data? (R1)

**Biomarker analysis**
- The biomarker correlation analysis (Section 5.2) lacks validation against established neuroimaging findings. Specifically, the hippocampus (usually damaged earliest in AD) is not identified as important, raising concerns about the biological plausibility of the identified correlations (R1). The manuscript should justify why certain correlations are significant and align with known neurodegeneration patterns (R1, R3 notes that more literature insights would be beneficial).
