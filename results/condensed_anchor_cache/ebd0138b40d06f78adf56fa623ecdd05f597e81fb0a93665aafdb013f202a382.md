## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper investigates using GPT-4 to generate scripted policies for contact-rich manipulation tasks by exposing an API with compliant motions, impedance control parameters, and stop conditions. The approach is validated on insertion and cable (un)routing tasks from FMB and NIST benchmarks, reporting >3× and 4× improvement over non-compliant baselines. Reviewer opinions are sharply divided: one reviewer (score 1, confidence 4) finds the paper weak and ad-hoc, arguing it fails to handle partial observability and lacks generality; the other three reviewers (scores 5, confidence 4–5) see promise, praising the action space design, real-robot validation, and ablation studies, but point out limited novelty, missing baselines, oversold claims, and insufficient experimental details.

### Strengths

- Addresses an important, underexplored problem: generating robot policy code for contact-rich and high-precision manipulation via LLMs [R1, R2, R4].
- Thoughtful design of the action space: using conditional compliant moves (impedance/admittance control) with variable stiffness and stop conditions enables LLMs to generate successful policies for contact-rich tasks [R2, R4].
- Ablation studies show the importance of predicting impedance values by comparing against position control and fixed-impedance baselines [R4].
- Real-robot validation on insertion and cable (un)routing tasks [R2, R3].
- Simple, easy-to-adopt approach using GPT-4; the trick of representing floating-point numbers as space-separated tokens is noted [R3].
- Clear articulation of the research question and experiments designed to answer it [R2].
- Writing is easy to follow [R3, R4].
- Empirical success rate improvements (up to 20% on peg-insertion, 70% on cable tasks over best baselines) are promising [R3].

### Weaknesses

- The method lacks a principled approach: it replaces search-based contact-rich planning with LLM-generated rules but does not address the inherent partial observability of contact formations and unobservable contact states; thus the approach appears ad-hoc and not general enough [R1].
- Limited novelty: the work is very similar to prior Code-as-Policy variants; the main contribution—allowing LLMs to generate impedance control parameters—is incremental [R3, R4].
- No comparison against alternative policies (e.g., imitation learning, RL, heuristic design) or discussion of why LLM policies are preferable [R2].
- Claims of “3× and 4× improvement” are oversold; the paper should frame improvements relative to the best alternative baseline rather than point-to-point [R3].
- Experimental validation is weak for claiming generalization: only two task families (insertion and cable (un)routing) are tested; many other contact-rich tasks from prior work (e.g., dishrack insertion, door opening, USB/plug insertion, screwing nuts on bolts) would demonstrate scaling ability [R2].
- The impact of hint inputs (e.g., “do a grid pattern search”) is not experimentally validated; these hints provide strong policy guidance [R2].
- The impact of perceptual noise or state estimation error is not examined; the paper assumes perception is solved [R2, R4].
- The scripted baseline may be unfairly weak: it is tuned to the easiest object (circle) and expected to generalize to star and half-pipe, while the LLM policy is given the object identity as a hint [R2].
- Few-shot examples in the appendix contain key solution snippets (e.g., “move up unless snag detected”) that directly guide the policy; with zero examples, LLM performance degrades significantly, especially on the routing task. Demonstrated generalizability would require examples that are not directly task-specific [R3].
- Continuous value prediction is not fully exploited: generated floats are drawn from a very small set (e.g., {0, 0.01, 0.02, 0.04, 0.001}); the continuous aspect is abstracted away by using `pose(1)` with relative transforms. The distribution of `pose(1)` distance to final target poses is not reported [R4].
- Section 5.1 (float tokenization) appears disconnected from the rest of the paper; the spacing trick is not visible in example prompts, and numbers do not utilize extended double-precision possibilities [R4].
- Not all prompt categories (especially Spatial Patterns) are used in the provided example prompt; the resolution of character-based patterns and representation of continuous orientations need clarification [R4].
- Missing experimental details: number of trials per condition for Tables 1 and 2 [R4]; optimization procedure for tuning model properties [R3]; number of shots used in few-shot experiments [R3]; evaluation protocol clarification for Section 5.2.3 [R2]; clarification of whether Figure 6 shows true history or LLM-generated points [R3].
- The paper does not clarify what decision variables are exposed to the LLM; is impedance the only parameter? How does the LLM tune impedance—through interaction with the system or a rule? [R1].
- Figure 8 suggests that while the type of errors changed with hints, the total number of errors remained constant; the paper does not explicitly state whether hint type affects success rate [R3].
