## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Half Fine-Tuning (HFT) for LLMs, which freezes half of the parameters during fine-tuning to mitigate catastrophic forgetting in sequential fine-tuning (SFT, DPO, continual learning). Experiments show that updating 50% of parameters can outperform full fine-tuning (FFT) and reduce training time by about 30%. The method is simple, easy to implement, and does not change model architecture.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-organized, easy to follow, and the method simple to implement. (R1, R3, R4, R5)
- The proposed method is simple and effective, and the forgetting problem it tackles is important. (R3, R5)
- Experiments are comprehensive, covering supervised fine-tuning, direct preference optimization (DPO), and continual learning settings. (R1, R2, R3)
- The method generalizes to any LLM and yields better or comparable results compared to full fine-tuning. (R2)
- Results demonstrate the effectiveness of updating a subset of parameters, with a 30% reduction in training time. (R1)

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient novelty and incremental contribution**: The design is trivial, as numerous prior works already explore partial optimization / parameter isolation for fine-tuning (e.g., AutoFreeze, SmartFRZ, BAdam, LISA). HFT appears to be a specific instance of freezing selection with a heuristic 50% threshold, and does not provide a robust framework for advancing parameter selection. (R1, R2, R4, R5)  *Note: One reviewer (R3, score 6) considers the method simple and effective, but the majority find it lacking novelty.*
- **Heuristic 50% selection**: There is no principled justification for updating exactly 50% of parameters; the choice is arbitrary. The paper lacks analysis on how parameters should be selected (e.g., by weight norm, gradient norm change, layer-wise parameter difference, or importance) and does not explore strategies for different layers, optimizer choices, or different selection ratios. (R1, R4, R5)
- **Missing ablation on layer depth and parameter importance**: The paper does not investigate the importance of each layer, how parameter selection should differ between SFT and DPO, or how selection performs across network depths. (R1, R4)
- **Lack of strong PEFT baseline comparisons**: HFT is not convincingly shown to be superior to established PEFT methods such as LoRA, Adapters, or BitFit. The claimed advantage (“allow LLMs to acquire new abilities while retaining prior knowledge”) is already inherent in all PEFT methods. (R4)
- **The “Half-Reset” technique is not novel**: The proposed “Half-Reset” is a specific case of selective parameter merging (merging at 0.5 ratio) and is already studied in the literature (e.g., “Mitigating Training Imbalance in LLM Fine-Tuning via Selective Parameter Merging”). The relevance and novelty are unclear. (R4)
- **Theoretical justification unconvincing**: The theoretical explanation on page 4 is not convincing and does not justify why HFT outperforms FFT. (R5)
- **Reason for outperforming FFT unclear**: Full fine-tuning could be considered an upper bound for downstream tasks; HFT’s improvement may simply be a trade-off between preserving pre-training knowledge and downstream performance, rather than genuinely addressing catastrophic forgetting. Many practitioners prioritize downstream abilities. (R5)
- **Continual learning setting is unrealistic**: The experimental setup trains only one sub-task at a time, which is a toy scenario with limited practical application. Effectiveness largely stems from small dataset sizes per sub-task (reducing overfitting) rather than truly solving catastrophic forgetting. (R2)
- **Experimental setup and baselines insufficient**:  
  - The TRACE benchmark task details are unclear (which tasks, whether task IDs are available for training, etc.); methods like LoraSeqFT exhibit high forgetting, raising concerns about the setting. (R4)  
  - Missing baselines: No comparisons with model averaging (a strong baseline for forgetting mitigation), non-continual learning baselines (e.g., pooling all data, training individual models per task and ensembling), or the continual learning method O-LoRA (Orthogonal Subspace Learning for Language Model Continual Learning). (R5, R3, R4)
- **Ease of implementation not distinct**: The claim that HFT is easy to implement because it does not change model architecture is not a differentiator; other PEFT methods (e.g., LoRA) are already available in easy-to-use APIs with minimal code. (R4)
- **Limited generalization demonstration**: Experiments should be extended to additional LLMs beyond those tested to show the method’s generalizability. (R3)
- **Unintuitive parameter selection strategy in Table 4**: Selecting 50% of parameters at the individual parameter level (instead of matrix-level, layer-level, or category-level) is unintuitive and may not precisely control the selection ratio. (R4)

*Reviewer disagreement*: Most reviewers (scores 3, 3, 3, 5) view the method as incremental and heuristic, while one reviewer (R3, score 6) finds it simple and effective. The higher-scoring reviewer also requested additional LLMs and comparison with O-LoRA, indicating room for improvement even in a positive assessment.
