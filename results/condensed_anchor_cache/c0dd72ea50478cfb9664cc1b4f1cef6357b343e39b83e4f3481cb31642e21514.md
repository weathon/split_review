## Merged Review

### Summary

Latent Weight Diffusion (LWD) learns a distribution over policy parameters (rather than trajectories) via a two-stage process: a VAE compresses demonstration trajectories into a latent space, a diffusion model learns the distribution of these latents, and a hypernetwork decodes the latent into closed-loop policy parameters. The method is applied to MetaWorld MT10, D4RL, and PushT. LWD builds directly on Hedge et al. 2023 and latent diffusion (Rombach et al.). It aims to produce smaller policy networks with fewer diffusion queries than trajectory-diffusion baselines. Reviewers disagreed on the paper’s novelty and sufficiency of experimental validation: one reviewer (Rating 3) found the method a direct application of prior work and the experiments lacking; three reviewers (Rating 5) considered the combination of latent diffusion with hypernetworks interesting but identified key missing baselines, ablations, and analyses.

### Strengths

- **Problem relevance and motivation**: The question of distilling a large multi-task policy into smaller ones for fine‑grained closed‑loop control is timely and well‑motivated (R1, R4). The application of latent diffusion to generate policy parameters is intuitive and conceptually promising (R1, R2).
- **Interesting combination of techniques**: Using latent diffusion (following Rombach et al.) with a hypernetwork (Ha et al.) to sample network parameters is novel within robotics (R2, R4). This enables fast online inference and diverse behavior generation while maintaining low latency (R4). The approach is a novel method for generating network parameters with diffusion (R3).
- **Encouraging experimental results**: LWD outperforms an undistilled multi‑task MLP in MetaWorld (Table 2) and shows better resilience to long‑action‑horizon settings in PushT (Figure 8) (R1, R2). Section 4.2 provides an insightful visualization of task difficulties/similarities and can help analyze task characteristics for imitation learning (R2). The experiments demonstrate that the distribution of generated policy parameters can capture multi‑modality (R3). As a proof‑of‑concept, LWD works better than a simple MLP in a multi‑task setting (R2).
- **Claimed benefits**: LWD aims for considerably smaller policy networks during inference and fewer diffusion model queries compared to trajectory diffusion (R3, R4).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited novelty and prior dependence**: The method is a direct application of Hedge et al. 2023 (latent diffusion for weight generation) to existing datasets, without architectural modifications (R1). This reduces the conceptual advance (R1). Related work citation is missing in Section 2.2 for the statement about learning smooth cost functions (R2).
- **Missing critical baselines**: 
  - Diffusion Policy (Chi et al.) is not compared in the main MetaWorld results (Table 2). The current comparison with a simple multi‑task MLP is insufficient to confirm the advantage of diffusion‑based generation. A proper comparison should use the same inputs (robot state + task indicator) and train on all tasks (R2, R3). No success‑rate comparison between LWD and Diffusion Policy is provided on any task, so the claims of smaller networks and fewer queries are unsupported (R3).
  - An ablation varying the LWD policy network size (e.g., adding a column with 370.4k parameters) is needed to understand the trade‑off between model size and performance (R2).
- **Unclear hypernetwork scalability**: 
  - The paper does not show results for decomposing a single long‑horizon task into subtasks where LWD generates a policy for each part. Dedicated experiments on this setup are necessary to strengthen the story (R1).
  - It is unclear how the hypernetwork handles very high‑dimensional policy parameters (e.g., millions) or pixel inputs (R1). No architecture details or appendix are provided for the hypernetwork decoder, especially how it handles the large output space (R1).
- **Insufficient analysis and missing ablations**:
  - Section 4.2 (latent space analysis) reports that the learned latent cannot differentiate trajectories well with shorter length, but the discussion is lacking. Statements like “Surprisingly, … faithfully behaving like their original policies” are not backed by experimental results (R1).
  - In Section 4.1, comparing foot‑contact distributions is not meaningful for generalization. A comparison of state variances or counting different success modes for manipulation tasks (e.g., pick & place, pushing) would be more insightful. The analysis is limited to locomotion (R2).
  - Table 2 is unclear: it does not specify whether the compared MLP predicts a single‑step action or a sequence. If single‑step, the comparison is unfair because LWD generates a sequence of actions (R3).
- **Robustness and stability concerns**: 
  - The paper does not address whether the generated policy can recover from states that deviate from the demonstration distribution without counterfactual reasoning or data on failed actions. The long‑horizon experiment might only show stability because the diffusion model’s limited capacity fails to capture the full high‑dimensional distribution (R4). No comparison under disturbances or perturbations is provided, making it hard to assess practical value (R4).
  - The term “closed‑loop” is used imprecisely: LWD regenerates a policy every \(H_a\) steps, but Diffusion Policy can also regenerate action sequences every \(K\) steps. Both are closed‑loop in the control sense (R3). The authors should clarify the distinction (R3).
- **Experiment presentation issues**:
  - Figure 8 shows only “Relative Performance Change” without the underlying success rates. The absolute success rates for both LWD and Diffusion Policy should be shown (R3, R1). The definition of “Relative Performance Change” is unclear (R1).
  - Table 2 does not clarify the action parameterization of LWD (is it not diffusion?) (R1).
  - Figure 3 would be clearer if merged into a single plot with smaller scatter points to show foot‑contact distribution overlap (R2). Other PCA dimensions beyond the two shown in Figure 5 are not reported (R1).
  - The paper exceeds the 10‑page limit by three lines; some derivations in Section 3 should be moved to the appendix (R1).
- **Missing implementation details**:
  - The number of diffusion steps typically needed to decode the policy network is not reported (R2).
  - In Section 3.1, the addition of normally distributed noise to the action (\(a_t = \pi(s_t,\theta) + e\)) is not justified (R1).
  - Symbols \(\tau\) and \(\varepsilon\) are not defined when first introduced in Sections 3.1 and 3.3 (R1).
- **Minority reviewer’s more critical assessment**: One reviewer (Rating 3, Soundness 2, Presentation 2, Contribution 2) found the paper’s novelty very limited, the reconstruction results mixed, and the hypernetwork scaling unaddressed. In contrast, the other three reviewers (Rating 5, Soundness 3, Presentation 2‑3, Contribution 2‑3) were more positive but still flagged missing critical baselines and analyses.
