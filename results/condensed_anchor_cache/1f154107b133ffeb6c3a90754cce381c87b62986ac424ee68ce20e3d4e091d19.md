## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a new attack, the "Certification Aware Attack," that uses a robustness certificate (randomized smoothing) to dynamically determine step sizes in an iterative gradient-based attack, aiming to find minimal-norm adversarial examples. The authors claim this paradoxically reduces security by exploiting the certification to craft more efficient attacks. Reviewers generally agree the idea of using certificates to guide attacks is novel and interesting, but there is strong disagreement about the paper's claims and framing. Reviewer 1 (score 1) finds the claims misleading and extreme, saying the paper does not break any theoretical guarantees and that the result is not a surprise; Reviewer 2 (score 6) sees a thought-provoking new setting; Reviewer 3 (score 5) finds the method valid but points to baseline issues; Reviewer 4 (score 3) finds significance unclear. The experimental scope (multiple certified defenses, datasets) is considered good by some, but several weaknesses around clarity, baseline selection, and computational overhead are raised.

### Strengths

1. **Novelty of the idea**: Using a certification algorithm to guide the search for adversarial examples is novel and not well explored in prior work (R1, R3, R2). The approach of determining step size dynamically via the certified radius makes sense and is interesting (R3).

2. **Experimental results**: The method is reported to find smaller adversarial examples (reduced median perturbation norm by >10% and <74% of the time) than comparable attacks, with improvement on models across datasets and defenses (R2, R3, R1). Some reviewers note the improvement in percentage for finding minimal adversarial examples is large (R3).

3. **Experimental scope**: The paper covers multiple certified defenses (Gaussian, MACER, IBP), datasets (CIFAR-10, ImageNet), and attack baselines (PGD, C&W, AutoAttack, DeepFool) (R4). R1 also notes the method demonstrates improvements in some metrics on some models.

### Weaknesses

1. **Misleading and confusing claims** (R1, R3, R4):
   - R1 strongly objects to the paper's framing that certification reduces security, saying it is "rather misleading" and "extreme." The paper does not break any theoretical security guarantees; in fact, strong certification helps quantify robustness, increasing security rather than reducing it. R1 states the result is not a surprise because certification and attack solve the same problem—a stronger certificate also yields a stronger attack. R1 further notes that the authors refused to change these claims after discussion.
   - R3 says using the word "paradox" is too strong; finding minimal adversarial examples using a certificate is not paradoxical.
   - R4 finds the significance unclear: the attack finds examples with radius larger than the certified radius, which is not a problem with certifications since they give no guarantees for larger radii. R4 suggests the issue is more about perturbation size thresholds for imperceptibility.

2. **Insufficient baseline comparison** (R1, R3):
   - For minimal-norm attacks, the primary baseline should be FAB (from AutoAttack), as it is explicitly designed for norm-minimization (R3). PGD and AutoAttack are typically used for fixed-norm budget, not for finding minimal norm; comparing them without proper hyperparameter tuning (e.g., binary search over perturbation norm) is unfair (R3). R1 also notes that attacks like C&W have a trade-off between success rate and norm, and the table reports only one number without showing this trade-off.
   - R1 points out that for the L2 norm, an improved version of PGD for L2 should be used as baseline, not the L-infinity variant.

3. **Computational overhead** (R3):
   - The claim that the proposed method is faster than PGD is suspicious because computing certificates is typically extremely expensive (order of seconds on large datasets). The proposed method is essentially PGD plus a certificate computation at every step, so it is unclear how total time can be lower. A clearer explanation of what the "Time" column captures is needed.

4. **Fair evaluation and number of queries** (R1):
   - The attack uses many samples to estimate the certificate. To make a fair comparison, baselines (PGD, AutoAttack, C&W) should be given the same number of model queries (e.g., via random restarts) so they can also become stronger.

5. **Clarity of metrics and presentation** (R1, R2, R4):
   - The metrics "%C" and "Best" are not clearly defined mathematically; %C is not defined in a formula (R1, R2, R4). R2 asks for a clear mathematical definition; R4 guesses %C is (distance to attack - certified radius)/certified radius and asks what "Best" means ("smallest attack proportion").
   - Figures lack visible legends and subplot subtitles; main observations are not easily extracted from long experimental text (R4). Bold best-performing in tables would help (R4). Some symbols (e.g., $B_P$ in Eq. 9) are unclear (R3).
   - R2 notes some symbols are undefined or hard to understand, hindering reproducibility.

6. **Practicality and threat model** (R2):
   - The scenario where an attacker has access to the certification mechanism and exploits it seems contrived. It is not clear how often this would occur in real-world applications. A more thorough discussion of realistic threat models is needed.

7. **Unexplained results in appendix and missing ablations** (R1, R3):
   - The proposed approach performs much worse on MACER models (attack success rate lower than PGD); why? How about other certified defenses like SmoothAdv? (R1)
   - Section 5.3 briefly mentions IBP models but does not use IBP bounds to guide attacks—could we search where IBP cannot certify? (R1)
   - In Figure 3, why do ImageNet results with PGD have weird steps while other baselines do not? (R1)
   - AutoAttack's FAB has parameters to trade off cost for norm-minimization (e.g., number of random restarts) that should be ablated (R3).
