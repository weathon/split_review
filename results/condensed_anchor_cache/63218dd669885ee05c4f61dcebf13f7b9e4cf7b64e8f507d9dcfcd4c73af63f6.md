## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an SO(3)-equivariant input representation for Vector Neuron (VN) that maps a 3D point to a high-dimensional feature space via a frequency-based lifting. A rotation operator is constructed by selecting a 3D subspace of SO(n) determined by Lie algebra elements. Experimental validation spans point-cloud completion, shape compression, normal estimation, registration, classification, and part segmentation.

### Strengths
- **Novel high-dimensional equivariant feature space.** The approach maps 3D points to a high-dim subspace of SO(n) while preserving equivariance, overcoming VN’s original limitation of using only three-dimensional features (R1, R4, R5). It is intentionally high-dimensional but uses only a subspace, differing from prior Fourier-like representations (R1).
- **Theoretical support.** Mathematical proofs and theoretical justification are provided for the construction and equivariance property (R2, R4). The transformation function D (Equation 1) is described as reasonable and provable (R4).
- **Multi-frequency design improves detail preservation.** The representation captures multiple frequencies, enabling VN to recover finer details (e.g., vehicle side mirror) and bridge the reconstruction gap with the non-equivariant baseline occNet (R5). Qualitative and quantitative results demonstrate effectiveness across tasks (R2, R3, R5).
- **Comprehensive experimental validation.** The method is evaluated on multiple downstream tasks: point-cloud completion, 3D shape compression, normal estimation, point cloud registration, classification, and part segmentation (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5). It performs on par with or better than most baselines (R1, R3, R5).
- **Strong empirical results.** Superior performance to VN is shown in all tasks; the method achieves competitive results compared to existing work (R3, R5). Both visualization and benchmarks confirm the effectiveness (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of analysis on the choice of dimension *n*.** The impact of *n* on performance and computational cost is not investigated. No ablation studies are conducted for this critical hyperparameter (R1, R2). Similarly, no per-frequency analysis is provided (e.g., which frequencies capture global shape vs. fine details) (R4).
- **Proof of Proposition 1 is missing.** The paper refers to the proof of Theorem 1, but Theorem 1 does not sufficiently cover Proposition 1 (R1). The soundness of the proof is therefore incomplete.
- **Presentation and clarity issues are significant (contradicts R2’s positive view of writing).** The paper is hard to follow for readers not deeply familiar with the Lie bracket; these concepts are introduced without adequate motivation (R1). The motivation for the method and the limitations of VN are insufficiently highlighted (R5). Notation is inconsistent: $\hat{u}$ and $\vec{u}$ are used interchangeably in Section 3 (R1, R4). Typographical errors include “Theorem 1. theoremIf” (R1, R5), and the phrase “so that it is equivariant” in Section 2.2 requires clarification (R4).
- **Missing recent comparisons and related work.** Baselines are mostly pre-2022; more recent comparisons from 2023 are missing (R3, R5). Important follow-up works to VN that improve detail capture via local partitioning (e.g., conv-occnet, and R5’s references [1–4]) are not discussed or compared (R5). This undercuts the claim that the 3-dim feature is the root cause of detail loss—occNet with *N*-dim features already preserves details (R5). The paper should clarify how the proposed lifting compares with tensor product networks (e.g., Clebsch–Gordan nets) in expressiveness (R5).
- **Scalability to larger scenes is not demonstrated.** All experiments are on small objects. It is unclear whether the method scales to room-level datasets (ScanNet, S3DIS) or urban-level (SensatUrban), and whether computational cost would be acceptable (R4). Extension to indoor scenes was not tested (R2).
- **No analysis of component alone.** The method is always combined with VN; whether it can work as a standalone equivariant representation is not addressed (R4).
- **Unclear ablation of sampled points in completion.** The completion experiment samples 300 points for evaluation; the effect of varying this number is not studied (R3).
- **Classification/segmentation performance not obviously better than existing work.** On classification and part segmentation, the method shows no clear gain over PaRINet; the reason is not discussed (R3).
- **Compression experiment is poorly described.** The compression ratio is not reported, and the experiment appears to measure reconstruction on the training set rather than true compression (R5). Also, why results for part segmentation omit axis *z/z* is unclear (R5).
- **Specific technical details are missing.** The map *R^z(û)* is not clearly defined: is it the 2D rotation about *z × û* that aligns the z-axis with û? (R1). The axis in the axis-angle representation is arbitrarily selected—why? (R1). Algorithm 2’s `createsearchspace` is not defined in the text (R1). The claim that the lifting replaces principal axes in R^3 with a 3D subspace in R^N: why is that helpful? (R5).
- **Minor inaccuracies.** The sentence “Equivariant neural networks change the output accordingly when the point cloud input is rotated without additional training” is imprecise; equivariant networks are more general (R5). The critique of spherical harmonics as solely from quantum mechanics is incorrect; they predate quantum mechanics and are analogous to Fourier series, which is a valid approach (R1).
