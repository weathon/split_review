## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper critiques Average Certified Radius (ACR) as a metric for randomized smoothing (RS). Theoretically, it shows a trivial classifier can achieve arbitrarily large ACR with a sufficient certification budget, and that ACR is much more sensitive to improvements on easy samples than on hard ones. Empirically, it demonstrates that existing training strategies that improve ACR reduce robustness on hard samples. The authors propose training modifications (discarding hard samples, reweighting by certified radius, adaptive adversarial attacks) that achieve state-of-the-art ACR by focusing only on easy samples, further exposing the metric’s flaw. They advocate for using certified accuracy at various radii instead. The paper is technically sound with well-designed experiments covering many training-based RS methods. Reviewers have differing views on the work’s significance and novelty: one reviewer (R3, score 6, confidence 5) finds high originality and significance; another (R1, score 3, confidence 5) sees limited impact and obviousness; a third (R2, score 5, confidence 4) notes limited novelty due to related work and unnatural logic.

### Strengths
- Reveals a critical flaw in ACR, a widely used metric in randomized smoothing, which has been largely unchallenged (R3). This rectifies past evaluation errors and promotes more scientific protocols (R1). Two reviewers (R1, R3) emphasize the paper’s originality and significance for future research; R2 considers it helpful but notes limited novelty.
- The theoretical analysis is technically sound and well-supported by empirical results (R1, R3). Experiments cover many training-based RS strategies with appropriate baselines and metrics (R3). Writing is clear (R1).
- The paper demonstrates that ACR can be artificially inflated by focusing on easy samples, and provides concrete strategies (discarding hard samples, reweighing, adaptive attacks) that achieve state-of-the-art ACR while ignoring general robustness (R2, R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and obviousness**: The unboundedness of ACR for a constant classifier is relatively obvious (R1). The sensitivity with respect to \(p_A\) is a direct consequence of \(\Phi^{-1}(\cdot)\)’s bounded domain and unbounded value domain (R1). Furthermore, a closely related work [1] shows that certified accuracy is closely related to sample size; Figure 4(a) in [1] demonstrates that as \(p_A \to 1.0\), certified radius significantly increases with sample size. The paper fails to reference [1] (R2). This reduces the novelty of the theoretical contribution.
- **Unnatural logic**: After proving ACR is a poor metric, the paper does not propose a better metric but instead introduces a training algorithm that exploits ACR’s weakness by giving more weight to easy samples (R2). A more logical next step would be to design a more reasonable metric (R2).
- **Limited impact**: ACR is a rather specific metric that mainly exists in the randomized smoothing literature, and it is used in conjunction with certified accuracy under various radii; the community is not overly focused on ACR yet (R1). (Contrast: R3 considers ACR a widely accepted and important metric.)
- **Scope limited to training-based RS methods**: It is unclear whether the findings are specific to training-based RS methods or RS in general (R3). The theoretical analysis is not specific to training-based methods, but the evaluation mainly critiques ACR for training-based RS methods and does not extend or discuss the impact to other RS methods (e.g., pre-trained models with added noise for certification) (R3). This leaves a gap in understanding the generalizability of the critique (R3). The paper would benefit from broader contextualization, including diffusion-purification methods such as “(Certified!!) Adversarial Robustness for Free!”, DensePure, and DiffSmooth, and ensemble-based training such as Horvath et al (boosting) and Yang et al (R1).
- **Lack of practical guidance for the proposed alternative metric**: The paper proposes certified accuracy at various radii as an alternative, but does not provide detailed guidance on how to implement it in practice (R3). Practitioners would need concrete steps or case studies on choosing appropriate radii and balancing performance across easy and hard samples (R3). Additionally, certified accuracy at various radii is more commonly used to evaluate other RS methods, which further limits the paper’s scope to training-based RS (R3). [Reviewer 2 also requested a more detailed discussion on designing a more effective comparison metric (R2).]
- **Computational cost of the proposed training algorithm**: The proposed training algorithm requires using Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) to compute adversarial examples, which is computationally expensive (R2). Methods like MACER and consistency choose attack-free training algorithms to mitigate computation costs (R2). [Reviewer 2 requested a comparison regarding computational overhead (R2).]
- **Minor**: The implication of Figure 1 on page 1 is not clear (R1).
