## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces DiffForce, an extension of DiffAb (Luo et al. 2022) that integrates a physics-based force field into the reverse diffusion sampling process for antigen-conditioned antibody CDR design. The method builds on guidance from Komorowska et al. (2024) but claims a novel way to predict final residue identities and orientations during sampling. Benchmarks against DiffAb and RAbD show improved estimated binding free energy, amino acid recovery, and RMSD. However, the evaluation is considered limited and not fully convincing by several reviewers.

### Strengths
- Important problem: structure-based design of antibody CDRs for binding has clear implications for drug discovery.
- Core idea of incorporating a differentiable force field into diffusion sampling is promising and could reduce physically implausible outputs (clashes, unstable structures).
- The paper is clear and easy to follow.
- Improved energy scores and marginally better sequence recovery over DiffAb demonstrate the force field’s influence.
- The theory behind the force guidance is well-motivated if the residue identity and orientation predictions are reliable.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited benchmarking**: Only DiffAb (a very similar model without guidance) and RAbD (a physics-based method) are compared. Missing comparisons with recent ML methods such as dyMEAN, HERN, RFdiffusion, and IgDiff. Table 1 lacks standard deviations, making it hard to assess statistical significance.
- **Not fully convincing despite claimed improvements**: The main result (lower estimated binding free energy) is expected because the force field explicitly minimises energy. It is not surprising that energy decreases. Additional orthogonal benchmarks are needed: packing scores (Rosetta), hydrophobicity, charge distributions, repeated sequence patterns, or likelihood from a language model. Individual structure examples in Figs 3 and 5 are anecdotal; aggregate plots should be provided.
- **Novelty concerns**: The theoretical derivations (Eqs 2–9) largely replicate the diffusion guidance framework from Komorowska et al. (2024), but the paper claims “We have derived an approach…”. It is difficult to tell which parts are genuinely novel. The method is seen as a straightforward application of diffusion guidance.
- **Potential data leakage in residue/orientation prediction**: Equations 12 and 14 appear to condition on information that should not be available at inference time (e.g., estimating sj0 conditioned on sj0, or accessing Rs for s < t without a separate rollout). This requires clarification. If the model uses future timestep information, it would have an unfair advantage.
- **Force normalisation may be inappropriate**: Normalising forces could prevent the system from exiting stable equilibria where the true force magnitude is small.
- **Generalisation not tested**: A key motivation for using physics-based guidance is better generalisation to out-of-distribution interfaces, but no experiments test this.
- **Hyperparameter and timing analysis insufficient**: Two force coefficients and the timing of force application (70% into sampling) are introduced but not thoroughly discussed. Only a brief mention appears in Appendix G. How would results in Table 1 change with different coefficient values or earlier/later force onset?
- **Guidance target mismatch**: The force field optimises total stability/energy, not specifically binding affinity. The paper does not comment on total energy improvements or whether this reduces the need for post-processing relaxation.
- **Missing implementation details**: The type of force field used and the speed of energy calculation are not specified.
- **Minor issues**: 
  - In Eq 2, the claim “no explicit notion of y” – is y not simply the conditioning variable C?
  - Section 4.3.2 mentions “particularly noticeable at earlier timesteps” – is T=100? What do timesteps 20–30 look like?
  - The ethical motivation citing animal testing is incomplete; phage display is a common alternative.
- **Reviewer disagreement**: Two reviewers rated the paper 5 (positive, though with reservations), while two rated 3 (recommending reject), citing limited novelty, weak evaluation, and data leakage concerns. The lower scores reflect significant doubts about the soundness of the central prediction module.
