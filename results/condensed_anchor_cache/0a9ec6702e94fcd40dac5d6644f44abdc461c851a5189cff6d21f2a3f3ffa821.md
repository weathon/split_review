## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes KidSat, a dataset pairing satellite imagery (Sentinel-2 and Landsat) with DHS survey data on multidimensional child poverty (UNICEF six dimensions: housing, sanitation, water, nutrition, education, health) across 16 Eastern and Southern African countries (1997–2022). The authors benchmark several feature representations—MOSAIKS, DINOv2, SatMAE—plus two no‑imagery baselines (Gaussian Process Regression, Mean Regression) on spatial and temporal generalization tasks. Reviewers 1 and 3 rated 3 (overall acceptable with weaknesses), while Reviewers 2 and 4 rated 1 (clear rejection). The minority negative reviewers stress fundamental methodological and framing concerns: the problem may not be well‑suited for a satellite ML benchmark, the gains over no‑imagery baselines are marginal, and the paper lacks in‑depth investigation of why certain pre‑training strategies work (or do not) for poverty mapping.

### Strengths

- **Important, real‑world problem with an extensive dataset focused on the Global South.** The dataset covers 33,608 images from 16 countries, pairing satellite imagery with child‑specific poverty indicators from DHS surveys (housing, water, sanitation, nutrition, education, health). Most existing remote sensing benchmarks target the Global North. [R1, R2, R3, R4]
- **Multi‑dimensional child poverty focus.** Unlike general economic metrics, the target follows UNICEF’s definition, providing a nuanced indicator relevant to policy and social science. [R1]
- **Methodical and reproducible pipeline.** The authors provide open‑source code for dataset construction, ground‑truth extraction, and model comparison, including fine‑tuning, cross‑validation, and spatial/temporal benchmarks. [R1]
- **Clear structure and useful visual aids.** The paper is well organized, with heatmaps of cluster locations and summary tables that facilitate comparison of model performance. [R1]
- **Two simple no‑imagery baselines (GPR, Mean Regression) anchor performance.** These help gauge the lower bound of what satellite‑based methods add. [R3, R4]
- **Applicability for data‑informed policy and future adaptation.** The benchmark can be extended to other social/environmental indicators. [R1]

### Weaknesses

1. **Unclear whether satellite imagery is informative enough for this task (severe reviewer disagreement).** Two reviewers (R2, score 1; R4, score 1) argue that the six poverty dimensions are not directly observable from optical satellite imagery (especially at Landsat/Sentinel‑2 resolution). The paper itself states “satellite imagery is *at best* a proxy” and the results in Table 2 show only marginal improvements over the no‑imagery baselines. R4 questions the risks of drawing conclusions from such a weak proxy and whether this problem should be formulated as a satellite ML benchmark at all. The more positive reviewers (R1, R3) acknowledge the difficulty but see value in the dataset. [R2, R4, also noted by R3: “none of the factors … are directly measurable from optical satellite imagery”]

2. **Incomplete description of satellite data inputs.** Key details are missing: processing level, selected spectral bands, spatial resolution of the imagery used as model input. The abstract mentions only “images, each 10 km × 10 km.” For Landsat, images come from Landsats 5, 7, 8 with known sensor differences; the paper does not state whether a harmonized product was used. The Sentinel‑2 archive starts in 2015/2016, but survey data go back to 1997, so less data may be used in Sentinel‑2 experiments—this discrepancy is not discussed. [R3, R4]

3. **Limited exploitation of multispectral data.** All experiments use only RGB bands (to “directly compare” DINO and SatMAE), discarding the rich multispectral information in Sentinel‑2 (e.g., vegetation indices, water body detection). Models specifically designed for satellite imagery (e.g., SatMAE) could benefit from the full spectral range. R4 notes this decision is insufficiently justified, as the methods are not directly comparable anyway (different temporal benchmark setups). [R1, R4; related: R3 notes missing spectral details]

4. **Temporal benchmark weaknesses.**
   - The temporal split (training on 1997–2020, testing on 2020–2022) shows poor generalization; models may overfit historical patterns. No temporal encoding or time‑series models were attempted. [R1]
   - SatMAE’s “temporal” model is used but receives no temporal information—only a single timestep input (replicated). This makes the comparison invalid and the reason for using that variant unclear. [R4]

5. **Spatial benchmark may not test true generalization.** The 5‑fold random split likely overlaps geographically, overlooking spatial autocorrelation. A region‑based split (e.g., by administrative boundaries or ecological zones) would be a stronger test. The strong performance of the Gaussian Process baseline suggests spatial structure may be captured trivially. [R3, citing Rolf et al., 2024]

6. **Missing key comparisons and ablations.**
   - No experiments with multispectral inputs (Sentinel‑2 full bands) or with nighttime lights (VIIRS, SDGSAT‑1), a standard proxy for poverty. [R1, R3]
   - No simple pixel‑based or non‑spatial satellite baselines (e.g., random forest on pixel values) to test whether spatial structure is necessary for the modest gains. [R4]
   - No comparison with other recent architectures (CNNs, transformer alternatives optimized for spatial analysis) or with models pre‑trained specifically on remote sensing tasks. [R1, R2]
   - No use of building‑footprint data (e.g., Open Buildings 2.5D Temporal Dataset) as an alternative input. [R3]
   - Methodological depth lacking: why do MAE‑style features (SatMAE) differ from joint‑embedding features (DINO) for poverty mapping? Do findings align with prior work (e.g., Shekhar et al., 2023)? [R2]

7. **No uncertainty estimation.** Predictions are deterministic; policymakers need confidence intervals or probabilistic measures, especially in data‑sparse regions. [R1]

8. **Insufficient validation against other poverty metrics.** The paper relies solely on DHS survey data as ground truth and does not cross‑reference with other surveys, censuses, or economic indicators. [R1]

9. **Fixed input size (224×224) may lose spatial detail.** Resizing high‑resolution satellite images can discard fine‑grained information. Adaptive or patch‑based processing should be considered. [R1]

10. **Visualizations inadequate for assessing spatial patterns.** The current maps cover extensive areas; examples over known deprived urban neighborhoods (e.g., Maputo, Mozambique) comparing model outputs to ground truth would better illustrate performance. [R3]

11. **Paper is overly verbose with redundant description; space could be better used for informative experiments.** [R4]

12. **Results not disaggregated by subgroup.** Performance is averaged across the whole test set; reporting by country or by the deprivation‑severity bins shown in Figure A.4 would give stakeholders more actionable insights. [R4]
