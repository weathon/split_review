## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces the concept of supermodular/submodular rank for set functions defined on a lattice, measuring how far a function is from being supermodular/submodular. The rank is the smallest number of terms needed to decompose a function into a sum of supermodular/submodular functions under different total orders on the variables. Based on this, the paper proposes R-SPLIT and R-SPLIT RATIO algorithms for monotone set function maximization with matroid constraints and for set function ratio minimization. These algorithms split a function of rank r+1 into 2^r pieces and run a simple algorithm (e.g., greedy) on each piece, returning the best solution. The paper provides theoretical approximation guarantees when the submodular rank is bounded and includes empirical evaluation.

### Strengths
- Novel definition of supermodular/submodular rank and elementary rank, providing a new way to grade the space of set functions. (Reviewers 1,2,3,4)
- The high-level idea is clear: decompose a function into a sum of π-supermodular/submodular functions and split the problem into submodular pieces, improving approximation when the rank is bounded. The technical details are involved and there is no such definition/decomposition in the literature. (Reviewer 3)
- The paper considers two classical models: matroid-constrained maximization and set function ratio minimization, extending previous results to a more general function class. (Reviewer 4)
- R-SPLIT and R-SPLIT RATIO offer a trade-off between computational cost and accuracy with theoretical guarantees. (Reviewer 1)
- Both theoretical analyses and experimental evaluations are provided. (Reviewers 2,4)

### Weaknesses
- **Severe presentation issues**: The paper is complex and notation-heavy, with a confusing structure. Background knowledge is moved to the appendix, making it hard to get the definition of π-supermodular. Key definitions (e.g., R(α,γ) in Theorem 25) are missing. Formal definitions of the considered models are not given until Section 4, and the connection between definitions and optimization problems is unclear until later. Intuitive explanations and motivating examples for the elementary submodular rank are lacking. (Reviewers 1,2,3,4)
- **Weak theoretical contribution**: The elementary rank essentially reframes the problem of identifying a subset of variables that, when fixed, make the function submodular; it does not offer a computationally tractable method for finding this subset. R-SPLIT performs a brute-force search over 2^r variable subsets. This makes the contribution incremental—it quantifies non-submodularity in terms of the size of the variable subset to be fixed but provides no efficient way to handle it. (Reviewer 2, with high confidence; Reviewer 3 also notes the practical limitation)
- **Practical limitations not discussed**: The improvement in approximation ratio (Table 2) occurs only when the elementary submodular rank is a constant. The paper does not discuss whether there exist important functions with constant rank, nor does it provide a way to compute the rank of a function for a given problem. When r is high, the algorithms become impractical. (Reviewers 2,3)
- **Comparison to existing solutions is unclear**: It is not obvious from Table 2 how the proposed algorithm outperforms existing solutions; the benefit of the computational overhead is not elaborated. (Reviewer 1)
- **Empirical evaluation is not convincing**. (Reviewer 2)
- **Minority contrast**: Reviewer 2 (score 3, confidence 5 – “absolutely certain”) is much more negative than the other reviewers (scores 5–6, confidence 2–3), stating firmly that the contribution does not meet the bar of top-tier conferences (ICLR). In contrast, Reviewers 1,3,4 rate the contribution as “good” (3/4) and find the core ideas interesting. This disagreement is most pronounced on the theoretical impact and presentation clarity.
