## Merged Review

### Summary
CG-Bench is a clue-grounded question answering benchmark for long video understanding (10–60 min). It comprises 1,219 manually curated videos, 12,129 QA pairs (perception, reasoning, hallucination), and a three-tier category system (14 primary, 171 secondary, 638 tertiary). The benchmark introduces two clue-based credibility evaluation methods: white-box (models must identify temporal intervals supporting the answer) and black-box (comparing accuracy with full video vs. only the clue interval). Evaluation of over 20 open-source and closed-source MLLMs reveals a large gap between model performance and human performance, exposing limitations of current models in long video understanding.

### Strengths
- The clue-grounded QA setup and the two clue-based credibility metrics (white-box, black-box) provide a novel, finer-grained evaluation beyond simple MCQ accuracy, addressing a key gap in long video evaluation (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5).
- The dataset is fully manually annotated from scratch, with 1,219 long videos, >12K QA pairs, and a detailed three-tier tagging system – making it the largest long video understanding benchmark to date (R1, R2, R4).
- Video lengths (10–60 min, mode ~20 min) are longer than typical benchmarks, and the variety of video types and question types is good (R4).
- The evaluation includes >20 current MLLMs, providing a comprehensive reflection of SotA; ablations (Table 4, Figure 7) yield interesting insights, such as performance improving with more frames and the surprising finding that audio does not help much (the dataset is not ASR-biased) (R3, R4).
- The benchmark reveals a large gap between model performance and human performance, underscoring significant room for innovation (R4).
- The paper is clearly written and easy to follow (R1, R2, R4).
- The clue-based credibility evaluation (CRR, long-acc, clue-acc) smartly uses temporal information without requiring explicit grounding predictions (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Dataset collection and pre-training overlap:** The paper claims videos are manually collected from the Internet to avoid pre-training overlap, but no details are given on how this is guaranteed (e.g., by license). Since videos come from the Internet, it is difficult to ensure they are excluded from MLLM pre-training (R1, R2). Video sources, search queries, filtering criteria, resolution distribution, and the distribution of video types are not disclosed, which is critical for interpreting the benchmark’s applicability (R2, R4). License issues for releasing the videos are not addressed (R3). For example, the presence of specific video styles (fast cuts, color grading) could significantly influence performance (R4).
- **Insufficient frame sampling and missing duration analysis:** Evaluations use at most 32 or 128 frames, which is far from sufficient for long videos (R1). Ablations show performance keeps improving with more frames, but cost may be a constraint (R4). A human evaluation under the same frame constraints (e.g., 16/32 frames) would help gauge dataset difficulty (R1). A finer-grained analysis grouping videos/questions by duration (e.g., 10 min, 10–20 min) to quantify the undersampling issue for longer videos is missing (R1).
- **Limitations of proposed metrics:**
  * The white-box evaluation is essentially an extension of video temporal grounding and is not novel (R1). The acc@IoU with τ=0 degenerates to MCQ accuracy; a naive solution of predicting the full video span as the clue would always satisfy the overlap condition, making the temporal aspect meaningless (R1).
  * mIoU scores are very low (<9% for all models, human only 35.5%), raising concerns about the reliability of temporal interval annotations (R3). A model may only need a few frames within the interval; recall could be a better metric (R3). The compliance rate with the required output format for temporal grounding is not reported, which could explain low mIoUs (R3).
  * mIoU scores in Table 3 appear >1, contradicting Eq. 1 (should be ≤1) (R4).
  * The black-box evaluation’s assumption that “long-acc. ≥ clue-acc.” is contradicted by results (clue-acc always exceeds long-acc), suggesting that CRR actually measures robustness to context dilution (models degrade with more irrelevant context) rather than clue-seeking ability (R4). This paragraph should be rephrased accordingly (R4). R2 also finds it counter-intuitive that full video should yield higher accuracy than clue interval, given the focused information in the clue.
  * White-box credibility is problematic when timestamps are unavailable to the model; in the F (frames only) setting, accuracy is on par or slightly higher than F+FT, but white-box scores are significantly lower, requiring explanation (R2).
  * The claim that GPT-4o is a “low-hallucination MLLM” (l.323) lacks literature support (R4).
- **Missing comparisons with existing QA+grounding works:** Table 2 and Table 3 should include and discuss existing QA+grounding datasets and methods (EgoTimeQA, MultiHop-EgoQA, ReXTime, E.T. Bench), which are specifically designed for settings similar to clue-grounded QA (R5).
- **Limited examples and insufficient description of hallucination questions:** Only three examples are provided; more examples, especially of hallucination questions, would help assess benchmark quality and difficulty (R4). The hallucination question type is not described in enough detail (R4).
- **Annotation and language details:**
  * Annotators use their native language to minimize expression loss; are questions then translated to English? If not, what languages are represented and what is their distribution? (R4).
  * Do annotators have access to audio and/or subtitles? If yes, what measures ensure answers are grounded in visual content? (R4). Are subtitles human-uploaded or machine-transcribed, and are they all in English? (R2).
  * The “small model & sparse frame check” quality-control step should be explained: what kind of questions were filtered? (R2).
- **Other clarifications and open questions:**
  * Fig. 5: clue coverage increases almost linearly with time bin index – is there a reason clues are more likely to be at the video end? (R4).
  * Tab. 5: what is the unit for bias? What does a score of 1.0 mean? How are trigger rate and recall rate defined? (R4).
  * Fig. 7d: GPT-4o’s CRR decreases first and stays below other models until 128 frames; any explanation? (R4).
  * l.458: what is the meaning of “refusal rate”? (R4).
  * l.464: when evaluators are provided only with GT and visual information, bias increases; does “fully leveraging visual information” refer to the “GT+Vis” columns? (R4).
  * l.214: what are “option probabilities”? (R4).
  * Minor: statement l.107 is not entirely fair given other long video benchmarks like Video-MME; method/dataset names should appear with citations; numerical columns in tables should be right-aligned for easier comparison (R4).
  * The definition of ‘clue’ (temporal boundaries) should be stated clearly in the main paper (R5).
