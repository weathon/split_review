## Merged Review

### Summary
Scores range from 3 to 6 (Reviewer 1: 6, confidence 4; Reviewers 2 and 3: 5, confidence 3; Reviewer 4: 3, confidence 4). Reviewers generally agree that the motivation (addressing OOD actions in zero-shot offline RL with Forward–Backward representations) is important and that the experimental evaluation is substantial. However, they raise concerns about mathematical clarity, the justification of the MC‑FB variant, novelty, baseline selection, aggregation of results, and the use of the term “world model.” There is notable disagreement: Reviewer 1 is positive about the method’s simplicity and finds it a strength, while Reviewers 3 and 4 consider the lack of novelty a weakness.

### Strengths
- Addressing OOD actions in offline multi‑task RL is important (Reviewer 1).
- Good experimental evaluation with a sufficient benchmark suite and a didactic toy example (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- Clear motivation and well‑structured paper; language is easy to follow (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- Method is simple (CQL applied to FB) and easy to understand (Reviewer 1).
- Good contextualization with prior work; novelty is clearly delineated (Reviewer 2).
- Limitations section is beneficial (Reviewer 2).
- Convincing experimental evidence that conservatism is not harmful on high‑quality datasets (Reviewer 2, Q3).
- Originality in the weighting approach for offline RL (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Mathematical clarity**:
  - Equation 5: the `z` variable does not appear as an argument to Q, inconsistent with task‑conditioned Q‑functions; connection between eq5 and eq6 is unclear. In eq6 the forward representation is defined as `<F(…,z), z>`, which does not match the Q‑value definition in eq5. (Reviewer 1)
  - In MC‑FB, `z` is sampled independently from `s^+`; the backward representation becomes independent of the task. How is this valid? In which cases does `z` correspond to the backward representation? (Reviewer 1)
  - Forward model function should be `F: S × A × Z → ℝ^d`? Do tasks and policies share the same embedding space? Notation `π_z: z ∼ Z` is ambiguous. (Reviewer 2)
  - Task distribution `Z` is not well‑defined. For the Cheetah environment, how is it defined in practice? (Reviewer 2, Q1)
  - References for the derivations in Section 2 are only given at the end; they would help the reader if placed earlier. (Reviewer 2)
  - Actor‑critic formulation is only mentioned briefly but is crucial for experiments. (Reviewer 2)
  - Minor: Equation 1 is not an expectation without an assumption on the distribution; “the successor measure satisfies a Bellman equation” needs a citation; “`s_+` is sampled independently from `(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1})`” should be “from the dataset”; “stochastically dominate” is unclear; abbreviations VC‑FB and MC‑FB are used before being defined; broken English before eq6; Figure 1’s caption should state the data is from an actual experiment. (Reviewers 1, 2)
- **Novelty and baselines**:
  - The approach is a straightforward adaptation of the CQL conservative term to zero‑shot RL; the domain gap is moderate, so novelty is limited. The core mechanism is largely unchanged from offline RL. (Reviewer 4; also Reviewer 3: essentially a reweighting strategy, with similar strategies already proposed and not compared against.)
  - Baselines are insufficient: only CQL and TD3 are used; no comparison to other zero‑shot RL methods from the FB paper (e.g., Successor Features, LAP‑RL, DeepMellow) or more recent offline RL algorithms such as Implicit Q‑Learning. (Reviewers 3, 4; Reviewer 2 also suggests IQL as a more stable conservative baseline.)
  - Evaluation setup is unfair: two variants (VC‑FB, MC‑FB) are compared against a single baseline (FB), giving twice the chance to outperform; moreover, both variants receive 3× the training duration of FB. (Reviewer 3)
- **Evaluation and results**:
  - Results are aggregated across non‑comparable domains; summed values and percentage improvements are inappropriate and can be misleading (e.g., a 1000% improvement means little if neither method is near solving the task). (Reviewer 3; Reviewer 2 also cautions about inflated percentages.)
  - Claims do not match the results: improvement over FB is inconsistent, and in the Random case the two variants are essentially identical to FB. The claim that conservatism would help most on poorer‑quality data is not observed. (Reviewer 3)
  - The justification for MC‑FB is not supported by the experiments: Figure 4 shows MC‑FB often underperforms VC‑FB, contradicting the motivation. This is only addressed in the discussion with intuition, not evidence. (Reviewer 1)
  - The difference between VC‑FB and MC‑FB is not explicitly analyzed in the experiments. (Reviewer 2)
  - Evaluation metrics are not fully specified; “Task Score” in Figure 4 is not defined. (Reviewer 2)
  - Failure cases: on Walker2D with the RND dataset, MC‑FB completely fails; on random Jaco there is a large gap to CQL. (Reviewer 1)
  - CQL may not be representative of the best conservative algorithm; newer methods (e.g., IQL) are more stable and should be compared. (Reviewer 2)
  - It is counterintuitive that conservatism works better on higher‑quality data; the paper does not explain why. (Reviewer 2, Q5)
  - Why use a max operator (as in CQL) instead of sampling from the policy distribution? An ablation would help. (Reviewer 2, Q2)
- **Terminology**:
  - The term “world model” is a mischaracterization; the referenced FB paper (arXiv:2209.14935) states that FB lies between model‑free and model‑based and is not a world model. (Reviewers 3, 4)
- **Disagreement on simplicity/novelty**:
  - Reviewer 1 considered the method’s simplicity a strength, while Reviewers 3 and 4 argued that the adaptation is straightforward and lacks novelty. This contrast is noted.
