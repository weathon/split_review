## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces SimXRD, a large simulated powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) dataset containing 4,065,346 patterns from 119,569 unique crystal structures under 33 simulated conditions that reflect real-world variations. The simulations are generated using PySimXRD, a custom software that incorporates comprehensive physical interactions (grain size, stress, temperature, orientation, background, drift, noise). The authors benchmark 21 sequence models (CNNs, RNNs, Transformers) for crystalline symmetry classification in in-library and out-of-library scenarios, and test generalization to real experimental data (RRUFF). Key findings: models struggle with low-frequency crystals, especially out-of-library; class imbalance is challenging; models trained on SimXRD can generalize to real data. Reviewers’ scores range from 5 to 8, reflecting a split: one reviewer (score 8) finds the dataset and simulator highly valuable and timely; another (score 5) questions the clarity and ML contribution; the other two (score 6 each) see potential but note substantial limitations.

### Strengths
- Largest open-source simulated PXRD dataset to date (4M+ patterns, 119K structures), with validation against experimental data (RRUFF). (Reviewers 1, 3, 4)
- Novel simulation method (PySimXRD) that models a wide variety of realistic settings (grain size, stress, temperature, orientation, background, drift, noise) not offered in other packages (e.g., Pymatgen). This is important for addressing the sim2real gap in computational crystallography. (Reviewers 2, 4)  
  - The explanation of the simulation technique in Appendix B4 is very strong, easy to read, and one of the strongest parts of the paper. (Reviewer 2)
- Comprehensive benchmarking across 21 models with detailed performance metrics, covering both in-library and out-of-library classification and testing on real experimental data. (Reviewers 1, 2)
- Identifies key challenges: current neural networks struggle with classifying low-frequency crystals (long-tail distribution), providing directions for future ML model development. (Reviewers 1, 4)  
  - Brings to light weaknesses of previous CNN-based works in dealing with unbalanced symmetry classification. (Reviewer 4)
- Well-presented and easily usable open-source code released early in the review process, enhancing reproducibility and community adaptation. (Reviewer 4)
- Demonstrates that models trained on SimXRD can generalize to real experimental data (RRUFF). (Reviewers 1, 4)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited validation of simulation fidelity against real experimental data.**  
  - The RRUFF dataset (≈3K patterns) represents only 0.07% of the simulated dataset size, and the comparison between simulated and real-world patterns is shown for only one case (Li₂MnO₃). A more comprehensive analysis of sim–real gaps and identification of which simulation parameters most affect model performance on real data is needed. (Reviewer 1)  
  - No analytical validation showing that SimXRD patterns match reality; the only justification is the simulation technique and the fact that ML models trained on SimXRD transfer to other datasets. (Reviewer 2)  
  - The generalizability test using RRUFF may not be representative of the wide variety of experimental conditions (materials type, physical condition, equipment, etc.). Without further validation, it is unclear how rigorous this assessment is. (Reviewer 3)  
  - No baseline comparison against other simulators (Pymatgen, GSAS-II). To demonstrate the value of SimXRD, the authors need to show that training on SimXRD leads to better sim2real transfer than training on patterns from those packages. (Reviewer 4)  
  - A proposed experiment: take ~10 experimental patterns with known ground truth structures, simulate patterns with SimXRD, Pymatgen, and GSAS-II, and compare R_w goodness-of-fit values. (Reviewer 4)

- **Poor performance on low-frequency (long-tail) crystal classes undermines dataset utility for practical use.**  
  - Models (including CNNs, one of the most common architectures) struggle with long-tailed distributions even with re-weighting. This undercuts the quality of the distribution; the dataset does not allow training models that perform accurately on rare symmetry groups. (Reviewers 1, 2)  
  - Reviewer 2 believes these limitations are “too severe for practical use” and that the dataset is “not ready for use.”

- **Lack of model interpretability and failure-mode analysis.**  
  - No investigation of what patterns CNNs learn, how they make decisions, or why they succeed/fail (especially for low-frequency crystals). Such analysis could guide future architectural improvements. (Reviewer 1)  
  - No analysis of systematic differences between simulated and real XRD patterns beyond the Li₂MnO₃ case study; no identification of which simulation parameters most affect model performance on real data. (Reviewer 1)

- **Unclear or misaligned main contribution.**  
  - The benchmarks do not provide much insight into ML research; no new architectures are introduced to address the identified challenges. If PySimXRD contains new/better functions than previous simulators, those should be specified, but even then the contributions lie in physics or materials science, not ML. One reviewer suggests the paper is better suited for venues like *Scientific Data* rather than ICLR. (Reviewer 3)  
  - Another reviewer notes the paper “fixates on the benchmark part” but could better emphasize the dataset’s value and sim2real generalization. (Reviewer 4)

- **Presentation and clarity issues.**  
  - Overall writing (outside the appendix) could be improved; explanations seem sufficient for those already familiar with the work but not for newcomers. Grammatical mistakes (e.g., missing commas on lines 69-70) cause confusion. (Reviewer 2)  
  - Figure 2 is hard to understand: many images with little explanation; it is referenced as a primary part of the paper but lacks descriptive text. (Reviewer 2)  
  - Presentation logic is poor: line 48 (“structure analysis being challenging for domain experts” is irrelevant to the search-match method), line 59 (unclear what challenges large-scale databases introduce), line 79 (repeated words), line 216 (details on the Materials Project are irrelevant). (Reviewer 3)  
  - Line 156 claims “existing symmetry methods rely on 1D CNNs” – this seems to contradict lines 296-308 and oversimplifies the field. (Reviewer 2)  
  - Lines 296-308 are written as if they list all techniques comprehensively, which is likely inaccurate. (Reviewer 2)

- **Missing discussion of end-to-end structure determination from PXRD.**  
  - The paper focuses on symmetry classification but does not cite or connect to recent work on generative structure prediction from PXRD (2024). These works are relevant, and the dataset could be valuable for that community; mentioning them would maximize impact. (Reviewer 4)

- **Choice of x-axis and experimental relevance.**  
  - The simulated dataset uses lattice plane distance as the x-axis; the relationship to 2θ (more commonly used in XRD data) is not explained, nor is the reason for this choice clarified. (Reviewer 3)

- **Minor presentation/writing issues (aggregated).**  
  - Line 36: “Transitional methods for what?” – extra clarity needed. (Reviewer 2)  
  - Line 157: “almost all related research … due to the lack of easily accessible datasets” is unclear. (Reviewer 3)  
  - Line 345: “being biased by classes of high frequency” is more related to the objective/loss function than to CNNs specifically. (Reviewer 3)
