## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Latent Preference Coding (LPC), a framework that models implicit factors of human preferences using discrete latent codes, and integrates with offline alignment algorithms (DPO, SimPO, IPO). It avoids predefined reward functions and hand-crafted combination weights. Experiments are conducted on multiple benchmarks with three base models (Mistral-7B, Llama3-8B, Llama3-8B-Instruct). Reviewers agree the core idea is interesting and the mathematical derivation is elegant, but they raise significant concerns about experimental design, practicality, reproducibility, and evaluation comprehensiveness. One reviewer (score 3, confidence 5) is considerably more negative, casting doubt on the method’s effectiveness, while the other three (scores 5,6,6) are more positive but still note several unresolved issues.

### Strengths
- The idea of using discrete latent variables to model multifaceted human preferences is intuitive, novel, and inspiring (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- The framework eliminates the need for manually pre-defined reward functions and combination weights (R3).
- Provides a unified representation that can be seamlessly integrated with multiple offline alignment algorithms, showing high applicability and scalability (R1, R3, R4).
- Extensive experiments are conducted across three base models (Mistral-7B, Llama3-8B, Llama3-8B-Instruct) and three alignment algorithms (DPO, SimPO, IPO) (R2, R3, R4).
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow (R1, R2).
- The mathematical derivation is presented in a fancy and elegant manner (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Experimental design fails to validate effectiveness on complex/conflicting preferences**: The chosen tasks (commonsense reasoning, mathematical reasoning, truthfulness) do not convincingly demonstrate LPC’s ability to model intricate, contradictory human preferences (e.g., helpfulness vs. harmlessness) (R1). This concern is echoed by another reviewer who notes insufficient validation of generalization across a broader range of tasks (R4).
- **Insufficient details on the reward model used in Table 1**: The paper does not specify which reward model provides the scores in Table 1; if the reward model is unreliable, the results are meaningless (R1). Additionally, Table 1 does not include the standard MMLU benchmark, making comparisons with existing work harder (R3).
- **Low scores in Table 3 raise concerns about experimental setup**: For example, Llama3-8B-Instruct fine-tuned with SimPO yields scores much lower than the 20+ reported in the SimPO paper; this suggests potential differences in settings or a flaw in the method (R3).
- **Analytical comments from a minority reviewer**: One reviewer (score 3, high confidence 5) argues that LPC resembles past latent-variable NLP works that had limited effectiveness, questionable robustness, and unstable training; they worry the architecture is too complex and impractical for widespread use (R1).
- **Derivation required per alignment algorithm reduces practicality**: LPC requires a new mathematical derivation for each different alignment objective (e.g., DPO, SimPO, IPO). There is no guarantee of correctness for new algorithms, undermining applicability (R1). One reviewer asks why some LPC results are worse than the baseline and requests an ablation to understand these cases (R1).
- **Missing hyperparameters and training details for the discrete latent space**: The dimensionality of the latent variable, codebook size, and codebook training procedure are not specified, hindering reproducibility (R3).
- **TSNE visualization lacks distinct clusters**: The visualization does not show clear separation between different preference factors, so the claim that latent codes capture distinct preference types is not well supported (R3).
- **Need for an ablation study with continuous latent representations**: To justify the use of discrete codes and show they are superior to continuous counterparts, an ablation replacing discrete codes with continuous ones is necessary (R2).
- **Unclear training time and computational cost compared to baseline methods**: The relative computational overhead of LPC versus DPO, SimPO, etc., is not discussed (R2, R4). This is especially relevant for large-scale models (R4).
- **Insufficient explanation of how the prior network captures complex preferences**: The authors should provide theoretical justification or cite prior work demonstrating that learning a prior over latent codes effectively models human preferences (R2).
- **Related work discussion is brief** (R4).
- **Missing standard benchmarks**: The evaluation would benefit from additional benchmarks such as MT-Bench or Arena-Hard to test generalizability (R3).
- **Unaddressed questions that relate to weaknesses**:
  - Clarify the summation in Equation (10): is \( h_{x,y} \) at each timestep summed with \( z \) (R3)?
  - Explain the purpose of the flipped-label experiment, how LPC mitigates the win-rate drop, and provide results for different flip rates (e.g., 10% or 90%) (R3).
  - Describe how human preferences correspond to each latent code and how different preference elements are distinguished (R1? Actually R4 asks this).
  - Specify the task division between the prior and posterior networks, their design purpose, how they operate when no preferences are observed, and how the codes are designed (R4).
  - Report LPC’s performance in extreme cases such as extremely noisy data or entirely missing preference labels (R4).
