## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Sequential Multiple Instance Learning (SMIL) with a bidirectional Transformer (BiSMIL) and a sequence-aware uncertainty metric (SMILU) to exploit the order of medical image sequences. Evaluated on ultrasound (UTD), brain CT (50,862 slices), and pulmonary CT datasets, BiSMIL outperforms three MIL baselines in final and early accuracy. Reviewers agree the problem is interesting and the method is clearly described, but they raise major concerns about unclear clinical motivation, vague definition of "sequence," limited methodological novelty (bidirectional architecture is well-established), insufficient comparisons (e.g., 3D CNNs), overclaimed interpretability, and unrealistic claims about reducing radiation exposure. One reviewer (R4, score 3) is notably more critical, citing a fundamental lack of understanding of clinical workflows and the sequential framework.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important problem: enhancing clinical efficiency by leveraging the sequential nature of medical image collections (R1, R3).
- The formulation of CT and ultrasound analysis as a sequential MIL problem is unique (R4).
- Multiple-instance learning is an interesting approach to image sequences; adding a sequence-aware method is a nice addition (R2).
- The sequence-aware uncertainty metric (SMILU) is a nice addition (R2) and is clearly described (R1, R2).
- Paper is well-organized and clearly describes the algorithm (R1, R2, R4).
- Comprehensive experiments with detailed sequence-length analyses demonstrate strong performance and robustness across multiple datasets (R1, R4).
- Demonstrates that BiSMIL requires 30–50% fewer images for comparable performance (R1, R2, R3, R4 – common finding).

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear clinical motivation and real-world applicability (all reviewers):** The paper does not provide a specific clinical scenario where clinicians actively adjust the number of images based on real-time observations; protocols are often predefined (R3). The claim of reducing radiation exposure is questionable for CT scans, where the entire volume is acquired in a single pass (R2); moreover, ultrasound does not use ionizing radiation (R3). The paper lacks a quantitative evaluation of the trade-off between image count and diagnostic accuracy, or of time savings/cost reduction (R1, R3). No realistic workflow integration is shown (R2).  
- **Vague and inconsistent definition of “sequence”: it is unclear whether the sequence refers to temporal (longitudinal, video frames) or spatial (CT slices) order, or to multiple acquisitions in a single study (R2, R4).** The paper does not explain what “earlier” or “later” means for ultrasound images – e.g., frames in a video, different views in a study, or multiple studies over time (R2, R4). For CT, treating 2D slices as a sequence is unusual; the scanning direction is usually known and fixed, not a clinician preference (R4). The paper’s justification for bidirectionality (“robustness to sequence reversals”) contradicts the claim that sequence order is important (R2). The Gaussian position encoding for reverse-sequence robustness is not explained (R3).  
- **Limited methodological novelty (R1, R2, R3, R4):** The BiSMIL architecture mainly applies a standard Transformer with bidirectional input and position encoding, which is an incremental adaptation of well-established techniques (Bi-LSTMs, attention) to MIL (R1, R3). The paper does not compare with non-MIL methods such as 3D CNNs, which are natural for volumetric CT data (R2, R4). The authors need to justify why 3D CNNs or other sequence models are unsuitable (R4).  
- **Model is not genuinely incremental (R3):** Each time a new image is added, the model re-evaluates the entire sequence, rather than updating incrementally; other MIL methods could be applied in the same way. The distinction is unclear. The subsequence prediction experiment (random subset? regular sampling?) is also not clearly described (R2).  
- **Overclaimed interpretability and lack of analysis (R1):** The uncertainty metric (SMILU) is presented as interpretable, but it primarily supports quality control rather than explaining model reasoning. No attention score visualizations are provided to show which parts of the sequence influence decisions. No examples or case studies demonstrate how the metric aids interpretation.  
- **Insufficient dataset description and experimental details (R2, R4):** The nature of the ultrasound sequence (image frames? different views?) is not specified. The data split (70% training) is not explicitly stated to be at the patient level (R2). For CT, it is unclear how subsequences are formed (e.g., randomly skipping slices? incremental scanning?) and how the model would inform a clinician to stop scanning in real-time when the optimal stopping point is unknown (R3). No statistical tests are reported for performance comparisons (R4).  
- **Minor presentation issues:** Grammar (“better … better”) and wording (“multiple number of image instances”) (R2, R4). References to figures are confusing (Figure 2 vs. Figure 1) (R2). The term “clinical imaging” could be replaced with “medical imaging” (R2).  

**Note:** One reviewer (R4, score 3) was significantly more critical, stating that the authors do not convey understanding of the application domain, that the sequential framework is completely unclear, and that comments about “clinicians creating images sequentially” and “scanning direction based on clinician preference” display a misunderstanding of clinical workflows. This reviewer also questioned why only MIL baselines were used when the data is inherently 3D (CT).
