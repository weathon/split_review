## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a “social learning” framework for LLMs, where teacher models with private data transfer knowledge to a student via natural language instructions or synthetic examples, aiming for privacy preservation. Experiments on benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K, BoolQ) using PaLM 2 show comparable performance to using original labels/prompts, with low memorization measured by a canary method.

### Strengths
- The core idea is clever and well-scoped: using LLMs to generate better instructions from data for privacy preservation (R4).
- The setting of social learning with consideration of dataset privacy is valuable (R3). The proposed metric for information leakage is novel (R1). The technique addresses broader privacy concerns by sharing lessons without exposing data (R2).
- The two proposed approaches (instruction sharing and synthetic example generation) are shown to be effective in transferring knowledge (R1). The majority-voting strategy for example selection is interesting (R3).
- The writing is intuitive and easy to follow (R4). Experimental results demonstrate that collaboration can outperform single-model zero-shot performance (R3).

**Note:** Two reviewers rated the paper 5/5, finding the work novel and significant; two others rated it 3/5, raising substantial concerns about formalization, baselines, and privacy verification.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of formalization.** The problem setup is described only with intuition and hand-wavy language (“agents collaborate”, “subset of information”). No formal definition of the student’s objective, teacher output space, aggregator, or the mapping from private datasets to summaries is given. This makes it hard to connect to related work and to precisely understand the approach (R4 W1; R3 also notes poor organization).
- **Insufficient privacy justification.** The privacy guarantee is not established. The canary-based memorization experiment has low ecological validity: a teacher has no incentive to communicate a canary (random password string) since it would not help the task. The experiment only shows that in-context learning does not memorize canaries, not that generated instructions/examples are free of private information (e.g., “Alice is never spam” could leak a fact). No comparison to existing privacy attacks (e.g., membership inference attacks, Shokri et al. 2017) is provided. Claiming reduced private data leakage from these experiments is misleading (R2a, R3 Q1/Q2, R4 questions).
- **Weak baseline and inadequate multi-teacher validation.** The main experiments use a random aggregation strategy, meaning the student effectively uses information from only one teacher at a time. This does not demonstrate that leveraging multiple teachers is beneficial. Missing comparisons: (a) multiple teachers vs. a single teacher, (b) the proposed aggregation method (random) vs. stronger aggregators like majority voting (shown only in an appendix) or LLM-based summarization (R4 W3; R1 weakness 3; R3 Q4). The design choice of number of teachers is not ablated; do more teachers always help, or does performance saturate/decline? (R4 W4).
- **Missing experiments on heterogeneous settings.** All experiments assume teachers have the same data distribution, which is unrealistic. No results are given for settings where teachers have different distributions, different model architectures, or different abilities. The teacher has no knowledge of the student’s capability (e.g., context window), which could cause it to generate examples that the student cannot process (R3 weakness 2, R3 Q4/Q5; R4 W4). The ability to handle distribution shift is a core requirement for the claimed collaborative learning scenario.
- **Limited novelty of the approach.** Existing works have already used synthetic samples generated by teacher models to teach a student; sharing via instruction or exemplars is not new (R1 weakness 1). The connection to social learning theory is unclear and distracts from the actual method; removing the reference would not change the contributions (R4 W2).
- **Missing references to related literature.** The paper does not cite prior work on knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., Ba & Caruana, Vapnik & Ismailov, Bucilu et al.) or curriculum learning (Bengio et al.), which are closely related and could help contrast the proposed natural-language-based transfer (R4 W5).
- **Clarity and organization issues.** Experimental setup details are relegated to appendices; the main text should explain what the teacher is given (e.g., 8 random examples?) and what the student observes more succinctly and earlier (R2 b/c). The experiments section is not well organized (R3 weakness 3). Also minor: bolding cells for non-significance is unconventional (R4 W6).
- **Unanswered questions about specific design choices.** How are continuations discarded due to privacy or faulty generation (line 159)? Is it manual or heuristic? (R3 Q3). The paper does not explore whether the teacher is only sharing formatting (“how to output”) rather than task-relevant information (R2 Q2). For GSM8K, could the teacher share chain-of-thought hints? (R2 Q2).
