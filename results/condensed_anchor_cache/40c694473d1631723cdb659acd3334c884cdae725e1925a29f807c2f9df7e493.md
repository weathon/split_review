## Merged Review

### Summary

All reviewers acknowledge that this paper proposes a stochastic semi-gradient descent (SemiSGD) method for learning in mean field games that jointly updates the policy and population estimate, eliminating the forward-backward fixed-point iteration and enabling online learning. The paper provides convergence analysis under linear function approximation with finite-time error bounds, and presents numerical experiments on several MFG examples. Reviewers are positive about the novelty and the practical potential, but raise concerns about the definition of equilibrium (Bellman operator vs. optimality), strong assumptions required for the theory, limited stationary setting, and the interpretability of some theoretical quantities.

### Strengths

1. **Well-motivated and novel approach.** The idea of treating policy and population as a unified parameter and using asynchronous (SGD-style) updates instead of forward-backward fixed-point iteration is new and significant. It can improve sample complexity and enable online learning in unknown environments.
   (R1, R2, R3, R4)

2. **Strong convergence guarantees.** The paper provides finite-time convergence analysis with rates, sample complexity bounds, and convergence to a stationary point. The analysis covers both small and larger Lipschitz factor regimes, including bounded bias results.
   (R1: “strong convergence guarantee with a rate”; R2: “convergence analysis includes sample complexity”; R3: “finite-time error bounds”; R4: “convergence to neighborhood”)

3. **Extensive and clear theoretical framework.** Assumptions are clearly stated and some explanations are provided. The analysis does not directly require contractivity or monotonicity, which is a notable extension.
   (R3, R4; R3: “convergence results without (directly) assuming contractivity or monotonicity”)

4. **Empirical validation with multiple problems.** The experiments cover different MFG examples (including flocking) and include baselines such as FPI and FPI+MD. Table 2 gives a useful summary of comparisons. The method empirically outperforms existing approaches in some problems in terms of exploitability.
   (R2: “experiments cover different examples and contain a few baselines”; R3: “outperform existing approaches in some of the demonstrated problems in terms of exploitability”; R4: “comparison in Table 2 provides a good summary”)

5. **Practical nature.** The method can be applied online to unknown models, which is a clear advantage over offline fixed-point methods.
   (R3)

6. **Intuitive and well-presented.** The paper is clearly written despite the technical subject, and the algorithm is presented in a way that is easy to follow.
   (R2: “relatively clear”; R3: “well written and relatively clear”; R4: “assumptions are clearly stated, and some explanations are provided”)

### Weaknesses

1. **Definition of equilibrium differs from standard MFG.** The paper uses the Bellman operator instead of the Bellman optimality operator, so there is no guarantee that the agent maximizes expected cumulative discounted reward. In the standard multi-agent learning setting, reward maximization is the natural objective. The paper should justify why this alternative definition is of interest, and whether the theory can be extended to the reward-maximizing (argmax) setting. Without such extension, comparisons with existing MFG work may be unfair.
   (R1: “primary concern”; R4: Assumption 2 cannot cover the standard argmax operator due to discontinuity, so the paper considers a “smooth” version; unclear if it is an approximation and under what conditions.)

2. **Strong assumptions limit theoretical significance.** The analysis relies on linear function approximation and regularized (smooth) policy operators, which may not hold in many realistic problems. The practical checkability of these assumptions is not addressed.
   (R2: “not clear to me how to check these assumptions in practice”; R3: “strong assumptions such as linear models and regularized solutions”; R4: needs justification for using smooth policy operator and its relation to standard Nash equilibrium.)

3. **Lack of clear comparison with prior assumptions.** The paper mentions that its assumptions are weaker than typical contractivity in a remark, but it does not provide a detailed comparison. For example, can the contractivity assumption in prior work (e.g., [1]) be recovered as a special case of Assumptions 2 and 4? For works using regularization (e.g., [2]), can Assumptions 2 or 4 be satisfied with sufficient regularization? The Lipschitz constants should be related to regularization parameters used in prior work to clarify whether the new assumptions are more or less restrictive.
   (R4, supported by R2’s question about FPI+MD vs. online mirror descent of Perolat et al. 2021, which is a related algorithm.)

4. **Meaningfulness of Theorem 2 (bias term).** The theorem shows convergence with bounded bias, but it is unclear whether this bias can be smaller than a random guess (e.g., setting \(\xi_T = 0\)). The magnitude and scaling of \(\bar{w}\) (and its upper bound \(w\) from Assumption 5) are not discussed. When is the bias small enough for meaningful convergence? What problem properties make \(\bar{w}\) small or large? For large bias, the result may be vacuous.
   (R4)

5. **Limitation to stationary MFGs.** The method assumes time-stationary mean fields, while most of the compared MFG literature addresses non-stationary settings. This mismatch should be clarified and the possibility of extension to non-stationary MFGs should be discussed.
   (R3)

6. **Unclear handling of non-unique MFE.** The paper does not require uniqueness of the mean field equilibrium. It is not explained how convergence can be achieved when multiple equilibria exist: does the theory imply convergence to one equilibrium, or is uniqueness implicitly assumed? Similarly, the bootstrap estimate before Equation (3) replaces the unknown MFE with the mean field under the current policy; the justification for this replacement is not given.
   (R3)

7. **Method does not directly optimize exploitability.** The approach minimizes a surrogate objective; using “true” gradients on the unified parameters to directly minimize exploitability is not attempted, and the difficulty in doing so is not discussed.
   (R3)

8. **Insufficient detail on numerical experiments.** More explanation is needed, particularly why exploitability is reported for the flocking game when reward maximization is not the focus. The baselines (especially FPI+MD) should be explained more clearly: is FPI+MD the same as the online mirror descent of (Perolat et al., 2021)? If not, the paper should compare with that algorithm, which is known to converge much faster than fictitious play.
   (R1: “more detailed explanation of the numerical experiments is necessary”; R2: “the baselines could be explained more clearly”; R2: Q2 on FPI+MD.)

9. **Minor presentation issues.** There are incomplete references and TeX errors in the reference list.
   (R3)

10. **Question about Assumptions 4 and 5.** Can the author provide an intuitive explanation of why Assumption 5 is relatively weaker than Assumption 4? Also, Assumption 5 introduces a upper bound \(w\) for \(\bar{w}\) that does not appear in Theorem 2; what is its purpose?
    (R1: question on Assumption 5; R4: question on Assumption 5’s \(w\) not appearing.)
