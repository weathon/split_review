## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes EBES, a benchmarking framework for event sequence classification and regression, including several publicly available datasets and a novel synthetic dataset (Pendulum). It provides empirical results with various models (GRU, Transformer, Mamba, mTAND, CoLES, etc.) under standard evaluation protocols. The authors aim to facilitate reproducible research and in-depth analysis of model behavior on event sequences. Reviewer opinions diverged: two reviewers (scores 5 and 5) found the effort valuable and applauded the unified framework, while two reviewers (scores 3 and 3) considered the work at an early stage with significant methodological and coverage limitations.

### Strengths
- Event sequences are a practically relevant problem setting with relatively less attention from the ML community; benchmarking efforts are important and applauded (R1, R4).
- EBES provides a unified framework including both datasets, models, and experimental protocols to facilitate reproducible research and consistent evaluations (R3).
- Datasets come from different application domains (medical records, transaction sequences, synthetic), enhancing diversity and applicability (R3).
- The framework includes a wide range of comprehensive models—general architectures (GRU, Transformer) and specialized ones (mTAND, CoLES)—ensuring thorough evaluation of model performance on event-sequence tasks (R3).
- The analysis of the importance of modeling time and sequence order in event-sequence data for prediction accuracy is insightful (R3).
- Adoption of hyperparameter optimization and Monte Carlo cross-validation for a fair comparison among models is a positive aspect (R2).
- The statistical analysis of results is noteworthy (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Dataset representativity and diversity**: Only one regression dataset (Pendulum, synthetic) and five classification datasets are included; the scale and domain coverage are limited. It is unclear whether these datasets should be considered a single domain—motivation is needed (R1, R2). The synthetic Pendulum’s complementarity to real-world datasets is unclear and may introduce bias, especially as the only regression dataset (R2). More real-world regression datasets are needed (R2). The selection of publicly available datasets shows little diversity in scale, formats, difficulty levels (e.g., number of classes), and domains. Assessment results indicate models behave similarly across datasets, making it hard to identify model-specific strengths/weaknesses (R2). The framework should incorporate datasets from additional domains such as smart city, weather forecasting, renewable energy, traffic, social networks (R3). The limited number of datasets (7) is insufficient for a benchmark (R4).
- **Lack of comparison with existing benchmarks**: No comparison with existing event-sequence benchmarks or frameworks (e.g., the multi-level task framework [1], [2], or the BPI challenge datasets and associated surveys [1,2,3,4]), making it difficult to assert the advantages of EBES over existing tools (R1, R3). The paper fails to explain the unique research challenges of event sequences versus general time series, and the models evaluated are general time-series models rather than those specifically designed for event sequences (R2). The need for a new benchmark is not justified given existing ones (R2). More specific state-of-the-art models for event prediction should be included (R2).
- **Evaluation methodology concerns**: A significant methodological flaw: splitting datasets should be based on order/timestamps (oldest for training, most recent for testing) rather than random selection; random splitting risks overfitting and does not reflect real performance (R4). The Monte Carlo cross-validation method (from a chemistry journal) is not suitable for event sequences or time series, and the original paper does not address this data type (R4). The evaluation process lacks scientific rigor and detail; the code is not yet available (R4). A credible benchmark must include a dedicated evaluation procedure and metrics for event sequence data, which are missing (R4). Questions remain: What is the detailed evaluation process? How is representativity verified without a domain expert? What defines a “high-quality” dataset? Preprocessing (e.g., missing values) is domain-dependent, making integration into a benchmark challenging (R4). Also, MLP (Multilayer Perceptron) is not defined and lacks references (R4).
- **Insufficient task complexity**: The benchmark focuses mainly on sequence classification with a small number of classes; time and order are not critical because they are not part of the predicted results. More complex and challenging prediction tasks (e.g., next event prediction, time-to-event, as mentioned in the introduction) should be included for a thorough study (R2). 
- **Result explanation lacking**: Results in Table 2 and Figure 3 need explanation: why GRU outperforms Transformer despite higher complexity; model scales (number of tunable parameters); conclusions to be drawn from Table 2; how pretrained models perform differently; why mTAND excels on Pendulum but fails on others; implications of no linear trend in Figure 3 (R2). The Taobao dataset is not described (R2).
- **Statistical test choice**: The choice of Holm-Bonferroni correction (controlling family-wise error rate) over Benjamini-Hochberg (controlling false discovery rate) is not motivated; the latter seems more relevant for this setting (R1).
- **Scalability**: The framework has not been tested on large-scale or real-time datasets to evaluate scalability and performance under real-world conditions (R3).
