## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a conformal prediction method for potential outcomes of continuous treatments, handling unknown propensity scores by using Gaussian smoothing over treatment values and optimizing a bandwidth parameter. It derives finite-sample prediction intervals under both known and unknown propensity, with theoretical guarantees. Experiments are conducted on synthetic and medical ICU datasets, benchmarking against MC dropout and ensemble methods. Reviewers acknowledge the significance of the problem and novelty but raise concerns about evaluation rigor, clarity of motivation, reliance on existing work (Gibbs et al. 2023), overly simplistic simulations, and unconvincing empirical results.

### Strengths
- The problem of conformal prediction for continuous treatments/dose responses is clearly significant and important for high-stakes applications like personalized medicine. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- Novel method providing conformal prediction intervals for continuous treatments, especially when the propensity score is unknown and must be estimated – a first in the literature. (R1, R2, R4)
- Solid theoretical results with finite-sample coverage guarantees, including a finite-sample theorem for the unknown propensity case. (R1, R2)
- Clear writing and accessible presentation of the research question and methods, using figures and concrete examples. (R2)
- Original formulation using Gaussian smoothing over treatments with an optimal bandwidth chosen per problem instance. (R3)
- Consideration of both hard interventions and soft interventions (incremental changes), which is practically relevant in medicine. (R3)
- Two of four reviewers are more positive (ratings 6) while two are more critical (ratings 5).

### Weaknesses
- **Evaluation focuses only on empirical coverage** without jointly considering interval length or closeness to nominal coverage. Simply achieving high coverage is insufficient (e.g., [-inf, inf] gives 100% coverage). A balanced evaluation should compare interval length and coverage simultaneously. (R2)
- **Numerical instability**: Results show many outliers in boxplots even with only 50 repetitions (e.g., Fig. 4 α=0.2, treatment 5; Fig. 5 α=0.1, treatment 1 and α=0.2, treatment 5). Authors should discuss this instability. (R2)
- **Medical dataset results not convincing**: At treatment range [0.6, 1], the predicted blood pressure can be meaningfully less than 0, and the intervals are too wide to be useful. Also, wider intervals than MC-dropout are not necessarily better. (R2, R3)
- **Motivation confusion**: The separation of challenge (a) and (b) is unclear. The main challenge for continuous treatments is the rarity of identical treatment values, requiring smoothing – this is not explicitly mentioned as a motivation. (R3)
- **Roles of soft and hard interventions** are presented as convenient for derivations (soft when propensity known, hard when unknown), but soft interventions are also of interest when the propensity is unknown (e.g., Chernozhukov et al. on average causal derivatives, Marmarelis et al. AISTATS 2024). (R3)
- **Unknown propensity bound M** (Assumption 1) is difficult to calibrate in practice. The method’s validity depends on this constant, resembling a sensitivity analysis. (R3)
- **Heavy reliance on Gibbs et al. (2023)** without clearly differentiating contributions. Notation, Theorem 1 and 2, Algorithm 1, and the tilting approach are directly borrowed. The added value beyond Gibbs et al. for continuous treatments needs explicit articulation. (R4)
- **Terminology issues**: “Finite-sample predictive intervals” should be “finite-sample validity guarantees” to align with field standards. “Exact” conditional coverage is misleading – exact conditional coverage is known to be unattainable. (R4)
- **Relation to Lei & Candes (2021)**: The paper claims they cannot handle unknown propensity, but in fact they define and prove a coverage gap based on error in the estimated likelihood ratio (which is the propensity in treatment effect). (R4)
- **Under-coverage in experiments**: Particularly for α=0.1, the empirical coverage shows variability. Number of runs (10) is too low to draw reliable conclusions. More runs needed. (R4)
- **Overly simplistic synthetic experiments**: Only one discrete confounder, continuous treatment, and continuous outcome; not in line with standard treatment effect literature. More realistic or semi-synthetic experiments would strengthen results. (R3, R4)
- **Weak baselines**: MC dropout only captures epistemic uncertainty, which is likely minimal in these simple settings. Benchmark against methods that also capture aleatoric uncertainty (e.g., Gaussian process, MLE) and include vanilla conformal prediction without covariate shift. (R4)
- **Computational complexity**: The optimization solver can be costly. Paper should report fitting and inference times in main text to assess practical applicability. (R4)
- **Equation (14) smoothness assumptions**: Using a kernel to approximate the indicator function may imply smoothness conditions that are not discussed. (R1)
- **Optimal σ trade-off**: How to choose σ empirically? Is there a rule like σ = c·n⁻²/⁵? The role of σ should be explained further. (R1, R3)
- **Unclear why hard interventions in synthetic experiments relate to X** rather than being purely hard interventions. (R4)
- **Assumption 1 (line 295–299)**: The estimation error bound c<sub>a</sub> is the same for all X given intervention a; should it depend on X (c<sub>a</sub>(x))? (R4)
- **Meaning of resulting σ and c<sub>a</sub> in the optimization problem** of Theorem 2 needs clarification. (R4)
- **Lemma 4 (lines 712–736)**: Only relevant for absolute residual non-conformity score. The proof is incomplete – it does not show the connection between Bonferroni-corrected significance level and the targeted significance level. In fact, it is just a Bonferroni correction application. (R4)
- **Minor**: Expand on why data ordering invariance from quantile regression is needed (line). Algorithm text is dense. (R3) Typo: line 476 should refer to Table 1. (R4)
