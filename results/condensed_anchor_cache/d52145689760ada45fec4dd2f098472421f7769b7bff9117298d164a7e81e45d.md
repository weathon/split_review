## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an algorithm to equip large language models with a metacognitive ability—accurate self-evaluation of their own competence before generating text—to reduce hallucinations. It draws inspiration from meta-learning: fine-tuning multiple model versions on diverse datasets (including the base model), randomly selecting versions per iteration, and using the average metacognitive error as the loss function. The paper also introduces a ground truth dataset of 16,000 question-answer pairs across 160 domains, constructed using ChatGPT, and fine-tunes ChatGLM-6B with LoRA in a meta-learning style.

### Strengths
- **Innovative concept**: Using meta-learning to address hallucinations and enabling self-assessment before output is a novel approach; it is the first method to mitigate hallucinations before the model generates text (Reviewer 2).
- **Clarity and accessibility**: The paper is written in an easy-to-understand manner, with vivid metaphors in the introduction making it readable (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).
- **Useful dataset**: The constructed 16,000-question dataset across 160 domains could be helpful for future research on hallucination evaluation and factual QA (Reviewer 4, Reviewer 1).
- **Adaptive metacognitive ability**: The algorithm enables the metacognitive capability to adapt to various fine-tuned versions of the primary model, aligning evaluations with the knowledge capacity of each version (Reviewer 1).
- **Training procedure**: In each training cycle, the algorithm randomly selects multiple fine-tuned model versions and evaluates their metacognitive abilities (Reviewer 1).
- **Interesting topic acknowledged**: The submission targets an underexplored intersection of meta-learning and hallucination, and the authors’ effort in putting together the manuscript is appreciated (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear problem definition and methodology**:
  - The paper’s motivation and main goal are not clearly defined—whether it addresses hallucination reduction or applies meta-learning to LLMs. The link between meta-learning and hallucination reduction is not well established (Reviewer 3).
  - The concept of “metacognition” is never formally defined, making it unclear how to quantify or evaluate it. The distinction between metacognition and model calibration is not discussed (Reviewer 4).
  - The transition from a general discussion of hallucination (Section 2) to model selection and metacognition (Section 3) is abrupt and confusing without adequate justification (Reviewer 3).
  - It is unclear how reframing the task from “instilling discretion to search only when necessary” to “teaching self-evaluation of capabilities” is justified; these are not interchangeable (Reviewer 4).
- **Inadequate experimental evaluation**:
  - Experiments only use ChatGLM-6B, a model with a limited knowledge base; no experiments are conducted on more robust and widely used models (e.g., LLaMA, GPT families) or multilingual/dialogue models such as BLOOM. The choice of ChatGLM-6B is not justified, limiting generalizability (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3).
  - The specific text generation task is unclear: related work mentions summarization and dialogue, but the experimental setup uses QA format. The task is not explicitly defined, making it difficult to evaluate the results (Reviewer 3).
  - No evaluation is performed on standard hallucination benchmarks, preventing comparison to existing methods (Reviewer 3).
  - Results lack quantitative support: Table 1 (and other tables) present raw numbers without any analysis, interpretation, or discussion of statistical significance or practical implications; the evaluation metrics are not described in sufficient detail (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3).
  - The experiments do not demonstrate that the model actually “learns metacognitive abilities” after training; the evaluation design cannot support this claim (Reviewer 4).
  - The paper seriously lacks quantitative experimental results to verify the method’s effectiveness and also lacks theoretical discussion validating the approach (Reviewer 2).
  - Practical application is challenging because the algorithm requires a substantial number of fine-tuned models (Reviewer 2).
- **Unsubstantiated claims**:
  - The introduction asserts that “RLHF is the main source of hallucination,” but MLE-trained models can also hallucinate, and RLHF can help reduce hallucinations; other similar ungrounded claims appear in the manuscript (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing related work and comparisons**:
  - The paper does not discuss related work on model calibration and retrieval-augmented models, despite mentioning them; it does not compare to those approaches (Reviewer 4).
  - No references are provided for foundational metacognition work from psychology or for meta-learning literature (Reviewer 3).
  - Missing citations when mentioning GPT-3, PaLM, Llama in Section 3.1; citation format errors (e.g., “(Feldman et al., 2023)” with unnecessary parentheses) (Reviewer 3).
  - The paper puts considerable effort into writing about retrieval-augmented models but does not use or compare them (Reviewer 4).
- **Writing and formatting issues**:
  - The manuscript’s clarity and structural integrity are poor; it reads more like a school report than a scholarly research paper (Reviewer 3).
  - The overall writing lacks coherence, with abrupt topic shifts (Reviewer 3).
  - Typos and formatting problems: unpaired quotation marks (e.g., ”hallucination”), table width issues (Table 5 and appendix tables extend beyond page margins), Figure 1 is actually tables (Reviewer 3).
  - The title phrasing “Reduce Hallucination Text Generation” is awkward; a more appropriate expression would be “Reducing Hallucinations in Text Generation” or “Reducing Hallucinatory Text Generation” (Reviewer 2).
- **Unresolved questions pointing to weaknesses**:
  - How can “meta-cognition” be formally quantified? What is the difference between “meta-cognition” and “model calibration”? (Reviewer 4)
  - Can the dataset (English, objective facts) generalize to broader scenarios and languages? (Reviewer 1)
  - In comparison to MAML, what do the support data and query data correspond to in the proposed algorithm? (Reviewer 1)
- **Disagreement in assessment**: One reviewer (score 6, fairly confident) commended the paper’s readable writing, adaptability, and training procedure, but still noted the lack of model diversity and missing analysis of results. The other three reviewers (scores 3, 1, and 3) were substantially more critical, highlighting severe deficiencies in experimental evidence, problem definition, and presentation.
