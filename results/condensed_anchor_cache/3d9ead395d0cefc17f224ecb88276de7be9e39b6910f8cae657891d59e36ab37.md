## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a modular framework called Knowledge Card, which uses domain-specific language models (knowledge cards) as parametric repositories to provide background knowledge to a base LLM. Three content selectors (relevance, pruning, factuality) control the generated documents. Two integration approaches (top-down and bottom-up) are introduced. The method achieves state-of-the-art performance on six benchmark datasets (MMLU, MidtermQA, LUN, etc.) for general-purpose QA, misinformation detection, and midterm QA.

### Strengths
- Novel idea of training individual language models as modular knowledge repositories, enabling dynamic synthesis and updating of knowledge from diverse domains, which is a significant advancement over static general-purpose LLMs and an alternative to retrieval-augmented methods.
- Consistent performance improvement across multiple benchmarks compared to existing retrieval-augmented language models and generated knowledge prompting approaches.
- Ablation study demonstrates the effectiveness of each module (relevance, pruning, factuality selectors) with detailed analysis.
- The three selectors cover a broader spectrum of dimensions (relevance, brevity, factuality) than considered in previous methods.

### Weaknesses
- The paper is missing many details and is hard to follow at times, especially in Sections 2.1 and 2.3. Specific missing details include: how information is stored in a knowledge card for unseen test questions; how the entailment classifier for the factuality score is trained; how the MidtermQA dataset was curated; the prompt and performance of the “Do you need more information?” classifier in the top-down approach; and how multi-hop retrieval works in the bottom-up approach (e.g., looking up “San Mateo’s senior senator” from “Who is the senior senator of Tom Brady’s birth place?”).
- Missing comparisons to existing models with similar modular knowledge organization (e.g., https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.05036.pdf, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.06311.pdf) and datasets that test temporal aspects (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03215.pdf).
- Reliance on LM-based modules introduces risks: knowledge cards can hallucinate or incorporate inaccurate information despite the factuality selector; the binary yes/no decisions in the top-down approach may oversimplify complex query-card relationships, leading to inclusion of irrelevant or exclusion of relevant knowledge.
- No analysis of how performance scales with the number of knowledge cards or their granularity. It is unclear whether many fine-grained cards are better than few coarse-grained ones, or if there is a point of diminishing returns.
- The benchmarks tested primarily focus on natural language understanding tasks; results on generative tasks are lacking.
- It is not ensured that all knowledge cards are useful (e.g., Wikipedia may already answer many simple MMLU questions).
- **Typos:** “handful of knowledge cardds” should be “handful of knowledge cards.”
- **Reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms/requests:**
    - (From R2, Tables 1-3) Are there potential reasons for the different performance trends among the three Knowledge Card variations?
    - (From R3) Can the bottom-up and top-down approaches be combined?
