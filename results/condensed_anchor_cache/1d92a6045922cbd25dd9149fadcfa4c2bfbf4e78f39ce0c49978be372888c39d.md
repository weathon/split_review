## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces CALF, a reference-based benchmark for evaluating automatic metrics on Long-Form Question Answering (LFQA). CALF is built from Chinese examination questions (translated into English) covering geography, history, politics, law, medicine, and psychology, with 1,476 examples. It includes human-written and model-generated responses, and three comparison settings (human vs human, human vs model, model vs model). The authors evaluate 13 metrics (7 traditional, 3 prompt-based, 3 trained) on CALF, finding that none reach human-level agreement. Two reviewers rated the paper 5, two rated it 3, reflecting a split on contribution and soundness.

### Strengths
- Studies an important problem: evaluating LFQA, which involves complex, paragraph-level responses.
- The three-way comparison framework (human vs human, human vs model, model vs model) is interesting and well-designed.
- CALF covers a wide range of subjects (geography, history, politics, law, medicine, psychology), ensuring domain breadth.
- Data sourced from real, expert-written examinations provides high-quality, knowledge-rich reference content.
- The benchmark is relatively large (1,476 examples) and diverse, offering a valuable resource for the research community.
- Comprehensive evaluation across multiple metric types (7 traditional, 3 prompt-based, 3 trained) and multiple models.
- Error analysis with examples is thorough.
- The conclusion that advanced LLM-based metrics can be outperformed by simple n-gram methods (e.g., ROUGE/BLEU) in agreement with human experts is interesting.
- Extensive experiments reveal a significant gap between automatic metrics and human-level performance in capturing nuanced long-form responses.

### Weaknesses
- The benchmark does not diagnose answers or provide detailed scoring explanations; it only compares/ranks responses. This limits practical contribution (reviewer 1, confidence 3).
- Using translated Chinese exam questions is a major concern: translation may introduce misalignment, cultural/linguistic biases, and additional noise, especially for culture-bound topics (law, history, politics, medicine, psychology). The metric evaluation may thus be biased and less credible (reviewers 1,2,3; confidences 3,4,2).
- The paper does not provide a clear path forward or solutions to the identified problems; it mainly diagnoses failures without proposing improvements (reviewers 1,2,3).
- Lack of a random-guessing baseline in Tables 3 and 4; such a baseline would clarify the absolute performance of metrics (reviewer 1).
- The introduction lacks examples to illustrate the evaluation pipeline and why LFQA evaluation is difficult (reviewer 1).
- The paper provides zero details on annotators (selection, qualifications) and data sources (annotation quality, copyright, licensing), raising soundness concerns about benchmark construction (reviewer 3, confidence 2).
- Description of the CALF benchmark in the main text is highly limited; readers need sample questions, answers, and characteristics (e.g., question/reference lengths, topics, difficulty levels). The appendix provides only category information, not enough detail (reviewer 3).
- Is there a need for all three evaluation settings? What actionable insights does each setting provide? (reviewer 2).
- Why is inter-annotator agreement highest in the human vs model setting (Table 2) while most evaluation metrics achieve their best performance in the human vs human setting (Table 4)? This discrepancy is not explained (reviewer 4).
- Criteria for selecting response pairs per question and the number of pairs per question are not stated (reviewer 4).
- Lack of related work on advanced LLM-based LFQA evaluation methods; G-Eval is mentioned early but not cited until later (reviewer 3).
- Paper organization could be improved; there are too many typos and unclear writing (reviewer 4, confidence 3).
- Overall novelty is limited: the paper mainly explores existing metrics without offering new evaluation methods or clear improvements, and the three settings have been explored previously (reviewer 3; note that reviewers 1 and 2 found the benchmark more valuable, reflecting a disagreement on contribution).
