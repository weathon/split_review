## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces MANGO, a benchmark consisting of 53 mazes from Zork-I textgames, paired with walkthroughs and synthetic destination-finding (DF) and route-finding (RF) questions. LLMs (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Llama-2, RWKV) are evaluated via direct prompting with Chain-of-Thought. The task is challenging for all models; GPT-4 performs best but still poorly on hard questions and hallucinates nonexistent locations or edges. Reviewers overall agree the paper is well-written and the experiments are thorough, but they disagree on the benchmark’s novelty and usefulness. Reviewer 4 (score 8) finds it well-motivated and useful for embodied AI. Reviewers 1, 2, and 3 (scores 6,5,6) question whether the dataset effectively tests mapping/navigation abilities, given its simplicity (graph-like structure, solvable by search or code-interpreter pipelines), its unclear differentiation from synthetic or embodied-AI datasets (e.g., SCAN, ALFRED), and its limited real-world relevance (assumes known environment, cardinal-direction actions only, no visual input, no SLAM). The lack of baselines (human performance, random, explicit map-building) and restricted model analysis are also noted. The paper does not discuss limitations or extensions.

### Strengths
- Well-written paper with clear explanations of dataset construction and experiments (R1, R3, R4).
- Focus on mapping and navigation abilities of LLMs is important for NLP and robotics; many robotics benchmarks treat navigation as an atomic operation, whereas MANGO directly probes spatial reasoning (R3, R4).
- Data collection protocol is carefully designed: 53 mazes from real textgames, walkthroughs, human examination to ensure quality (R2, R4).
- Evaluation metrics for DF and RF questions are clear and effective for assessing understanding of spatial positions and actions (R1, R4).
- Thorough comparison between GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 with controlled experiments that quantify challenge factors (number of locations, imputed edges) and reveal insights such as hallucinations and the failure of non-GPT models despite prompt tuning (R2, R4).
- Downstream navigation experiment (Section 3.4) shows that better mapping/navigation ability benefits task performance, offering a starting point for real-world navigation links (R4, though another reviewer notes it is a simple case).
- R4 specifically praises the dataset size, appropriate complexity, rich text descriptions, and clear presentation of statistics, examples, and visualizations.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and differentiation from existing work**:
  - Unclear why real textgames are necessary over purely synthetic mazes (like SCAN); no comparison to synthetic datasets is provided (R2, R3).
  - How does MANGO compare to embodied AI datasets such as ALFRED? (R2).
  - The mapping can be trivially converted to a graph (locations as nodes, directions as relations) and solved by traditional search algorithms (DFS/BFS) or by translating NL into `<source, path, destination>` triples and using code or search libraries, especially since the NL is generated by patterns. This suggests the task does not truly test LLM-specific capabilities and may have limited research impact, possibly following the GSM8K path of using code interpreters (R3, moderate-to-high confidence).
  - Does the dataset reveal strengths/weaknesses of LLMs that are overlooked on other datasets? (R2).
- **Real-world relevance and applicability**:
  - The benchmark assumes a known environment (walkthrough), while real-world navigation is partially observed and requires simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) (R4, high confidence).
  - Actions are limited to cardinal directions; real movement involves rotations, variable speeds, and object interactions (R4).
  - Spatial layouts are highly simplified compared to complex real environments (houses, streets) (R3, R4).
  - Input is purely textual; hard to extend to visual inputs (VLMs like GPT-4V), and no evaluation of integrating visual and textual information (R4, R1).
  - R1 questions transferability of game environments to human or industrial real-world settings.
- **Limited model scope and analysis**:
  - Focus is mainly on GPT-3.5/4; paper should analyze why RWKV and Llama-2 perform much worse and why model size does not affect their performance (R2). Test additional models, especially those pre-trained on code and instruction fine-tuned (R3). Why restrict to GPT-based models? (R1).
  - How do specific model designs (attention, position embedding, instruction tuning) affect performance? (R2).
- **Missing baselines and human comparison**:
  - No random response baseline to measure information gain (R1).
  - No comparison to trained human performance on the same task (R1).
  - Missing baseline that explicitly builds a map from the walkthrough (e.g., by graph construction or search), which would likely achieve much higher performance than direct prompting with CoT, reflecting how humans solve the task (R2, R3).
- **Experimental details and missing analyses**:
  - Purpose of Figure 6 is unclear (too much information) (R1).
  - Claims about what makes a maze challenging (page 7) need supporting results (R1).
  - No discussion of limitations or how MANGO can be extended to practical scenarios (R4).
  - R1 asks for runtimes, a minival subset, how location ambiguity is resolved (relation to text description length).
  - R4 suggests using navigation-oriented metrics like Success weighted by Path Length (SPL) (Anderson et al., 2018) in addition to DF/RF success rates.
  - R4 asks whether results are from single-run or multiple runs; results with different prompts.
  - R4 asks how results change if location names are replaced with IDs (e.g., L01), if detailed walkthrough descriptions are removed, when VLMs like GPT-4V are used, and when LLMs are fine-tuned on MANGO with adaptors.
- **Minority reviewer contrast**: Reviewer 4 (score 8, soundness 4, presentation 4) is very positive and finds the benchmark novel, technically sound, and useful. The other three reviewers (scores 6, 5, 6) are more critical, with Reviewer 2 (score 5) rating contribution as 2 and soundness as 2, and Reviewers 1 and 3 (score 6) raising significant concerns about novelty and real-world relevance. This disagreement underscores the contested value of the benchmark despite its careful construction and analysis.
