## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a scalable ensemble diversification method (HDR) that uses a hardness measure based on cross-entropy loss to identify OOD-like samples from the training set and encourages ensemble members to disagree on them, without requiring external OOD data. Additional components include a predictive diversity score (PDS) for OOD detection, stochastic sum, and shallow disagreement to reduce computational cost. Experiments on ImageNet evaluate OOD generalization (output-space and weight-space ensembles) and OOD detection.

### Strengths
- The research direction is promising: scaling ensemble diversification to large-scale data while minimizing dependence on external OOD data.
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow; the abstract is particularly clear.
- The method is efficient, notably requiring only two models per batch even for a 50-model ensemble.
- The analysis justifying adaptive weights (Section 2.2) and quantifying diversity (Table 1) is well-executed.
- Additionally proposed components – PDS, stochastic sum, and shallow disagreement – seem practical.
- Extensive experiments across two tasks (OOD generalization and OOD detection) demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method.
- The motivation to encourage diversity on hard samples for single-domain generalization is reasonable and easy to understand.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and related work:** The novel contribution is considered not significant. The hardness measure based on cross-entropy loss is not adequately linked to prior work (e.g., hard example mining). The paper lacks discussion of how this approach differs from such existing directions.
- **Justification of hard examples as OOD proxy:** It is not clearly explained why hard examples from the training set are a valid proxy for OOD data. Using hard examples as OOD samples while the main objective enforces agreement on all training data creates potential contradiction.
- **Conflicting objectives on the same data point:** A main concern is the simultaneous presence of agreement (cross-entropy) and disagreement terms on the same training sample, which is labeled as OOD by the hardness criterion but also used for ID training. The membership status (ID vs. OOD) of each sample can fluctuate at each epoch; analyzing this dynamic (e.g., which samples are persistently hard, how membership shift frequency affects performance) is missing and would provide valuable insight.
- **Limited performance gains:** Performance improvements over baselines are modest. In Table 2, 3 out of 5 domains show only marginal improvements, and OOD generalization results are often close to a single model. No analysis is given for why gains are limited on certain domains.
- **Missing experimental details and ablations:**
  - The meaning of "Div HPs" in tables is not clarified in captions (though explained in text).
  - The weight values applied in Table 2 are missing, and an ablation study exploring how varying these weights impacts performance is requested.
  - The baseline methods DivDis and A2D are missing from some tables (e.g., DivDis absent from Table 1; both missing from Table 2 rows 5 and 6).
- **Missing comparisons and experiments:**
  - Performance on widely used single-domain generalization benchmarks (e.g., PACS, DomainNet) would allow comparison with more baselines, including ZooD.
  - An experiment in a domain where obtaining unlabeled OOD data is challenging (e.g., medical imaging) would better demonstrate the advantage of not requiring external OOD data.
- **Scalability and method justification:**
  - Using only 2 models per batch for a 50-model ensemble needs more explanation of how effective diversification is achieved.
  - The advantages of stochastic sum and shallow disagreement over the parallel computation strategy used in DivDis are not clearly articulated.
  - It is unclear what distinguishes the proposed method from existing approaches that use explicit OOD data, given that obtaining unlabeled data for natural images is often not difficult.
- **Presentation:** The paper is described as a bit unkind (e.g., missing table annotations); one reviewer found presentation a weakness (score 2).
