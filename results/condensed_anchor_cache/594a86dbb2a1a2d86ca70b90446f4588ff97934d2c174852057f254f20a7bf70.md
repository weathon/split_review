## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces 3DMRL, a pre-training framework for Molecular Relational Learning (MRL). It constructs a 3D virtual interaction environment by placing small molecules around a larger molecule, then uses contrastive learning between 2D and 3D representations along with an intermolecular force prediction task. Experiments on multiple benchmarks show performance improvements across various 2D MRL models.

### Strengths
- Novel introduction of 3D geometric pre-training specifically for MRL (R1, R2).
- Efficient and reproducible construction of the virtual interaction dataset (R2).
- Significant performance gains across diverse downstream tasks and multiple 2D backbone models (R1, R3, R4).
- Paper is well-written and clearly structured (R3, R4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Chemical validity of the virtual interaction environment**  
   - (R1) The 3D GNN cutoff may truncate long-range intermolecular forces and does not differentiate intermolecular interactions from chemical bonds.  
   - (R3) The fixed number of small molecules (n=5) lacks scientific basis; the heuristic selection of atoms only from non-aromatic rings ignores that aromatic rings can also participate; the positioning method lacks chemical justification.  
   - (R4) Simply moving molecules together with random noise is far from realistic simulation of complex molecular interactions.

2. **Questionable pseudo force definition and its utility**  
   - (R2, R4) The pseudo force label defined as the vector direction between atoms of different molecules lacks theoretical motivation and is artificial; (R3) real intermolecular forces (e.g., hydrogen bonds, van der Waals) vary in magnitude and type, so this simple direction-based definition is questionable.  
   - (R1) Obtaining accurate force labels is costly, and force-field‐generated labels may be unsuitable.  
   - (R2, R3) The force prediction task contributes only marginal performance gains, further raising doubts about its value.

3. **Ambiguity in contrastive learning and comparison to prior methods**  
   - (R1) Aligning multiple 3D solvent representations to the same 2D representation introduces ambiguity, potentially causing feature alignment that deviates from true chemical/physical properties.  
   - (R2) The ‘only cont.’ variant outperforms prior 2D–3D contrastive methods (GraphMVP, 3D Infomax) without a clear explanation of the primary distinction.  
   - (R4) The rationale for what specific interaction information is learned via the contrastive task is unclear.

4. **Lack of clarity on experimental details and missing comparisons**  
   - (R1, R4) How are initial positions and molecular pairs selected? What relation exists between the two molecules in each pair?  
   - (R1) For datasets without energy labels (e.g., Chromophore, DDI), how are force labels obtained?  
   - (R1) Does the ratio of large to small molecules affect results?  
   - (R3) Why are only the 2D representations used in downstream tasks instead of the 3D representations?  
   - (R2) Notation issue: output of ‘Projection’ function is given as $\mathbb{R}^d$ instead of $\mathbb{R}^3$ (Line 307).  
   - (R3) Include purely 3D methods (e.g., Uni-mol) as additional baselines.

5. **Potential view as data augmentation**  
   - (R3) The virtual interaction geometry could be seen merely as data augmentation; the paper should clarify whether random perturbations or other augmentations would be equally effective, and what specific factors make the proposed construction beneficial.

*Reviewer disagreement:* Two reviewers (R1, R3) rated the paper 5, praising its novelty and empirical gains; two reviewers (R2, R4) rated 3, expressing strong reservations about chemical validity and theoretical justification of the core components. The weaknesses above reflect concerns from all reviewers, with the more critical reviewers placing greater emphasis on the lack of realism and justification.
