## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Activation Prompting (AP), which extends input-level Visual Prompting (VP) by applying learnable universal perturbations to intermediate activation maps within a pretrained model. The authors claim AP generalizes VP, is closely related to normalization tuning, and provides theoretical insights into layer-specific behavior. Extensive experiments on 29 datasets across CNNs and ViTs are conducted. Reviewers note the writing is clear and easy to follow, but a majority (scores 3,3,3) find the novelty limited, the distinction from Visual Prompt Tuning (VPT) unconvincing, and the empirical gains insufficient. One reviewer scored 6, praising the approach as novel and highlighting its targeted customization and extensive evaluations.

### Strengths

- The paper is clearly written and well-structured, with intuitive figures that showcase the proposed method (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- The theoretical analysis offers valuable insights into data complexity and the relationship between layer depth and prompt application (Reviewer 1).
- AP introduces a targeted, layer-specific customization that improves VP’s effectiveness and better adapts to different model architectures (Reviewer 3).
- Extensive evaluations across 29 diverse datasets and models (CNNs and ViTs) underscore AP’s adaptability and robustness (Reviewer 3).
- The findings, including the contrasting layer preferences between ResNets and ViTs, are interesting (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited novelty and confusion with VPT**: AP is essentially identical to VPT, or a special case of VPT (Reviewers 1, 2). VPT-deep (Jia et al.) already studied adding prompts to intermediate features; lines 172–192 are well-covered by VPT (Reviewer 2). The authors’ discussion (lines 67–86) trying to distinguish AP from VPT is confusing and not substantial (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- **White-box vs. black-box assumption**: AP requires white-box access to internal layers, whereas traditional VP operates in a black-box setting (Reviewer 1, 3). This makes the claim that AP generalizes VP overstated (Reviewer 1). AP may be more aligned with VPT than with VP.
- **No performance gain over baselines**: In Table 4, AP outperforms none of the listed baselines (Reviewer 2). The comparison of efficiency (time, parameters, memory) is meaningless when performance is degraded (Reviewer 2). On ViT-B/16 (Table A2), AP achieves only middle-level performance with middle parameter usage (Reviewer 4).
- **Layer selection instability**: The observed layer preferences (line 245) require grid search across layers to find the best index; Figure 4 shows performance varies significantly with layer index, potentially leading to unstable training (Reviewer 4). The claimed clear separation might mislead the community (Reviewer 4).
- **Limited effectiveness on small models**: AP’s performance is not pronounced on small models like ResNet-18 or ViT-Tiny (Reviewer 3).
- **Weak connection between theory and AP design**: The theoretical contributions are interesting, but their link to the design of AP is insufficiently strong (Reviewer 1). The discussion of AP and normalization tuning alone does not make a large contribution (Reviewer 2).
- **Writing and unsupported claims**: Much of the introduction (lines 57–65 and surrounding) contains hand-wavy, unsupported claims (Reviewer 2). Some claims (e.g., that PT is inferior to fine-tuning) are not entirely accurate (Reviewer 3, referencing “Facing the Elephant in the Room”).
- **Missing empirical analysis**: More analysis on computational latency is needed since AP requires a larger parameter budget than VP (Reviewer 3). The reliance on pretrained model size may pose overfitting risks in few-shot learning (Reviewer 3).
- **Experiments on non-common models**: Main experiments use ResNet-101 and ViT-Large/16, which are not standard practice when comparing with VPT (Reviewer 4). Abbreviations like FGCV (should be FGVC) appear (Reviewer 4).
