## Merged Review

### Summary
Three reviewers evaluated the paper, which proposes using meta-models (neural networks that take another network’s parameters as input) to automate mechanistic interpretability. The meta-model is applied to reverse‑engineer RASP programs from transformer weights and to detect backdoors in small models (average 3,000 parameters). The meta-model achieves >99% accuracy on backdoor identification. Reviewer scores range from 5 to 1, reflecting strong disagreement about the paper’s soundness and contribution. Reviewer 1 (score 5, confidence 3) finds the approach interesting and the variety of tasks appealing, but is fundamentally skeptical about using a black‑box model to explain another. Reviewer 2 (score 3, confidence 3) praises open‑source contributions and high accuracy but criticizes data dependence, model size, and task scope. Reviewer 3 (score 1, confidence 5) states the amount of work is insufficient and the paper is less than 9 pages long.

### Strengths
- **Novel approach to an important problem**: Using a meta‑model to predict properties of a function being interpreted is a creative direction; the use of the tracr library is a good fit for the tasks. (Reviewer 1)
- **Variety of tasks**: The method is demonstrated on RASP program recovery (via next‑token prediction) and backdoor detection. (Reviewer 1)
- **Open‑source contribution**: The paper commits to releasing datasets, enabling replication and extension. (Reviewer 2)
- **High accuracy**: The meta‑model achieves over 99% accuracy in backdoor identification. (Reviewer 2)

### Weaknesses
- **Fundamental limitation of explaining black boxes with black boxes**: The meta‑model itself is an uninterpretable neural network, so its outputs must be trusted rather than verified. The approach does not solve the interpretability problem—e.g., the meta‑model could learn spurious signals or contain its own backdoors. Reviewer 1 considers this a “non‑starter” for reliability. (Reviewer 1, high severity)
- **Unclear what constitutes a mechanistic interpretation for backdoor detection**: The paper does not explain how the meta‑model’s classification (backdoor vs. clean) provides interpretability. A proper mechanistic explanation would be input‑specific (e.g., which inputs trigger the backdoor). (Reviewer 1)
- **Scalability concerns – model size**: All experiments use small models (average 3,000 parameters). The paper does not address how the meta‑model would scale to large models (millions or billions of parameters), nor does it test on realistic architectures (e.g., ImageNet‑sized vision transformers). (Reviewers 1 & 2)
- **Scalability concerns – training data**: The meta‑model requires many pairs of (neural network weights, program representation). Generating this data is computationally expensive (each pair may require training a separate network). This dependence on extensive training data limits applicability. (Reviewer 2)
- **Scope of tasks is too simple**: The evaluated tasks (RASP program recovery, backdoor detection on small models) are far simpler than the full problem of reverse‑engineering large, real‑world neural networks. Generalizability to complex scenarios is unproven. (Reviewer 2)
- **Missing baseline comparisons and ablation**: The paper does not include enough baselines to validate the meta‑model’s performance relative to existing methods. Adding more baselines is needed. (Reviewer 2)
- **Insufficient example verification**: The paper lacks concrete examples (e.g., in the appendix) comparing the original tracr program with the meta‑model’s reconstruction. (Reviewer 1)
- **Potential over‑fitting to tracr’s sparse weights**: Transformers compiled via tracr produce much sparser weights than SGD‑trained models. The meta‑model’s success on tracr may not transfer to realistic, dense weights. (Reviewer 1)
- **Unclear path to practical deployment**: It is not explained how the meta‑model approach could be used on real models (e.g., a vision transformer) given the need for many training examples of such models. (Reviewer 1)
- **Insufficient work volume**: The paper is less than 9 pages and the amount of work is not enough. (Reviewer 3, very high confidence)

*Note: Reviewer 3 (score 1, confidence 5) is far more negative than the other reviewers on overall work quantity and quality. Reviewer 1 (score 5) acknowledges the approach’s promise but expresses deep reservations about reliability, while Reviewer 2 (score 3) is cautiously positive but points out significant scalability and data issues.*
