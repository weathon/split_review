## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Mul2vec, a framework to learn modality-free semantic representations of entities under incomplete data (noisy/missing modalities). It uses neural tensor factorization (NTF) and two training objectives (DLM, ILM) to align contextual and semantic representations. Experiments on image-text retrieval using COCO and Flickr30 datasets show performance gains over baselines.

### Strengths

- Tackles an important practical problem: learning multi-modal representations robust to missing or noisy modality information.
- Mul2vec learns representations that are invariant to missing data and is compatible with pre-trained models, showing performance gains on downstream retrieval tasks (Tables 2, 3, 6).
- The method can visualize semantic subtraction across modalities (e.g., algebraic operations), indicating some cross-modal semantic understanding.
- Successfully proposes a novel framework using entity ID-text-word tuples instead of modality-specific feature pairs.

### Weaknesses

- **Poor writing and undefined terminology:** Many typos/grammar errors (e.g., “prameter” in Fig. 1). Acronyms (NTF, DLM, ILM, I2T, T2I) are introduced without explanation. Key terms like “entity,” “DID,” “D,” “L,” “contextual vs. semantic information” are never clearly defined or distinguished. Entities (IDs) are used as inputs, but it is unclear how random IDs can yield meaningful representations (Reviewer 3 is completely confused on this point). The difference between “text” and “words” in the (entity ID, text, words) tuple is not explained.
- **Core components borrowed from prior work:** NTF was proposed in Wu et al. (2017); ILM and DLM training objectives are from previous works, significantly weakening the novelty.
- **Limited and incomplete experimental evaluation:**
  - Only evaluated on image-text retrieval (two datasets: COCO, Flickr30). No other downstream tasks (e.g., classification, captioning) are tested despite claims of learning general semantic representations.
  - Despite the paper’s focus on incomplete data, **no ablation studies** are performed to show robustness to different levels of missing/noisy modalities. The main claim is not empirically supported.
  - In Tables 3 and 6, only the performance *difference* is reported, not absolute numbers, making it difficult to assess overall quality of the method.
- **Method unclear for multi-modal and pre-trained model integration:** Sections 3.3 and 3.4 focus almost entirely on text. It is not explained how multiple modalities (e.g., images) are handled, how their different feature spaces are reconciled, or how pre-trained multi-modal models are integrated (only “simple modifications” are mentioned without details).
- **Confusing embedding design:** The model learns two sets of embeddings (e in 3.4, g in 3.3). Their relationship is unspecified; it is unclear which set is used for evaluation in Table 2 and why images use e while text uses g during retrieval.
- **Discussion/limitations section includes models, datasets, and results not discussed elsewhere in the paper.**
- Equations and notation are poorly explained (e.g., negative vector e_n, e_p relation to v_p, “gaps between texts” in 3.3, how topics are obtained in Table 4). Algebraic operations used to verify representation learning are not elaborated.
- The supplement references a figure that does not exist in the document.
