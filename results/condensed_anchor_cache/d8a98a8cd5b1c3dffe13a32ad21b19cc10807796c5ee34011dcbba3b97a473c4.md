## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Welfare Diplomacy (WD), a general-sum variant of the zero-sum board game Diplomacy that balances military conquest and domestic welfare to benchmark cooperative capabilities. Contributions include: (1) WD rules and open-source engine implementation; (2) baseline agents using zero-shot prompted language models; (3) experiments showing state-of-the-art models achieve high social welfare but are exploitable. Reviewers agree the concept is interesting and the environment is clearly motivated, but concerns about rigor of motivation, theoretical analysis, experimental depth, and stability of equilibria were raised.

### Strengths
- The paper is clearly written and easy to follow (R1).
- Effectively explains differences between Standard Diplomacy (SD) and Welfare Diplomacy, showing how rule changes incentivize peace and cooperation (R1, R2).
- Provides a theoretical analysis (Nash equilibrium analysis of WD) that verifies the reasonability of the proposed environment (R1).
- The proposed game and prompts are open-sourced, and experimental results are extensive (R2).
- Develops a language model (LM) scaffolding system to create competent zero-shot baseline agents for WD (R3).
- The new environment facilitates benchmarking of agent cooperation ability, with experiments successfully differentiating models (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation rigor**: The paper repeatedly claims Standard Diplomacy is zero-sum and incentivizes cooperation-undermining capabilities, but prior work [1] notes that SD involves both cooperation and competition, so it is not necessarily zero-sum. This weakens the motivation for WD as a distinct benchmark (R2, most critical reviewer).
- **Theoretical clarity**: The Nash equilibrium analysis in Section 3.2.1 and Theorem 1 is hard to interpret. The meaning of π^k as a NE and how Theorem 1 relates to the main claim need clarification (R2). Additionally, the gap between the theoretical simplification and the real WD game is not elaborated (R1).
- **Experimental depth**: Missing ablation studies on prompt sensitivity and hyperparameter effects (R1). Also, human-LLM mixed experiments are absent; they would help understand how human engagement influences LLM performance (R1). 
- **Policy analysis**: No human analysis of LLM policy outputs to better understand performance (R1). Regarding baseline agents, it remains unclear why most models (except advanced LLMs like GPT-4) cannot achieve policies significantly better than random baseline (R1).
- **Technical detail clarity**: The terms "zero-shot prompted language model", "zero-shot baseline", and "zero-shot evaluations" are used without specific explanation (R2). Construction of the exploiter agent is not justified, and it is not shown whether better exploitative agents exist (R2).
- **Metric concern**: The "basic proficiency" metric includes "fraction of global SCs owned by any player and not left neutral" as a component; this seems more like a social welfare measure than an indicator of basic proficiency (R2).
- **Equilibrium stability**: Pareto-efficient Nash equilibria are often unstable in complex scenarios, with deviations possible due to imperfect information, externalities, or strategic behavior. How to effectively tackle this challenge is unclear (R3). Attaining Pareto-efficient equilibria and achieving optimal Nash welfare remains difficult (R3).
- **Language communication role**: The paper does not fully clarify why LLM-based agents are necessary for Welfare Diplomacy if optimal actions could theoretically be derived. Language may help in bargaining and equilibrium selection, but other benefits are not explored (R1).
