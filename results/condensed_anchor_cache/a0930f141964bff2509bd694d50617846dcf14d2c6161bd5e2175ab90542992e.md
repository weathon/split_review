## Merged Review

### Summary

MissDiff proposes a diffusion-based framework for learning from tabular data with missing values, using a masked score-matching objective to avoid the biases of impute-then-generate pipelines. The paper provides theoretical connections to likelihood bounds and experiments on multiple tabular datasets under MCAR, MAR, and NMAR scenarios. Reviewer assessments are mixed: one finds the framework rigorous and impactful (score 8), one moderately positive (score 6), one sees limited novelty and theoretical gaps (score 5), and one questions the proof and practical advantage (score 3).

### Strengths

- **Novelty and intuition**: The method is novel and avoids two-stage inference (impute then generate), reducing potential biases introduced during imputation (R1). The one-shot approach is algorithmically simpler than prior two-stage methods (R1).  
- **Theoretical contribution**: The paper provides theoretical justification by minimizing an upper bound on the KL divergence between true and learned distributions (R1). R1 found this compelling; other reviewers disagreed (see Weaknesses).  
- **Avoids adversarial training**: Unlike GAN-based approaches (e.g., GAIN, misGAN, Yoon et al. 2018, Li et al. 2019), MissDiff does not require unstable adversarial training (R1).  
- **Well-written and motivated**: The paper is clearly written and the motivation for handling missing values in tabular data is well presented (R2).  
- **Experimental strengths**:
  - Extensive experiments with varying missingness rates, incomplete columns, and incomplete rows (R4).
  - Evaluation under MCAR, MAR, and NMAR scenarios (R2, R4).
  - Promising simulation results (R2).
  - Demonstrated performance advantages over existing approaches (R2).
- **General framework potential**: The framework is rigorous and general, expected to benefit tabular ML and future multimodal models combining textual and tabular data (R4). It is the first neural-network-based diffusion approach for missingness that does not rely on GBDT-specific assumptions, enabling broader applicability (R4).

### Weaknesses

- **Motivation for generation vs. imputation** (R1): The paper does not adequately motivate why generating new samples (beyond imputation) is necessary, nor why imputation alone is insufficient. Specific examples would strengthen the motivation.
- **Missing comparisons with related generative imputation methods** (R1):
  - GAIN (Yoon et al. 2018) and misGAN (Li et al. 2019) both train a generator that outputs complete data; they can likely be straightforwardly extended to generate new samples by setting the mask to all zeros (R1, questions 2–3). The paper should acknowledge these as earlier unified imputation/generation algorithms and compare with them experimentally.
  - misGAN is conceptually similar: it trains a generator to match observed data after masking, minimizing a divergence between \(p_{obs}\) and \(p_\theta\). The KL divergence in Theorem 3.3 is similar to misGAN’s objective up to a constant. The paper should discuss similarities and differences (R1).
  - Another relevant work uses normalizing flows to maximize observed likelihood (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.12628) that could also be extended to generation (R1).
- **Theoretical justification of imputation** (R1, R3):
  - R1 questions why the imputation algorithm (Algorithm 2) works. Training adds noise only to observed coordinates (due to the mask), so the learned score model seems to be a *marginal score* rather than a conditional score. The justification for using this joint score model for imputation is unclear; the paper should derive the optimal solution of Eq. 5 and the corresponding forward/backward process.
  - R3 argues that MissDiff is itself an impute-then-generate method: observed data are imputed with zeros, then trained with a mask to downweight loss. Why does this not suffer from the same inconsistency issues that the paper attributes to other impute-then-generate pipelines?
  - R3 finds the proof of Theorem 3.2 unconvincing. The claim that the score function of the Gaussian product recovers the complete-data score does not hold for \(s_\theta(x(t), t)\); imputing the i-th element with zero changes all elements of the score estimate. The derivation in lines 773–778 involves an expectation over \(p(x^{obs}_0, m)\), which conditions the score network on observed data, but the proof attempts to relate this to the complete-data score \(x_t\) which is not directly accessible during training. The justification for recovering the complete-data score via the masked objective is unclear.
- **Overstated theoretical claims** (R2):
  - The paper claims to provide a "general framework" for learning on incomplete data, but missing data assumptions (MCAR, MAR, MNAR) are crucial for statistical efficiency and identifiability, and these are not discussed in the theoretical analysis. R2 asks: How do the theoretical guarantees change under different missing mechanisms? What assumptions does MissDiff implicitly make? Can partial identifiability be linked to the diffusion framework? Without such analysis, the claim of a "general framework" is premature.
  - R2 notes that ignoring observations with missing elements can lead to invalid inference and loss of efficiency; the paper does not address how MissDiff avoids these issues or how it benefits users in the absence of explicit missingness assumptions.
- **Limited technical novelty** (R2, minor): The algorithm is essentially a masked score-matching loss on top of standard diffusion models, which is a minor adaptation (R2).
- **Missing experiments and comparisons**:
  - **MAR and NMAR baselines** (R2): In the experiments on these settings (Table 4, Appendix C2), MICE is not compared despite being a strong baseline. R2 expects MICE to be competitive.
  - **ForestDiffusion benchmark** (R4): The paper should evaluate on the larger and more diverse 27-dataset benchmark from ForestDiffusion (https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07738). This benchmark shows MissForest outperforming GAIN (which is more plausible than the paper’s current benchmark). Comparison with DiffPuter and UnmaskingTrees would also be valuable.
  - **High-dimensional data** (R3): The paper does not demonstrate the method’s effectiveness on high-dimensional data (e.g., images). R3 notes no challenge in scaling, so providing image imputation results would strengthen the claim.
- **Related work omissions** (R4): Works using GBDTs to avoid impute-then-generate (ForestDiffusion, DiffPuter, UnmaskingTrees) are not discussed. These may be parallel works, but the paper should clarify whether prior diffusion-based approaches can be seen as instances of the proposed framework.
- **Implementation and presentation details** (R4):
  - Line 351: The baseline "MICE based on random forest (MissForest)" needs clarification: is it MICE-Forest or true MissForest?
  - The main text should indicate that a neural network architecture is used (rather than leaving all details to the appendix).
