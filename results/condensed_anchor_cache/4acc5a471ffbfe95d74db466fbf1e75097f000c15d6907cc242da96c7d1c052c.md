## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper argues that existing membership inference (MI) attack evaluations for foundation models are flawed because members and non-members are sampled from different distributions. The authors analyze nine published MI evaluation datasets and show that *blind* attacks—which do not query the target model—outperform state-of-the-art MI attacks by exploiting distributional shifts such as temporal differences, data replication biases, and distinguishable tails. They recommend using datasets with minimal distribution shifts (e.g., Pile, DataComp) for future evaluations.

### Strengths
- The paper highlights an important and timely issue: current MI evaluation datasets often introduce distribution shifts between members and non-members, making attack success measures unreliable.
- It provides a systematic evaluation of nine public MI evaluation datasets (both LLM and VLM) and identifies three common sources of distribution mismatch (temporal shifts, replication biases, distinguishable tails) with empirical support.
- The experimental results are extensive and convincingly demonstrate that simple blind attacks (e.g., date detection, bag-of-words classifiers, greedy rare word selection) can outperform state-of-the-art MI attacks (e.g., LiRA, RMIA) on these biased datasets.
- The paper is well written and supports most claims with empirical evidence.

### Weaknesses
- **Originality and prior work overlap.** The core finding—that distribution shifts between members and non-members inflate MIA performance—has been established in prior and concurrent works (Duan et al. 2024, Maini et al. 2024). The paper frames these as “concurrent” despite being available 4 to 8 months before the deadline, making the contribution incremental and largely a measurement study rather than providing new technical insights.
- **Definition of “blind” attacks is misleading.** The proposed blind attacks are not blind to the dataset: date detection exploits temporal annotations, bag-of-words uses 80% of labeled member/non-member data to train a classifier, and greedy rare word selection uses test-set metrics to select features. These attacks incorporate significant domain knowledge about data collection patterns, which is not typically available to a truly blind adversary (see Hui et al. 2021 for the standard definition). This gives them an unfair advantage over existing MI attacks that aim to be general-purpose.
- **Overstated claims.** The conclusion (L413, L464–465) dismisses all current MI evaluations, but the experiments only show that blind attacks exploit dataset biases, not that state-of-the-art attacks capture *no* membership signal. The claim that MIAs “fail to extract meaningful membership information” is not tested on less biased datasets (e.g., Pile, DataComp). If SOTA attacks perform near random on such datasets, the claim would be stronger, but no such experiments are provided.
- **Lack of experiments on unbiased datasets.** The paper recommends Pile and DataComp for future work but does not apply blind attacks on them to verify they are indeed unbiased. Without this, it is unclear whether recommended datasets also suffer from distribution shifts.
- **Insufficient experimental robustness.** Experiments are performed on one fixed split of members/non-members. As noted by Dubinski et al. (2024), results can vary significantly with different splits; reporting only a single run without variance undermines reliability.
- **Missing experimental details.** The paper does not specify which target models were used for the MI attacks, nor does it clarify whether the “Ours” blind attack results in Table 2 are measured on the same filtered samples (e.g., only texts with dates) as the “Best Attack” results taken from original papers. This makes the comparison potentially unfair. Additionally, Table 2 omits AUC-ROC for some entries and does not distinguish between LLM and VLM datasets.
- **Limited practical implications beyond pointing out flaws.** The proposed blind attacks exploit dataset construction artifacts rather than model behavior, so they do not actually measure membership leakage from the model. The paper neither proposes a robust evaluation methodology nor provides deeper analysis of model behavior, which limits its contribution for top-tier venues.
- **Generalizability concerns.** Blind attacks based on temporal or lexical biases may not transfer to other domains (e.g., vision-based foundation models), where similar easily exploitable gaps may not exist. The paper focuses heavily on text-based models.
- **Weakness in auditing unlearning claim.** The statement that MI attacks can audit unlearning (L467) is undercut by prior work showing that even with proper train-test splits, MI attacks do not work well for unlearning auditing.
- **Oversimplified assumptions.** The assumption about future dates (L154) ignores legitimate cases in fiction, climate research, and policy documents. The protocol for greedy rare word selection appears to use test data to sort words by TPR/FPR, which would leak information from the test set into attack design.
- **Structural issues.** The experimental section is organized by dataset rather than by the three identified distribution shift types, making it hard to map claims to evidence. The introduction focuses on blind attack success rather than on the core flaw of biased evaluation.
