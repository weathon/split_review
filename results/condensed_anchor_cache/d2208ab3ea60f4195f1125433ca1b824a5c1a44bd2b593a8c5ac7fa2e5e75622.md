## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces NetFormer, a Transformer-based model that uses a linearized attention mechanism to estimate time-varying (nonstationary) connectivity between neurons from population activity recordings. It treats each neuron’s recent history as a token and learns a state-dependent attention matrix \( \mathbf{A}_k = \tilde{\mathbf{X}}_k \mathbf{W}_Q \mathbf{W}_K^\top \tilde{\mathbf{X}}_k^\top \). The model is validated on synthetic data (including spike-timing-dependent plasticity and task-trained RNNs), a Mexican-hat connectivity test, and real calcium-imaging recordings from mouse visual cortex, where it predicts neural activity and recovers cell-type-specific connectivity matching separate ground-truth physiology. Reviewers broadly praised the conceptualization and significance but raised concerns about baseline comparisons, interpretability in practice, positional encoding rationale, reproducibility, and limited evaluation scope. Scores were 8, 8, 6, 8; one reviewer (R3) was notably more critical.

### Strengths

- Addresses a genuine gap in neuroscience tools by providing an interpretable method for recovering nonstationary connectivity, whereas most existing methods assume static weights. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- The conceptualization of using linearized attention for dynamically evolving connectivity is well-conceived and bridges limitations of static models. (R3)
- The method is simple and interpretable in its mathematical formulation, directly encoding time-varying connections without softmax nonlinearities. (R2, R4)
- Thorough validation on both simulated and real data: synthetic tasks with known ground truth (STDP, task-trained RNNs, Mexican-hat connectivity), and real calcium-imaging recordings. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- Robust performance demonstrated by comparing against an oracle baseline and outperforming static baselines, especially in synthetic tasks with full control. (R3, R4)
- Demonstrates auxiliary utility, such as inferring cell type from learned positional embeddings. (R4)
- Represent one of the first actionable applications of Transformer models in systems neuroscience. (R1)
- Theoretical and experimental validations together support the model’s relevance for biological experiments. (R1, R2)
- R1 later noted that substantial revisions (addition of task-trained RNNs, Mexican-hat experiment, baselines [2] and [7]) made the paper a solid contribution.

### Weaknesses

- **Weak baselines and missing comparisons**: The baselines used are predominantly classical methods or static RNNs. Several reviewers noted the lack of comparisons with methods that can account for time-varying connectivity, including:
  - Low-tensor-rank learning (Pellegrino et al. 2023, [2]) (R1)
  - The method of Sani et al. 2024 ([7]) for Fig. 4 (R1)
  - Time-varying linear models estimated over a sliding window via OLS, or their nonlinear versions (R4)
  - Recent models such as Neural ODEs, dynamic GNNs, Neural Data Transformers, and GLMs with state-switching (R3)
  These omissions weaken claims of state-of-the-art performance. (R3, R4)

- **Interpretability in practice remains limited**: Although the model is mathematically interpretable, several reviewers found it difficult to track how specific interactions are captured in real neural data.
  - The relationship between learned attention weights and known neural mechanisms or connectivity patterns needs deeper biological contextualization. (R2, R3)
  - R3 suggested visualizations linking attention weights to neural trajectories or connectivity structures (e.g., case studies) to enhance transparency.
  - R4 cautioned that the estimated connectivity, being a generalized covariance of recent neural activity, may be especially susceptible to spuriously inferring connections between unconnected but correlated neurons (Das & Fiete 2020). This scenario was not explicitly tested; R4 recommended a synthetic Mexican-hat connectivity test with white driving noise (the authors later added this, addressing R4’s concern).

- **Positional encoding rationale unclear**: The paper applies positional encoding across the spatial dimension of multi-neuron time series, but it is not based on physical neuron locations. The motivation for using positional embeddings to distinguish neurons, and specifically to classify excitatory/inhibitory types, is inadequately justified. (R3)

- **Reproducibility concerns**: No code repository or scripts are provided; only pseudocode in supplementary material is available. This raises concerns about independent verification. (R3)

- **Limited evaluation scope**: 
  - The real neural data is restricted to calcium imaging, which has lower temporal resolution than electrophysiology, potentially limiting applicability for capturing fast synaptic interactions. (R3)
  - Additional testing on datasets with varying noise levels and different recording modalities (e.g., electrophysiology) is needed to assess robustness and generalizability. (R2, R3)
  - Scalability to larger datasets (e.g., thousands of neurons) and the effect of partial observability are not addressed. (R2)
  - R1 questioned whether the dataset (hundreds of neurons) qualifies as “large-scale” and noted that real large-scale datasets often have 10k–millions of neurons.

- **Specific validation gaps and missing clarifications**:
  - R1 asked for a clearer explanation of how Fig. 5’s results are consistent with Fu et al. 2014, and suggested adding a comparison panel.
  - R4 questioned the details of the cell type–averaged postsynaptic potential comparison in Table 1: the ground truth comes from a single patch-clamp condition, yet the model predicts state-dependent connectivity. R4 asked whether the ground truth reflects an average over behavioral states, and requested supplementary figures showing fitted connectivity vs. ground truth for all models and activity traces.
  - R4 also noted that the reported MSE uses the average connectivity (over time) against the ground truth, and asked whether the average MSE of the time-varying connectivity (penalizing deviations at all times) would be a more appropriate metric.
  - R2 asked whether using different nonlinear activation functions significantly alters model performance.

- **Complexity and readability**: The mathematical foundations may be too dense for a broader neuroscience audience; more illustrations would help. (R2, R3)

- **Minority reviewer (R3, score 6) was more critical** than the others (scores 8), particularly citing insufficient baseline comparisons, interpretability challenges, unclear positional encoding, reproducibility issues, and dataset specificity. R3 stated that if these concerns are addressed, the score could be increased.
