## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces TF-HOT, a training-free optimization pipeline that uses differentiable rendering and off-the-shelf 2D perception models (MMPose, SAM2) to reconstruct hand‑object pose trajectories from RGB‑D videos. The method requires no annotated dataset for training, relies on a known 3D object shape model, and is evaluated on a subset of DexYCB and a self‑collected in‑the‑wild dataset. An application in imitation learning is demonstrated where extracted trajectories are used to learn dexterous manipulation policies that outperform RL and IL baselines.

### Strengths
1. **Training‑free and generalizable**: The method does not require human‑annotated datasets and can handle any object category (performance depends on the off‑the‑shelf models and heuristic weights). This training‑free nature helps generalization and makes the approach adaptable to diverse environments. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4)
2. **Clear writing and intuitive concept**: The paper is well‑written and the optimization approach is intuitive. (Reviewer 1)
3. **Interesting PTF algorithm**: The proposed “Pose Trajectory Following” (PTF) algorithm is interesting and shows potential for imitation learning. (Reviewer 1; other reviewers note the demonstration is useful but question its novelty.)
4. **Ablation study**: The paper provides an ablation study that demonstrates the complementarity of the optimization terms, which collectively improve performance. (Reviewer 3)
5. **Practical applicability**: The application of TF‑HOT to imitation learning shows real‑world relevance and the method’s effectiveness in learning dexterous manipulation policies. (Reviewers 1, 4)

### Weaknesses
1. **Strong assumption of accurate object shape model**: The method requires a clean, precise 3D object point cloud, which is often unrealistic for in‑the‑wild videos. Even with modern object reconstruction, occlusions and varying lighting produce noisy or incomplete models. This assumption restricts practical applicability and makes the comparison with baselines (e.g., HOISDF which first estimates an object SDF) potentially unfair. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)
2. **Missing comparison with video‑based hand pose estimation methods**: No comparison is made with state‑of‑the‑art methods like HaMeR (CVPR 2024) that directly estimate hand pose from RGB‑D video. This omission makes it difficult to assess the significance of the proposed method relative to existing video‑based approaches. (Reviewers 1, 2)
3. **Limited novelty**: The core optimization stage reuses existing techniques (penetration terms, 2D projection terms, etc.) that have been studied in prior work. The paper does not clearly articulate which of the loss terms are new contributions, and the incremental advance is small given that perception comes entirely from pre‑trained models (SAM, MMPose). (Reviewers 1, 2, 3; Reviewer 1 also asks which losses are the authors’ own.)
4. **Insufficient details on self‑collected datasets**: The in‑the‑wild video datasets lack information about data collection, scene diversity, and types of interactions. This hampers evaluation of generalizability and reproducibility. (Reviewers 1, 2)
5. **Unfair experimental design**: The proposed method uses MMPose predictions to compute \(L_{2D}\) during optimization, while baselines do not have access to this information. The evaluation metrics (J2E, IOU_obj, SD_obj) are directly tied to the optimization objective, creating an information leak that favors TF‑HOT. Additionally, the paper does not follow the evaluation metrics used by the baseline methods (HOTrack, HOISDF) and does not report results on the HO3D dataset, making direct comparison impossible. (Reviewers 3, 4)
6. **Poor methodological presentation**: The method section introduces many loss terms without a high‑level overview. Figure 1 is referenced but not explained, and several symbols are undefined (e.g., \(f(\cdot,\cdot)\) in Eq. 5, the first \(P\) in Eq. 6). This makes the pipeline difficult to understand. (Reviewer 4)
7. **Minor writing issues**: \(L_{\text{surf}}\) should be \(L_{\text{sdf}}\) (line 244); J2E is written as J2D in Table 1; “visibility‑aware” is misspelled as “visible‑aware”. (Reviewers 3, 4)
8. **Concerns about initialization and hand shape**: Hand pose and shape are initialized randomly, whereas off‑the‑shelf pose estimation could be used to initialize the whole sequence. The method does not ensure accurate hand shape, which may artificially inflate error. (Reviewer 3)
9. **Lack of timing/real‑time analysis**: No FPS comparison with learning‑based baselines is provided, leaving the practical applicability of the optimization‑based pipeline unclear. (Reviewer 4)
10. **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: The method uses many hyperparameters (\(\lambda\) and \(w\) values, Table 3). The selection process and difficulty of tuning are not discussed. (Reviewer 4)
11. **Role of imitation learning demonstration unclear**: If the imitation learning application is intended as a main contribution, the RL baselines are not competitive; if it is merely a demonstration, the claim of superiority is overstated. (Reviewer 3)
12. **Counterintuitive ablation result**: Removing the temporal smoothness term leads to large performance decline and severe penetrations (Fig. 6), which seems surprising and is not explained. (Reviewer 3)

**Reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (score 5) considers the method clear and the PTF algorithm interesting, while the other three reviewers (score 3) raise substantial concerns about novelty, evaluation fairness, and practical assumptions.
