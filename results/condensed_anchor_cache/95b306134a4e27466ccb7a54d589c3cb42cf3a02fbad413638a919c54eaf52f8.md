## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper formalizes a Context-aware Bayesian Incentive-Compatible Recommendation Problem (CBICRP) that combines exploration-exploitation trade-off and Bayesian incentive compatibility. It proposes a two-stage algorithm (RCB) – a cold-start exploration stage followed by exploitation via inverse proportional gap sampling – and proves sublinear regret and ε-CBIC. Experiments on simulations and a warfarin dosing dataset are presented. Reviewers differ: two (scores 5,5) find the problem important, the framework reasonable, and the theoretical results sufficient; the other two (scores 3,3) find the motivation unclear, the presentation poor, the experiments insufficient, and the cold-start phase inadequately explained.

### Strengths
- The research questions (exploration-exploitation trade-off, user heterogeneity, incentive compatibility) are critical.  
- The algorithmic framework – decomposing training into a cold-start period and an exploitation period – is intuitive and reasonable.  
- Theoretical analysis provides regret upper bounds and ε-CBIC guarantees.  
- Experiments show that the approach meets Bayesian incentive compatibility and outperforms compared baselines.  
- The method is problem-driven and has potential for wide application.  
- The algorithms are well explained, guaranteeing reproducibility (noted by one reviewer; other reviewers disagree on clarity).  
- The paper studies an important problem with theoretical and application value.  
- It proposes a novel bandit algorithm with regret bound in a simplified recommendation system setup.

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear motivation and problem statement**  
  - The exploration-exploitation trade-off is already extensively studied in recommendation; using it as motivation is too broad.  
  - Bayesian incentive compatibility is not convincingly argued to be a common real problem; the introduction provides no evidence or references.  
  - After reading, the specific technical challenges being addressed remain unclear.  
  - The related work simply enumerates prior work without stating how this paper advances the literature.  
  - The problem setup (lines 141–146) is hard to match to modern industrial recommendation systems (e.g., ads, videos, products).  
  - The warfarin dosing application is confusing: bandit algorithms are iterative, but the real challenge is determining an initial dose; it is unclear how the algorithm helps with that or if it converges faster than physicians.  
  - The practical meaning of terms like τ_𝒫₀, ρ_𝒫₀, and τ_{m₀-1} in the theorems is not explained.

- **Poor presentation and notation**  
  - Many symbols are undefined before use: Iₜ (line 141), OffPos (Algorithm 2), τ_𝒫₀ (Assumption 1).  
  - Notation misuse: L is used both as the upper bound of ‖x‖ and as a probability.  
  - Definition 1 is incomprehensible: where does j come from in Equation (1)? The optimal policy π* has no formal formula.  
  - The notation system is poor; e.g., the policy takes a sigma-algebra as a parameter in the definition, but that parameter disappears in Equation (2).  
  - The key challenge stated at the end of Section 3 is vague and broad.  
  - “Decision length T-free algorithm” (line 091) is unclear.  
  - The appendix is missing, but several contents are said to be there.  
  - One reviewer found the algorithms well explained, but three others note missing definitions and unclear presentation.

- **Insufficient experiments and baselines**  
  - Only two baselines are compared: the Physician Algorithm (a trivial assignment method) and the LASSO bandit (Bastani & Bayati, 2020). More baselines for the exploration-exploitation trade-off are needed.  
  - The claim that the method is “robust against different priors” is not supported; only single priors are tested.  
  - The warfarin application comparison with the LASSO bandit is questionable because that paper is a main reference for the problem; the experimental support for outperforming state-of-the-art is weak.

- **Cold-start phase and parameter update issues**  
  - It is unclear how the algorithm obtains an estimate of the posterior mean for aₜ* in the cold-start stage.  
  - No parameters (e.g., βᵢ) are updated during cold-start; only the prior is applied throughout. This seems irrational if the prior is improper.  
  - Should the algorithm use a mixture of prior and posterior instead of hard truncation (line 253)?  
  - If all arms are pulled N times, does the cold-start phase end? Also, line 258 says “N samples” but should likely be “at least N samples” (if N is a threshold).

- **Exploration-exploitation trade-off not clearly addressed**  
  - In the cold-start phase, the “Promoted Recommendation” and “Organic Recommendation” parts are not novel and do not sufficiently realize the trade-off.  
  - There is no evidence that the method achieves Pareto optimal points or directly addresses the stated challenge.  
  - The authors mention the trade-off as a major challenge but do not show how the proposed method handles it.

- **Other concerns**  
  - The definition of “nonmyopic behavior” of users and what it means for a user to “not adhere to the platform’s recommendations” is unclear in the online recommendation setting.  
  - Statements and assumptions throughout the paper are given without justification or evidence.
