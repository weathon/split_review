## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper theoretically studies the error-regret mismatch problem in reward learning: a learned reward function with low expected error on the training distribution can still yield a policy with high regret when evaluated under the true reward. This issue arises from distributional shift during policy optimization. The paper defines safe and unsafe data distributions for tabular MDPs, and provides both positive and negative results. Key findings: (1) sufficiently low error guarantees low regret, but (2) for any fixed error level, there exist unsafe data distributions that cause error-regret mismatch, even requiring error of order \((S \times A)^{-1}\) to avoid high regret. The analysis extends to regularized policy optimization, including RLHF. The results highlight the need for new reward evaluation methods beyond standard error metrics.

### Strengths

- The paper is well-written and clearly presents a complex theoretical framework, making abstract findings comprehensible (Reviewers 1, 3). However, Reviewers 2 and 4 found the paper dense and not polished (see Weaknesses for details).
- The problem is important and relevant to AI alignment and reward learning in RL (Reviewer 1).
- The theoretical statements appear rigorous and sound (Reviewers 1, 4).
- The analysis is extensive, covering both unregularized and regularized policy optimization, including RLHF, and providing multiple results that address natural questions about the setting (Reviewers 3, 4).
- The figures effectively convey the main message (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty and relation to prior work**: Several reviewers noted that the core insight—low error on the training distribution does not guarantee low regret due to distribution mismatch—is intuitive and follows from well-known lower bounds in offline RL that require coverage assumptions (Reviewers 2, 3, 4). The paper does not clearly differentiate its contributions from existing results, e.g., Sun et al., *The Importance of Online Data: Understanding Preference Fine-tuning via Coverage* (Reviewer 2). The related work section cites many references but does not explain how they relate to the presented results (Reviewer 2).
- **Presentation and clarity**: Although some reviewers found the paper well-written (1, 3), others (2, 4) described it as dense, hard to read, and not polished. Specific complaints include: figures (Fig 1 and 2) are disproportionately large (Reviewers 2, 4); notation is not explained when introduced, e.g., \(\max J_R\) and \(\min J_R\) in Section 2 (Reviewers 2, 4); sentences are unclear, e.g., the discussion of PAC-like bounds and references (Nika et al., 2024; Cen et al., 2024) (Reviewers 2, 4). The writing needs improvement for clarity.
- **Choice of error metric**: The paper assumes a bound on the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) in Eq. (1), but most supervised learning algorithms minimize Mean Squared Error (MSE). The justification for using MAE is not provided (Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of practical guidance and experiments**: The paper does not provide concrete solutions or actionable steps for practitioners to avoid high-regret policies (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). No experimental validation is included to demonstrate the practical significance of the theoretical findings (Reviewers 2, 4). The discussion of alternative reward evaluation methods in Section 7 is not assessed for whether they alleviate the identified issues (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing details and clarifications**:
    - The definition of \(J^R\) (policy evaluation function) is ambiguous: which policy is used? Over what are the max/min taken? (Reviewer 2)
    - The relationship between Proposition 3.1 and 3.3 is unclear: could a data distribution be both unsafe (by Prop 3.3) and safe (by Prop 3.1) for the same \(\epsilon\), leading to a contradiction? This needs clarification (Reviewer 2).
    - The assumptions in Corollary 3.4, particularly the second bullet (supports of policies are completely disjoint), seem too strong, especially when \(\epsilon\) is small and the policy set \(\Pi_L\) is large. A toy example would help illustrate feasibility (Reviewer 2).
    - Line 372: the claim "a provided data distribution is safer than an initial reference policy" is hard to understand (Reviewer 2).
    - Line 402: how can \(\hat{R}\) be defined equal to \(R\) when \(R\) is unknown? (Reviewer 2)
    - Do similar results (Proposition 3.2 and Theorem 3.5) exist for the regularized policy optimization setting? (Reviewer 2)
    - The paper does not discuss whether the results can motivate novel algorithms for reward design (Reviewer 2).
- **Practical relevance of unsafe distribution**: Regarding Definition 2.1, it would be helpful to understand how detrimental unsafe data distributions are in practice, e.g., the likelihood of choosing a "good" versus "bad" reward function under an unsafe distribution (Reviewer 1). A concrete real-world example of an unsafe distribution leading to undesirable behavior would clarify the practical implications (Reviewer 1).
