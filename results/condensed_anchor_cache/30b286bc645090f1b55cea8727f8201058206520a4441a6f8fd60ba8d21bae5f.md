## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper formulates GNN training as a bi-level optimization task to control the trade-off between interpretability (via sparsity) and prediction performance. The outer loop uses reinforcement learning to prune edges or nodes, while the inner loop trains a GNN for graph classification. The trade-off is controlled by a hyperparameter. Experiments on nine chemical graph classification datasets show competitive accuracy against baselines using full graphs, while achieving sparser subgraphs.

### Strengths
- Novel bi-level optimization formulation to explicitly trade off interpretability (sparsity) and performance. (R1)
- Using reinforcement learning is a reasonable approach for the discrete sparsification problem (R2). The variants of pruning nodes vs. edges are meaningful (R3).
- The problem is practical: real graphs contain redundant/noisy edges, and preserving performance with increased sparsity is important (R2).
- The full algorithm and detailed experimental results are provided in the appendix, aiding reproducibility (R2).
- The method works well on multiple benchmarks against the chosen baselines (R3, R4). Reviewer 4 noted the numerical results are generally strong.
- The interpretability challenge in GNNs is important and underexplored; the method gives users flexibility to balance tradeoffs (R4).
- The bilevel formulation and loss functions are presented elegantly and efficiently (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of interpretability validation:** The connection between sparsity and interpretability is not convincingly justified. There is no direct metric to measure the faithfulness of the resulting interpretability (e.g., Fidelity). The authors do not demonstrate that the sparser subgraphs are easier to interpret or provide better explanations. The achieved sparsity levels (10–85%) may not bring dramatic interpretability improvements, and a GNN on a sparse graph may still be hard to interpret. (R1, R2, R3)
- **Missing comparisons with interpretability methods:** No comparison against GNNExplainer, PGExplainer, or similar methods that also aim to explain graph classifications. The authors could compare with selecting top-k nodes/edges based on mask matrices from such methods. (R1)
- **Insufficient baselines for sparsification/ablation:**
  - Missing comparison with other sparsification methods, e.g., spectral sparsification (arXiv:0808.4134), or pooling methods such as gpool, Eigenpooling, self-attention pooling. (R3)
  - Missing a simple multi-objective optimization baseline: e.g., training with a combined loss L = L_classification + λ L_sparsity. (R1)
  - No ablation comparing the proposed bilevel approach to a sequential pipeline: first train a GNN, then fix parameters and train RL for sparsity. This would clarify the motivation for bilevel optimization. (R1)
- **Limited base model:** Only GIN is used; at least two different base GNN architectures should be tested to demonstrate generality. (R2)
- **Runtime and scalability concerns:** The approach is up to 100× slower than the base model (Table 8). Runtime comparisons against baselines are missing. It is not shown whether the method outperforms simple random sparsification with the same time budget. The effectiveness on large graphs is questionable due to RL training cost, and traditional interpretability methods may already achieve a good trade-off on smaller graphs. (R1, R2)
- **Dataset details and variability:** The sizes (#nodes, #edges) of datasets are not described. There is wide variability in sparsification across datasets without explanation. It is unclear whether the method was tested on large graphs. (R1, R3)
- **Notation and presentation issues:** Several symbols are not defined when first used (e.g., θ and φ in Eq. (3)–(4); "clip" and a_t in Eq. (1)). Notation errors: V ∈ 1,…,n should be V = {1,…,n}; missing curly braces in y_i ∈ {1,…,K}. Typo line four: missing space before "However". Error in definition of ε_S in the Edge Removal Policy. "euclidean" should be capitalized. The paper is hard to follow for readers not familiar with RL. (R1, R2, R4)
- **Lack of theoretical insights:** Results are strong but inconsistent (e.g., poor performance on Enzymes). Better theoretical understanding of when the method is appropriate is needed. (R4)
- **Connectivity constraint:** It is not discussed whether the graph is constrained to remain connected during pruning. (R3)
- **Generality:** The paper only addresses graph classification. It is unclear if the method can be adapted to node classification or graph regression. (R4)
