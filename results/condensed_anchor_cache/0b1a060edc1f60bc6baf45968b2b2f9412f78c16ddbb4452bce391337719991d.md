## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a novel diffusion-based sampling method for solving inverse problems in imaging, deriving a stochastic RED (Regularization by Denoising) regularization from a variational perspective. The method uses a pre-trained diffusion model with only iterative optimization and an SNR-based weighting. Reviewers generally agree that the paper is well-written, the method is original and simple, and the experiments on diverse tasks with thorough ablations are a strength. However, significant concerns were raised about theoretical rigor (especially the unimodal Gaussian assumption, the connection to RED/PnP with non-symmetric Jacobians, and proofs), missing comparisons with PnP and DiffPIR, sensitivity to noise variance, and overstatements about linking to variational methods. Two reviewers (scores 6) were more positive overall, while two (scores 5) were more critical; after rebuttal, one of the positive reviewers noted that concerns were addressed and found the paper acceptable.

### Strengths
- Clear, well-written, and easy to follow; the paper does not engage in unnecessary technicalities.
- The proposed algorithm is novel, straightforward to implement, and effective, using a first-order stochastic optimizer (Adam) to solve the stochastic RED objective.
- Strong experimental evaluation: diverse linear and nonlinear inverse problems (inpainting, deblurring, super-resolution, phase retrieval, etc.), exhaustive ablation studies, and state-of-the-art performance in PSNR/SSIM and visual quality (KID/LPIPS).
- The variational perspective relates the method to RED in an interesting way, providing a new connection between diffusion-based sampling and classical regularization.
- The theory naturally yields a hyperparameter formula (SNR-based weighting) that simplifies tuning.
- Proposition 2 is promising, suggesting a conservative vector field interpretation.

### Weaknesses
- **Unimodal approximation vs. avoiding unimodal assumptions**: The authors claim to avoid the unimodal assumption on \(p(x_0|x_t)\) used in prior works, but their variational approach replaces it with a unimodal Gaussian \(q(x_0|y)\) to approximate the posterior. The paper does not justify why this new unimodal assumption is more valid or analyze its impact on reconstruction quality, especially when the true posterior is multimodal.
- **Link to RED and variational methods is tenuous**: (a) The Jacobian of the denoiser \(\epsilon_\theta\) is not symmetric in general, so the regularization term cannot be expressed as the gradient of a scalar function—a known issue from (Reehorst & Schniter, 2018). The paper does not address this, undermining the claimed connection to a variational loss. (b) The explicit regularization term in RED holds only under strict assumptions that deep denoisers do not satisfy; both RED and PnP are better described through fixed-point iteration, not a variational objective. (c) The discussion of fixed points after Eq. (9) is misleading because the loss changes each iteration; the paper should say “a fixed point” rather than “the fixed point.” (d) The claim to link with variational methods is overstated—the paper only links to RED, not general variational formulations like TV or other classical regularizers.
- **Missing critical comparisons**: (a) No comparison with state-of-the-art PnP methods (e.g., DPIR, RED) or the recent DiffPIR that also combines diffusion with inverse problems. (b) No comparison with pure variational methods (e.g., TV or TGV), which could provide baselines—e.g., in Figure 11 (top row) a simple TV method would likely achieve high PSNR. (c) The phase-retrieval experiments use unrealistic settings and yield weak PSNR; more realistic setups (coded diffraction patterns, oversampled Fourier) are recommended.
- **Noise variance sensitivity and robustness**: The weighting and hyperparameter formulas assume known noise variance. The paper does not investigate the effect of over- or underestimating the noise variance, which is important for practical applicability.
- **Sampling strategy and computational cost**: (a) The ablation on timestep schedule only compared descending vs. random sampling, concluding descending is best—this is unsurprising given time dependencies. Alternative descending strategies (log, exponential) that could reduce the number of steps were not explored. (b) The method remains computationally expensive (thousands of iterations), which is a limitation for high-resolution images or real-time use.
- **Theoretical gaps**: (a) The derivation from KL minimization with \(\sigma=0\) collapses to a MAP problem, but the authors do not explain why they first consider sampling before taking \(\sigma=0\) or verify that \(-\log p(\mu)\) equals the proposed regularization term. (b) The proof of Proposition 1 relies on a result from Song et al. that is an inequality, not an equality; an exhaustive proof is needed. (c) The regularization is defined with an expectation over \(t\), but in practice \(t\) follows a deterministic descending schedule; this discrepancy between theory and algorithm is a major issue. (d) Algorithm 1 is presented without any convergence analysis. (e) Before Eq. (8), it should be explicitly stated that the score is assumed to be exactly approximated by \(\epsilon_\theta\).
- **Missing references and outdated citations**: Important references from the variational and PnP literature are absent, e.g., DPIR, DiffPIR, Hurault et al. (Gradient Step Denoiser), Xu et al., Pesquet et al. Also, several papers by Chung et al. are only cited as arXiv preprints despite being published in conferences.
- **Minor issues**: (a) Some equations are unclear: why is \(\epsilon\) kept in Proposition 2 when the appendix suggests otherwise? (b) Appendices A.2 and A.3 could be merged into Proposition 2 with consistent constants—A.3 is an interesting but hidden result. (c) Several typos (e.g., “stropped,” “bellows up,” “approaches to zero; .”).
