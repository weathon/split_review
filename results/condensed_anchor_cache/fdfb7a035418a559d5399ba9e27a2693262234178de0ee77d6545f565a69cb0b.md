## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper introduces a domain adaptation method for neural contextual bandits (DABand). The proposed algorithm adapts a bandit model from a low-cost source domain to a target domain with distribution shift by using unlabeled data from both domains for representation learning and alignment, and by controlling a data divergence term via adversarial training. Theoretical analysis shows a sub-linear regret bound in the target domain, achieved by solving an online network lasso problem with time-dependent regularization. Empirical evaluations on three real-world datasets demonstrate that DABand outperforms existing state-of-the-art contextual bandit algorithms.

### Strengths

- The problem setting—domain adaptation in contextual bandits—is interesting, challenging, and has practical relevance (e.g., drug reaction from mice to humans; implications for reinforcement learning with human feedback). [Reviewer 2]
- One reviewer (score 8) particularly praised the algorithm design as originating from an observation that leveraging data across domains leads to sub-linear regrets, making the method simple yet elegant. [Reviewer 1]
- The theoretical analysis provides a regret bound that is sub-linear in the target domain, supported by proofs that decompose the regret into source-domain regret and a data divergence term. The use of an online network lasso with time-dependent regularization is a technically sound contribution. [Reviewers 1, 2, 3]
- The method effectively uses unlabeled data from both source and target domains to learn robust representations and align them across domains, enabling efficient domain adaptation. [Reviewer 2]
- Extensive empirical testing on three real-world datasets shows clear improvements over existing contextual bandit baselines. [Reviewers 1, 2, 3]

### Weaknesses

- The theoretical justification has gaps: (i) the proof does not explicitly discuss why sub-linearity in the source-domain regret \(R_S\) leads to sub-linearity in the target-domain regret \(R_T\) (Theorem 3.1); (ii) the claim that the data divergence term is sub-linear is not sufficiently elaborated in either the main text or the appendix. This was raised by multiple reviewers. [Reviewers 1, 3]
- Definition 3.6 is unclear: the notation \(x\) and \(\mathbf x\) are conflated, and it is not explicitly stated that \(h \in \mathcal H\) and \(x \in \mathbb R^n\). The mathematical formulation needs rephrasing. [Reviewer 3]
- The adversarial training paradigm used to control the data divergence term lacks justification. The original adversarial training was designed for classification tasks, while contextual bandits are regression problems. The paper does not clarify how the hypothesis class is generalized or why this generalization is valid in the bandit setting. [Reviewer 3]
- The paper does not discuss how the proposed method compares to linear contextual bandit algorithms (e.g., LinUCB) in terms of regret bounds or performance in the cross-domain setting, nor does it explain why simply applying linear models or neural network models without domain adaptation would fail. A side-by-side theoretical or empirical comparison with LinUCB would help contextualize the results. [Reviewer 3]
- The method builds on the NeuralLinUCB framework and the loss function adaptation draws heavily from established domain adaptation techniques (e.g., aligning feature representations). This similarity potentially limits the novelty of the approach. The paper would benefit from a more detailed discussion of how the specific implementation differs from or improves upon existing domain adaptation methods. [Reviewer 2]
- The sensitivity of the algorithm to the choice of the regularization parameter in the online network lasso problem is not explored. The paper should include an analysis or discussion of how this parameter affects regret and performance, as it is critical for practical use. [Reviewer 2]
- Minor: Section 4.1 title incorrectly references “eq (16)” instead of “eq (5)”. [Reviewer 3]
