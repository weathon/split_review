## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes DyNet, a dynamic family of networks for all-in-one image restoration (denoising, deraining, dehazing) based on a weight-sharing mechanism that enables switching between bulkier and lightweight variants (DyNet-S and DyNet-L) in a single training round. A dynamic pre-training strategy simultaneously trains both variants, reducing GPU hours by ~50%. The Million-IRD dataset (2M high-quality, high-resolution images) is introduced. DyNet achieves state-of-the-art results with a 56.75% reduction in parameters and 31.34% reduction in GFlops compared to baselines like PromptIR.

### Strengths
- The dynamic pre-training strategy is considered novel by most reviewers (R2, R3, R4) and effective: it enables simultaneous training of multiple model scales with shared weights in one session, reducing GPU hours by ~50% while maintaining performance. However, one reviewer (R1) found the novelty marginal given weight-sharing and pre-training are well-known techniques and noted a limited literature review.
- DyNet achieves state-of-the-art results on all-in-one restoration benchmarks with a more compact model (56.75% fewer parameters, 31.34% fewer GFlops) compared to prior methods like PromptIR (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- The Million-IRD dataset of 2M high-resolution images is a valuable contribution to the community (R2, R3, R4).
- The paper is clearly written with detailed technical explanations and architectural diagrams (R3, R4).
- The weight-sharing idea is straightforward and could potentially be applied to other architectures (R2).

### Weaknesses
- Ablation studies are limited. Table 5 couples the effect of the pre-training method and the dataset; it is unclear whether improvement comes from the masked dynamic pre-training or the larger Million-IRD dataset (R1, R2). A control experiment (pre-training on Million-IRD without masked training) is missing (R2). Apart from Table 5, experiments do little to illustrate or provide intuition for the weight-sharing mechanism, which is the paper's primary contribution (R3). Additional analysis on sensitivity of weight-sharing frequencies and dataset variations is needed (R4).
- The proposed pre-training approach lacks clarity on why training on "cheap" synthetic degradations (noise, JPEG compression) is beneficial for all-in-one restoration targeting haze, rain, and noise (R3). The relationship between DyNet and the pre-training method is weak; they could be used separately (Table 5), but no discussion is provided (R2). The pre-training approach is not well distinguished from prior low-level vision methods like DegAE (CVPR 2023) or CoLoRA (ECCV 2024) (R3). 
- The claim of 50% training time savings lacks practical justification. If only one model size is needed, only one training process is required; if multiple sizes are needed, one could train a smaller architecture (e.g., CNN) with fewer iterations. The paper does not provide use cases where obtaining two similar variants simultaneously is necessary (R2). Whether this approach impacts model robustness or generalization across tasks, and whether conventional pre-training could suffice, is unclear (R4).
- Scalability and limitations of the weight-sharing mechanism are unexplored. Experiments only show two model scales (DyNet-S and DyNet-L); can three or four variants be trained simultaneously without increasing parameters? Is it feasible to apply the 16M parameter model at a smaller or larger scale, or start with a smaller model and scale up? (R1, R2, R3). The frequency of training switching (e.g., whether the smaller model needs fewer training iterations) is not explored (R2).
- Evaluation is limited to three common degradations (haze, rain, noise). Generalizability to more diverse or challenging scenarios (e.g., motion blur, low illumination, real degradations) is not established (R3, R4). The pre-training's effect on generalization for complex settings (e.g., five degradations) is unknown (R3). The finetune datasets have a large imbalance in images per task (70k for dehaze vs. 200 for derain), which may affect conclusions from Table 6 (R2).
- The experiments do not analyze training efficiency explicitly (R1). They do not address why the Million-IRD dataset is important for all-in-one restoration specifically (R1). The paper mentions interest in scaling laws (line 295) but only presents two model scales without addressing the amount of data necessary for effective pre-training (R3).
- Minority reviewer (R1, score 1) was strongly critical: found novelty marginal, experiments insufficient to support contributions (e.g., "easy switching" only shown with two variants, training efficiency not analyzed, dataset importance not justified), and rated presentation and contribution low (1 each). Other reviewers (scores 5) rated presentation 3 and contribution 2.
