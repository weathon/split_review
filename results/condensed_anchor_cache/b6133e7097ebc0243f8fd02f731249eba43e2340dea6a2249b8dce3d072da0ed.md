## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes L<sub>p</sub>-convolution, which masks convolution kernels with a parametrizable function derived from the multivariate p-generalized normal distribution (MPND). By training the mask parameters (p, scale, rotation, distortion) alongside network weights, the method can produce receptive fields ranging from box-like to Gaussian-like shapes. Experiments on a Sudoku benchmark show learned diversely shaped RFs. On CIFAR-100 and Tiny ImageNet, adding L<sub>p</sub>-convolution to several architectures (AlexNet, VGG, ResNet, ConvNeXt, LargeKernel) yields small accuracy gains, especially with larger kernel sizes. Representational similarity analysis (RSA) against mouse V1 recordings indicates that networks with smaller p (more Gaussian-like masks) achieve slightly higher peak neural alignment.

### Strengths
- Creative and novel inductive bias transfer from biology to CNNs (R1, R3, R4, R5).
- Well-motivated by the Gaussian-like spatial distribution of synaptic inputs in early visual cortex (R1, R4, R5).
- Rigorous experimental methodology: models tested with multiple random initializations and appropriate statistical testing to highlight significant differences (R1, R4, R5).
- Code released for reproducibility (R1, R4).
- The paper is generally well-written and organized; section 6 (alignment with biological models) is particularly attractive (R2, R3, R4; R5 adds “overall well-written”).
- The basic idea is compelling and the connection to biological neural networks is well supported (R2, R3, R4).
- Results tables include standard deviations (R4).
- The representational similarity analysis provides a direct comparison with mouse visual cortex data (R2, R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity issues (minority strong criticism):** Reviewer 1 finds the paper hard to read, especially Sections 2 and 3 introducing L<sub>p</sub>-convolution, due to an abundance of notations and symbols introduced after first use. R1’s presentation score is 1 (worst), while other reviewers rate presentation 3–4. This contrast indicates that a significant revision for readability is needed by a subset of readers.
- **Small effect sizes (major concern of two reviewers):** All three main experiments show small absolute improvements.  
  - Sudoku: row/column vs. block accuracy difference <10%, and p=2 vs. “Large” (7×7) difference <1% (R5 asks if error rates would show larger differences).  
  - Image classification: improvements of 2–3% on CIFAR-100 and Tiny ImageNet in the 60–70% accuracy range, while modern architectures exceed 90% (R5 questions significance; would similar gains appear in more modern nets?).  
  - Representational similarity: peak SSM improvements of 0.5–2% (R5 notes that differences between AlexNet and ConvNeXt-T are of the same order).  
  R1 also states that the similarity of L<sub>p</sub>-conv with small p to mouse RFs is not very convincing, partly because it is only a qualitative comparison.
- **Unclear benefits of non-Gaussian masks and optimization of p:**  
  - Table 1 suggests that p=2 (Gaussian) often performs similarly to other p values; the value of non-Gaussian versions is unclear (R3).  
  - The learned p appears not to change much during training (Fig. 2, Table 1), casting doubt on effective optimization; R3 suggests a loss term may be needed.  
  - R5 asks: if masks are applied during training (not after), the network could compensate by learning larger weights where the mask is small, potentially negating the effect.  
  - R3 asks whether the method is genuinely novel or merely a new way to produce RF structures that CNNs already learn via other means.
- **Experimental details missing or unclear:**  
  - **Sudoku experiment (Fig. 3):** R5 asks what “Large” and “Lp<sup>†</sup>” refer to; R4 asks why Sudoku is a good test case and what “distortion” in Fig. 4 means. R4 notes diagonal masks’ role is unclear given the horizontal-vertical-box structure.  
  - **Image classification (Table 1):** R5 asks whether ResNet’s larger first-layer kernels are kept unchanged.  
  - **Representational similarity (Fig. 5):** R5 asks for a clear definition of “Max. SSM” and why there is not one plot per brain area.  
  - **Mask application:** R5 asks if masks are applied during or after training.  
  - **Optimization procedure:** R4 asks how the covariance matrix C and p are trained; the update process is not explained.  
  - **Figure 7 issues (AlexNet conv1 filters):** R1 and R3 note that untrained filters display a checkerboard/grid structure not expected from random initialization, and trained filters mostly show a bright line along the bottom. R1 additionally points out that the trained filters look very different from those in the original AlexNet paper (Fig. 3 of [1]), raising a discrepancy that needs explanation.  
  - **Fig. 1g caption:** R3 asks which comparisons were tested and whether correction for multiple comparisons was applied.  
  - **V1 vs. subregions:** Page 8 says “mouse V1,” but the appendix lists VISal, VISam, etc.; R3 asks if this is a typo.  
  - **Definition of “functional synapses”:** R3 asks for elaboration on the logic in Appendix A.2, and how it relates to biological functional synapses.  
  - **Parameters and dimensionality:** R4 notes that introducing a covariate matrix C adds d² parameters per filter (roughly doubling free parameters), which might account for accuracy gains rather than L<sub>p</sub>-convolution itself.  
  - **Notation and wording:** R4 points out that Eq. (1) uses a superscript *p* where subscript is more standard; “mask take one” should be “equal one”; “reference position of the RF” may be unclear.  
  - **Background placement:** Section 7 (related work) appears late in the paper (R4).  
  - **Missing or incorrect citations:** R4 notes backprop was not introduced by LeCun; more complete citations are needed.
- **Other conceptual concerns:**  
  - R5 questions why the spatial distribution of synapses is the right comparison for CNN weights, given that presynaptic neurons have their own extended RFs, so the V1 neuron’s RF envelope in pixel space is not identical to retinotopically mapped synaptic locations.  
  - The term “Gaussian-like structured sparsity” may be a misnomer because Gaussian weightings do not drive values to zero (R4).  
  - R2 asks how the work relates to the concept of foveation (with ref DOI). This overlaps with a missing comparison and should be addressed.
