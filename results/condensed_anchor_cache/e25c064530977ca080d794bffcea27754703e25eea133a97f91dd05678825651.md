## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies supervised pre-training of transformers for sequential decision-making problems without transition probabilities (a class covering bandits, dynamic pricing, and newsvendor problems). The authors identify an out-of-distribution issue caused by a distribution mismatch between training and test-time action sequences, framed as a performative prediction problem. They propose a mixed pre-training algorithm that augments simulated data with transformer-generated action sequences to mitigate this mismatch. The paper provides theoretical analysis (including convergence to Bayes-optimal policy) and empirical comparisons against UCB and Thompson Sampling, claiming advantages in exploiting prior knowledge, handling model misspecification, and short-horizon performance. While the theoretical contributions and proposed algorithm are appreciated, reviewers raise concerns about clarity, novelty, fairness of comparisons, computational overhead, narrow scope, and insufficient justification for using complex transformers over simpler methods.

### Strengths
- Rigorous theoretical analysis establishing conditions for minimizing cumulative regret and asymptotic Bayes-optimality (Reviewer 1, also noted by Reviewer 4). The theoretical grounding is comprehensive.
- The two-phase training strategy (mixing simulated and self-generated actions) is simple yet effective, and maintains theoretical rigor (Reviewer 1 – more positive; other reviewers also note the algorithm is well-motivated).
- The connection to performative prediction is intriguing and provides a fresh perspective (Reviewer 2).
- The paper introduces clear notation and a framework for studying pre-trained transformers in sequential decision-making, and demonstrates practical performance improvements over UCB/TS in certain settings (Reviewers 2, 3).
- Empirical results show cases where transformers outperform traditional algorithms, contributing to understanding transformer capabilities in decision-making (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity and notation issues**: Multiple reviewers found the presentation confusing.  Specific problems: the decision function \(f\) is used ambiguously (generates \(a\) or \(a^*\)?); Eq. (4)–(5) and Eq. (7) are inconsistent – Eq. (5) claims to generate \(a^*\) but only shows \(a_\tau\); line 192 introduces \(a_t^*\) not present in Eq. (5). Section 2 is overly long and the use of \(f\) is vague. The OOD argument below Eq. (9) is mischaracterized (training on some parameters and testing on others is a standard OOD problem, not the stated issue). The text above Eq. (9) is unclearly written and has a typo (“pertraining”). Math notation is confusing and sometimes incorrect/inconsistent (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- **Unfair/baseline comparisons**: Comparing transformer (with extensive pre-training) against standard UCB/TS (no prior data) is misleading. The paper claims UCB/TS are less effective at using prior knowledge, but these algorithms do incorporate observed data (empirical means, uncertainty). The short-term advantage claim is unconvincing because modified UCB/TS variants (e.g., reducing exploration) could improve short-term performance. The paper should compare against UCB/TS that are also given the same pre-training data, and explore more recent structured algorithms designed for short horizons (Reviewer 2). Additionally, the paper does not compare against other baselines like different LDFs (Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of discussion of disadvantages and computational cost**: The paper does not quantify the computational and memory overhead of transformer pre-training relative to UCB/TS. Pre-training on many environments and trajectories is expensive; it is not clear that the benefits outweigh the cost, especially since simple methods like TS can learn optimally from scratch in bandit settings. The use of a complex transformer on bandit-style (state-free) problems seems like overkill, and the paper fails to articulate a compelling practical justification (Reviewers 2, 3). Reviewers request computational comparisons (e.g., align x-axis with wall-clock time or flops) (Reviewer 2).
- **Narrow scope and limited novelty**: The considered problem class is very narrowly defined (state-free bandit-like tasks). Given that transformers excel on sequential data, studying state-free bandits seems less meaningful; the paper should focus on more complex sequential problems or provide a stronger rationale. The novelty is limited: the mixed training phase is a straightforward extension of existing works [1-3]; the theoretical analysis, while thorough, does not sufficiently justify the empirical contribution. The simple experimental setup does not demonstrate practical significance (Reviewer 4; also noted by Reviewer 3 for narrow scope).
- **Missing motivation for Learned Decision Function**: Section 4 introduces the LDF but does not clearly explain its necessity or how it relates to the pre-trained transformer. The relationship is unclear on first read, and the experiments use different baselines, adding confusion (Reviewer 3).
- **Need for better related work and clarifications**: The paper should discuss related work on combining prior data with algorithms like Thompson Sampling, and clarify how the setting differs from standard contextual bandits. The use of RL terminology is confusing (Reviewer 2). Also, the paper claims RL algorithms are hard to combine with prior knowledge, but many methods exist (initializing replay buffer, offline learning, transfer learning) – this should be addressed (Reviewer 3). Figures 1 and 2 are not referenced in the main text (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing experiments/ablations**: The paper should consider a curriculum learning approach where self-generated actions gradually increase instead of a sharp two-phase switch, as an ablation (Reviewer 1). Also, the impact of varying environment parameters (\(\gamma\)) during training on generalization and bias should be explored (Reviewer 1).
