## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Concept Bottleneck Large Language Model (CB-LLM), adding concept bottleneck layers to LLMs for interpretable text classification and generation. In classification, each bottleneck neuron corresponds to a concept (e.g., “sport”), and Automatic Concept Correction (ACC) is used to map concepts to labels. In generation, interpretable neurons enable concept detection and controllable text generation. Experiments use RoBERTa-base for classification and GPT-2 for generation, with LLaMA-3-8B used for concept labeling and ChatGPT for concept generation. Results show comparable or better performance than black-box baselines, with interpretability features. Three reviewers rate it 6, one rates it 5; the minority reviewer found the presentation less clear and the classification part less convincing.

### Strengths
- **Novel and well-motivated idea**: First work to apply concept bottlenecks to LLMs, addressing both classification and generation interpretability (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- **Text classification performance**: CB-LLM with ACC surpasses black-box baselines on several datasets; ReLU activation removes negative activations and reduces semantic ambiguity (Reviewer 1).
- **Human evaluation evidence**: Positive ratings for Activation Faithfulness and Contribution Faithfulness, indicating alignment with human understanding (Reviewer 1).
- **Text generation potential**: Simple and interesting method for concept detection and controllable generation (Reviewers 2, 3). Rich figures and examples make the paper easy to follow (Reviewer 2).
- **Thorough experimental validation**: Extensive experiments, visualizations, and case studies across multiple metrics (Reviewer 4).
- **Practical applications**: Demonstrated for tasks like chatbot toxicity moderation (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited backbone models evaluated**: Only RoBERTa and GPT-2 are used as the actual CB-LLM backbones; LLaMA-3-8B is only used for labeling. The method should be tested on larger and more diverse model families (e.g., LLaMA, T5, OPT) and sizes to demonstrate scalability and robustness (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). One reviewer specifically notes that “only one LM is used” per task (Reviewer 3).
- **Insufficient interpretability validation for text generation**: Concept detection experiments are only performed on a classification dataset (Section 4.2), not on a generation task, so it is unclear whether concepts identified during generation faithfully appear in the generated text (Reviewer 1). The explanation space is also limited to class labels; full-vocabulary contrastive explanations would be more fine-grained (Reviewer 4). No analysis of the meaning of latent concept neurons or their causal impact on generated tokens is provided (Reviewer 3).
- **Steerability and generation evaluation gaps**: Input prompts used for steerability experiments are under-detailed; no baseline comparison to original generations without control is provided (Reviewer 4). No discussion or comparison with existing controllable text generation literature (e.g., [3]) (Reviewers 3, 4). Diversity of generated sentences is not evaluated (Reviewer 4). The claim that “a small difference in perplexity would not affect generation quality” lacks a quantitative definition of “small” (Reviewer 3).
- **Concept alignment and generalizability concerns**: It is uncertain if generated concepts consistently align with human intuition across all datasets; evaluation may suffer from selection bias (Reviewer 2). Transferability of learned concepts across domains (e.g., movie reviews to product reviews) is not explored (Reviewer 2).
- **Reliance on ChatGPT and sentence embeddings**: The non-deterministic nature of ChatGPT and choice of sentence embedding model introduce performance variability; multiple runs or model selections are needed to assess sensitivity (Reviewer 2).
- **Text classification interpretability claim is misleading**: The method merely replaces the original label set with a new set of concepts generated by GPT; it does not explain why certain concepts are chosen for a given input text (Reviewer 3). The sparsity constraint introduced during training is not adequately justified—how sparsity improves interpretability is unclear and lacks theoretical or empirical support (Reviewer 1).
- **“Unlearning” terminology is misused**: Removing a subset of concepts does not change model parameters and is not standard unlearning (Reviewer 3).
- **Unfair criticism of prior work**: The paper criticizes C3M for requiring human-annotated concepts, yet the text generation part also requires human-annotated concepts (e.g., Figure 3) (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing experimental details and comparability**: The variable `S_c` in Equation 2 is undefined (Reviewer 3). Why not use CEBaB and IMDB datasets as in C3M? (Reviewer 3). Which parameters are optimized in Equation 5? (Reviewer 3). “Concept accuracy” in Section 4.2 is not defined (Reviewer 3). Full name of CAV in Section 2 is missing (Reviewer 4). The agreement coefficient (e.g., Kappa) for human evaluations in Tables 3 and 4 is not reported (Reviewer 4).
- **Clarity issues**: The variable `y` in Equation 2 lacks sufficient explanation (Reviewer 1). Tables 2 and 6 both list “RoBERTa-base fine-tuned (black-box)” but with different values; Table 6 should refer to GPT-2 instead (Reviewer 1). Figure 3 is confusing—the modules are not clearly distinguished; add identifiers (Reviewer 1). The word “We” in the first sentence of Section 4 should be lowercase (Reviewer 4). A brief description of the “concept bottleneck” concept from computer vision should be included in the related work section to aid readers from other fields (Reviewer 1). One reviewer found the text classification part overly verbose (Reviewer 3).
