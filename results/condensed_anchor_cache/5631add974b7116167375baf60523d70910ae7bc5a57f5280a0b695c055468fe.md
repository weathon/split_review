## Merged Review

### Summary

MathEval is a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating LLM mathematical reasoning, merging 22 datasets (some newly created, noted with “(ours)”) across languages (English, Chinese), educational levels (primary through high school), and problem types (arithmetic, word problems, competitive, higher math). It uses automated answer extraction and comparison via a two-stage pipeline: regex then GPT-4, with a publicly available fine-tuned DeepSeek-7B model as an alternative. Prompt adaptation strategies (zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought) are tailored per model and dataset. The benchmark includes a dynamic update feature (adding Gaokao 2023 problems annually) to reduce data contamination. Experiments evaluate 52 models (closed- and open-source) and report results aggregated by arithmetic mean across datasets. The paper also presents a human annotation validation of the automated grading.

### Strengths

- **Comprehensiveness**: The benchmark covers a wide range of languages (English, Chinese), problem types (arithmetic, word problems, competitive, higher math), and difficulty levels (primary to high school). 22 datasets are included, some newly created. This diversity is useful for the community.
- **Standardized evaluation pipeline**: The two-stage approach (regex + GPT-4) for answer extraction and comparison is well-designed. The open-source fine-tuned DeepSeek-7B model for answer comparison is a notable contribution that lowers the barrier for researchers without GPT-4 access.
- **Thorough experimentation**: 52 models are evaluated across multiple configurations (prompting strategies), and results are reported with human annotation validation to demonstrate precision.
- **Clear problem framing**: The introduction clearly distinguishes three core issues in evaluating mathematical reasoning: comprehensiveness, adaptation, and consistency.
- **Prompt adaptation templates**: The paper provides flexible prompt templates (zero-shot, few-shot, CoT) that adapt to model and dataset characteristics, aiding reproducibility.
- **Human annotation validation**: Human annotation of a sample of answers is used to verify automated evaluation quality, with inter-rater agreement (Cohen’s Kappa = 0.8871) reported.
- **Open model for grading**: The fine-tuned DeepSeek-7B model for answer comparison is made publicly available, which is useful for cost-sensitive research.
- **Number of evaluations**: The scale of model and configuration testing is large.

### Weaknesses

- **Limited novelty/insight**: Several reviewers found the contribution to be primarily an aggregation of existing benchmarks and evaluation methods. The results confirm well-known findings (closed-source models outperform open-source, math-domain fine-tuned models perform better, few-shot > zero-shot) without providing new insights. The paper does not analyze correlations between datasets, making it unclear whether the 22 datasets measure distinct reasoning abilities or could be reduced to a smaller subset.
- **Inability to evaluate reasoning process**: The benchmark evaluates only final answers, not the reasoning steps. For multi-step mathematical reasoning, it cannot assess whether the solution process is correct or moving toward the correct answer. This is a significant limitation for evaluating true reasoning capabilities.
- **Prompt adaptation motivation unclear**: Reviewers questioned why prompts are adapted per model and dataset rather than using a fixed, naturalistic prompting setting. The paper does not justify whether adaptation yields more ecologically valid comparisons or whether it cherry-picks optimal prompts. It is unclear how to apply the benchmark to a new model without also optimizing prompts per dataset.
- **Evaluation validation issues**: The validation of GPT-4-based evaluation is confusing. The human annotation sample size is not stated. The reported inter-rater agreement (Cohen’s Kappa = 0.8871) is good, but another metric (“overall average score” 0.6264 in Table 7) is not clearly defined. Figure 5 uses “absolute difference” between human and automated scores—it is unclear how a binary correct/incorrect answer yields a difference metric. The comparison is done for only 19 out of 22 datasets, without explanation. These ambiguities undermine confidence in the evaluation quality.
- **Aggregate scoring issues**: The arithmetic mean across datasets gives equal weight to easy and hard problems, which is not justified. Aggregate results may mask which datasets are very difficult or where models still fail badly. The paper notes that results correlate highly with GSM8K and MATH, raising the question of what additional insight MathEval provides over those two.
- **Lack of very difficult problems**: The benchmark primarily covers K-12 levels; higher-level college mathematics (e.g., real analysis, Putnam competition) is absent. This limits insight into model performance on the hardest reasoning tasks. Future updates should consider including undergraduate and competition-level math.
- **Readability and clarity issues**:
  - Figure 2 (taxonomy) is hard to read due to acronyms; use icons or expand text.
  - Figure 3 (pipeline) uses light green text on gray background—low contrast. Colorblind-friendly palette needed.
  - Figure 4 (framework components) could include arrows to show flow between parts.
  - Table 2 datasets summary: only in appendix; a brief summary table in main paper would help readers understand scope, difficulty, and concepts.
- **Missing experimental details**:
  - Details of the "calculation scheduling" algorithm and why it was chosen are needed (currently in prompts section, should be in appendix).
  - Training details for the DeepSeek-7B comparison model are sparse.
  - How the dataset is automatically updated (dynamic update) is not explained.
  - How the “(ours)” datasets in Table 2 were generated requires elaboration.
  - Implementation details of the evaluation pipeline (e.g., regex patterns, GPT-4 system prompt) are lacking, hindering reproducibility.
- **Lack of more middle school datasets**: Only Arith3K covers middle school; more balance would be beneficial.
- **Choice of language**: Only English and Chinese are included. Why Chinese in particular? What advantages does this offer over other languages?
- **Prior work on open-source grading**: The paper claims novelty for the open-source comparison model, but prior work (e.g., https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.00900) has used open-source LLMs for grading. The fine-tuning contribution is still notable but not entirely new.
- **Minor issues**:
  - Conclusion line 1: “In this papar” (typo).
  - Conclusion mentions “2 datasets” instead of 22.
  - Triple quotes ('''xxx''') are non-standard.
  - Reviewer 5 questioned why not first try regex and only use GPT-4 on regex-incorrect answers to save compute.
  - The “dataset-level higher” setting (selecting max of few- and zero-shot per dataset) is trivially expected to outperform either alone.
- **Rough classification of mathematical reasoning**: The categorization (arithmetic, word problems, competitive, higher math) is coarse and does not capture nuanced reasoning types (e.g., spatial, probabilistic, algebraic).
- **Disagreement among reviewers**: Two reviewers (scores 6,6) were more positive, emphasizing the practicality of the benchmark and open model, while three reviewers (scores 3,3,3) found the contribution insufficient for a research paper due to lack of novel insights and weak evaluation validation.
