## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents EVA, a sampling-based coupled flow framework for motif scaffolding in protein design, formulated as a Geometric Inverse Design task inspired by image inversion. EVA uses a pretrained flow-based generative model without retraining, employing motif-aligned priors and a motif-interpolated posterior to straighten the probability path and align generative directions with guidance in early sampling steps. On benchmarks, EVA achieves 70× faster inference than RFDiffusion with competitive or better performance, and demonstrates applicability to vaccine design, multi-motif scaffolding, and motif optimal placement. All reviewers acknowledge the method’s efficiency, while many have serious concerns about clarity, experimental rigour, novelty, and whether motif geometry actually improves designability.

### Strengths
- **Problem framing and motivation**: R1, R2, R3, and R5 all appreciate formulating motif-scaffolding as an inverse design problem, drawing an analogy with image inverse problems, identifying the generation‑vs‑guidance trade‑off, and motivating the solution through analysis of failure cases. R1 specifically notes the clear definition of research questions.
- **Novelty and practicality of the approach**: The use of spatial geometry of the motif to construct motif-aligned priors and a motif-interpolated posterior is considered straightforward and novel (R2, R4). The method does not require retraining (R4), and the core idea of straightening the probability path is reasonable (R3, R6).
- **Efficiency**: All six reviewers acknowledge that EVA significantly reduces inference time. R1 notes the 70× speed‑up over RFDiffusion; R2 states “improved efficiency without compromise of quality”; R3 says the experiments support computational efficiency; R4 and R5 mention faster inference; R6 says sampling time is improved by a significant margin.
- **Competitive performance**: R1 states performance is “best or on par” with SOTA methods; R2 says “without compromising quality”; R4 notes on‑par results. (Contrast: R5 and R6 question whether designability is actually maintained – see Weaknesses.)
- **Dataset contribution**: R2 highlights the introduction of a new vaccine‑design benchmark as a valuable contribution to the community.
- **Real‑world applicability**: The inclusion of real‑world tasks (vaccine design, multi‑motif scaffolding, motif optimal placement) is appreciated by R5 and R6.
- **Well‑written (minority view)**: R6 found the paper “well written and easy to understand”, in contrast to the majority who found it difficult to follow (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of clarity and poor presentation**: The paper is widely criticised for being hard to follow. R1 notes that core concepts (motif‑aligned prior, interpolated posterior, “spatial context”) are barely explained, and Figure 2 is “convoluted and unreadable”. R3 calls the mathematics “clunky”, with inconsistent notation (bold/non‑bold x, italic vs upright subscripts, Exp vs exp) and coding‑style comments in the algorithm description. R4 finds the correspondence between the main text, Figure 2, and Algorithm 1 unclear; terms like “compositional gradient” appear only in the figure. R5 points out inconsistent terminology (e.g., “Evolution Verse Adoption” vs. “Evolution via Adaptation” in the appendix). In contrast, R6 found the paper well written – a clear reviewer disagreement.
- **Insufficient experimental details and missing analyses**:  
  – R1: Training details of the OT‑Flow backbone (hyperparameters, data split cut‑off) are absent; references to FrameFlow/FoldFlow are insufficient. Inference settings (batching, hardware, number of timesteps) are not specified, making the 70× speed‑up claim hard to contextualise (vanilla FrameFlow takes ~6 s for 100 residues; EVA is >5× faster than its own backbone). R1 requests a timestep study (success rate vs. steps).  
  – R2 and R5: Number of random seeds, key hyperparameters (t₀, γ) are not reported; standard deviations are missing from all tables (e.g., Table 1 across 24 targets, Table 4). R2 notes that mRMSD values of 48, 47, 46 for <100 length are too close to claim superiority without error bars. R5 adds that RFDiffusion’s results come with error bars in prior work.  
  – R3: Metric thresholds (mRMSD%<1, scRMSD%<2, TM>0.5) appear arbitrary; sensitivity analysis is missing. Non‑parametric metrics could be provided. The speed‑up claim is “unjustified” when compared to RFDiffusion (which produces higher quality); speed‑up should instead be reported relative to the faster baseline FF‑G. Thorough time‑complexity analysis with confidence intervals, hardware categorisation, and more efficient baselines is needed.  
  – R5: Section 5.4 discusses speed but does not directly answer its own question (Question 2 about designability for various lengths).  
  – R6: The unconditional generation results (Figure 3, Table 2) appear out of place; they could be moved to the appendix. The paper does not clearly demonstrate improvements over the backbone OT‑Flow; in fact, backbones often have better designability.
- **Novelty concerns**: R1 argues that the motif‑aligned prior is very similar to Chroma’s approach (Appendix K.2), which first introduced motif‑aligned priors and optimal index selection. The only difference is classifier guidance vs. EVA’s approximation, yet Chroma is not cited. R1 questions the novelty of this component.
- **Limited benefits beyond speed**: Several reviewers question whether motif geometry improves sample quality. R6 states that empirical evaluation (Tables 1, 2, 3) shows designability *deteriorates* with motif‑coupled information. R5 points out that mRMSD scores are not higher for longer residues (≥100) and that RFDiffusion achieves a Pareto performance with only 1% higher mRMSD (and no error bars, so significance is unclear). R1 requests evidence (e.g., normalized path energy) that the prior actually leads to a straighter probability path. R2 asks whether the sampling time is really a bottleneck and whether performance is otherwise “remarkable”.
- **Scalability concerns**: R3 and R6 note that EVA performs best on smaller residue lengths (<100) and worse on longer lengths. R6 questions whether motif geometry is only informative for small complexes and how EVA can be adapted for larger sizes.
- **Missing comparisons and unresolved issues**:  
  – R1: How does EVA differ fundamentally from gradient guidance? Request comparison of motif‑residue trajectory length (rotation/translation) with FrameFlow‑guidance.  
  – R2: Why is pre‑training a conditional model considered expensive when EVA also uses a pretrained unconditional model? What makes EVA faster at the algorithm level (not just fewer timesteps)? Can EVA generate better proteins with more fine‑grained timesteps?  
  – R3: The paper discusses image inverse problems but never demonstrates the method on such a problem, weakening the analogy.  
  – R4: How are prior residues selected for comparison in Eq. 7 (motif index selection)? Is the equation number in Algorithm 1 line 1 correct? How do the vector fields in Eq. 9 and 11 relate to self‑conditioning? How is the coupling strength (Eq. 12–13) determined?  
  – R5: In Algorithm 1, π* is assumed given; is this realistic for large proteins? If more computation time is available, can EVA produce more accurate motif reconstruction?  
  – R6: What concrete benefits does motif information provide aside from faster sampling? How might it aid designability in future work?
- **Applicability beyond motif scaffolding**: R2 questions whether the method can be applied to general inverse problems (e.g., inverse imaging), suggesting that a problem‑specific approach may have limited impact for a machine‑learning conference.
