## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Hi-DLR, a Hessian-informed differential learning rate method that uses second-order Taylor approximation and a diagonalization technique to assign per-group learning rates, and introduces a per-parameter influence (PPI) metric for parameter-efficient training. Reviewers generally appreciate the clear intuition, simplicity, and effectiveness of the idea, and find the PPI metric interesting. However, major concerns include computational overhead and insufficient measurement (e.g., lack of wall-clock comparisons), unclear integration with AdamW, missing reproducibility details, and one reviewer raising substantial questions about novelty. There is also disagreement on presentation quality and the significance of the contribution.

### Strengths
- Well-motivated and clear intuition: leverages second-order approximation to assign different learning rates per parameter group, based on the observation that different parameters have different loss curvature. The idea is simple but effective.
- Efficient algorithm: uses \(K\) random directions to obtain a low-dimensional second-order approximation for learning rate search, combined with a diagonalization technique that reduces computational cost while separating contributions of different parameter groups.
- Per-parameter influence (PPI) metric that identifies the most influential parameters, enabling flexible parameter-efficient tuning (PET) by freezing less-contributing parameters. This approach is model-agnostic and adapts to various tasks and models.
- Applications demonstrated on NAM (Neural Additive Models) and LoRA.
- Extensive experiments covering synthetic data, toy objectives, and multiple advanced practical learning tasks (e.g., fine-tuning ViT, LLMs).

### Weaknesses
- **Computational overhead and incomplete measurement:** The algorithm introduces time-consuming steps (sampling/evaluating loss along \(K\) directions, diagonalizing the Hessian) that are not adequately quantified. The paper lacks wall-clock time comparisons (all training loss figures are plotted against iterations, e.g., Figures 4,5) and a breakdown of time spent on Hessian computation, parameter updates, etc. Without this, it is difficult to assess real-world speedups or applicability in resource-constrained environments. A more rigorous complexity analysis as a function of \(K\) and model dimensionality is needed. The method’s dependence on manual parameter grouping and the choice of \(K\) (especially for large models) requires more justification; suboptimal groupings can hinder performance. When \(K\) is large, the update period (every \(\phi\) iterations) increases, which may affect convergence.
- **Unclear integration with AdamW and other optimizers:** The derivation of the proposed learning rates is based on substituting \(\mathbf{d}\) in Eq. (2.1) with \(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{[K]} \mathbf{g}_{[K]}^{\text{optim}}\), but if intended for use with AdamW, it would be more logical to set \(\mathbf{d}\) to the actual difference between iterates in AdamW, thereby incorporating the entry-wise adaptive learning rates inherent to AdamW. The notation \(\mathbf{g}_{[K]}^{\text{optim}}\) is insufficiently explained. Questions remain: when fitting the quadratic function (Eq. 3.1), does the method use raw gradients or corrected gradients (like Adam’s momentum)? How does the proposed method interact with Adam’s momentum and adaptive schedule—are they orthogonal or do they influence each other? The paper lacks a theoretical comparison with established optimizers and does not analyze convergence properties relative to Adam.
- **LoRA ratio interaction:** For LoRA, the optimal learning rate ratio between matrices A and B can be fixed (Hayou et al., 2024). It is unclear how the proposed method interacts with this ratio; if \(\lambda\) is fixed, the algorithm may not work further on LoRA+.
- **Novelty concerns (minority reviewer disagreement):** One reviewer strongly questions the novelty, noting that the idea bears strong resemblance to Newton’s method (second-order Taylor approximation) and that approximating the Hessian with a diagonal matrix is not new (citing prior work). In contrast, other reviewers find the approach simple yet effective, and consider the PPI metric interesting and novel.
- **Missing baseline comparison:** It is unclear whether standard AdamW under identical conditions would achieve similar results; the paper does not provide a direct comparison with a plain AdamW baseline to demonstrate that Hi-DLR improves upon it.
- **Insufficient reproducibility:** Experimental details are lacking, especially for ViT training (e.g., data augmentation, batch size, specific version including classifier type, head dimensions, dropout, number of patches per image). The authors have not provided source code.
- **Lack of pre-training experiments:** The paper focuses on fine-tuning relatively large models; experiments on pre-training (e.g., ViT pretraining) would give a more comprehensive understanding of the method’s effectiveness.
- **Presentation issues (minority reviewer):** One reviewer notes that several notations and experimental setups are not clearly explained, and the paper lacks a well-structured introduction section with context and motivation. (Other reviewers found the paper well-written or adequate.)
- **Ground truth for PPI metric:** The authors claim that existing PET methods consistently select highly influential parameters with approximately \(10^4 \times\) higher PPI than the majority of parameters. However, PPI is an estimate; what is the ground truth for parameter influence? Without knowing the ground truth, how can we be certain that PET methods have indeed selected the highly influential parameters? This claim requires further validation.
