## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies bandits with anytime knapsacks (BwAK), a variant of BwK where an average cost constraint must be satisfied at every time step rather than only overall. The authors propose SUAK, a UCB-based algorithm that uses a skipping mechanism and slight under-utilization of the budget to meet the anytime constraint, and prove an instance-dependent regret bound of \(O(K \log T)\). Simulations compare SUAK against a baseline (standard BwK algorithm + skipping) and OPS. While some reviewers find the model natural and the analysis sound (scores 8), others see the contribution as incremental and question the novelty and literature connections (scores 5). Overall, the paper is considered clearly written but with significant concerns about prior work comparisons, assumption strength, and technical details.

### Strengths
- The bandits-with-knapsack topic is relevant and important for ICLR; the paper is fairly well written and easy to follow (Reviewers 1,2,3,4).
- The model (anytime cost constraint) is simple, interesting, and has not been well studied before (Reviewers 3,4).
- The theoretical result—a problem-dependent \(O(\log T)\) regret—is strong and contrasts with the typical worst-case \(O(\sqrt{T})\) bound; the analysis is largely sound and rigorous (Reviewers 3,4).
- Numerical experiments are provided and deliver convincing results, with SUAK performing well in practice (Reviewers 1,4). Reviewer 3 notes that the simulations reveal interesting behavior (OPS performs much worse in one setup but fairly well in another), which the paper could further explain.

### Weaknesses
**Incremental novelty and naturalness of approach (Reviewers 1,2,3)**
- The algorithmic idea (“skip rounds where the constraint may be violated + under-spend a bit to minimize skips”) is seen as somewhat natural and incremental relative to existing BwK work (Reviewer 1).
- Reviewer 3 questions the technical novelty of SUAK compared to Algorithm 3 in Kumar & Kleinberg (2022), noting the “under-utilization” principle appears similar, and asks for a more detailed discussion of differences.
- Reviewer 2 strongly believes there are easy reductions from very recent works on bandits with constraints [1,2,3] that can obtain the same results. For example, take any algorithm that satisfies constraints in high probability apart from \(O(\sqrt{T})\) violation at any time, instantiate with \(B - \sqrt{T}\) initial budget, and run it. Is the skipping mechanism not implicitly embedded in those works via a strictly feasible action? A detailed discussion of technical differences is necessary (Reviewers 1,2).

**Insufficient analysis of baseline (Reviewer 1)**
- The paper empirically compares against a single standard BwK algorithm complemented with a skipping strategy, but it would be more convincing to prove that any non-anytime knapsack algorithm would fail if equipped with extra skips. It is also unclear whether such algorithms would still enjoy the instance-independent \(\sqrt{T}\) regret bound.

**Assumptions and parameter knowledge (Reviewers 1,3)**
- Assumption 2 is not entirely motivated: once \(\omega\) is known, setting the underspending parameter is easy, but why is the assumption necessary? (Reviewer 1)
- Assumptions 1 and 2 imply the decision maker has prior access to a very small \(\omega \le \delta\). This is restrictive; can it be relaxed? (Reviewer 3)
- The instance-dependent \(O(\log T)\) regret relies on strong non-degeneracy assumptions and knowledge of \(\omega\). What about the worst-case scenario? Could OPS achieve \(O(\sqrt{T})\) regret without knowing \(\omega\)? More results are needed to gain deeper understanding (Reviewer 3).

**Literature comparison deficiencies (Reviewers 1,2)**
- The comparison with the “replenishment” literature (e.g., Bernasconi et al.) is unsatisfactory. The claim that only positive drift (replenishment) is considered is incorrect—they only require the existence of a void action that replenishes the budget, which may be equivalent to the skipping action. A natural fix for the “starting with 0 budget” issue would be to wait for initial rounds. A more comprehensive comparison is needed (Reviewer 1).
- Reviewer 2’s concern about reductions to bandits with constraints (with small initial violation) also falls under insufficient literature discussion.

**Proof and technical issues (Reviewer 3)**
- The proof requires great polishing. Specific unclear/unrigorous statements:
  - Lines 1086–1094: not rigorous.
  - Line 1100: “≥” should be “≤”?
  - Lines 1183–1190, 1197–1205: unclear.
  - Line 1269: how is the result derived from lines 1265–1267? Why is \(N_i^s(T)\) an expected number while \(N_i(T)\) is random?
- Line 32: “+” should be “-”? (typo)
- Line 240: the skipping mechanism is said to be “ignored in practice”; need more elaboration and formal evidence to support that claim (Reviewer 3).

**Experimental details and design (Reviewer 3)**
- In the two simulation experiments, OPS performs much worse in the first but fairly well in the second. Why? Is it due to instance-dependent metrics \(\delta\) and \(\Delta\)? How is \(\omega\) decided in SUAK? More clarification is needed.

**One-dimensional constraint only (Reviewer 4)**
- The paper only considers a one-dimensional cost constraint, whereas classical BwK typically involves multiple cost constraints. Is non-degeneracy still an issue when the constraint is one-dimensional? Can the methods generalize to multi-dimensional constraints? (Reviewer 4)

**Reviewer questions that overlap with above criticisms (included as part of weaknesses):**
- Reviewer 2: “What are the technical reasons why the problem cannot be reduced to recent works on bandits with constraints, which provide small constraint violations?”
- Reviewer 3: “Can you provide more elaboration why knowing \(\omega\) is necessary and whether it can be relaxed?”
- Reviewer 3: “Is it possible to show a worst-case \(O(\sqrt{T})\) regret for a policy like OPS, which does not require \(\omega\)?”
- Reviewer 3: “How do you decide \(\omega\) in SUAK?”
- Reviewer 4: “Is non-degeneracy still an issue when the constraint is one-dimensional? Can the methods generalize to multi-dimensional cost constraints?”
