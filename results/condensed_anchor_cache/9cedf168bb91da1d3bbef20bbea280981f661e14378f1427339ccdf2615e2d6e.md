## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies identifiability in latent variable models with multiple views, where each view is a nonlinear mixture of a subset of causally related latents. It proves that the information shared across any subset of views can be identified up to a smooth bijection using a single encoder per view and a contrastive loss (Eq. 3.1). An “identifiability algebra” provides graphical criteria for which latents are identifiable. The framework unifies and extends prior results in multi-view nonlinear ICA, disentanglement, and causal representation learning (e.g., von Kügelgen et al. 2021, Daunhawer et al. 2023). Experiments on synthetic data, Causal3DIdent, and Multimodal 3DIdent validate the claims, reporting R² scores.

### Strengths
- **Writing and clarity**: The paper is extremely well written, reads like a textbook, and uses a running example and “intuition” paragraphs to explain definitions and theorems (R1, R3, R4).
- **Simple and intuitive loss**: The loss function (Eq. 3.1) that yields identifiability is simple and intuitive, giving hope for eventual practical applicability (R1).
- **Unifying contribution**: The general identifiability result captures and unifies a wide variety of prior works in nonlinear ICA and causal representation learning, explaining the empirical success of existing methods (R1, R2). R2 specifically calls it a “neat unifying contribution.”
- **Efficient architecture**: The approach avoids an exponential number of encoders by using a single view-specific encoder, which is crucial for efficiency (R2).
- **Extension of prior work**: The paper extends content-identification results (von Kügelgen et al. 2021, Daunhawer et al. 2023) to multi-view distributions and introduces the “identifiability algebra” (R3).
- **Thorough empirical evaluation**: Experiments are conducted over multi-view, multi-modality, and multi-task datasets, well‑designed to substantiate the theoretical findings (R3). (One reviewer notes these are not fully realistic, see Weaknesses.)

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns** (minority view of R3 and R4, while R1 and R2 are more positive): The technical contribution is seen as incremental relative to prior work (von Kügelgen et al. 2021, Daunhawer et al. 2023) (R3). R4 states that theoretical contributions and proof techniques are mostly derived from Zimmermann et al. 2021 and von Kügelgen et al. 2021. R3 and R4 rated contribution “fair”; R1 and R2 rated it “good” or “excellent”.
- **Strong invertibility assumption**: Each view‑specific generating function is assumed invertible, requiring shared blocks to be duplicated multiple times, which simplifies the identification problem. Recent work [1] (unclear which reference) has attempted to weaken this assumption (R3).
- **Lack of realistic datasets**: The experiments do not use fully realistic datasets (R1). This is acknowledged by the authors and is a common limitation in this area, but it remains a weakness. (R3’s “thorough” evaluation does not contradict this, as the datasets used are still synthetic/semi‑synthetic.)
- **Questionable R² metric**: The paper reports R² scores, but these can be inflated. Alternative metrics like MCC (e.g., from [1] and [2] in the review) should also be reported (R2). This is a missing comparison/ablation.
- **Missing motivation for generality and encoder design**:
    - What practical benefit does identifying content blocks for *all* subsets of views simultaneously bring? Was this generality necessary to unify existing results? (R1 question – overlaps with missing justification.)
    - Why are view‑specific encoders needed? The theoretical reason is given, but in practice one would train with all views available (R4 question – overlaps with missing practical motivation).
- **Unclear definition and insufficient comparison**:
    - Definition 5.3 is unclear: why assume \(l_0\) to be the same rather than that the two vectors are the same? (R4 question – suggests a lack of clarity in the definition.)
    - The loss function’s relation to content alignment in related contrastive CRL works (e.g., [1] in R2’s references) is not discussed (R2 question – missing comparison). Also, variants of VaDEs might serve as a soft alignment inductive bias (R2) – missing discussion.
