## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SMORE-DRL, a transformer-based multi-objective deep reinforcement learning framework for molecular optimization. It introduces a hybrid fragment-SMILES tokenization, pre-trains a transformer encoder with masked language modeling and contrastive learning, then fine-tunes a masker-actor-critic architecture using gradient alignment (AMTL) to optimize multiple pharmacological properties (binding affinity, SA score, logP). Experiments compare against DeepFMPOv3D, DeepFMP, and MolDQN, claiming superior quality, efficiency, scalability, and generalization. One reviewer (score 5) is notably more positive; the other three (scores 3) raise major concerns.

### Strengths
- Tackles an important and fundamental problem: multi-objective molecular optimization in drug design.
- Hybrid fragment-SMILES tokenization captures diverse molecular structures and reduces the chemical search space.
- Gradient alignment technique (AMTL) mitigates conflicting/dominating gradients when optimizing multiple properties.
- Novel integration of a masked language modeling task into a DRL actor-critic framework (masker, actor, critic).
- SMORE-DRL outperforms baselines (DeepFMPOv3D, DeepFMP, MolDQN) on the fraction of molecules satisfying all property constraints (Table 2).
- Experiments demonstrate scalability and stability across large datasets.
- Appendix Figures 5, 6, 7 aid method comprehension.
- Anonymous code base is provided for reproducibility.

### Weaknesses
**Note:** The majority of reviewers (scores 3,3,3) raise the following critical concerns; one reviewer (score 5) is more positive but also notes issues.

- **Missing state-of-the-art baselines.** The paper does not compare against recent transformer-based methods (e.g., Chemformer (Irwin et al. 2022), He et al. (2022), REINVENT 4 (Loeffler et al. 2024)) nor against Pareto-based multi-objective optimization methods (e.g., Zhu et al. 2024, Xia et al. 2024). Without these, the claimed superiority is unsupported.
- **No similarity evaluation to lead molecules.** A core goal of lead optimization is generating molecules that closely resemble the starting lead while improving properties, but this metric is entirely absent.
- **Poorly structured and unclear methodology.** The method section is confusing: MDP construction is not explained before agent details; variables are introduced without definition (reward `R_{t,k}` in Eq. 4, advantage function `A_k` in Eq. 2); the phrase “output of the reward function” (lines 315-317) is ambiguous; motivation for design choices (e.g., why a pre-trained encoder is necessary) is not clarified.
- **Insufficient technical novelty.** The fragment+atom tokenization is not novel; previous Pareto-based multi-objective molecule optimization work is not discussed; the overall contribution over existing methods is unclear.
- **Formatting issues.** Font sizes in Figures 1, 2, 3 are too small for readability; the paper does not follow the official template.
- **Misleading term “contrastive learning.”** There is no explicit repulsion component for negative samples, which is a standard characteristic of contrastive learning.
- **Random fragment switching risk.** Randomly replacing a fragment can drastically alter molecular properties, and the method does not address this.
- **Limited target evaluation.** Section 4.2.1 evaluates on only one protein target, limiting generalizability conclusions.
- **Scalability claim not novel.** Parallel optimization of multiple leads is feasible for other transformer-based models and does not represent a significant technical advance.
- **Binding affinity threshold choice.** The threshold of −6 kcal/mol (?) is questioned as insufficient for a strong drug; justification is needed.
- **Inconsistent BAS focus reward results.** In Figures 8 and 9, the model with BAS focus reward does not produce more BAS-qualified molecules than the original model, and the original model’s count differs between figures—this discrepancy is unexplained and visualization should use distinct colors for variants.
- **Open questions requiring clarification:**
    - What specific GPUs were used for the reported computation time?
    - How is the “partial reward” assigned to incomplete molecules (line 305)? What is the rationale?
    - All SMILES are canonicalized for pretraining; how does the model avoid generating non-canonicalized SMILES, and why was SMILES randomization not employed?
