## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper categorizes current work in Federated Class-Incremental Learning (FCIL) into exemplar-based and data-free approaches, noting respective drawbacks (memory/privacy vs. efficiency). The authors propose a Hybrid Rehearsal (HR) approach combining both paradigms, using a customized autoencoder with a Lennard-Jones Potential formulation to generate synthetic data while maintaining low memory overhead. A mathematical framework formalizes local and global forgetting. Experiments show HR outperforms baselines on multiple FCIL benchmarks with lower compute and memory footprints.

### Strengths
1. **Accurate problem motivation and categorization**: The paper correctly identifies limitations of current exemplar-based methods (memory constraints, privacy risks) and data-free methods (efficiency issues), justifying the hybrid approach.
2. **Low computational and memory overhead**: Experimental results demonstrate that HR achieves lower overhead compared to several optimal baseline methods.
3. **Important problem addressed**: Combining data-based and data-free approaches to tackle both local and global forgetting is a relevant and timely challenge in FCIL.
4. **Effective hybrid integration**: The approach integrates latent exemplars (for local forgetting) and synthetic data generation (for global forgetting) efficiently; results show HR works better than pure exemplar or data-free methods.
5. **Theoretical foundation**: A mathematical formulation describing forgetting issues provides theoretical backing for the proposed solution.
6. **Useful ablation studies**: Ablation experiments contribute insights into different aspects of HR and improve interpretability.
7. **Novel replay mechanism with class centroids**: A replay mechanism based on centroids of each category is presented.
8. **Extensive evaluations**: Experiments span multiple benchmarks and include comparisons with state-of-the-art baselines.
9. **Well-organized and properly cited**: The paper is clearly structured and covers relevant related work.

### Weaknesses
*Note: Reviewers 1 (score 5) was notably more critical than the other three (all score 6). Several points below reflect that minority view.*

1. **Unclear explanation of local and global forgetting mechanisms** (all reviewers, strongly emphasized by R1 and R3): Figure 1 does not clearly indicate how client-specific data distributions contribute to local forgetting and how aggregation leads to global forgetting. The interaction between the two phenomena is not well-defined. A separate, detailed articulation of each mechanism is needed. The presentation is not clear; e.g., exemplars in HR and other exemplar-based methods seem different but are used interchangeably.
2. **Insufficient explanation of the autoencoder's role and training** (R2, R4):  
   - How the autoencoder addresses local forgetting is vague. The paper should provide a step-by-step breakdown: how the latent space is structured, how it facilitates replay of previous classes, whether the autoencoder is trained jointly with the classifier or separately, and how gradients are backpropagated.  
   - **Encoder forgetting**: The encoder itself may forget; stored features are fixed while the encoder is continuously updated, creating distribution inconsistency. The paper does not detail how latent space is regularized to prevent drift (R4).  
   - **Generative quality concerns**: The method relies on latent exemplars and class centroids for data generation, but VAEs are known to be suboptimal for high-quality synthetic data. Over time this could degrade latent features and classification performance. No analysis of generated sample quality is provided (R2).
3. **Missing comparisons and insufficient baselines** (R1, R2):  
   - The study lacks essential comparative methods, particularly state-of-the-art methods addressing similar challenges (R1).  
   - Comparisons with other hybrid methods such as REMIND are insufficient; the paper needs to explicitly highlight what makes HR unique architecturally and algorithmically (loss functions, optimization strategies, replay mechanisms) (R2).
4. **Insufficient experimental evaluation** (R1, R3):  
   - **Forgetting metrics missing**: No visualizations of accuracy on old tasks after each task; essential forgetting metric Backward Transfer (BWT) is absent. The evaluation metric is not clearly specified; table results do not clarify whether they represent incremental accuracy or accuracy of the last task. Higher incremental accuracy does not directly indicate lower forgetting (R1).  
   - **Limited skewness settings**: Results are limited to LDA setting with alpha=1. Extended experiments under different skewness (e.g., alpha=0.1 or more) should be included (R3).  
   - **Memory footprint analysis**: The number of parameters stored and transferred during communication should be reported (R3).  
   - **No visual representation of HR**: Figure 1 only illustrates the problem, not the proposed solution. A diagram showing data flow, encoder-decoder-memory interaction, and steps for local/global forgetting mitigation would significantly enhance understanding (R2).
5. **Motivation partially outdated** (R1, minority view): Literature (e.g., [1]) indicates that class imbalance within clients has already been effectively addressed, making the motivation somewhat less novel.
6. **Mathematical framework not closely linked to proposed approach** (R3): The formulation and the HR method are not well-connected, especially for global forgetting. It appears HR benefits from class centroid embeddings, but further analysis of how exactly the theoretical framework justifies the design is needed. The Lennard-Jones formulation is frequently referenced but insufficiently explained: its purpose in aligning class centroids to mitigate global forgetting, and the impact of its parameters, are not clarified (R2).
7. **Lack of discussion on limitations and future work** (R2): The conclusion does not address potential failure cases (e.g., highly complex data distributions, extreme class imbalances) or future directions (e.g., attention mechanisms, other generative models).
8. **Potential bias in global model due to heterogeneous client data** (R4): The paper does not adequately address how bias from multiple clients each learning a subset of categories is resolved when merging the global model, nor how robustness to imbalanced class representation across clients is ensured.
9. **Uncertain synthetic data generation for unseen classes** (R4): If a client receives class centroid embeddings for classes it has not seen, how is synthetic data generated, and could this be detrimental? The process and potential harmful effects are not explained.
10. **Error in literature review** (R3): Prototype reminiscence and augmented asymmetric knowledge aggregation [1] only addresses CIL, not FCIL, yet it is placed among FCIL methods.
11. **Minor presentation issues** (R2, R3):  
    - Acronym "AHR" is introduced in Section 4, line 232, without prior definition.  
    - Table 1 caption could be more descriptive.  
    - Data partition details are unclear: for ImageNet-Subset (10/20/100/10 and 20/10/100/10) and Tiny-ImageNet (10/5/300/30), total number of classes differs from dataset sizes; need explanation regarding class disjointness across tasks (R3).  
    - Algorithm 1 only updates theta_h, but Algorithm 2 begins h=1; why does each client need to train from task 1? (R3)  
    - In Equation (3), subscripts i and j and in Equation (4) subscript j_l need definition (R1).  
    - The paper should clarify how Eqs (10) and (11) achieve sample replay (R1) and how the classifier is configured (task-incremental or class-incremental) when clients train on different classes of the same task (R1).
12. **Practical concerns**: Can the proposed HR handle stragglers in practical FL systems? (R3) Memory cost estimate compared to exemplar-based methods and computational cost of forwarding latent exemplars through decoder should be quantified (R2).
