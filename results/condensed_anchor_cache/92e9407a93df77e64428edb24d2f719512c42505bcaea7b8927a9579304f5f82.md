## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Dynamic Scene Transformer (DyST) to learn latent neural scene representations from monocular dynamic videos without pose information. It decomposes scene content, per-view scene dynamics, and camera pose via a Camera Estimator and a Dynamics Estimator. A novel co-training scheme on monocular videos and the new synthetic dataset DySO (Dynamic ShapeNet Objects, with 5 scene dynamics and 5 camera views per dynamic scene video) enforces separation. The model enables novel view generation with independent control over the camera and the scene content.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles the more difficult setting of moving objects and moving cameras from monocular video without per-scene training, removing a key assumption of prior work (R1, R3). All reviewers agree this is a valuable direction.
- The training scheme for latent separation is a key contribution: the tricks in Eq.5 and Eq.6 (R1) and the latent control swap training (R2) enforce independence between camera pose and scene dynamics without architectural difference. Results in Fig.3 show a high improvement in PSNR (R2) and convincing specialization (R1). Co-training on synthetic and real data transfers dynamics and camera control to natural videos, as Fig.5 indicates that dynamics are encoded independently of camera pose (R2).
- The new synthetic dataset DySO provides multi-view, multi-dynamics data that enables the training strategy (R3).
- A more positive reviewer (score 8, R1) found the experiments (Fig.3, supplementary videos) convincing and the work reproducible; another (R3) considered the approach well-motivated.

### Weaknesses
- Motion amplitude and generalization: One reviewer (R1) noted that the motion in Fig.7 is tiny and not evaluated, raising concerns that accuracy may be limited by motion amplitude. Several reviewers (R1, R2) call for experiments on unseen camera poses and scene dynamics (e.g., horizontal shifts not in DySO training), larger displacements/rotations, and more complex motions. The ability to handle occlusions and self-occlusions is also unclear (R1).
- Backgrounds and scene complexity: The DySO dataset has clean backgrounds (R2). Reviewers (R1, R2) require experiments on videos with cluttered backgrounds, varying lighting, and intricate object interactions to test robustness; a distance analysis like Fig.5 for unclean scenes is suggested (R2).
- Quantitative comparison: The paper acknowledges a quality gap with NeRF (objects disappearing, blurring). Two reviewers (R2, R3) request quantitative comparisons (PSNR, LPIPS) with NeRF on real data. (R3 asks for any quantitative results on real data.)
- Multiple objects: The paper does not evaluate scenes with multiple objects (R2). Results for latent distance analysis (Fig.5), PSNR, and LPIPS (Fig.3) on multi-object scenes are needed (R2).
- Similarity to prior work: One reviewer (R3) notes that the encoder, decoder, and camera estimator are very similar to RUST, raising concerns about architectural novelty.
- Inference procedure: A reviewer (R3) questions whether the target view is required as input to obtain camera and dynamics latents, which would imply that the novel view image is needed to generate itself.
- Control of latent code: It is unclear how the latent code can be directly controlled (R3, regarding Fig.7) and whether dynamics not present in the data can be generated (R1, questioning interpretability and whether the approach can recreate dynamics that do not yet exist in the data).
- Additional missing ablations/justifications: One reviewer (R2) asks why 3 input views are needed for the latent control swap scheme and how performance changes with fewer or more views. Also, the purpose of the contrastiveness metric used in Table 1 is not explained (R2).
- Computational cost: The paper does not discuss training and inference cost (R1).
