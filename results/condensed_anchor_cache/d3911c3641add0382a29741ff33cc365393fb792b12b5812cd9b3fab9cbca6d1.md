## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SPEAR, a receiver-to-receiver neural warping field that predicts the relative transfer function (RTF) between two microphone positions in a fixed environment with a single stationary source. It is trained on synchronized recordings from two robots, bypassing explicit room geometry knowledge. The model is guided by three physical principles and evaluated on synthetic, photo-realistic, and real-world datasets. Reviewers note the novelty of the formulation but raise major concerns about motivation, relation to existing RTF literature, experimental rigor, and practical limitations.

### Strengths
- The receiver-to-receiver formulation is innovative, offering a new paradigm for spatial acoustic modelling that does not require prior knowledge of room geometry or acoustic properties (R1, R3).
- The method is relatively easy to apply: it needs only two synchronized recordings per training pair and no detailed environment information (R2).
- The work is methodologically rigorous with theoretical grounding (three physical principles) and comprehensive experiments across synthetic, photo-realistic, and real-world datasets (R1, R3).
- The paper is clearly written, well-structured, and contains meaningful illustrations that clarify the core ideas (R1, R3).
- The approach has value as an attempt to alleviate the data acquisition difficulty in sound field synthesis (R4).
- SPEAR achieves computational efficiency gains within moderate memory efficiency (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Ill‑conditioning of the ratio of transfer functions.** Estimating the ratio H₂/H₁ is prone to ill‑conditioning; the paper uses only clipping/zeroing without a robust regularization scheme. The frequency of ill‑conditioned cases (e.g., when H₁ or H₂ is zero) and their impact on performance are not quantified (R2, also noted by R4 regarding linear‑equation simplifiability).
- **Dense sampling and scalability.** Accurate results require dense sampling of receiver positions, but the computational cost (memory, processing time) is not adequately addressed. The minimum sampling rate needed to maintain acceptable performance is unexplored, and the method’s sensitivity to sampling density is not studied (R1). From the same measurements one could extract RIRs if the source signal is known, making the data requirement similar (R3). Extrapolation to unsampled regions is questionable – the method may only interpolate between very close measured locations (R2).
- **Horizontal‑plane restriction.** All receiver positions are assumed to lie on the same horizontal plane. There is no analysis of performance degradation when this constraint is violated, limiting applicability to multi‑level or irregular environments (R1). Receiver location constraints are otherwise not discussed (R2).
- **Unclear motivation and insufficient acknowledgment of prior work.**
  - The warping field is the Relative Transfer Function (RTF), an idea known for decades. Prior works have proposed learning RTFs from source‑receiver locations [2–4]; these are not cited, and the difference in contribution is not clarified (R3).
  - The paper is very similar to Deep Neural Room Acoustics Primitive [5], with the main difference being RTF estimation (requiring a fixed source) instead of RIR estimation. The key contribution over [5] is not made clear (R3).
  - The claim that receiver‑to‑receiver requires less prior information than source‑to‑receiver (e.g., NAF) is not convincingly justified. Training uses a known sine‑sweep signal, so the source signal is known – how is that more efficient than collecting RIRs? If the source were unknown, the prevalence of such scenarios and the model’s ability to generalize across different signals are not addressed (R4). One can directly obtain H_pr and H_pt from any source‑to‑receiver model and compute the warping field, raising the question of what the receiver‑to‑receiver learning adds (R4). The space of all possible warping fields (two‑receiver pairs) is combinatorially larger than RIRs (one receiver + source), which seems inefficient (R3).
  - The paper states “SPEAR superiority” (contrib. 3) without specifying the baseline of comparison (R2). Benefits/tradeoffs relative to conventional source‑to‑receiver methods are not clearly explained (R2).
- **Theoretical claims lack rigor.**
  - Proposition 1 is essentially Rayleigh reciprocity; the claim that the warping field is “independent of the audio source” is ambiguous – due to diffraction, the field should be frequency‑dependent. The mathematical space in which existence and uniqueness are claimed is not clearly defined (R4).
  - Proposition 2 is over‑general and its proof is vague; identifiability conditions from ICA are not acknowledged (R3).
  - Several statements are misleading (e.g., Principle 1: “the entire 3D space is involved”) and the LTI assumption is not explicitly justified (R4).
- **Experimental evaluation is insufficient and often subjective.**
  - Evaluation relies heavily on visual inspection of frequency‑domain warping field plots (Figs 4, 6, 7, 8). The estimated field often looks very different from the ground truth, and visual analysis alone is not robust (R2).
  - MSE values in Fig 5 are absolute and uninterpretable; a normalized MSE (e.g., in dB) is needed to assess quality (R2).
  - Table 3: it is unclear whether the metric is measured for the same signal; the dependency on frequency content is not discussed (R2).
  - Baseline selection is weak: at least INRAS should be included; also AV‑NeRF, DiffRIR. The ablation study does not sufficiently isolate the contributions of the components (R4).
  - Missing details: reverberation time, signal length, noise training conditions (R3). The warping field’s sensitivity to noise is not characterized (R3).
  - Some baselines are missing from figures: NN is absent from Fig 4 (R3) and Fig 5a (R3).
  - Figure ordering is inconsistent (Fig 8 appears before Figs 6‑7) (R3, R4). Wrong references to “Fig. B” and “Fig. C” (R4).
  - Appendix D is empty (R3). Failure case characteristics are not discussed (R3).
  - Table 3 reports a feature dimension of 384 and “43” in the initial token representation, and includes a pruning step at the output – none of which is explained (R3).
- **Practical usage and applicability are unclear.**
  - No guidance is given on how close receivers must be (and whether this is frequency‑dependent), what source signal to use, or how many grid points are needed to cover an environment (R2). The method requires retraining for a new source position or a new room (R2).
  - Real‑time optimizations and applications are not addressed (R1, R2).
  - Validation on non‑sine‑sweep signals (e.g., a clap) is missing; all datasets (Pyroomacoustics, Sound Spaces 2.0, MeshRIR) are essentially shoebox‑like, so performance in multi‑room or non‑line‑of‑sight scenarios (e.g., Replica Apartment) is untested (R4).
- **Notation, minor errors, and missing explanations.**
  - Inconsistent notation: p₁,p₂ vs pᵣ,pₜ for receivers and warping field (R2, R3). Equation (2) confusion (R2).
  - Lines 132‑139: relation to time‑series prediction unclear (R2).
  - Loss function (L1+L2) not explicitly given; no weighting parameter or motivation for using both (R2).
  - Line 151: notation $\mathcal{F}\leftarrow(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P})$ is unclear (R3).
  - Line 294: bilinear interpolation of grid features not explained (R3).
  - Motivation for Transformer architecture over simpler alternatives (MLPs, WaveNet) is absent; what the attention between patches learns is not discussed (R3, R4).
  - Why Fourier transform instead of STFT is not justified (R3).
  - Intuitive explanation for the multiplicative frequency‑domain warping claim (lines 162‑165) is missing (R2).
  - Determinant condition in Eq. 7 also applies to Eq. 5, but this is not mentioned (R2).
  - Typo: “PSESQ” → “PESQ” (R4). Lines 182, 205 should clarify that only LTI room acoustics are assumed (R4).
