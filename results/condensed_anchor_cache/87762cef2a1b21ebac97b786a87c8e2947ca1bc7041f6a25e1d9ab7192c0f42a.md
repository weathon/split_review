## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Direct Judgement Preference Optimization, applying DPO to train LLMs as evaluation judges by constructing preference data from existing labeled datasets for three tasks: pairwise comparison, single rating, and binary classification. Three training variants are used: Chain-of-Thought (CoT) critique, standard judgment, and response deduction. The trained models (8B, 12B, 70B) are evaluated on 13 benchmarks (7 pairwise, 4 single rating, 2 classification) and claim to outperform baselines including GPT-4o and specialized judge models. The work also reports bias mitigation, flexible prompting, and downstream model improvement.

### Strengths
- **Comprehensive evaluation framework**: The model handles three evaluation settings—pairwise comparison, single rating, and classification—making it adaptable (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- **Strong empirical performance**: The trained models, especially the 8B parameter version, outperform GPT-4o and specialized judge models on multiple benchmarks across all three tasks (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). Convincingly better results are reported (Reviewer 3).
- **Bias mitigation**: The model counters common biases (e.g., position and length bias) and shows robust generalization (Reviewers 1, 4).
- **Use of DPO with positive/negative pairs**: Applying DPO to improve evaluation is considered novel and well-motivated (Reviewer 3). Response deduction is a nice idea (Reviewer 3).
- **Extensive experiments and analyses**: Thorough evaluation on 7 pairwise, 4 single rating, and 2 classification benchmarks, plus bias evaluation, ablations, flexible prompting, and downstream improvements (Reviewers 2, 3, 4). Three training examples (CoT, standard, deduction) are thoroughly analyzed (Reviewer 4).
- **Significance of releasing a good evaluation model** (Reviewer 2, though notes release statement is missing).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and comparison to prior work**: 
  - The paper does not clearly differentiate its data curation from existing studies (Vu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024c) and appears incremental (Reviewer 2). 
  - Missing discussion of CriticGPT (RLHF for critique) and Themis (DPO-based evaluation using automatic preference construction). Themis is very similar in motivation and implementation; the paper lacks careful comparison (Reviewer 4). 
  - A comparison to multi-model evaluation systems like Reconcile would strengthen the work (Reviewer 3).
- **Data curation details**: 
  - Lack of clarity on how preference data is constructed for each task: e.g., how are positive/negative examples selected for pairwise comparison (human or model-generated)? How is correctness ensured? (Reviewer 2) 
  - Potential biases from the data curation process are not discussed; inherited dataset biases may affect fairness (Reviewer 2). 
  - The choice of 70%:15%:15% for DCoT, DStd, DDed appears arbitrary and lacks reasoning (Reviewer 3). 
  - Hardness of negatives is unexplored; quantifying hardness and whether a mix of easy/hard negatives helps is missing (Reviewer 3).
- **Scalability and real-world use**: 
  - The method relies heavily on human-annotated judgment labels to construct preference datasets, limiting scalability (Reviewer 4). 
  - The model requires an additional evaluation protocol input (human-crafted or GPT-4-generated) beyond the query and response, similar to Prometheus, which limits flexibility in real-world scenarios (Reviewer 4).
- **Coarse-grained evaluation aspects**: Training data uses only general quality aspects (factuality, helpfulness, safety) compared to fine-grained criteria in Auto-J and Prometheus (e.g., 58 tasks) (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing evaluation of critique quality**: Only judgment scores are evaluated; the quality of the Chain-of-Thought textual critiques is not assessed, despite prior work showing that score-consistent CoT can have flaws, affecting downstream self-improvement (Reviewer 4).
- **Design philosophy and ablation**: 
  - The rationale for each component and their relationships is not explained well; an ablation study (e.g., removing individual components) is missing (Reviewer 1). 
  - The relationship between Direct Judgement Preference Optimization and original DPO is unclear; how using three types of data differs from original DPO is not explicitly stated (Reviewer 1). 
  - Figure 4 (ablation) may miss the “SFT+DPO w/o CoT critique” condition (Reviewer 2).
- **Presentation and reproducibility issues**:
  - Long paragraphs need structuring (Reviewer 1). 
  - Tables and figures are far from their text descriptions, making them hard to read together (Reviewer 2). 
  - Evaluation metrics for pairwise and single rating tasks are not clearly defined; only classification accuracy is given (Reviewer 2). 
  - Hardware/software details missing, hindering reproducibility (Reviewer 2). 
  - Notations (M_t vs M_teacher in Section 3 and Fig. 2) mismatch; citep vs citet typos (e.g., line 41) (Reviewer 3). 
  - It is unclear which baseline models are trained for which tasks, how results were obtained (from papers or by running), and whether the proposed model was fine-tuned on benchmark data (Reviewer 2). 
  - The claim in line 464 about “average performance across all three tasks when removing each training task” is not clearly supported by Fig. 4 (Reviewer 2).
- **Minority positive contrast**: Reviewer 3 (score 8) considers the approach novel and results convincingly better, while Reviewers 1 and 2 (score 3) and Reviewer 4 (score 6) raise significant concerns about novelty, clarity, missing comparisons, and scalability.
