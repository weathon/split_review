## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a greedy learning-to-optimize (L2O) method that learns a sequence of preconditioners for gradient descent by minimizing the function value at the next iteration. The greedy training avoids memory constraints of unrolling, enabling training over many iterations. The preconditioner is linear, so parameter learning reduces to a convex problem at each step. Several parameterizations are explored, including a novel convolutional preconditioner for imaging. Convergence guarantees are provided under a “better than gradient descent” (BGD) assumption, and experiments on image deblurring and Computed Tomography show improved performance over classical optimizers (NAG, L-BFGS) and comparisons with unrolled training.

### Strengths

- **Novel and timely L2O approach.** The greedy training scheme is a novel and interesting way to scale L2O; it decouples iterates, avoids the memory bottleneck of unrolling, and is very timely for the area (R1, R3). Training is fast due to the simple linear parameterization, and the paper includes comparisons of greedy vs. unrolled training (R4).

- **Clear theoretical story.** The theoretical part is well-written, provides a clear and intuitive story, and offers a good starting point for generalizing L2O analysis (e.g., to non-convex settings) (R1, R4). The proof structure is accessible.

- **Promising empirical results.** The learned preconditioners (especially convolutional) outperform classical algorithms (NAG, L-BFGS) on inverse problems (image deblurring, CT), with impressive performance (R1, R3, R4). The learned kernels have a clear structure (Fig. 1c, 5c) that invites further investigation (R4).

- **Relevant contribution to L2O formalization.** Bringing convergence guarantees to L2O is a new and relevant contribution to the community (R2). The proposed algorithms show promising results in the experimental setup (R2).

### Weaknesses

- **Convergence theory relies on insufficiently justified BGD assumption.** The theoretical guarantees depend critically on the “better than gradient descent” (BGD) condition, which is assumed rather than proved (R2). The claim that a learned linear preconditioner *can* satisfy BGD does not mean it *will* in all cases; rigorous justification that the training procedure ensures BGD for the learned parameters is missing (R3). The “with Convergence Guarantees” claim is not fully convincing (R3). Corollary 1 is not sufficiently supported; the connection between Lemma 2 and Corollary 1 needs to be made explicit (R3). Theorem 2 uses t→∞ while requiring a final training iteration T—contradictory? (R2). The BGD assumption may be unnecessary for the unseen-function proof: a similar argument could be made without it if the preconditioner is positive definite (R2). Theorem 3 assumes a bounded sequence, which is a strange assumption to appear in this context (R1). Proposition 2 assumes linear independence arbitrarily—what if it does not hold? (R1). Propositions 1 and 2 lack the assumption that no entry of ∇f(x) is zero (R2).

- **Reliance on hand-crafted safeguards (global step‑size τ, λ_t search).** The algorithm requires knowledge of the maximum Lipschitz constant (or step‑size τ) over all training problems. The search for λ_T (p.18) seems arduous: is it performed at every iteration t, and how many problems must be solved to find λ_t? (R4). The condition for choosing λ_t forces G_φ to be positive definite, which is confusing given the paper’s claim that the method converges for non-p.d. preconditioners (R4). Relying on a fixed global τ across all training problems and iterations necessitates a conservative choice for the worst case, potentially hindering performance (R2). This does not fundamentally differ from alternative approaches that require safeguarding or search within a predefined set that guarantees convergence (R2).

- **Experimental validation has major gaps.**
    - **Generalization not clearly demonstrated.** Training and test problems only differ in x and b; the linear operator A is fixed. This is an easy problem because the goal is essentially to learn a preconditioner approximating the inverse of A^TA, which is the same across training and test. This does not adequately demonstrate generalization to different problem structures (R2). Only a single example is shown; no evaluation over the full dataset (R1). The algorithm may learn a preconditioner highly specific to the fixed A rather than a generalizable optimization strategy (R2).
    - **Missing comparisons.** No comparison with other existing L2O methods (R3), nor with state-of-the-art provably convergent L2O schemes (e.g., Banert et al.) (R3). No comparison with specialized algorithms for deblurring with convolutional preconditioners (e.g., Eboli et al.) (R2). For quadratic problems, comparison with a regularized version of (A^*A)^{-1} preconditioning would be illuminating (R4).
    - **Unclear source of improvement.** The benefit from the convolutional preconditioner could come from the convolutional structure itself rather than from the “learning to optimize” approach; a BFGS-like method using convolution might achieve similar results (R2). Why are fully learned (non-convolutional) preconditioners poor? Is this observed across all problems? (R1).
    - **Scale and parameter choices.** Only small-scale problems (40×40, 96×96) are tested; behavior at larger sizes (256×256, 1024×1024) is unknown (R1). The step‑size τ = 1/L is used for NAG, but in practice larger step-sizes often maintain convergence; a proper comparison with tuned parameters for NAG and L-BFGS would help isolate the effect of pixel interactions (R4). How are ground truths x* computed? (R4). Wall-clock time at test-time would be useful (R4).
    - **Interpretation and presentation.** The numerical section is difficult to read, mixing parameter choices with explanations; it should be better summarized (R1). Summary/interpretation is missing—what do the results show beyond efficiency over classical methods? (R1). Figure 3a is presumably an initialization, not a reconstruction; Figure 5 lacks a ground truth image, only showing sinogram and reconstruction (R1).

- **Memory and scalability issues.** The greedy approach stores a different preconditioner for each iteration; this may scale linearly with the number of training iterations (R1, R2). The claim of “constant memory usage” needs clarification (R2). The method is limited to convex and differentiable functions (R1), and cannot learn iterative schemes that use memory of past iterates (momentum), a significant limitation compared to unrolling-based L2O (R3). A hybrid approach combining greedy with unrolling might yield better performance (R3). These limitations should be acknowledged more explicitly (R1, R3).

- **Minor technical and presentation issues.**
    - Equation 2 should clarify that y is varied over some underlying distribution (R1).
    - The value of assuming X is a finite‑dimensional Hilbert space is unclear (R1).
    - Equation 9 uses a different regularizer than Equation 2; notation should distinguish them (R1).
    - Equation 16 is unclear; what is B_k? (R2).
    - Line 327: should be “(PC)” instead of “(PP)”? (R4).
    - Section 6: what is Y? (R1).
    - Proposition 4 can be proved more succinctly; Proposition 5 can use coordinate projection (R4).
    - Proof of Lemma 1 has notation inconsistency and the first inequality mismatches the BGD definition (R4).
    - Proof of Theorem 1 needs clarification on telescoping arguments (R4).
    - Line 40 should reference Theorem 1 when claiming convergence of sums of f_k (R4).
    - Line 737 typo: RHS should be *g*_{t,0}(\tilde{θ}) and ∇*f_k* on second line (R4).
    - A short reference to App. D.2 (choice of full-image convolution) would be helpful in the main text (R4).
    - The method’s focus on imaging with convolutional preconditioners should be clearer in the title/abstract/introduction (R3).
    - The paper’s question about whether L2O can overcome the issues in https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.06148 is raised (R1).

- **Theoretical guarantees on generalization are weak.** Theorem 2 only guarantees existence of functions where the algorithm works—a claim akin to “there exist unseen samples the algorithm classifies correctly,” which is much weaker than a typical generalization guarantee (R2). Would it be possible to guarantee convergence on *all* 1/τ‑smooth problems, or to characterize how the learned preconditioner leads to better performance when train and test share the same operator A? (R2). The related work discussion on optimization (e.g., Armijo line-search, optimal diagonal preconditioners, AdaGrad, adaptive bound optimization, multidimensional Armijo, parameter-free methods) should be acknowledged (R2). The convolution-based preconditioner generalizes Laplacian smoothing gradient descent (Osher et al., 2022); have the authors considered other linear preconditionings that might perform better? (R4).
