## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a method for Euclidean (k-means) clustering that is claimed to be closed-form, based on the observation that clustered data lie near a subspace whose projection operator encodes the clustering. The key step is a singular value decomposition. Under the assumption that clusters are “well-separated” (sufficiently separated and intra-cluster scatter small), the method is proven to deterministically recover the true centroids and assignments. Experiments on synthetic data and several single-cell sequencing datasets are presented. Reviewer opinions diverge: three reviewers assign a score of 3, while one reviewer (R4, confidence 2) assigns a score of 5, finding the method simple and effective, though less confident in their assessment.

### Strengths
- **Clear motivation and goal** (R1). Reasoning and proofs in Sections 3 and 4 appear solid (R1).
- **Well-written paper** (R2, R3). Technical presentation is clear, easy to follow, and concise (R2, R3).
- **Diverse experimental setting** including synthetic data and real single-cell sequencing datasets (R2). The application to single-cell sequencing provides significant value (R3).
- **Simple and effective method** (R4). Under the “well-separated” assumption, the method gives a deterministic guarantee on correctness (R4).
- **Minority positive view** (R4, score 5): Considers the method simple but effective and the deterministic guarantee valuable, in contrast to the more critical assessments of R1–R3.

### Weaknesses
- **Strong assumptions limit generality.** The theoretical result relies on a very strong assumption that clusters are well-separated (R2, R3, R4). Many other k-means algorithms perform well (both theoretically and practically) without such assumptions (R2, R3). The assumption is similar to α-perturbation resilience used in prior work, but the paper does not compare with that line of work (R4, citing Cohen-Addad & Schwiegelshohn 2017; Angelidakis et al. 2017).
- **Missing comparison with existing work on separated/well-separated instances.** There is a body of work showing that Lloyd-type methods, k-means++, or spectral methods can achieve near-optimal clustering under separability assumptions (R2, R3). The paper fails to cite and compare with, e.g., Ostrovsky et al. (JACM 2013), Jaiswal & Garg (WAOA 2012), Vempala & Wang (JCSS 2004), Dasgupta (FOCS 1999) (R3).
- **Unclear relation to k-means clustering.** The problem studied (recovering a planted clustering defined by additive noise) differs from the standard unsupervised k-means optimization problem (R3). The phrase “true clustering” is ambiguous; the k-means objective can have multiple optimal solutions (R3). The abstract’s claim that “this paper gives a closed-form solution to Euclidean clustering, also known as k-means clustering” is too strong and false in general—k-means is NP-hard (R3). The equivalence “k-means = Euclidean clustering” is not standard (R3).
- **Practical closed-form claim misleading.** The algorithm is not truly closed-form because it relies on relaxations that require alternating methods sensitive to initialization, so the theoretical guarantees from Section 3 no longer apply (R1). Additionally, the main result requires knowledge of ||Z||, i.e., the optimal clustering, to set the threshold (R3).
- **Slow and lacking complexity analysis.** According to Figure 2, the proposed method is slower than other iterative methods (R1). No detailed algorithm or time/memory complexity is provided (R1, R4). It appears linear in K and n but much steeper than Lloyd’s (R1). It is unclear if the method scales to large (e.g., million-scale) datasets (R4).
- **Incomplete experimental details.** The datasets used (both synthetic and real) are not described in terms of dimensionality, number of samples, or other key characteristics (R1, R4). No visualizations of the data are provided to verify that the data satisfy the “well-separated” assumption (R1). The experimental section is considered outdated; comparisons with strong baselines such as greedy k-means++ or random-walk based methods are missing (R2). Table 1 shows mixed performance: the proposed CF method outperforms baselines on Buenrostro6 and Buenrostro7 but not on Larry, Nestorowa, or Stassen, while AONMF is consistently good (R1). The reasons for these performance differences are not discussed (R1).
- **Unsubstantiated claims about guarantees.** The paper states that “no practical algorithm provides correctness guarantees” for k-means, but existing algorithms (e.g., k-means++, PTAS, local search) provide multiplicative approximation guarantees on the objective, including under separability (R3). The guarantee discussed in the paper is about recovering the true labeling, not about the k-means objective (R3).
- **Irrelevant and underdeveloped sections.** The mentions of Hierarchical Clustering and Robust Estimation on page 6 are only future possibilities, not concrete analysis, and are unrelated to the paper’s contributions (R1).
- **Missing theoretical comparisons and ablations.** The assumption that centroids are drawn from a standard normal distribution is strong, and no experiments with other distributions (e.g., Poisson) are shown, despite the paper claiming distribution-free guarantees (R1). The effect of unknown K on the method and the theory is not explored (R1). The scalability to large datasets is not addressed (R4).
