## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper benchmarks ten fairness-aware graph learning methods (pre-2022) on seven real-world datasets for node classification. It evaluates group fairness, individual fairness, utility, and computational efficiency, and provides practical guidance. Reviewers generally acknowledge the timeliness and scope of the benchmark, but raise concerns about limited novelty (mostly reproducing known methods), insufficient depth of analysis, missing recent methods (post-2022), lack of experimental details, and focus only on node classification. One reviewer (R5, score 6) finds the work well-written and a useful first benchmark, while others (R3, R4, score 3) consider the contribution low and the evaluation insufficient. There is broad agreement that practical insights are limited and that many key aspects (e.g., structural bias, temporal fairness, scalability) are not addressed.

### Strengths
- **Comprehensive benchmark scope.** The paper provides a systematic evaluation of ten fairness-aware graph learning methods (including group and individual fairness) with a clear timeline and categorization (R1). The empirical evaluation covers seven datasets from different domains (social networks, finance) (R1, R3).
- **Well-organized experiments.** Extensive experiments are conducted with multiple utility, group fairness, and individual fairness metrics; hyperparameter tuning and multiple runs are reported (R1, R2). The results are partially compared in figures to validate findings (R1).
- **Practical guidance.** The paper offers guidance for practitioners to select fairness-aware methods (R1), though reviewers note it does not fully consider efficiency trade-offs (R1) or task scope (R5).
- **Clarity and reproducibility effort.** The work is well-written and easy to follow (R2, R5). Key objectives and methods are introduced clearly, and metrics are well explained (R2).
- **Originality and significance acknowledged.** Several reviewers note that this is a needed reference point for comparing fairness-aware graph methods (R2, R5). The distinction from prior benchmark efforts is clear (R2).
- **Reproduction of pre-2022 methods.** The authors successfully reproduced and compared pre-2022 fairness methods (R4).
- **Minority positive view.** One reviewer (R5, score 6) emphasizes that the paper tackles a useful problem not previously benchmarked and is well-organized.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and depth.** The evaluation is mostly descriptive—reporting that different methods perform differently—without providing an in-depth discussion of *why* (e.g., how different objective functions favor certain fairness notions) (R1). The results largely confirm known fairness-utility trade-offs without offering novel insights beyond practitioner experience (R2). The systematic evaluation protocol amounts to running ten baselines on standard datasets; no novel architecture or system design is introduced (R5).
- **Inclusion of outdated methods.** The benchmark only covers methods up to 2022 and omits recent advancements such as Graphair (ICLR 2023), FairSAD (WWW 2024), and methods addressing intersectional biases, temporal fairness, or structural bias propagation (R2, R3, R4). This limits relevance for contemporary applications (R2).
- **Limited task scope.** The benchmark evaluates only node classification, which limits its informativeness and impact. Applicability to link prediction, hybrid fairness objectives, or other graph-based tasks is not addressed (R2, R5). The practical recommendations should explicitly state they hold only for node classification (R5).
- **Insufficient experimental details.** Important details are missing: hyperparameter search ranges, validation criteria, dataset preprocessing steps, and final hyperparameters used (R2). The exact ranges for grid search and how they were adjusted per dataset are unclear (R2 Q1). This hinders reproducibility (R2).
- **Dataset selection not justified.** The choice of datasets is not transparent: group density, class/group balance, and how the dominance of one group affects performance are not reported or discussed (R1). The newly constructed datasets AMiner-S and AMiner-L are not found in the supplemental materials (R1 Q1).
- **Lack of deeper analysis on fairness metrics.** The rationale for using multiple individual fairness metrics is not explained; potential conflicts among them and how to interpret whether metric values indicate good or poor performance are missing (R1, R3). The significance of comparing individual vs. group fairness given their known trade-off is not addressed (R4).
- **Confusions and contradictions in findings.** There are inconsistencies: Finding 1 (for RQ1) states GNNs best balance group fairness and accuracy, while Finding 3 (for RQ3) suggests DeepWalk-based methods balance all three metrics best, but “Utility” in RQ3 is not defined (e.g., not AUC-ROC), making reconciliation difficult (R5). The statement “neither DeepWalk nor GNN yields top-ranked performance under utility” is unclear because DeepWalk ranks lower in utility than fairness (R1 Q2). Figure 3 dot colors are not explained (R1 Q3). RQ3 is written confusingly (R5).
- **Missing ablations and crucial baselines.** The paper does not test GNNs without node attributes (or with random attributes), despite claiming that the “absence of bias brought by node attributes” is important (R2 Q5). The evaluation lacks fairness-enhancing architectures or hybrid models beyond the selected ten (R2). The balance between fairness and utility is not compared with existing trade-off baselines (R4).
- **Insufficient efficiency and scalability analysis.** Although computational efficiency is mentioned, it is not incorporated into the practical guidance (R1). Scalability on large-scale graphs (e.g., social networks, knowledge graphs) is not thoroughly investigated; the evaluation should include a detailed analysis of computational cost and memory requirements across varying graph sizes (R2).
- **Statistical validity concerns.** The confidence intervals in Figure 2 heavily overlap, casting doubt on the statistical significance of the claims (R2 Q6).
- **Interpretation of figures lacking.** Figure 4’s “blurred trade-off” is not explained; the insights it provides regarding balance between fairness metrics are unclear (R4). Figure 5 does not include fairness metrics for baselines, which is unjustified (R4).
- **Missing context on domain variation.** The benchmark does not account for how fairness definitions and bias impact differ across domains (e.g., financial vs. social networks) (R2 Q4).
- **Contradictions in claimed broader applicability.** The paper claims its approach is “the most widely used and impactful works” but does not define or substantiate this; other fairness methods can also operate across multiple datasets (R4). The proposed framework shows performance gaps in both utility and fairness compared to stronger methods, undermining its practical advantage (R4).
- **Efficiency evaluation limited.** Efficiency comparison only considers performance (utility) without fairness metrics; the contribution of comparing efficiency alone is questioned (R4 Q3).
