## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies Bayesian optimization (BO) for discovering general reaction conditions that work well across multiple chemical substrates. The problem is formulated as a generality-oriented optimization over curried functions, where the goal is to maximize expected performance over a distribution of substrates. The authors benchmark several BO strategies on real-world experimental chemistry datasets. They find that simple sampling strategies—in particular, random selection of substrates—often match or outperform more sophisticated methods, and that effective optimization is determined by exploration of both parameter and substrate space.

### Strengths
- The problem formulation and motivation are clearly presented; the curried functions perspective adds mathematical clarity to the problem of finding general optima.
- The work focuses on a significant real-world scientific problem (general parameters discovery in chemistry) and uses real-world experimental datasets, giving practical insight.
- The benchmark is well-established, and the code implementation is well-structured and clear.
- The experiments are comprehensive, comparing multiple recent BO algorithms; the “negative result” that a simple random-greedy strategy works well is refreshing and honestly reported.
- The paper is well written and the method is well described.

### Weaknesses
- **Audience fit and presentation depth.** The paper is strongly domain-specific (chemistry) and places many crucial details in the appendix, making it difficult to follow. Several reviewers noted limited relevance to the ICLR audience (little on representation learning). The work may be better suited for a chemistry or data-driven-engineering journal.
- **Problem formulation novelty.** The formulation in Section 2 is not new: depending on the aggregation function Φ, the problem reduces to Bayesian Optimization with Expensive Integrands (mean, threshold) or Robust/Minimax Bayesian Optimization. The curried-function framing adds unnecessary complexity and obscures the core objective (estimating expected performance over substrates). Section 2.2.2 is not representative of existing literature and requires substantial revision.
- **Insufficient related-work discussion and baselines.** The paper lacks systematic discussion and experimental comparison with stochastic BO and distributionally robust BO (DRBO). Only chemistry-domain methods are compared; state-of-the-art BO algorithms from the general BO literature are not included. This raises doubts about whether the reported advantage of random selection is genuine or an artifact of weak baselines.
- **Weak experimental analysis of substrate sampling.** The conclusion that difficulty stems from the substrate sampling strategy is based on a narrow comparison (random vs. posterior-variance acquisition for w under a single fixed x strategy). A more comprehensive analysis should compare multiple acquisition policies for both w and x (e.g., UCB with varying β, pure exploration, Vizier Bandit). Furthermore, the sudden performance surges of random methods in figures are not explained.
- **Limited algorithmic novelty and insight.** The work mainly applies existing BO methods with little algorithmic novelty. Deeper analysis of why simple strategies perform well is missing; for instance, the effectiveness of random substrate sampling suggests the surrogate model for w is ineffective or the prior is misspecified, yet this is not investigated. The design of the statistical model over unordered substrates is not discussed in sufficient detail.
- **Minor presentation issues.** The paper contains unclear sentences (e.g., penultimate sentence on page 1), inconsistent tenses (Section 3 vs. 4), inconsistent capitalization of section headings, missing/incorrect citations (e.g., “SAA” without citation, GPyTorch citation missing), and formatting errors (e.g., “thresholdBetinol” missing space, “, that” → “, which”). Additionally, clarification is needed for the GAP metric (full name and nature) and for the justification of the random forest oracle (e.g., Spearman correlation on a held-out test set).
