## Merged Review

### Summary
FairLoRA is a parameter-efficient debiasing method that combines LoRA modules with group discriminators. The discriminators predict whether a sample belongs to a minority group; the corresponding LoRA block is activated only for those samples, preserving majority‑group performance. The approach supports chained fine‑tuning for multiple sensitive attributes, requires few sensitive‑attribute labels, and is evaluated on vision and NLP tasks. Theoretical conditions are provided under which bias mitigation without performance loss is possible.

### Strengths
- FairLoRA leverages parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning (LoRA), making it much more efficient than retraining from scratch or full fine‑tuning (R1).
- Selective activation of LoRA blocks only on minority‑group samples strategically maintains majority‑group performance while improving minority‑group performance (R1, R2).
- Supports multiple sensitive attributes and intersectional fairness through chained fine‑tuning; earlier adjustments are not degraded when later attributes are added (R1, R4).
- Very few conditions for application, making it a versatile solution for enhancing fairness of pretrained models (R2).
- Significant improvements in worst‑group accuracy (WGA) are observed even when only a small number of sensitive‑attribute labels are available (Table 3) (R2).
- Experiments in both vision (CelebA) and language (HateXplain, MNLI) are presented (R2, R4).
- Multiple fairness metrics are used to support the claim of improved or maintained performance (R1, R3).
- The paper provides theoretical theorems and proofs (Thm 1–3) (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical contribution overclaimed and insufficient.** The conclusion of Thm 3 (that the ratio between TPR and FPR must be high to prevent performance degradation) is obvious; Thms 1 and 2 appear to be intermediate steps and should be lemmas (R2). The analysis does not guarantee that FairLoRA satisfies its own fairness condition (Eq. (7)) (R4).
- **No direct head‑to‑head comparison with existing debiasing methods.** FairLoRA is only applied on top of ERM and fairness baselines (e.g., GroupDRO, DFR); it is not compared as a standalone alternative (R3). The novelty of integrating discriminators or LoRA for fairness is already well explored in the literature (R4).
- **Requires a balanced dataset for fine‑tuning** (majority‑group size = minority‑group size), which forces downsizing of already scarce minority‑group examples and may not benefit extremely underrepresented groups (R1). The method also still requires knowledge of which samples contain the sensitive attribute, limiting applicability in unsupervised scenarios (R4).
- **Experimental results are limited and not always significant.** For CelebA, only one attribute combination (“male” and “blond hair”) is tested; additional combinations should be evaluated (R1). In Table 1, most improvements are within standard deviations, questioning significance (R3, R4). The number of random seeds is not reported; only a single set of hyperparameters is used (R3).
- **Vague experimental details.** The models used for Experiments 1 and 3 are not specified; the claim that they are “state‑of‑the‑art” is unsubstantiated (R3). Which dataset splits (training/validation/test) are used is not clearly stated (R3). The claim that FairLoRA “operates under a partial‑information setting, where group labels are observed only for the validation set” is incorrect for the experiments as described; it can *also* be applied in that setting, but the experiments do not enforce it (R3).
- **Writing and presentation need improvement.** Section 3 is hard to follow (training pipeline details and algorithms are relegated to the appendix) and the caption of Figure 1 is insufficient (R4). The variable *M* in Section 4.1.1 is not defined (R1). Minor: Table 3 has a typo (“WRA” should be “WGA”) (R2), and there are grammatical typos (e.g., “can significantly reducing” → “can significantly reduce”, “may worsens fairness” → “may worsen fairness”) (R1).
- **Dataset and terminology issues.** MNLI is a natural‑language‑inference dataset, not a fairness benchmark; the paper should use dedicated fairness datasets like Winogender, HolisticBias, or CrowS‑Pairs (R1). The paper refers to “African” instead of “African American”/“Black American” when describing a racial‑bias group, which conflates a continent with a racial group; similarly “FairLoRA Africa” should be reworded (R1).
- **Missing analyses.** Worst‑group accuracy is not reported in Table 2 (R4). The effect of changing the sequence of debiasing (e.g., gender → racial vs. racial → gender) or of eliminating multiple biases simultaneously (instead of sequentially) is not explored (R4). If the base adaptation technique learns representations that are already invariant to the sensitive attribute, FairLoRA’s discriminators may have limited information to work with—this scenario is not discussed (R2). The reasons for the improvement in equalized odds (EoD(R)) when mitigating gender bias on top of racial debiasing (Table 2) are not explained (R3). Limitations of the work should be explicitly addressed (R4).

**Reviewer disagreement:** Two reviewers found the approach promising (scores 6 and 8) and highlighted its efficiency, versatility, and strong WGA gains with few labels. Two other reviewers raised significant concerns (scores 3 and 3), focusing on insufficient novelty, weak significance of experimental results, vague experimental details, and overclaimed theory.
