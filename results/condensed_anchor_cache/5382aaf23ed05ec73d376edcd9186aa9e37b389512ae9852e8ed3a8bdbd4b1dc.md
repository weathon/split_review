## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper introduces SAFEMLLM, an adversarial tuning framework to defend multimodal large language models (MLLMs) against jailbreak attacks. It operates in two stages per iteration: (1) generating adversarial perturbations via a contrastive embedding attack (CoE-Attack) that optimizes token embeddings, and (2) updating model parameters to neutralize these effects while preserving utility on benign inputs. Evaluations across six MLLMs and six jailbreak methods (image, text, image-text) demonstrate defense effectiveness.

### Strengths

- **Novelty and relevance of the problem**: The paper addresses an underexplored area—multimodal adversarial training for VLMs—and provides insights into design choices for this context (Reviewer 3). Most reviewers acknowledge this as a novel contribution, though one reviewer strongly questions novelty (see Weaknesses). The work is crucial for enhancing secure deployment of VLMs (Reviewer 2).

- **Comprehensive and thorough experiments**: The framework is evaluated across six MLLMs and six jailbreak methods, covering image-based, text-based, and image-text jailbreak attack scenarios (Reviewer 1, 2, 3, 4). Robustness is examined from multiple perspectives, facilitating comparison between multi-modal and LLM-only robustification approaches (Reviewer 3). State-of-the-art attack methods are used, and exhaustive hyperparameter ablations are conducted (Reviewer 3).

- **Clear presentation**: The writing is fluid, clear, and easy to read (Reviewer 1, 4). The idea is easy to understand and the paper is easy to follow (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited novelty compared to prior work**: Several reviewers question the contribution’s novelty. The two-stage algorithm is essentially standard adversarial training with non-typical loss functions; the framing could be clearer (Reviewer 3). The attack is only on the embedding layer with no modification to image encoder or other VLM-specific components. Prior LLM latent adversarial training methods (Sheshadri et al. 2024, Casper et al. 2024) have demonstrated attacks on multiple layers (including embedding) and then fine-tuned against them—it is unclear why these cannot be directly applied to MLLMs (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 2 also asks why optimization is done at the embedding token level rather than the representation level.

- **Clarity of formulas and optimization details**: Definitions are unclear: what do \(P_h\) and \(P_t\) represent (lines 240-244), and why initialize two tokens instead of one? (Reviewer 2). It is unclear how Eq.2 guarantees output quality and relevance to the query (Reviewer 2). There is formula redundancy: Eq.3 and Eq.4 describe the same optimization objective (loss_adv and loss_def)—this should be reduced (Reviewer 2). Table 3 is unclear: results are not shown separately for the ImgJP and AdvBench datasets; the distinction between \(J_{target}/J_{contra}\) and \(L_{target}/J_{contra}\) is ambiguous (Reviewer 2). Figure 3 tests only 100 samples; 500 benign samples are recommended to effectively assess changes in model output quality (Reviewer 2). The dataset used for Figure 3 is not specified (Reviewer 3). Table 1 has many missing values for the VLGuard metric (Reviewer 1).

- **Garbled output problem**: Using \(L_{contra}\) and \(J_{contra}\) during fine-tuning can lead to meaningless garbled outputs (e.g., repeating “safe”) (lines 511-513). The authors need to explain why this occurs and how to prevent it, and provide examples of model outputs in both successful and failed optimization scenarios (Reviewer 2).

- **Limited scope of modalities**: The framework only considers image and text data, excluding audio, video, and other multimedia types. This restricts real-world applicability; many jailbreak attacks could exploit temporal processing vulnerabilities (Reviewer 1). Figure 2 only shows noisy image and text—can the framework incorporate other modalities? (Reviewer 1).

- **Missing experimental details and comparisons**:
  * The claim that optimizing adversarial perturbations on both image and text simultaneously is computationally intensive lacks empirical support. No experiments show that the framework can reduce overall computing resources (Reviewer 1).
  * No ablation on whether images are used during attack optimization (Reviewer 3). Starting from an encoded image initialization could be helpful—has it been ablated? (Reviewer 3).
  * Hyperparameters of competitor approaches are not provided; there are no ablations regarding the “best” reasonably achievable robustness with different methods, making direct comparison difficult (Reviewer 3).
  * More recent attack methods (GPTFuzzer, PAIR) should be added to evaluation (Reviewer 4).

- **Dataset selection and rationale not justified**: Why were the four specific datasets chosen? What are their criteria and suitability? For LLaVA-Instruct-80K, why was it chosen to assess utility of fine-tuned models? A brief summary of each dataset’s relevance would help (Reviewer 1).

- **Explanation gaps**: The introduction claims VLGuard is effective against black-box but not white-box attacks—further explanation is needed on why an attacker’s parameter/gradient knowledge enables more effective attacks and how defense differs (Reviewer 1). How was over-refusal measured after safety training? (Reviewer 3). The argument in line 90 only applies to Mazeika et al.; the framing could be improved (Reviewer 3). The information that “in all MLLMs, the image is always placed before the text as input” would be helpful in the method description to give intuition about \(P_0^h\) (Reviewer 3).

- **Minor presentation issues**: The Introduction could more clearly emphasize the article’s unique contribution, originality, and effectiveness (Reviewer 1).
