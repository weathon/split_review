## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces MIRAGE, a multivariate time-series forecasting model with interpretability. It extends the T-DPSOM architecture with a Deep Markov Model (DMM) that adds a state-transition loss to handle non-smooth data. Additional components include an Attention Module (AM), Damping Factor (DF), Forecasting Fine-tuning (FFT), and a Self-Organizing Map (SOM). The model learns Markov state structures via clustering and maps trajectories to a SOM-VAE latent space (proposed in prior work). Attention weights and SHAP values are extracted for explainability. Experiments use public datasets (eICU, ETTh) and real-world data (game player, medical ICU).

### Strengths
- Interpretability in time-series forecasting is an important, open problem.
- The paper presents specific insights into how the MIRAGE architecture can be extended, offering a clear path for future research.
- It challenges the assumption of smooth latent changes in prior work, introducing interesting ideas.
- A variety of time-series datasets (public and real-world, including game player and medical ICU) are considered, providing some breadth of analysis.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of novelty and motivation**: The exact novelty over the T-DPSOM baseline is unclear. The motivation for the DMM architecture is not well explained. What specific failure of T-DPSOM does DMM address? The paper reads as a large, complex collection of unmotivated components with little insight into why each is necessary.
- **Underperformance relative to baselines**: In Table 1, MIRAGE underperforms Crossformer on MSE and MAE for all public datasets. The paper bolds MIRAGE’s values, but Crossformer clearly has lower scores, creating potential misinterpretation (typo or intentional?). Table 2 only shows T-DPSOM on ICU data; MIRAGE analysis on that dataset is missing. Table 3 does not specify which dataset(s) are used (is it average performance?). For player data, MIRAGE shows negligible improvement over Crossformer. Given these results, the usefulness of the MIRAGE architecture is questionable.
- **Limited interpretability analysis on most datasets**: The interpretability analysis focuses heavily on the player dataset, while eICU, ETTh, and other datasets are treated superficially. A detailed examination of feature importance and their temporal dynamics is lacking for those datasets, undermining the generalizability of the interpretability claims. Reviewer 1, who was more positive overall (score 5 vs. scores 3 from others), still identified this as a major weakness.
- **Absence of quantitative interpretability evaluation**: The interpretability results rely on a few illustrative examples (it is unclear if these are randomly selected or cherry-picked). There are no rigorous numerical evaluations of interpretability (e.g., correctness of latent trajectories, consistency of attention/SHAP explanations). How are “smooth interpretations” determined in Figure 7? Can authors provide numerical metrics to support interpretability? Questions such as “How can HUFL be interpreted as contributing to a drop in OT, while LULL also shows a trend over a similar timescale?” highlight the need for deeper validation.
- **Missing comparisons and ablations**: Key comparisons are omitted: e.g., Temporal Fusion Transformer (suggested by Reviewer 1). Why was an LSTM used instead of a transformer for prediction? How is the number of clusters $C$ determined? The reconstruction error is not reported (Table 2 missing eICU MSE). A proper ablation study separating the contributions of DMM, AM, DF, FFT, and SOM is missing. The loss function component $L_{DPSOM}$ is not defined in the paper; without reading the T-DPSOM paper, the model is incomprehensible.
- **Poor presentation and readability**: The paper is very hard to follow, especially Section 4 (Evaluation). The model description is not step-by-step; the interaction of components (DMM, AM, DF, FFT, SOM) and their mathematical formulations are insufficiently detailed, hindering reproducibility.
    - Figures: Figure 1 is overcrowded, with undefined elements, poor alignment, and low quality. Figure 2 is not labeled; what are “CCE loss” and “shift loss”? Experimental figures (e.g., Fig 4) have tiny text, no legends (dashed vs. solid lines), and unlabeled axes. Figures 3–5 and the heatmap generation process (e.g., whether the heatmap represents attention weights) are unclear. Table/Figure captions are not self-explanatory.
    - Notation: Many symbols are undefined (e.g., index $w$, difference between $Z$ and $z$, “alternate features”).
    - Language: Overcomplicated and filled with undefined or unclear phrases (“proactive comprehension of trajectory to an extremity”, “observations are competitively mapped”, “part of its learning stride”, “collaboratively trained”, “results are assuring”, “agrressive” typo, “movement to criticalities on temporally chaotic datasets”). Abbreviations are introduced without definition (e.g., “acf”, “CCE”, “shift loss”) and many appear in the abstract. References are not placed in parentheses when they should be. Hyphenation is missing in compound words (“outcome oriented”, “down stream”, “scale varying”).
    - Labeling errors: Table 6/1 has labeling issues. The text in figures is generally too small to read.
- **Insufficient explanation of datasets and experiments**: For medical/health data, more context is needed: what do the time-series represent, sequence lengths, patient conditions (e.g., is bilirubin a time-series feature?). The trajectory tracking and heatmap generation processes are not explained. The evaluation section jumps between figures (e.g., starts with Fig. 5, then jumps back to Fig. 3) without a clear narrative.
- **Overall, the paper is not ready for publication** due to these major unaddressed issues in novelty, presentation, and empirical rigor.
