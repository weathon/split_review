## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes DiffEBM, which replaces standard MCMC sampling for training Energy-Based Models (EBMs) with sampling from a jointly trained diffusion model. The diffusion model is learned to align with both the data distribution and the current EBM, and the EBM is then updated by maximizing likelihood on these diffusion-generated samples. Experimental validation is performed on 2D synthetic datasets (e.g., Gaussian mixtures, 2Spirals, Swissroll). Reviewers agree that the idea is reasonable and eliminates MCMC bias, but they raise major concerns about computational cost, limited evaluation, missing baselines, incremental novelty, and unclear advantages over diffusion-only models.

### Strengths
- The method eliminates the need for MCMC sampling in EBM training, avoiding the bias and mode‑collapse issues of short‑run MCMC.
- The joint training framework with a diffusion model is a reasonable and innovative approach to address MCMC limitations (Reviewer 1).
- Provided the diffusion sampler is well‑trained, the maximum likelihood estimator can be unbiased (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited evaluation and scalability**: The experiments are restricted to 2D synthetic datasets (Gaussian mixtures, 2Spirals, Swissroll). Testing on high‑dimensional data (e.g., images) is missing, so scalability remains unproven (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Missing baselines and comparisons**:
  - No comparison with standalone diffusion‑based generative models (e.g., using only the diffusion model as a baseline) or a self‑baseline using samples from the paper’s own diffusion model (Reviewer 1).
  - No comparison with other MCMC‑free EBM training methods: Variational Inference (e.g., [1]), Noise Contrastive Estimation (NCE) [2], or Energy Discrepancy (ED) [3] (Reviewer 3).
- **Significant computational cost**: Each training iteration requires a full sequence of diffusion sampling (Algorithm 1, line 223), making the training computationally intensive. Replacing MCMC with a second generative model that must be repeatedly sampled from does not clearly reduce overall complexity (Reviewer 1).
- **Potential training instability**: The iterative joint training of EBM and diffusion model may create oscillating learning dynamics if the two models diverge, risking convergence failure (Reviewer 1).
- **Redundancy of the EBM**: If the diffusion model alone can accurately capture the data distribution (as evidenced by its sample quality), the additional EBM training may be redundant. The paper does not clarify the unique advantages of having an EBM when a high‑capacity diffusion generator already exists (Reviewer 1).
- **Incremental novelty**: The diffusion sampling method used is directly taken from recent work by Richter & Berner (2024). Extending it from sampling to learning EBMs is a straightforward, incremental step without addressing a major new challenge in applications (Reviewers 2, 3). (Note: Reviewer 1 considered the approach innovative, but the majority view is that the contribution is limited.)
- **Insufficient explanation of core equations**: Equations (8)–(11) are borrowed from previous literature but are not explained in the authors’ own words, and the text does not clarify why the proposed diffusion sampling should be better than MCMC (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing ablations**:
  - The effect of omitting the DSM loss in sampler training should be shown, as the sampler may be biased when the EBM is underfitting early in training (Reviewer 3).
  - The effect of omitting the sampler‑matching loss during EBM training should also be reported (Reviewer 3).
- **Clarifications needed**:
  - The experimental section refers to “Denoising Score Matching” – what is its relationship with the “sliced score matching” mentioned in Figure 3? (Reviewer 1)
  - In Figure 3, why are the ground‑truth samples different for the two compared methodologies? (Reviewer 1)
