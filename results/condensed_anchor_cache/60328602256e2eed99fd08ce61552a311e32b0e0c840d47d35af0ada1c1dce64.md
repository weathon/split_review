## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Steady and Fair Robustness Evaluation (SF Robustness), a metric based on the standard deviation of Shapley values of neurons, aiming to provide a more stable and attack-independent assessment of adversarial robustness. It also introduces a training strategy that minimizes Shapley SD to improve robustness. Reviewers’ opinions are split: two reviewers (scores 5,5) find the idea novel and the experimental scope broad, yet they identify significant gaps in theoretical justification, evaluation rigor, and clarity. Two other reviewers (scores 3,1) are much more critical, questioning the motivation (whether an attack-independent metric is needed), the flawed use of accuracy drop, the lack of quantitative correlation evidence, and the small practical improvements. The most negative reviewer (rating 1, confidence 4) considers the metric unsuitable, the motivation unclear, and several technical statements incorrect.

### Strengths
- Proposes a robustness evaluation framework based on the standard deviation of Shapley values, offering a new perspective that is potentially independent of specific attack methods.
- Establishes an explicit theoretical connection between Shapley values and adversarial robustness.
- Conducts experiments across multiple datasets (e.g., CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100), model architectures (e.g., ResNet, VGG), and adversarial training methods, demonstrating generalizability.
- Integrates the Shapley-value-based metric with existing adversarial training to enhance model robustness.

### Weaknesses
1. **Definition and derivation of Shapley value are unclear.** The paper presents approximations and specific equations (e.g., Eq 10) without first giving a precise definition of the Shapley value. The derivation from the standard definition to Eq 4 and the Taylor approximation is not clearly explained, especially for readers unfamiliar with game theory. The notation in Section 2 (a<sup>l</sup>, w<sup>l</sup>, b<sup>l</sup>) and the discussion of layer‑wise changes are misleading because only the penultimate layer is used.

2. **Lack of rigorous theoretical connection between Shapley SD and robustness.** The paper does not provide a proof or systematic analysis of why the standard deviation of Shapley values necessarily reflects overall model robustness. Neither empirical/theoretical conditions nor extreme counterexamples (models with low Shapley SD but poor robustness, or vice versa) are discussed.

3. **Correlation evidence is weak.** The claimed correlation between SF robustness and adversarial robustness in Figures 3–4 is not visually obvious and shows many outliers. The paper does not provide quantitative correlation metrics (e.g., Pearson correlation coefficient) or statistical significance tests. The positive correlation between robustness and individual attack accuracy also undermines the unique contribution of SF robustness.

4. **Limited attack evaluation.** The evaluation only uses four white‑box attacks: FGSM, PGD, CW, and AutoAttack. It does not consider other common attacks like DeepFool or black‑box attacks, limiting the evidence for the method’s generality. It is also unclear whether the global Shapley value approximation differs for different attack types.

5. **Improvements from SF‑loss training are small and not statistically validated.** The robust accuracy gains reported in Table 2 are small and may not be significant. The experiments (Table 2, Figure 5) should be run with multiple random seeds to show variance and significance.

6. **Inadequate comparison with existing evaluation methods.** The paper lacks a comprehensive comparison with conventional robustness metrics (e.g., PGD accuracy, CW accuracy, AutoAttack accuracy, or their average). It does not quantify the stability and fairness of SF robustness relative to these methods.

7. **Accuracy drop metric is unsuitable.** The accuracy drop used to measure robustness is flawed. For example, Model A (90% natural, 40% robust) has a smaller drop than Model B (95% natural, 40% robust) even though Model B is clearly preferable. An extreme case of random initialization (1% natural, 1% robust) yields 0% accuracy drop, misleadingly indicating robustness.

8. **Technical inaccuracies.**  
   - The statement “Adversarial training aims to reduce Equation 2 during training time” (line 112) is incorrect; adversarial training minimizes cross‑entropy or KL divergence of predictions, not the activation differences in Eq 2.  
   - The explanation of Eq 10 (minimizing S) is misleading; minimizing S could drive its value to negative infinity; the authors likely intend to drive S toward zero.  
   - The claim that results match the correlation between Shapley value and activation difference (line 206) does not address the paper’s motivation to find a metric independent of attack method.

9. **Missing analysis and details.**  
   - No discussion of cases where the evaluation method fails.  
   - No verification for special architectures (VGG, DenseNet, attention mechanisms).  
   - How to determine appropriate SD thresholds for judging robustness.  
   - Whether the method reflects performance under unknown attacks.  
   - Whether simply masking neurons with high Shapley values in a standard‑trained model leads to better robust accuracy.  
   - Details of Figure 1 (which dataset) and whether data points in accuracy drop vs. SF robustness figures correspond to specific works.  
   - No upper bound on the Shapley value approximation error and whether it aggregates in deeper networks.

10. **Adaptive attacker consideration.** It is unclear how adversarial examples are constructed when evaluating models trained with SF loss, and whether the attacker is aware of and adaptive to the additional SF loss.
