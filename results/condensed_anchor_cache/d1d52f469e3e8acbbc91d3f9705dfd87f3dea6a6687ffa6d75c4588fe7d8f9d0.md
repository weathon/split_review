## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes InCPL (InCP), a test-time prompt tuning method for frozen vision-language models like CLIP. It uses labeled “in-context” examples and an unlabeled test sample to jointly optimize visual and textual prompts via a cyclic learning strategy with a language-to-vision translator (TokenNet). The goal is one-step unsupervised adaptation at test time. Experiments are conducted on fine-grained classification datasets and distribution shift settings, reporting state-of-the-art results.

### Strengths
- The task of test-time prompt tuning is worth investigating, and the idea of in-context prompt learning for vision-language models is novel (R3, R4). Combining NLP and computer vision insights is interesting (R1).
- The connection between textual and visual prompts is well-motivated, and experiment results support the idea (R2).
- The ablation studies are well-designed and comprehensive (R2).
- The methodology is well-structured and the paper is exceptionally clear, conveying complex concepts effectively (R1).
- The proposed method shows consistent improvement on fine-grained classification datasets and competitive performance on distribution shifting datasets (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Terminology and conceptual framing**: 
  - The term “in-context learning” is inappropriate because the method optimizes parameters (prompts) using labeled examples, whereas in-context learning in language and vision literature does not involve parameter updates. The method is more akin to few-shot learning or semi-supervised learning (R2, R3, R4).
  - The use of labeled context samples to tune the network is different from traditional in-context learning where samples are part of the input prompt. The authors confirmed after rebuttal that the model benefits from avoiding predicting categories of context samples, which makes the evaluation unfair (R3).
  - The relationship between the context samples and the test sample is unclear; the “context” is not well defined (R4).

- **Fairness of experimental comparisons and information leakage**:
  - InCP uses labeled “in-context” examples from the target dataset during test-time adaptation, but baselines like CoOp and CoCoOp are trained on ImageNet. A fair comparison would use the same few-shot examples as InCP (R2).
  - The context samples are randomly sampled for each test sample, potentially causing information leakage of the test set. The fact that a fixed subset underperforms random sampling (Appendix A.2) suggests the gain may come from seeing a wider range of test samples (R3). A stricter implementation should use the same subset of labeled data for all test samples (R3).
  - The method is dataset-specific (context samples come from the same dataset), which makes it a domain-specific few-shot approach rather than a true test-time adaptation method. If context is dataset-specific, this is not TTA (R4). TPT+CoOp has much better performance (R4).

- **Inference efficiency**:
  - Efficiency is crucial for test-time adaptation. The paper must explicitly report and compare inference time metrics for InCP against methods like TPT (R1, R4).

- **Experimental omissions and marginal gains**:
  - Missing results on SUN397 (Table 1) and ImageNet (Table 2) – these datasets are used in TPT and comparisons are needed for clarity (R1).
  - The performance improvement in Table 2 appears marginal; further explanation or additional results are needed to demonstrate significance (R1).
  - The ablation studies (Tables 4 and 6) are only on three datasets, not on all datasets from Table 2. Why these three? They are not representative (R3).

- **Method clarity and missing details**:
  - Implementation details in Appendix A.1.2 are incomplete. Many unclear points about the method and experiment setup (R2).
  - In Figure 4, both P_v and P_t and TokenNet seem trainable, but the learning objective in Eq. (2) only involves P_v (R2).
  - Cyclic prompt learning involves multi-step optimization, contradicting the claim of single-step optimization (Section 3.4 vs. earlier text) (R2).
  - During testing, when a new test sample arrives, are prompts and TokenNet reset to their initial state or are updates accumulated? (R2)
  - How is TokenNet trained? If randomly initialized and only optimized for one step at test time, it cannot effectively “translate” text tokens to visual prompts (R2, R3).
  - How are P_v and P_t initialized? How many tokens are used? How big is TokenNet? How many in-context examples are used? (R2)
  - The “generic-language prompt” baseline in Table 6 is not found (R2).
  - The figures (Figures 2, 3, 1c) are confusing: token space is unclear, colors are not explained, dimensions do not match (e.g., P_t dimension of 3 vs. 4 prefix tokens) (R3).

- **Limited novelty and discussion**:
  - The main difference from fully test-time adaptation is the use of additional labeled data; the novelty is limited to semi-supervised fine-tuning (R3).
  - The pros and cons of the proposed method are not discussed thoroughly (R4).
