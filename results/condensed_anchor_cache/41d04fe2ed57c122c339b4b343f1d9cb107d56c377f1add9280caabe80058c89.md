## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces MineCraft Universe (MCU), a fully automated benchmarking framework for evaluating generalist agents within Minecraft. MCU includes a task generation mechanism using LLMs, over 3,000 composable atomic tasks, and a VLM-based evaluation system that assesses performance across six dimensions (task progress, action control, material usage, task efficiency, error recognition, creative attempts). The automated evaluation achieves over 90% agreement with human ratings. Experiments with VPT, STEVE-1, and GROOT show that existing agents struggle with task diversity and difficulty. Scores range from 1 to 6, with reviewers highly polarized: one reviewer (score 1, confidence 5) finds the paper severely flawed; two (scores 6, confidence 4) see significant contributions but also major gaps; one (score 3, confidence 1) is concerned but open to improvement. All reviewers note that code was not provided.

### Strengths

- Creative use of LLMs and VLMs for both task generation and evaluation (R1, R2, R3, R4). The VLM evaluator is a significant contribution, as earlier benchmarks required expensive human evaluation (e.g., MineRL BASALT) or were limited to programmatic rewards (R4).
- The automated evaluation pipeline shows good concordance with human assessments across multiple dimensions, lending credibility to scalability (R1, R2, R4). 
- The framework provides over 3,000 atomic tasks with varying difficulty and compositional complexity, enabling intra- and inter-task generalization (R2). The task generation and verification pipeline is a non-trivial engineering effort (R3).
- The benchmark improves over MineDojo by introducing open-ended tasks, real-game simulation, and difficulty levels (R3). The instruction set is more carefully curated than MineDojo’s (R4).
- Evaluations of existing Minecraft agents (VPT, STEVE-1, GROOT) are performed (R3, R4).
- Minecraft is an excellent domain for testing generalist agents, and this area is of increasing importance (R1, R2, R4). The environment’s open-ended nature tests adaptability in realistic dynamic scenarios (R2).

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of code:** The paper does not include anonymized code, which is highly unusual for a D&B paper and severely hinders reproducibility and thorough evaluation (R1). This also makes it unclear how easy the benchmark is to use and how much time/resources a full evaluation requires (R3).
- **Overclaims and imprecise terminology:** The paper overclaims by calling agents “generalist” when the evaluation relies solely on Minecraft. A more precise description would be “open-ended embodied agents” (R3; citing Hughes et al., 2024). The claim that evaluation offers “outstanding performance and profound insights” (lines 359-361) is unsupported (R1). The title should reflect that “Open World” is a simulated game world (R3).
- **Incorrect or unclear claims about prior work:** The assertion that BEDD does not include comprehensive capability evaluation mischaracterizes that work (R1). The claim that long-horizon tasks reduce composability is not well explained, as long-horizon tasks are often compositions of simpler tasks (R1). The paper states that some prior work fine-tunes foundation models for Minecraft but does not explain why those are not studied or considered insufficiently general (R1).
- **Unclear writing and missing details:** Multiple passages are ambiguous. For example, “We hire 20 people [...] annotate 1 hour of data” – unclear if per person or total (R1). “These trajectories exhibit highly similar poses for many steps, thereby increasing the evaluative complexity” is not explained (R1). Many citations (e.g., line 49), section references (e.g., line 148), and formatting (LaTeX marks) are incorrect (R3). 
- **Results presentation lacking:** Figures lack error bars, making statistical significance unclear (R1). Figure 4 is unclear: it is not specified whether it aggregates all evaluations or specific examples, and large gaps in some evaluations raise questions about the reported Kendall’s tau (R1). Sorting the x-axis by increasing human scores would help interpretability (R4).
- **Evaluation methodology contradictions:** The paper mentions evaluation options like “a is better,” “b is better,” “tie,” “both are bad” in one section, and scoring intervals “very poor, poor, fair, good, excellent” in another. It is unclear which method is used or if both are employed (R1). 
- **Unclear benefit of LLM use:** The paper states LLMs autonomously generate tailored criteria based on six high-level criteria, but does not explain why this is desirable or whether criteria are consistent across trials. It is unclear if there is a fixed set of factors for each high-level criterion (R1).
- **Missing key experimental details:**
  - How the 3,000+ atomic tasks were collected and what quality control was performed (R4). A comparison to MineDojo tasks (clustering, filtering, diversity) is needed to justify claim (2) that MCU is better (R4).
  - How the Mineflayer verification pipeline works: given a natural language atomic task and an LLM-generated initial state, how is a Mineflayer bot created to verify solvability? (R4). What fraction of LLM-generated scenes are rejected by Mineflayer, and what is the false positive rate? (R4).
  - How VPT was evaluated: VPT does not accept natural language instructions, so it must be placed in the environment and checked if it happens to solve the task—this should be explicitly stated (R4). The paper should specify which VPT model is used (earlygame_keyword, contractor_house, or both) (R4).
  - Human rating system description is insufficient; need more detail in main text and appendix (R3).
  - Prompts for LLM scene generation and VLM evaluation are not provided, even in appendix (R4).
- **Insufficient validation and justification for key claims:**
  - Claim (2) that MCU provides a better training/evaluation set than MineDojo lacks evidence. The paper should cluster tasks from both benchmarks and qualitatively compare, or show that filtering MineDojo yields many fewer high-quality tasks than MCU (R4).
  - Claim (3) that diverse initial state distribution is important is unsubstantiated. The authors should run evaluations and finetuning with single vs. multiple initial states to show qualitatively different conclusions (R4).
  - Evaluation of the verification pipeline (e.g., false positives from Mineflayer) is missing (R4).
- **Limited agent selection:** The majority of tested models (VPT, STEVE-1) are early-stage developments. The framework’s effectiveness with more recent, advanced models (e.g., Gemini, GPT-based agents) is unclear. API-based agents cannot currently be used without code and explanation (R2, R3). The selection seems limited to publicly available implementations (R2).
- **Minecraft-specific dynamics may limit generalizability:** Results may be biased toward models fine-tuned for Minecraft’s environment. The block-based world and discrete action space may not translate to continuous control or different physics (R2).
- **Insufficient analysis of evaluation metrics:** The paper lacks a detailed breakdown of how each metric (e.g., error recognition, creative attempts) impacts generalization across task types, and how metrics correlate with each other (R2). 
- **Missing references:** Missing citation at line 49 (R2, R3). Need citation for Mineflayer (line 267) (R4). The Guss et al. citation (line 507) is for Diamond, but the correct citation for BASALT is Shah et al. 2021 (R4). Missing reference to CRAB: Cross-environment Agent Benchmark for Multimodal Language Model Agents (R2). Missing reference to Hughes et al. 2024 on open-endedness (R3).
- **Evaluation robustness not tested:** The paper should try different LLM judges to see if there are qualitative differences in evaluation (R3). 
- **Specific numerical issues:** Table 4 does not report numbers for VPT(rl) – why? (R4). Malformed figure reference in line 1182 (R4).
- **Questions with critical implications:** 
  - How are difficulty settings systematically implemented? (R1). 
  - Why is task definition tool-independent? In Minecraft, mining dirt with a diamond shovel is more efficient and requires more tech tree traversal – so tool-independence seems problematic (R1). 
  - Where do the human gameplay videos come from? (R1). 
  - How is the finding in 4.2.2 different from BASALT’s finding that creative tasks remain difficult? (R1). 
  - What is the interrater agreement for human evaluations? (R4) – important for judging how close the VLM is to human performance (R4).
  - Can MCU be adapted to other open-world environments or is it largely Minecraft-specific? (R2).
  - How well does MCU handle highly complex, multi-step tasks requiring advanced planning? (R2). 
  - What criteria were used for selecting the 3000 atomic tasks, and how representative are they of real-world challenges? (R2).
