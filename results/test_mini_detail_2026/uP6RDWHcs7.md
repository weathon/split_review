## Summary
2-3 sentence summary of the paper's contribution.

The paper introduces Marginal Flow, a density estimation framework where the density is defined as a mixture of simple parametric distributions (e.g., Gaussians) whose component parameters are sampled from a learned distribution \(q_\theta(\mathbf{w})\), itself parameterized by feeding noise through an unconstrained neural network. The method claims efficient exact density evaluation and sampling without architectural constraints like bijectivity, and demonstrates orders-of-magnitude speed improvements over normalizing flows, flow matching, and free-form flows across synthetic and real-world tasks.

## Strengths

- **Novel and conceptually clean framework**: The core idea — defining the model density as \((1/N_c)\sum_i q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w}_i)\) with \(\mathbf{w}_i\) resampled from a learned distribution generated by an unconstrained neural network — is simple, original, and sidesteps the bijectivity constraints, Jacobian computations, and ODE solving required by normalizing flows and flow matching. This is concretely expressed in Eqs. (2–3) and the model diagram (Figure 2).

- **Empirical speed advantages are substantial and well-documented**: Figure 3 shows Marginal Flow is orders of magnitude faster than NF, FM, and FFF for both sampling and density evaluation across dimensions \(10^2\)–\(10^5\), and avoids OOM errors where NF/FM fail. Figure 7 shows Marginal Flow converging to high test log-likelihood in a fraction of the wall-clock time of baselines on 5 synthetic datasets. These speedups are a genuine practical contribution.

- **Flexibility demonstrated across diverse problem settings**: The framework accommodates (i) forward and reverse KL training (Figures 6–8), (ii) lower-dimensional base distributions (Figure 4), (iii) conditional modeling via the SBI benchmark, (iv) Wishart mixtures for positive-definite matrices (Figure 9), and (v) conditional manifold learning in VAE latent spaces (Figures 10–11). The Wishart extension (Section 4.3) is a particularly clean demonstration of the framework's modularity — simply swapping \(q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w})\) from Gaussian to Wishart enables a new domain without changing the algorithm.

- **Reverse KL training works out-of-the-box**: Figure 8 shows Marginal Flow trained via reverse KL (no observations, only unnormalized target density) achieves lower test KL than Normalizing Flow on multiple distributions. This is notable because most density models cannot be trained this way, and it leverages MF's symmetric efficiency at both evaluation and sampling.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None. The paper's core method is valid and the experiments support its practical utility, even though some claims need reframing.

### Major

- **"Exact density evaluation" is misleading and conflates two different notions of exactness.** The paper defines \(q_\theta(\mathbf{x}) := \frac{1}{N_c}\sum_i q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w}_{\theta,i})\) with \(\mathbf{w}_{\theta,i} \sim q_\theta(\mathbf{w})\) resampled at each evaluation (Eq. 2). This expression can indeed be *computed exactly* for a given set of \(\mathbf{w}_i\) — there is no bound or approximation in the computation itself. However, the *value* of \(q_\theta(\mathbf{x})\) is a random variable that changes each time the \(\mathbf{w}_i\) are resampled. By contrast, Normalizing Flow (NF) provides a *single deterministic* density function. In Table 1, both MF and NF receive a ✓ for "Efficient exact likelihood" as if they offer the same guarantee, which is misleading. The runtime comparison in Figure 3 ("exact density evaluation") measures time to compute the mixture once, but does not account for the variance of the estimate or the fact that \(N_c\) may need to increase to match the precision of a deterministic density. This does not invalidate the method — it is a legitimate stochastic model — but the claim of "exactness" in the same sense as NF is inaccurate and the comparison is not apples-to-apples as presented. The paper should explicitly characterize the Monte Carlo nature of the density, discuss the bias-variance tradeoff controlled by \(N_c\), and either qualify the ✓ in Table 1 or compare against other approximate-likelihood methods (VAE-IW, diffusion likelihood approximations) as well.

- **The value of \(N_c\) — the number of mixture components used in every experiment — is never stated in the paper.** The paper defines \(N_c\) in Eq. (2) and discusses its conceptual role, but the experimental sections never report what value was used for any dataset or figure. This is a critical missing detail: the runtime vs. quality tradeoff depends directly on \(N_c\), and without it the results cannot be reproduced or properly interpreted. The paper should report \(N_c\) for each experiment, ideally with an ablation showing how runtime and log-likelihood vary with \(N_c\).

- **The "manifold learning" claim is imprecisely stated.** The paper repeatedly states that choosing \(m < d\) for the base distribution \(p_{\text{base}}(\mathbf{z})\) allows "learning a density with support on a lower-dimensional manifold" (line 66) and "learning the correct manifold" (Figure 4 caption). However, because \(q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w})\) is a Gaussian with full support on \(\mathbb{R}^d\), the overall density \(q_\theta(\mathbf{x})\) has positive density everywhere — it does not have support confined to a submanifold. What the model actually learns is a *concentration* of density near a low-dimensional set of means (i.e., the learned means lie on a low-dimensional manifold, but the density itself does not). This is a meaningful difference from proper manifold density estimation (e.g., Brehmer & Cranmer 2020) where the density has zero support off the manifold. The qualitative results (Figures 10–11) are interesting, but the terminology should be corrected throughout (e.g., "learned low-dimensional mean manifold" or "concentration on a low-dimensional structure").

### Minor

- **Comparison fairness with baselines is not fully established.** The paper states baselines received "a comparable amount of parameters" (Section 4.1), but parameter count alone does not ensure comparable capacity across different model classes (NF requires bijective layers with Jacobian determinants; MF uses a small unconstrained MLP). The failure of Free-form Flow to capture multi-modal targets in Figure 5 (collapsing to a single blob) and the lack of any NF variant in the multi-modal comparison raise the question of whether baselines were adequately tuned. An ablation varying baseline capacity would strengthen the comparison.

- **SBI results are relegated entirely to the appendix.** The main paper claims "state-of-the-art results" for simulation-based inference (Section 4.2) but provides no quantitative summary — no table, no plot, not even a sentence reporting the C2ST scores. Readers cannot evaluate this claim without accessing the appendix. At minimum, a summary table should be in the main text.

- **No uncertainty quantification on density estimates.** Since \(q_\theta(\mathbf{x})\) is stochastic, the log-likelihood values reported in Figures 6–7 are themselves random variables depending on the sampled \(\mathbf{w}_i\). No error bars, confidence intervals, or variance estimates are provided for these quantities. This is particularly relevant for the reverse KL results (Figure 8), where 95% CI bars are shown (good) but it is unclear whether these capture the Monte Carlo variance of the density estimate or just training seed variance.

### Trivial

- Figure 1 (motivation) compares a fixed GMM with Marginal Flow, showing MF produces a smooth density. This is helpful but the GMM's failure is expected — a fixed GMM is not learning a density. The comparison conflates two differences (marginalization vs. optimization AND resampling vs. fixed components). The text (line 68) correctly notes this, but the caption could be clearer.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation on \(N_c\) showing the tradeoff between runtime, variance of density estimates, and log-likelihood quality would significantly strengthen the paper and clarify the practical range of operation.
- A comparison with approximate-likelihood methods (e.g., VAE with importance weighting, diffusion with likelihood approximation via ODE) would make the efficiency comparison more comprehensive and fairer.
- Quantitative metrics (e.g., FID, manifold fidelity scores) for the image manifold experiments (MNIST, JAFFE) would move these beyond qualitative demonstrations.
- A brief discussion connecting the model to kernel density estimation / Parzen windows would help readers situate the contribution within the broader literature.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely insightful meta-level observation from synthesizing the reviews is that the paper's central tension mirrors a recurring pattern in generative modeling: models that circumvent architectural constraints (bijectivity, invertibility, Jacobians) often do so by introducing stochasticity into their density evaluation, and the community has not converged on clear terminology for what counts as "exact" density computation. Marginal Flow's contribution — a learned mixture with resampled parameters — occupies a genuinely interesting point in the design space between deterministic exact-likelihood models (NF) and approximate-likelihood models (VAEs, diffusion), but the paper's framing obscures rather than highlights this position. A more honest framing as a "stochastic density model with efficient exact computation of mixture densities" would be both more accurate and more interesting to the field.
