## Summary

Marginal Flow proposes a density estimation framework where the target density is defined as a mixture of simple parametric distributions (e.g., Gaussians), with component parameters sampled from a learnable distribution rather than optimized directly. The key idea is to resample these parameters at each step from a distribution generated by an unconstrained neural network, which produces smooth densities that avoid the "spiky" behavior of finite mixture models. The method simultaneously provides efficient density evaluation (via a closed-form mixture of simple densities) and efficient single-step sampling, a combination not jointly available in existing approaches like Normalizing Flows, Flow Matching, GANs, or VAEs. Experiments on synthetic data, simulation-based inference, Wishart mixtures on positive-definite matrices, and image latent spaces demonstrate the framework's flexibility and computational efficiency.

## Strengths

- **Simultaneous efficient exact-likelihood evaluation and single-step sampling (Table 1, Figure 3).** Marginal Flow is the only method among GANs, VAEs, Energy-Based models, Flow Matching, Normalizing Flows, and Free-form Flows that checks both "Efficient exact likelihood" and "Efficient (single-step) sampling" in Table 1. Figure 3 validates this with runtime measurements up to d=10⁵ dimensions, showing orders-of-magnitude speedups over NF, FM, and FFF without out-of-memory errors that break NF and FM at high dimensions. This directly supports the paper's central claim of overcoming the efficiency trade-off that plagues prior density estimators.

- **Flexibility for lower-dimensional manifolds (Table 1, Figure 4).** The framework can learn densities on unknown lower-dimensional manifolds by simply choosing a lower-dimensional base distribution for the component parameters. Figure 4 provides a concrete demonstration: on a spiral dataset on a 1D manifold, Marginal Flow recovers both the density and correct manifold, whereas Normalizing Flow and Flow Matching cannot handle the dimensionality change. This capability is absent from most exact-density methods.

- **Effective handling of multi-modal targets (Figure 5).** On a five-cluster multi-modal target with only 150 training points, Marginal Flow accurately captures all modes while Flow Matching and Normalizing Flow produce blurred densities and Free-form Flow collapses to one mode. The paper correctly notes that this is not simply a mixture model — the components are resampled, so the model cannot "memorize" individual modes.

- **Adaptability to non-Gaussian parametric families (Sections 4.3, Figure 8–9).** The framework accommodates different choices of $q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w})$ beyond Gaussians, demonstrated with Wishart distributions for positive-definite matrices (Section 4.3) — a setting where Normalizing Flows become computationally prohibitive at $100 \times 100$ scale. The paper also validates training with both forward KL (log-likelihood) and reverse KL, which is made feasible by the method's efficiency at both sampling and density evaluation.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Missing comparison against neural mixture models and MDNs.** The paper positions Marginal Flow as a novel density estimation framework, but the core idea — generating component parameters via a neural network and forming a mixture — has substantial precedent in neural mixture models, Mixture Density Networks (MDNs), and implicit generative models with kernel density estimates. None of these baselines are cited or compared against in Table 1 or the experiments. Without such a comparison, it is difficult to isolate the specific benefit of the resampling/marginalization mechanism versus simply having a flexible learned mixture with a large or infinite number of components. The paper should clearly differentiate its approach or acknowledge the relationship.

- **No discussion of Monte Carlo variance in the density estimator.** The model density $q_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{N_c} \sum_i q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w}_i)$ depends on the random draw of $\mathbf{w}_i$'s. Evaluating the same $\mathbf{x}$ with a different random seed yields different density values. This is a practical concern for downstream applications requiring stable log-likelihoods, and the paper never mentions it. The term "exact density evaluation" is used throughout (abstract, introduction, conclusions) without qualification, even though the evaluation has Monte Carlo noise that diminishes only as $N_c \to \infty$. While the model definition in Eq. 2 is exact *for a given draw of $\mathbf{w}_i$'s*, the paper should acknowledge this source of variance and discuss the $N_c$-accuracy trade-off — especially since $N_c$ is a hyperparameter that directly affects both quality and runtime.

### Minor

- **Overclaiming "perfect" reconstruction.** Section 4.1 states Marginal Flow can "perfectly learn all densities" (Figure 6), and the conclusions repeat "perfectly reconstruct synthetic datasets." Visual inspection of Figure 6's OCR description ("diffuse, blurred versions") suggests approximation errors that contradict "perfect." The Checkerboard and Swiss Roll densities in particular appear to have visible artifacts. More measured language ("accurately learn" or "faithfully approximate") would be more appropriate and avoid inflating expectations.

- **No table of final test log-likelihoods — emphasis on speed over accuracy.** Figure 7 plots test log-likelihood vs. wall-clock time and emphasizes convergence speed ("orders of magnitude quicker"). However, the paper does not report final log-likelihood values in a table or directly discuss whether Marginal Flow matches or trails competitors at convergence. The reader cannot determine from the main paper whether the method's asymptotic density quality is competitive. While the figure caption says "In most cases, Ours achieves a higher log-likelihood faster," the absence of explicit numerical reporting makes it difficult to evaluate the accuracy-speed trade-off transparently.

- **Image latent-space results are purely qualitative (Section 4.4).** The MNIST and JAFFE demonstrations show sensible interpolations along the learned manifold, but no quantitative metric (e.g., FID, reconstruction quality, or comparison to a standard interpolation baseline) is provided. The results are anecdotal and would benefit from any quantitative validation, especially since the two-stage procedure (VAE + Marginal Flow) introduces compounding errors that are not discussed.

- **$N_c$ values not reported in the main text for runtime benchmarks.** The runtime comparison in Figure 3 is impressive, but the paper does not state the $N_c$ used for Marginal Flow in these benchmarks. If $N_c$ is small, the runtime advantage partly comes from using a small ensemble. The appendix reference (Section A.3.1) may contain this detail, but the main text should at minimum mention the $N_c$ value or discuss how it affects the speed-accuracy trade-off.

### Trivial

- The "SBI results are state-of-the-art" claim (Section 4.2) is placed in the appendix and cannot be evaluated from the main paper alone. This is a consequence of space constraints rather than a flaw, but it weakens the in-paper evidence for that claim.
