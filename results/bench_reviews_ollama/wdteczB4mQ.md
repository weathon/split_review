## Summary
This paper proposes the first learning-based approach to Gröbner basis (GB) computation using Transformers. The authors identify two new algebraic problems—random GB generation and backward transformation from a GB to a non-GB set—and solve them for 0-dimensional radical ideals in shape position via a Bruhat-style construction F = U₁PU₂G (Thm. 4.3, Prop. 4.2). They also propose a hybrid (discrete + continuous) coefficient embedding and show experimentally that a vanilla Transformer can learn GB computation within this class.

## Strengths
- The formalization of the random-GB and backward-GB problems (Problems 1 & 2 in Sec. 4) is genuinely novel; these were absent from the computational-algebra literature because forward computation had been the sole focus.
- The shape-position-based sampler (Sec. 4.2) reduces random GB generation to sampling n univariate polynomials with a single degree condition — a clean algorithmic insight.
- Theorem 4.3 gives a clean necessary-and-sufficient characterization (via syzygies) for AG to generate the same ideal as G; this goes beyond what is strictly needed for the experimental pipeline.
- Backward generation is dramatically faster than naive forward generation against std/slimgb (e.g., 1411 s → 7.91 s at n=5, Table 1), making million-sample training tractable.
- The empirical separation between support accuracy and full coefficient accuracy (Table 2), and the field-dependent performance gap (𝔽_p vs. ℚ/ℝ), provide concrete diagnostic insights into what is and isn't being learned.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **Headline ML claim is broader than the evidence.** The training and test distributions are both produced by the same generator F = U₁PU₂G with U₁, U₂ unimodular upper-triangular and P a permutation (Sec. 4.3, Sec. 6.2). What Table 2 measures is the model's ability to invert this specific structured perturbation of a shape-position GB — not "GB computation" in any general sense. The paper does state in Sec. 4.1 that "we should focus on a particular class of ideals and pursue in-distribution accuracy," but the abstract, introduction, and conclusion ("first learning approach to … GB computation") still frame the result more broadly than the experiments warrant. No out-of-distribution evaluation (different A-construction, or A satisfying only Thm. 4.3(3) but not (2), or forward-sampled F) is reported.
- **No measurement of how far the generated F's are from being GBs.** Because A = U₁PU₂ is unimodular over k (Prop. 4.4) and the density σ of U₁, U₂' decreases sharply with n (σ = 1.0, 0.6, 0.3, 0.2 for n = 2..5), it is plausible that a substantial fraction of generated F's are only mildly perturbed re-mixings of G. The paper provides no statistic (e.g., number of Buchberger reductions to reach G, distance between ⟨LT(F)⟩ and ⟨LT(I)⟩, fraction with ⟨LT(F)⟩ = ⟨LT(I)⟩) that would let a reader gauge task difficulty. Combined with the previous point, this makes it hard to know what Table 2's accuracies actually demonstrate.
- **Hybrid embedding is dominated by the discrete baseline it is meant to improve.** In nearly every row of Table 2, discrete outperforms hybrid, often catastrophically (ℚ, n=5: 86.5 vs. 0.0; 𝔽₃₁, n=4: 51.1 vs. 0.1). The motivating ℚ example with coefficients like 569520/427411 (Sec. 5) is real, but the paper does not show the hybrid embedding solving that problem — it shows the opposite. Presenting it as a third contribution is not supported by the experiments. Sec. 6.2's hand-waved attribution to "autoregressive error accumulation" lacks a direct diagnostic (e.g., coefficient MSE vs. output position).

### Minor
- **"A few orders of magnitude" overstates the speedup against the right baseline.** Against stdfglm (the apt forward baseline once FGLM is available), backward generation is 7.22→5.23, 8.29→5.46, 21.0→7.05, 164.3→7.91 (Table 1). Only at n=5 does this approach ~20×; for n=2, 3 it is essentially the same order of magnitude. The comparison is also against forward computation on F's generated by the backward process, which may not be where F4/F5 are most stressed; the paper itself notes this in Sec. 4.3 but moves past it.
- **Shape-position is the easiest GB regime.** The GB is forced to {h(x_n), x_1 − g_1(x_n), …, x_{n−1} − g_{n−1}(x_n)} (Def. 4.2), so "predicting the GB" reduces to predicting n univariate polynomials in x_n. This is explicit in the paper but should be flagged more clearly when discussing learnability claims.
- **Thm. 4.3(3) is stated but not used.** The if-and-only-if condition is the natural lever for producing a more diverse class of non-GB inputs that go beyond left-invertible A; not using it forecloses the most obvious experiment for testing OOD generalization.
- **"Useful hint via Traverso" claim is untested.** Sec. 6.2 argues that even partial predictions are useful as input to a leading-term-aware solver (Traverso 1997), but the paper never measures how often predicted support is correct and how much downstream speedup that delivers vs. running F4/F5 on F directly.
- **No non-Transformer baseline.** A heuristic that guesses the shape and performs one round of polynomial division would provide a useful floor for interpreting Table 2.

### Trivial
- The Sec. 1 bullet claiming Transformer training is "a compromise to NP-hard problems" is broader than what experiments (n ≤ 5, d ≤ 5, sparse A) speak to.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Stratify Table 2 by σ to see whether accuracy collapses as F drifts further from G.
- Add a side-by-side "easy F vs. hard F" case study (more informative than the embedding heatmaps in Fig. 1).
- Reframe the ML contribution as "learning to invert Bruhat-structured perturbations of shape-position 0-dim GBs" — a defensible and useful first result — and use that as the launching point for harder distributions.
- Test whether predicted leading-term sets actually accelerate downstream Traverso/F4 computation end-to-end.

## Removed Points
*These points are flagged to be removed; treat them with caution.*

- *(From the strength finder)* "Provides an efficient and theoretically grounded solution to the dataset generation bottleneck" — partially correct but overlaps with the major weakness that the bottleneck is only resolved within a narrow distributional regime; subsumed by the more specific strengths kept above.
- *(From the strength finder)* "Transformers achieve up to 93.7% accuracy … directly supporting the claim that GB computation is learnable" — kept as evidence but stripped of the overclaim; the same number is reinterpreted as in-distribution accuracy on a structured-perturbation task, not as evidence of GB-learnability in general.
- *(From the strength finder)* The hybrid embedding strength — removed because it conflicts with a verified weakness (the embedding is dominated by the discrete baseline in nearly every Table 2 row).

## Novel Insights
The genuinely novel observation in this work is that random GB generation and the backward GB problem are well-posed algebraic questions in their own right, and that for 0-dimensional ideals in shape position they admit a clean Bruhat-decomposition-based solution (Prop. 4.2, Thm. 4.3). Beyond that and the paper's own contributions, no further novel insight emerges.

## Suggestions
- Tighten the headline claim from "learning GB computation" to "learning to invert Bruhat-structured perturbations of shape-position 0-dim GBs," and reserve broader claims for after OOD evidence is produced.
- Report Buchberger-reduction count (or an analogous difficulty metric) per generated F, and stratify Table 2 by it.
- Run at least one OOD evaluation: F generated by a different mechanism (e.g., a non-left-invertible A from Thm. 4.3(3), or random F with its forward-computed G).
- Either drop hybrid embedding from the contributions list or diagnose its failure (coefficient MSE vs. output position; effect of regression-by-classification per Shah 2023) and revisit.
- Replace "a few orders of magnitude" with the actual numbers against stdfglm; the speedup is real at n=5 but not at n=2,3.

## Quality Axes
- **Originality:** High — both the algebraic problem framing and the ML application are new.
- **Importance of question:** High — GB computation matters across cryptography, statistics, and systems biology.
- **Claims well supported:** Partially — algebraic claims are well supported; the central ML claim and the hybrid-embedding claim are not.
- **Soundness of experiments:** Moderate — no OOD test, no difficulty stratification, no baseline beyond the model variants.
- **Clarity:** Good — definitions, theorems, and the algorithmic pipeline are clearly presented.
- **Value to community:** Real — the dataset-generation infrastructure and Theorem 4.3 will likely outlast the specific Transformer experiments.

## Score and Decision
The paper has a genuine algebraic contribution (formalization of two new problems; a clean backward generator; Thm. 4.3) and a useful piece of infrastructure for future work. The ML headline is overstated and the hybrid-embedding contribution is empirically refuted by the paper's own data, but the core ideas are nontrivial and the experiments, properly scoped, do support a first-of-its-kind learnability result in a well-defined class. This is a borderline paper that I lean toward accepting with a request that the ML claim be calibrated.
