## Summary

This paper proposes two contributions for subgraph-structure membership inference attacks (SMIA) on GNNs: (1) a two-stage defense (TSD) that alternates train-test training with label flattening to reduce overfitting, and (2) an improved end-to-end SMIA attack using learnable multiset functions (Deep Sets) instead of fixed similarity metrics. Experiments on four datasets (three homophilic, one heterophilic) and multiple GNN backbones show TSD reduces attack AUROC by 14.30% over the prior SHNA defense while improving model utility by 10.05%, and the new E-SMIA attack outperforms the prior similarity-based attack by 12.31%.

## Strengths

- **Addresses a genuine and understudied problem:** SMIA defense for GNNs has only one prior work (Wang & Wang, 2024). This paper is a meaningful contribution to an important and underexplored area where subgraph-structure membership leaks more information than individual node membership.
- **Improved SMIA attack with clear motivation:** The end-to-end multiset function attack (E-SMIA) replaces fixed heuristic similarity metrics with learnable permutation-invariant Deep Sets embeddings, reducing complexity from O(k³) and enabling adaptation to heterophilic graphs where similarity metrics perform poorly. The improvement (12.31% average AUROC increase) is well-demonstrated across datasets.
- **Evaluation on heterophilic graphs:** Including Chameleon (heterophilic) with GPRGNN is valuable — the paper correctly identifies that prior SMIA attacks fail on heterophilic graphs and shows E-SMIA handles them better.
- **Transparent about AUROC interpretation:** The paper explicitly acknowledges that AUROC below 0.5 can be flipped, stating "When the AUROC is less than 0.5, the attackers can flip the prediction results to make the AUROC greater than 0.5."

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **TSD's defense gains are confounded by semi-supervised training on test data, with no appropriate baseline to isolate the mechanism's contribution.** TSD's second stage trains on test-set features using pseudolabels (Section 4, Figure 1b). This is semi-supervised learning that reduces overfitting by giving the model access to more data. The ablation study (Table 3) confirms that two-stage training (i.e., the test-data-access component) accounts for most of the defense improvement. However, the ablation lacks the critical control: a single-stage semi-supervised baseline that trains on train labels + test pseudolabels without the alternating schedule. Without this, it is impossible to distinguish whether TSD's gains come from any specific defense mechanism (alternating stages, flattening) rather than simply from training on more data — a well-known regularizer. The paper acknowledges that TSD "can directly train the target model with the full trainset" and "utilizes testsets in the second stage" (Section 6.2), but frames this as an advantage rather than a confound. This matters because if the defense effectiveness is primarily attributable to semi-supervised training rather than a principled defense mechanism, the contribution is incremental rather than novel.

- **The MIA defense comparison with DMP is structurally unfair in utility.** TSD uses a 1:1 train-test split and then trains on both sets, while DMP further splits the training data into private (0.45) and reference (0.45) sets plus a 0.1 test set. The resulting utility advantage (4.35%) is expected given that TSD sees substantially more labeled and pseudolabeled data than DMP, not because of any defense mechanism. The paper acknowledges this data-access asymmetry but still presents it as a meaningful comparison. The LBP comparison is more fair since both use the same data split.

- **The aggregated defense improvement percentages may be inflated by sub-0.5 AUROC conditions.** Although the paper acknowledges that AUROC below 0.5 means an attacker can flip predictions, the headline numbers (14.30% decrease, 16.64% decrease) average across all conditions including sub-0.5 cases. A drop from 0.65 to 0.45 is reported as a 31% defense improvement, but the attacker flips and achieves 0.55 — a mere 15% reduction. Using `min(AUROC, 1−AUROC)` as the metric or excluding sub-0.5 conditions from aggregate calculations would give a more accurate picture of defense effectiveness.

### Minor

- **SMIA defense is compared against only one baseline (SHNA).** While SHNA is the only prior SMIA-specific defense, comparing against general-purpose defenses (e.g., dropout, L2 regularization, label smoothing) applied in the same setting would strengthen the claim that TSD is specifically effective for SMIA, rather than just being a better training recipe.
- **The attacker's shadow models are trained on undefended targets, but not on TSD-defended targets.** If the attacker knows TSD is used and trains shadow models accordingly, the defense may weaken. This threat model consideration is unexplored and relevant for practical deployment claims.
- **Hyperparameters α and β in the flattening strategy lack systematic analysis.** The claim that flattening "increases the mean and variance of the training loss distribution" is supported by only a single qualitative figure (Figure 2) on one dataset-backbone combination, with no sensitivity analysis.

## Novel Insights

The most important insight from cross-referencing the reviews is that TSD operates in a fundamentally different threat model paradigm than noise-based defenses like SHNA: rather than perturbing model outputs to obscure membership signals, TSD changes the training distribution to reduce train-test behavioral divergence. However, this very mechanism — training on test data with pseudolabels — makes the defense's effectiveness hard to attribute. The ablation study inadvertently reveals that the dominant component (two-stage training) is essentially semi-supervised learning, raising the question of whether TSD is a *privacy defense* or simply a *better training procedure* whose privacy benefits are a byproduct of reduced overfitting. This distinction matters: if TSD's contribution is the latter, it should be framed as such rather than as a defense mechanism per se.

## Suggestions

- Add a semi-supervised baseline (one-stage training on train + pseudolabeled test) to the ablation study. This is the single most important experiment to validate or refute the claim that TSD's alternating schedule and flattening provide benefit beyond additional training data.
- Reframe the DMP utility comparison to note that the comparison is not apples-to-apples due to data access asymmetry, and focus the MIA claims on LBP (same data access) or on defense effectiveness (AUROC) rather than utility.
- Use `min(AUROC, 1−AUROC)` as an additional metric to ensure defense claims are not inflated by sub-0.5 AUROC values.
