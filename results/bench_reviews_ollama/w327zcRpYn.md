## Summary

SUBER proposes using LLMs as user simulators within a modular RL environment for recommender systems. Rather than using LLMs as recommenders directly, the framework generates synthetic user profiles and ratings to train RL agents (A2C, PPO, TRPO, DQN), evaluating them via ablation studies, human evaluation, and downstream recommendation metrics — all within the LLM-generated simulation itself.

## Strengths

- **Novel positioning of LLMs as environment simulators rather than recommenders.** The paper explicitly contrasts its approach with prior work that uses LLMs as the recommender system (Section Related Work, lines 79–81), noting that LLMs struggle with large item catalogs due to context length. Using them as user simulators circumvents this limitation and is a distinct paradigm shift documented in Table 1.
- **Practical and useful ablation findings.** The paper identifies and empirically resolves LLM-specific simulation challenges, most notably that tokenization ambiguity causes catastrophic performance drops on a 1–10 rating scale (aggregate score 0.50 in Table 2, row 7) versus 0–9 (0.82). This is a concrete, actionable insight for anyone building similar systems.
- **Modular, well-structured framework with Gymnasium API.** The architecture (Figure 1) separates memory, preprocessing, LLM, and postprocessing cleanly, and the KV-cache engineering (Section 4.1, line 164) addresses a real efficiency concern. The open-source release and standard API lower barriers for RL researchers.
- **Ablation across prompt strategies, rating scales, retrieval methods, and LLM families** (Tables 2–3, Figure 3) provides practical guidance that would benefit future work in this direction.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **No evidence that SUBER-trained policies transfer to real user settings — undermining the paper's core claim.** The paper positions itself as addressing "the persistent challenge of training recommender systems in the absence of real user interactions" (Conclusion, line 369), but all evaluation is internal to SUBER: the LLM generates user profiles, preferences, ratings, and reward signals, and the same LLM is used to evaluate the trained agents. This creates a circular evaluation loop. While RecoGym and RecSim also lack transfer evaluations, those papers make more modest claims. SUBER's introduction and conclusion make stronger claims about real-world applicability ("practical training environments") without any held-out evaluation on real data (e.g., MovieLens test split). The ablations confirm that LLMs produce *coherent* ratings (they distinguish liked from disliked genres), but coherence is far weaker than fidelity to human behavior.

- **Entirely synthetic users with no behavioral grounding in real human data.** Synthetic user profiles are generated by an LLM (Listing 1), their preferences are assigned by the LLM, and their ratings are produced by the LLM. The "Similarity to Real Rating Distribution" ablation (Section 4.2, lines 255–257) only compares the *marginal* rating distribution against MovieLens — matching aggregate statistics says nothing about whether specific user-item pairs are rated similarly by real humans and the LLM. The paper acknowledges in Future Work (line 360) that fine-tuning the LLM on real data like MovieLens would improve simulation fidelity, but this is the experiment that should validate whether the base LLM is a credible human simulator in the first place.

- **Poor RL agent performance even within the simulator, with no failure analysis.** The best agent (A2C) recommends items matching user preferred genres only 49% of the time (Table 4, line 300), meaning over half its top-5 recommendations don't align with the user's explicitly stated preferences. DQN achieves 0.00 personalization (line 297), an apparent training failure that receives no discussion. In an environment where user preferences are explicitly provided in the prompt, these are disappointing results that raise questions about whether the environment provides learnable reward signals.

### Minor

- **Human evaluation is too small and uncontrolled to support realism claims.** The study (Section 4.3) evaluates only 10 user-movie interactions per LLM, rated by colleagues on a 1–5 scale. Confidence intervals are large relative to effect sizes (e.g., Mistral 7B: 3.80 ± 1.27). The study evaluates whether humans "agree" with LLM ratings, not whether the LLM's numerical ratings match what real users would assign. The random baseline also includes an LLM-generated explanation, confounding rating quality with explanation quality (line 327).

- **Reward perturbation and shaping parameters lack empirical grounding.** The Gaussian/greedy noise and the decay formula (Eq. 2, line 179) are motivated conceptually (concept drift, satiation) but no analysis validates that they model real human behavioral dynamics. Different values of q could produce very different learning outcomes with no calibration against real data.

### Trivial

- None beyond what is already noted above in minor.

In simulator-based RL, training and evaluating within the simulator is standard practice; the real issue is whether the simulator is realistic enough for transfer, not that the training-evaluation loop is logically circular. The paper appropriately follows RecoGym/RecSim methodology. Kept the transfer concern but rephrased.

- **Strength Finder claim: "Rigorous validation of the simulator via targeted ablation studies."** — The ablations validate coherence (genre recognition, high/low ratings), not realism. The paper itself calls them "sanity checks." Overclaimed.

- **Strength Finder claim: The idea is "novel."** — While the specific application to recommender systems is new, the broader idea of using LLMs as user simulators has precedents (Park et al. 2023, Argyle et al. 2023, both cited by the paper). The novelty is in the specific application framework, not the general concept.

- **Critic demand for comparison to alternative simulators (e.g., RecoGym, RecSim).** — This would be valuable but is not standard for simulator frameworks. RecoGym and RecSim don't compare against each other either. Moved to Nice-to-Have.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most concrete contribution is the empirical finding that tokenization breaks LLM-based rating generation on a 1–10 scale (aggregate score drops from ~0.82 to 0.50), while shifting to 0–9 restores performance. This is a non-obvious, practically important result for anyone building numeric-output LLM simulators. The framework design is sensible and reusable, but the core claim that SUBER enables training of real-world recommender systems remains unvalidated — the paper shows you can train RL agents in an LLM simulation, but not that those agents would be useful outside it.

## Suggestions

- Add an experiment measuring user-item-level correlation between LLM ratings and real MovieLens ratings (using shared demographic features), to move from validating marginal distributions to validating per-pair predictions.
- Discuss and diagnose DQN's 0.00 personalization — this is a conspicuous failure that readers will notice and want explained.
- Temper claims in the conclusion about addressing "real-world usage scenarios" and "practical training environments" until transfer results are available; the current evidence supports only simulator-internal effectiveness.

## Evaluation

**Originality:** Moderate. The idea of using LLMs as environment simulators (rather than recommenders) is a reasonable paradigm shift applied to a new domain, though the general concept of LLM-as-simulator has prior work. The tokenization finding and framework design are original contributions.

**Importance of research question:** High. Training recommender systems with RL in the absence of online data is a well-recognized challenge. The question is important.

**Whether claims are well supported:** Weak. The central claim about practical applicability is not supported by transfer evidence. The RL results within the simulator are modest at best, and the human evaluation is underpowered.

**Soundness of experiments:** Moderate. The ablation studies are well-designed and thorough. The RL experiments and human evaluation are less convincing — the former shows poor agent performance, the latter is too small.

**Clarity of writing:** Good. The paper is well-structured, the framework is clearly described, and figures/tables are informative.

**Value to the community:** Moderate. The open-source framework and ablation findings (especially tokenization) are useful, but without validation that the simulation produces transferable policies, the practical value is limited.
