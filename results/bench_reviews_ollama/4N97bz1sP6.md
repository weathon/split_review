## Summary

The paper proposes a weakly-supervised audio separation framework that leverages pretrained CLAP audio-language embeddings to generate contrastive supervision for single-source text queries during training, addressing the train-test distribution shift inherent in mix-and-separate methods. The method combines a contrastive loss (CLAP-based similarity between separated audio and text prompts) with consistency reconstruction loss and unsupervised reconstruction loss, and demonstrates significant improvements over baselines on MUSIC, VGGSound, and AudioCaps datasets.

## Strengths

- **Significant performance improvements even under fair comparison.** Against the fairest baselines (*-marked, sharing the improved U-Net architecture), the proposed method achieves 7.9 vs. 5.4 SDR (CLIPSep-Text*) on 2-source training—~46% relative improvement—and reaches 97.5% of the supervised upper bound (7.9 vs. 8.1). This is a substantial and meaningful gap that validates the core contribution. (Table 1)

- **Clear superiority of CLAP-based supervision over classification-based alternatives.** The timbre classifier ablation (Table 1: pretrained classifier achieves 6.1 SDR vs. 7.9 for CLAP) demonstrates that the improvement comes from CLAP's pretrained semantic embeddings rather than simple classification, providing valuable insight into the mechanism. (§4.1, Table 1)

- **Principled loss design combining weak supervision with reconstruction constraints.** The combination of contrastive loss (L_CNT for semantic alignment), consistency reconstruction loss (L_CRL for physical constraint), and unsupervised reconstruction loss (L_URL for fine-grained signal) is well-motivated; the paper correctly identifies that pure contrastive supervision cannot enforce spectrogram-level fidelity. (§3.3–3.4)

- **Consistent semi-supervised improvements.** Table 2 shows monotonic improvements from 5% → 100% supervised data combined with unsupervised data (8.8, 8.9, 9.2, 9.5, 9.9 SDR), and the cross-dataset generalization (VGGSound supervised + AudioCaps unsupervised: 4.3 vs. 2.9) demonstrates practical applicability. (Table 2)

- **Inflated headline numbers in the abstract and introduction.** The prominently claimed "71% SDR boost" is computed against a baseline that does not share the authors' improved U-Net architecture (CLIPSep-Text: 4.6 → 7.9), while the fairer comparison against the *-marked baseline gives ~46% (5.4 → 7.9). This matters because it overstates the methodological contribution relative to what the experiments actually demonstrate.

- **"Beats supervised" claim lacks statistical rigor.** The semi-supervised result (8.8 ± 0.28 vs. supervised 8.1 ± 0.31 on MUSIC) is a central narrative claim ("our semi-supervised approach still beats it by +0.7"), yet the confidence intervals nearly overlap (8.52 vs. 8.41). The VGGSound comparison (3.1 ± 0.31 vs. 2.3 ± 0.23) is more convincing, but the language overstates the evidence for MUSIC. Since the paper motivates the problem as handling multi-source training data, this trend partially undermines the narrative and receives no discussion.

## Novel Insights

The ablation comparing CLAP-based contrastive loss to a timbre classifier with the same architecture reveals a key insight: the improvement comes not just from the contrastive structure but from CLAP's rich pretrained audio-language embeddings. The concurrent classifier (5.0 SDR) underperforms even baselines without weak supervision, while the pretrained classifier (6.1) lags far behind CLAP (7.9). This suggests that the quality of the semantic embedding space matters more than the loss formulation, which has implications for future work—better pretrained models would yield proportionally better separation.

## Suggestions

- Rewrite the abstract to present improvements against both non-* and * baselines side-by-side (e.g., "46–71% relative SDR improvement depending on the baseline architecture"), making the fairer comparison prominent.

- Report statistical significance or at least tightened confidence intervals for the semi-supervised claims, and moderate the "beats supervised" language to acknowledge the marginal nature of the MUSIC difference.

- Discuss the 4-source degradation in semi-supervised training and analyze why performance drops below supervised when the unsupervised data becomes more complex.
