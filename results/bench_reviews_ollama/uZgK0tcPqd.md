## Summary

GazeReward is a framework that augments reward models (RMs) for LLM alignment by injecting synthetic eye-tracking (ET) features — generated by frozen ET prediction models — alongside text embeddings via either concatenation (GazeConcat) or elementwise addition (GazeAdd). The paper evaluates three base LLMs (Llama-3-8B-Instruct, Llama-3-8B, Mistral-7B), two ET prediction models, and three feature combinations across OASST1, HelpSteer2, and RewardBench, reporting consistent accuracy improvements over baselines.

---

## Strengths

- **First integration of ET signals into reward modeling**: Section 5 explicitly confirms no prior work has embedded ET or other implicit feedback directly into RMs; Kiegeland et al. (2024) used ET only for dataset creation in a task-specific setting, whereas GazeReward incorporates it architecturally into any preference-optimization pipeline.
- **Consistent improvements across diverse configurations**: Gains appear in virtually every tested configuration across two preference datasets. For example, Llama-3-8B on OASST1 rises from 58.6% to 73.1% (+24.8%, GazeConcat/fcomb₂.₂), and Mistral-7B on HelpSteer2 rises from 51.3% to 63.3% (+23.6%, GazeConcat/fcomb₁), suggesting the signal is not dataset- or model-specific.
- **Broad ablation coverage**: The paper systematically varies 3 base LLMs × 2 ET generators × 3 feature combinations × 2 integration methods × 3 benchmarks, providing 18+ experimental cells per dataset — appropriate for an early exploratory contribution.
- **Practical and scalable design**: Using frozen ET prediction models eliminates real ET data collection, equipment costs, and privacy concerns, making the approach directly deployable for large-scale preference data pipelines.
- **Interesting finding on model initialization**: Pre-trained unaligned models (Llama-3-8B, Mistral-7B) gain more from ET augmentation (>20%) than the already-instruction-tuned Llama-3-8B-Instruct (~10%), suggesting the ET signal is most valuable before prior alignment has been performed.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None — the paper's empirical improvements are real and consistent; they are not invalidated outright.

### Major

- **Missing mechanism validation — no control to isolate the ET-specific signal from the effect of added trainable parameters.** The GazeReward method adds an MLP projector (two linear layers, two dropout layers, two LayerNorm layers, ReLU) applied to the ET features. The paper never trains a control that routes random token-level features or simple surface features (token length, TF-IDF weights) through the same MLP architecture instead of ET outputs. Without this, it is impossible to determine whether the improvement comes from (A) ET-specific cognitive information (fixation duration correlating with word salience, reading difficulty signaling response quality) or (B) the added non-linear transformation of any token-level feature, which regularizes or enriches the RM's representations regardless of content. Given that synthetic ET features are themselves a deterministic function of text, hypothesis (B) is at least as plausible as hypothesis (A). This is the critical ablation the paper needs to support its specific technical narrative.

- **Conceptual mechanism underspecified — why does word-level reading difficulty predict response preference?** The paper claims ET features "provide valuable information for capturing human preferences" (line 141) but does not articulate the mechanism. Synthetic ET features reflect text readability patterns (word frequency, syntactic complexity) as learned on corpora like Dundee, ZuCo, and PROVO — they describe how *difficult a word is to read*, not which of two responses a human judges to be better. The paper conflates "reading-time features of text" with "evaluative preference signals" without justifying how the former informs the latter. A plausible story exists (coherent responses may have smoother reading profiles), but neither a theoretical argument nor a direct empirical validation of this link is provided. This leaves the central premise unsupported beyond the empirical correlation.

- **Suspiciously weak baselines inflate reported relative gains.** RewardBench baselines are 46.9% (Llama-3-8B-Instruct), 50.9% (Llama-3-8B), and 41.2% (Mistral-7B) — with Mistral well below chance on a binary task. On HelpSteer2, baselines are 54.3%, 53.2%, and 51.3% — all near chance. While the OASST1 in-distribution numbers are more reasonable (62–65%), the RewardBench below-chance results suggest either a distribution shift not acknowledged, a template mismatch, or misconfiguration. The paper does not investigate why the Mistral baseline is at 41.2%, nor does it verify that the baseline RM is correctly implemented. When a baseline is near or below chance, any systematic modification — including adding random features — will produce large relative gains. The headline "over 40% relative improvement" for Mistral on RewardBench is computed from a below-chance denominator, which makes it misleading rather than informative.

### Minor

- **Missing GazeAdd entries for Mistral-7B with no explanation.** All GazeAdd rows for Mistral-7B are dashes in Tables 1, 2, and 3. The paper provides no explanation. If there was a convergence failure, computational limitation, or other issue, it should be stated. As it stands, it is unclear whether this omission is methodological (GazeAdd is incompatible with Mistral's tokenizer) or selective.

- **Large negative result for fcomb₂.₅ + GazeAdd not explained.** GazeAdd with fcomb₂.₅ causes −19.6% for Llama-3-8B-Instruct and −7.2% for Llama-3-8B on OASST1 (Table 1). The paper notes the pattern in Section 4.2 ("In some instances, using fcomb₂.₅ results in worse performance than the baseline") but offers no mechanistic explanation for why the five-feature combination degrades performance in this integration mode specifically. Understanding failure modes is important for the framework's credibility.

- **No statistical significance testing.** Test sets are 416 (OASST1) and 364 (HelpSteer2) examples. No confidence intervals, standard deviations, or significance tests are reported. At these sample sizes, differences of 1–3 percentage points between configurations are not reliably interpretable.

- **Tokenization remapping for GazeAdd is underspecified.** Line 116–158 describe that ET model and RM tokens differ and are handled by "a linear weighted approach" with a sliding window, but the exact weighting function and sub-word alignment procedure are not formally defined. This is a reproducibility-relevant implementation detail for the GazeAdd method.

### Trivial

- The claim that ET "does not suffer from human biases" (line 23) is technically inaccurate for synthetic ET features, which inherit biases from the populations and corpora used to train the ET prediction models. This is minor mismatch in framing rather than a methodological error.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Random/surface-feature control ablation** (e.g., random Gaussian features, token length) fed through the same MLP projector to confirm that ET-specific information — not the projector architecture itself — drives improvements.
- **Qualitative analysis of ET feature differences** between chosen and rejected responses (e.g., do preferred responses have consistently lower TRT on high-content words?) to validate that the ET signal is aligned with the evaluative direction the paper claims.
- **End-to-end evaluation**: Apply the best GazeReward RM in rejection sampling or PPO and measure downstream win-rates or benchmark scores (MT-Bench, AlpacaEval). The paper's own Discussion (line 282) flags this as missing; it would transform the work from "improved RM accuracy" to "improved alignment."
- **Investigation of the below-chance RewardBench baseline**: Verify that the Mistral-7B RM is not misconfigured (wrong chat template, incorrect label orientation) for this out-of-distribution evaluation.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel insight is the possibility that frozen, text-only ET prediction models — trained on general reading corpora — encode a signal that is *complementary* to what the LM backbone learns from preference data alone. The finding that unaligned pre-trained models benefit more from ET augmentation than instruction-tuned models, combined with the pattern that TRT and FFD features are more consistently helpful than nFix/GPT/fixProp, suggests there may be a genuine property of linguistic surface form (reading difficulty distribution) that correlates with response quality in preference datasets. However, without the missing ablation, this insight cannot be confirmed as ET-specific rather than a general phenomenon of auxiliary token-level features.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a random-feature projector control** in the same MLP architecture — this single experiment would either validate the ET-specific mechanism or reveal that any token features help, fundamentally changing the paper's message.
2. **Investigate and report the cause of the below-chance Mistral RewardBench baseline** (41.2%); if it is a template or configuration issue, fixing it would produce a more credible OOD comparison.
3. **Explain and formally define the tokenization remapping** for GazeAdd in a methods section (e.g., Algorithm box) to ensure reproducibility.
4. **Report statistical significance** (e.g., bootstrap confidence intervals) on the test accuracy figures, especially for small test sets where single-point differences are unreliable.
5. **Include at least a brief qualitative analysis** comparing ET feature profiles (TRT distributions) between chosen and rejected responses on a few examples to give interpretive support for the claimed mechanism.
