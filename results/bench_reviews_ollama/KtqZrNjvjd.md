## Summary

Video Active Perception (VAP) introduces a training-free method for long-form video QA that selects key frames by comparing real video latents against those generated by a text-conditioned video generation model (CogVideoX). Frames with the lowest cosine similarity to their generated counterparts—interpreted as "surprising" relative to prior knowledge—are selected for VLM inference. VAP is evaluated on EgoSchema, NExT-QA, ActivityNet-QA, and CLEVRER, claiming state-of-the-art zero-shot accuracy and up to 5.6× efficiency improvement ("frames per question") over standard VLM inference.

## Strengths

- **Clear, testable core idea**: Using a generation model's prediction error in latent space as a proxy for frame informativeness is a concrete, falsifiable proposal for frame selection that does not require captioning models or iterative retrieval loops (Section 2.2, Algorithm 1).

- **CLEVRER reasoning gains are strong and meaningful**: VAP achieves 154.0%, 71.43%, and 8% relative gains over VideoTree on explanatory, counterfactual, and predictive CLEVRER questions (Table 1, Section 3.5). These gains are particularly informative because CLEVRER uses the same 5-frame count for VAP and baselines, effectively isolating the contribution of frame selection. This demonstrates that preserving visual frames rather than captions matters for causal reasoning.

- **Training-free and VLM-agnostic design**: The method requires no fine-tuning of either the generation model or the VLM, and is evaluated across three VLM families (Gemini, GPT-4o, LLaVA-OneVision), demonstrating practical generality (Section 3.3).

- **Non-obvious finding from initial-frame ablation**: Table 3 shows that increasing initial frames improves VAP up to a point, then hurts it—because the generation model predicts too well, flattening the surprisal signal. This is a genuinely interesting and non-trivial finding (Section 3.5).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Missing uniform-sampling ablation at matched frame counts on the same VLM** — The paper's central claim is that VAP's frame selection mechanism identifies the most informative frames. However, the critical ablation—comparing VAP-selected frames against uniformly-sampled frames at the same count (e.g., 32) on the same VLM backbone—is never performed. All efficiency comparisons in Figure 1 and Section 3.4 compare VAP (32 frames) against standard VLMs using many more frames (180, 44, 90), showing only that fewer frames suffice, not that VAP's *choice* of frames matters. Table 2 varies the number of VAP-selected frames with GPT-4o mini but never includes the uniform-sampling baseline at the same frame counts. If uniformly sampling 32 frames with the same VLM achieves comparable accuracy, then VAP's contribution reduces to the trivial observation that fewer frames are sufficient—and the generation model overhead is unnecessary. This missing experiment is essential for validating the core contribution.

- **Misleading efficiency claims** — The headline "5.6× efficiency improvement" is measured exclusively in "frames per question" fed to the VLM. However, VAP itself incurs substantial computation: encoding ALL real video frames through the 3D VAE (the paper explicitly states "We use the 3D VAE encoder... to encode all real frames," line 72; for EgoSchema this is ~5,400 frames), running a 50-step diffusion forward pass (line 120), RIFE interpolation, and computing all pairwise cosine similarities. None of this overhead is accounted for in the efficiency metric. The paper states "VAP greatly improves the efficiency of long-form video QA for the flagship VLM inference" (Section 3.4), implying overall efficiency, but the 5.6× figure only measures the VLM portion. Without wall-clock time, FLOPs, or total pipeline cost, the claim cannot be evaluated. The efficiency advantage may still exist (VLM inference on 180 frames is very expensive), but the paper does not establish this.

### Minor

- **Unvalidated connection between latent surprisal and question relevance** — VAP selects frames with lowest cosine similarity in CogVideoX's compressed latent space (4×8×8 downsampled from pixels) as the "most informative" frames. However, no evidence is provided that deviations in this space correspond to question-relevant events rather than generation artifacts (hallucinated backgrounds, incorrect motion, model failure). The qualitative examples in Figure 4 are cherry-picked successes. A correlation analysis between surprisal scores and ground-truth important frames, or comparison against alternative selection criteria (e.g., optical flow, diversity sampling), would strengthen the validity of the mechanism claim (Sections 2.2, 3.6).

- **Unclear VLM assignment in Table 1** — VAP is evaluated across three VLM families but Table 1 does not clearly indicate which specific VLM backbone is used for each VAP result, making it difficult to isolate VAP's contribution from the backbone VLM's capability when comparing against baselines that use different VLMs.

- **"Lightweight" characterization of CogVideoX is unsupported** — The paper repeatedly calls the generation model "lightweight" (Abstract, Section 1, Section 2.1) but provides no parameter counts, runtime measurements, or FLOPs to support this characterization. CogVideoX is a large-scale diffusion transformer, and without quantitative evidence, this claim is unsubstantiated.

## Novel Insights

The non-trivial finding from Table 3—that feeding more initial frames to the generation model eventually hurts VAP because it eliminates the surprisal signal—is a genuinely novel observation. It reveals a fundamental tension in the VAP framework: the prior model must be informative enough to produce meaningful predictions but not so well-conditioned that it predicts everything correctly. This creates a "Goldilocks zone" for the prior model's conditioning information, suggesting that the quality of active perception is bounded by the calibration of the prior's uncertainty.

## Suggestions

- Add a uniform-sampling-at-32-frames baseline for each VLM backbone on each dataset. This is the single experiment that would most strengthen (or weaken) the paper's core claim, would cost very little to run, and would be decisive for reviewers.
- Report total wall-clock time or FLOPs for the complete VAP pipeline vs. uniform sampling baselines so the efficiency claim can be properly contextualized. Even if VAP's total pipeline cost is still lower than running a VLM on 180 frames, this needs to be demonstrated explicitly.

## Score and Decision

The paper presents an interesting and well-motivated idea—using generation model surprisal for frame selection—but two major weaknesses undermine its core claims. The absence of a uniform-sampling baseline at matched frame counts means the paper cannot establish that VAP's frame selection mechanism itself is responsible for the observed performance gains, as opposed to simply using fewer frames with a capable VLM. The efficiency claim is structurally misleading because it ignores VAP's own significant computational overhead. These are addressable concerns—the uniform-sampling ablation is straightforward to add—but in their absence, the paper cannot substantiate its primary contributions. The CLEVRER results (where frame counts match and VAP's selection clearly matters) partially mitigate the first concern, but the other datasets lack this validation. The paper has genuine merit in the idea and the strong CLEVRER results, but needs critical additional evidence.
