## Summary

The paper introduces the Entity-Deduction Arena (EDA), a 20-questions-style interactive framework for evaluating LLMs' multi-turn conversational reasoning and planning. Models act as "guessers" asking Yes/No/Maybe questions to deduce an unknown entity held by a GPT-3.5 judge. The paper benchmarks several LLMs, conducts ablations disentangling planning from reasoning, and demonstrates that both behavior cloning and reinforcement learning from game-play (RLGP) can improve open-source Vicuna models.

## Strengths

- **Novel and well-suited evaluation framework**: The 20-questions game is a clean, intuitive proxy for assessing multi-turn strategic question-asking under a clear objective function. It avoids annotation reliability issues of open-ended conversational tasks while requiring genuine state tracking, planning, and inductive reasoning in synergy (Section 3).

- **Insightful internal-state probing analysis (Table 3)**: Prompting GPT-4 to reveal its top-5 candidates at each turn provides a mechanistic window into how the model's implicit search tree evolves. The observation that the model sometimes asks questions that eliminate *all* top-5 candidates to re-explore overlooked categories is a non-obvious finding about LLM strategic behavior.

- **Useful failure-mode taxonomy**: Categorizing how weaker models fail (early enumeration, redundancy, inconsistency) in Section 4/Table 2 provides diagnostic insight beyond aggregate score comparisons.

- **Cross-domain generalization analysis (RQ4)**: Testing whether strategies learned on Things transfer to Celebrities is a meaningful probe of whether BC teaches genuine strategies versus surface mimicry. The finding that V-FT 7B (Things) transfers to Celebrities while V-FT 7B (Celebs) fails on Things is informative.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The planning-vs-reasoning ablation (RQ2, Table 4) has an unaddressed distribution shift confound**: The experiment swaps the guesser model for only the last turn of a trajectory generated by the other model. When GPT-4 takes the last turn of a Vicuna-generated conversation, it operates on conversation histories it never produces—a distribution shift that may systematically disadvantage it (Vicuna's histories may contain redundant or inconsistent turns). Conversely, when Vicuna takes the last turn of a GPT-4 conversation, the coherent history may make the final step easier. The paper concludes that "planning deficiency could result in an unproductive trajectory" and that planning is more important than reasoning, but the comparison does not cleanly isolate planning because the final-turn model faces an out-of-distribution context. This directly undermines the paper's central claim about the relative importance of planning versus reasoning. (Lines 113–121)

- **The "GPT-4 outperforms human players by a large margin" claim relies on a loosely controlled human baseline**: Human players (108 volunteers, 140/68 sessions on Things/Celebrities) were provided with "optional GPT-3.5 retrospection generation for training purposes" (Line 83), whose impact on strategy is uncharacterized. More importantly, the human study is underdocumented: it is unclear how many sessions each human played per entity, what their motivation or familiarity was, or whether the interface was comparable to the programmatic API call used for LLMs. The headline claim in the abstract ("GPT-4 outperforms human players by a large margin") is therefore made on the basis of an uninterpretable comparison—not because GPT-4 cannot beat humans, but because the human baseline does not support the strength of the claim. This should be substantially qualified.

### Minor

- **Small test set with no statistical significance tests**: The evaluation rests on 30 entities per dataset with 5 repetitions (150 total game sessions per model per dataset). Standard deviations are reported (Table 1) but no significance tests are provided. Many claimed performance differences, especially in the BC and RLGP comparisons, could fall within noise given this sample size. This is a recognized limitation that the authors partially address by reporting standard deviations, but the fine-grained conclusions (e.g., V-FT 7B (Suc.) "outperformed the teacher model (GPT-3.5) on Things") would be strengthened by proper statistical testing.

- **The score metric discards partial progress information**: The metric (Eq. 1) assigns score 0 to any losing game regardless of how close the guesser came, while awarding scores from 0.9 to 1.0 for games won in 6–10 turns. This means a model that consistently narrows down to 2–3 candidates but never gets the exact match looks identical to one that is completely lost. This limits the informativeness of comparisons between models that have low success rates. However, the paper also reports success rate and #Turns separately, partially mitigating this concern.

- **The judge model's noise may be systematically biased, not random**: The paper acknowledges GPT-3.5 judge inaccuracies but argues this is acceptable because "user responses in real-world scenarios can also be noisy" (Line 67). However, the concern is not noise per se but potential *systematic* bias: models whose question-answering style is more similar to GPT-3.5's training distribution (likely GPT-4, which shares architectural and training similarities) may receive more favorable judge responses than open-source models with different phrasing patterns. The paper does not characterize the judge's error profile or test with an alternative judge. This is a minor concern rather than major because all models face the same judge, but it could differentially affect certain models.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Running evaluation with an alternative judge (even a simple rule-based one for structured entity attributes) to test robustness of performance rankings.
- Expanding the test sets beyond 30 entities to increase statistical power for fine-grained claims.
- Conducting a more controlled human baseline study (no retrospection, clear instructions, repeated sessions per entity) to make the human comparison meaningful.

## Removed Points

- **Harsh Critic's claim that the human study's GPT-3.5 retrospection "may have hurt rather than helped performance by biasing strategy"**: The retrospection is described as "optional" and "for training purposes." While its impact is uncharacterized, claiming it actively hurt performance is speculative. The real concern is that the human baseline is insufficiently documented, not that it was sabotaged.

- **Harsh Critic's claim about RLGP being a "negative result" because it strengthens existing successes rather than learning new items**: The paper actually discusses this finding explicitly (Section 5.3, Line 159) and contrasts it with BC fine-tuning, which helps models succeed on *new* items. This is presented as a genuine observation, not hidden; it enriches rather than undermines the contribution.

- **Harsh Critic's demand for statistical significance tests** was classified as minor rather than major because the authors do report standard deviations in Table 1, which provides some indication of variance. Significance testing would strengthen but not fundamentally alter the paper's claims.

- **Harsh Critic's claim that training details for RLGP are "missing"**: The paper describes key training details (PPO, trlX, intermediate reward for "Yes" responses, 209 training entities). While more hyperparameters would be nice, this is standard for an empirical paper of this scope and falls under the "nice-to-have" category rather than a major omission.

- **Harsh Critic's concern about data contamination from web-text**: The paper addresses this (Line 94), acknowledging that "a small portion of entity deduction game sessions may exist in the webtext" but arguing this is "a minuscule fraction." This is a legitimate concern but one the authors acknowledge; it does not invalidate the framework.

- **Strength Finder's claim about "large-scale human baseline with 108 participants" as a supporting strength**: 108 participants producing only 140 sessions is not "large-scale" for 30 test entities. This is better characterized as a useful but limited baseline, not a strength.

- **Strength Finder's claim that the ablation "cleanly separates" planning from reasoning**: As verified above, the ablation has a distribution-shift confound. This claim conflicts with a verified weakness and is removed.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most interesting finding is the qualitative probing of GPT-4's internal candidate evolution (Table 3), which reveals that the model does not always optimally partition its top-5 candidates—suggesting it uses a more complex strategy that includes backtracking to re-explore overlooked categories. However, the same probing reveals a tension with the planning-vs-reasoning ablation: if GPT-4's strategy is dynamic and context-dependent, then swapping only the final-turn model creates not just a distribution shift but a context where the replacement model lacks access to the evolving internal state that guided prior planning decisions. This observation reinforces the methodological concern about the RQ2 ablation.

## Suggestions

- Qualify the "GPT-4 outperforms humans by a large margin" claim to acknowledge the limitations of the human baseline, or describe the human study conditions in more detail.
- Acknowledge the distribution-shift confound in the RQ2 ablation experiment and frame the planning-vs-reasoning conclusion as suggestive rather than definitive.
- Report p-values or bootstrap confidence intervals for at least the key comparisons in Table 1, given the small test set.

## Score and Decision

The paper introduces a genuinely interesting evaluation framework with useful qualitative insights (failure modes, internal-state probing). However, the two most novel claims—that planning dominates reasoning (RQ2) and that GPT-4 substantially outperforms humans—both have substantive methodological issues: the ablation is confounded by distribution shift, and the human baseline is insufficiently controlled. These are major weaknesses that cannot be fully resolved in a rebuttal but do not invalidate the broader contribution of the framework and the BC/RLGP results. The small test set (30 entities) limits the statistical reliability of fine-grained claims. On balance, the paper makes real contributions (task design, probing analysis, BC/RLGP improvement) that are partially undermined—not fundamentally invalidated—by weaknesses in two headline claims.

**Originality**: Moderate. The 20-questions game is a natural but well-chosen testbed. The internal-state probing and BC/RLGP training are incremental but solid contributions.

**Importance**: Moderate. Multi-turn evaluation frameworks are needed, but the task's constraints (Yes/No/Maybe answers, exact match) limit direct applicability to real clarification-question scenarios.

**Claims support**: Partially. The framework and BC/RLGP results are well-supported; the planning-vs-reasoning and human comparison claims are weakened by methodological issues.

**Experimental soundness**: The small test set and confounded ablation limit reliability.

**Clarity**: Good. The paper is well-organized and the research questions structure the narrative effectively.
