## Summary

This paper develops a physics-augmented classification strategy for single-molecule force spectroscopy (SMFS) data. The key idea is to generate a large simulated dataset via a Monte Carlo model of protein unfolding (covering no-molecule, single-molecule, and multiple-molecule scenarios), pre-train deep learning classifiers on it, and then apply the models to experimental force curves from non-specific pulling of three multi-domain proteins (Titin I27O, utrophin, dystrophin). The authors release experimental datasets and code. They report that pre-training on simulated data yields up to 25.9% higher ROC-AUC than training directly on limited experimental data, and that adding a small amount (~100 examples) of experimental data via transfer learning further improves performance by 6.8% accuracy and 0.06 ROC-AUC.

## Strengths

1. **Novel comprehensive Monte Carlo simulation covering all three experimental scenarios** – Prior simulation frameworks only modeled single-molecule force curves; this work extends to no-molecule and multiple-molecule cases, including stochastic adhesion and detachment events. This directly enables the physics-augmentation strategy by providing a large, well-annotated training set (Sec. 4.1: "To build a comprehensive training dataset, we incorporate the real cases of force curves generated by no molecule and multiple molecules").

2. **Physics-augmented pipeline (simulation pre-training + optional transfer learning) outperforms direct training on limited experimental data** – On all three protein datasets, ResNet pre-trained on simulation data achieves higher ROC-AUC and accuracy across training proportions 0.05–0.2, with up to 25.9% higher ROC-AUC (Sec. 6.1, Figure 4). This pipeline-level comparison demonstrates practical value for a domain where labeled data are scarce.

3. **Transfer learning with ~100 experimental examples bridges the simulation-experiment gap** – Incorporating 20% of experimental data via transfer learning increases average accuracy by 6.8% and ROC-AUC by 0.06 over purely simulation-pre-trained models, and the improvement is especially large (0.2 higher accuracy) at the smallest training proportion (Sec. 6.1, Figure 4).

4. **First publicly available SMFS experimental datasets from non-specific pulling of three multi-domain proteins** – The paper releases experimental force curves for Titin I27O, UtrN-R3, and DysN-R3 along with code, providing a benchmark for reproducible research in a domain where such resources are scarce (Abstract, Sec. 5).

5. **Validation across three structurally distinct proteins including challenging natural proteins** – Models are tested on an engineered protein (Titin I27O, identical domains) and two natural proteins with heterogeneous domains (UtrN-R3, DysN-R3), showing that transfer learning is particularly beneficial when simulation parameters for heterogeneous domains are unavailable (Sec. 5, Sec. 6.2).

6. **Probabilistic threshold tuning enables high-precision single-molecule identification** – Using ROC curves, the classifier can be tuned to achieve 97%, 83%, and 82% precision for single-molecule class on Titin I27O, UtrN-R3, and DysN-R3 respectively, which is practically useful for applications where false positives must be minimized (Sec. 6.2).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The reference-curve augmentation confounds attribution of improvement to physics-based pre-training.** The physics-augmented models use a multi-channel input that includes the difference between the input curve and randomly sampled **simulated** reference curves (Sec. 4.2). The direct-training baseline (dashed lines in Figure 4) apparently does **not** use this mechanism — the paper states reference curves are "simulated force curves from the training dataset," and the direct-training baseline by definition has no access to simulation data (Sec. 6.1). This means the comparison conflates two factors: simulation-data pre-training AND the multi-channel reference-curve input. It is possible that the reference-curve augmentation alone (even with non-physics-based references) accounts for part of the improvement, or that models trained directly on experimental data would benefit from the same multi-channel input using experimental curves as references. The paper provides no ablation to separate these effects. Without such a control, the central claim that the **physics content** of the simulation drives the performance gain is not persuasively supported. **Why it matters:** The paper's narrative attributes improvement to "physics augmentation," but the reader cannot tell whether the WLC-based physics simulation or simply having a multi-channel reference-curve input format is responsible.

### Minor

1. **Simulation parameters require experimental characterization, moderating the advertised autonomy.** The paper states the strategy "does not need expensive expert adjudication of the experimental data" and "avoids the need for expensive expert adjudication and human bias." While true that individual force curves do not require manual labeling, the Monte Carlo simulation requires molecular parameters (contour length, persistence length, energy landscape parameters) that are derived from prior experimental characterization of the same proteins. For a completely new protein without established parameters, additional experiments would be needed. The paper acknowledges this in its limitations (Sec. 7) and notes the method does not depend heavily on accurate parameters (Sec. C.5), but the main narrative could more carefully distinguish between the labeling effort saved and the parameter-estimation effort required.

2. **Single-expert annotation as ground truth.** The experimental test labels come from one expert's visual inspection (Sec. 5: "Annotation is performed through visual inspection of the unfolding force curves"; Sec. 7). This means reported accuracies measure agreement with that expert's judgment rather than an objective ground truth. The paper acknowledges this but does not assess inter-annotator variability, so the absolute accuracy numbers (75.3%) should be interpreted as an upper bound on how well the model can replicate that specific expert.

### Trivial

1. **Clarity on experimental splits and training proportions.** The description of the experimental split (70% test, 30% reserved) and the meaning of "training proportion" in Figure 4 could be clearer. A training proportion of 0.05 corresponds to ~27 curves (from a ~550-curve dataset), which is extremely small. While this supports the point that labeled data are scarce, stating the absolute numbers explicitly would improve readability and avoid any impression of an unfair comparison.

2. **No confusion matrices reported.** The paper reports accuracy, F1-score, and ROC-AUC but not confusion matrices for the three-class classification on experimental test sets. Confusion matrices would reveal which confusions dominate (e.g., single vs. multiple, single vs. none) and add valuable diagnostic detail.

3. **M=1 reference-curve choice not motivated in main text.** The choice of one reference curve (M=1) for main experiments is discussed in the supplementary (Sec. C.3) but not justified in the main text. A brief reasoning or reference would improve readability.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Ablation of reference-curve augmentation.** The single most informative experiment would compare four conditions: (i) direct training on experimental data without reference curves (current baseline), (ii) direct training on experimental data *with* reference curves drawn from the experimental training set, (iii) pre-training on simulation data without reference curves, and (iv) pre-training on simulation data with reference curves (current approach). This would isolate the benefit of physics-based pre-training from the benefit of the multi-channel input.

- **Comparison against a simple non-physics synthetic augmentation.** Replacing the Monte Carlo simulation with a naive generative process (e.g., adding noise to sawtooth patterns without WLC mechanics) and comparing pre-training performance would directly test whether the physical realism of the simulation is the source of improvement, or whether any family of curve-like patterns suffices.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation across the reviews is the structural trade-off between the simulation parameters requiring prior experimental characterization and the claim of reducing expert effort. The paper's honest admission that transfer learning helps most for heterogeneous-domain proteins (utrophin/dystrophin) where simulation parameters are less accurate — combined with the finding that even the pure simulation-pre-trained model outperforms direct training — suggests a practical recipe: use a coarse-grained simulation (requiring only approximate parameters) for pre-training, then correct the domain-specific discrepancies with a small amount of labeled experimental data. This two-stage strategy may be more broadly applicable to other scientific domains where simulation-experiment gaps exist and labeled data are expensive. However, this insight is somewhat undercut by the reference-curve confound, which is the paper's most significant methodological gap.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a controlled ablation experiment** comparing: (a) direct training without reference curves, (b) direct training with reference curves from experimental data, (c) simulation pre-training without reference curves, and (d) simulation pre-training with reference curves. This is the single most impactful experiment the authors could add.

2. **Specify explicitly whether the direct-training baseline uses the multi-channel reference-curve input** or only the raw force curve. If it does not, state this clearly and acknowledge the confound.

3. **State the absolute number of training curves** (not just proportion) for each experimental condition in Figure 4, so readers can directly see how small the training sets are.

4. **Include confusion matrices** for the three-class prediction on the experimental test sets to show which confusions dominate.

5. **Clarify the parameter-dependence** in one visible location: which parameters are from prior literature, which are estimated from the same datasets, and what minimal characterization a new protein requires.
