## Summary

The paper introduces Linear Circular Optimal Transport (LCOT), a metric for circular probability measures that linearizes the Circular Optimal Transport (COT) distance by fixing a reference measure (e.g., the uniform distribution). The core contribution is a closed-form embedding mapping each circular measure into a tangent space, yielding a computationally efficient distance that provably defines a true metric. When the reference is uniform, the embedding reduces pairwise comparison complexity from O(K²N log(1/ε)) to O(K²N). The paper consolidates scattered results on circular OT, provides explicit Monge map formulas, and illustrates the method through qualitative MDS experiments and timing comparisons.

## Strengths

- **Closed-form embedding with proven computational savings.** The paper derives an explicit LCOT embedding (Equation (12)) when the reference is the uniform measure, eliminating the binary search for the optimal cutting angle α that COT requires. This reduces per-measure embedding cost to O(N) and pairwise comparison of K measures to O(K²N), versus O(K²N log(1/ε)) for COT. The wall-clock timing experiments (Figure 4) empirically confirm orders-of-magnitude speedups for the uniform-reference case across varying sample sizes and numbers of measures.

- **Rigorous theoretical foundation.** The paper consolidates scattered results on circular OT into a clear framework (Theorem 1) with explicit formulas for the optimal Monge map. It proves that the LCOT discrepancy defines a true metric on the space of circular probability measures (Theorem 2) and establishes invertibility of the LCOT embedding (Proposition 2). These theoretical results are essential for the method to be used reliably downstream.

- **Computationally efficient barycenter computation.** The invertibility of the LCOT embedding enables direct, optimization-free barycenter computation (Experiment 3, Figure 6) — a practical advantage over the iterative procedures typically required for COT barycenters.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Experiments are entirely qualitative; quantitative evidence for ML utility is absent.** The paper claims "superior clustering outcomes" and aims to demonstrate benefits "in manifold learning, measure interpolation, and clustering/classification of probability measures" (line 22), yet provides zero quantitative metrics — no clustering accuracy, no silhouette score, no classification accuracy, no retrieval rank. Experiment 2 claims LCOT "stands out for its superior clustering outcomes, featuring distinct boundaries between the actual classes, outperforming the other methods" (line 336), but this conclusion rests entirely on visual inspection of MDS plots. For a paper that prominently advertises the benefit of a linear embedding for ML, the absence of any quantitative task evaluation is a significant gap. A classification or clustering benchmark with known labels (e.g., k-NN using LCOT as a metric) is needed to support the central claim that the embedding allows one to "seamlessly modify the underlying metric for the ML algorithm to LCOT" (abstract). Qualitatively inspecting MDS plots does not verify this.

2. **The computational advantage claim lacks necessary qualification.** The abstract and introduction frame LCOT as "computationally efficient" without qualification, yet the major speedup (O(K²N) vs. O(K²N log(1/ε))) relies on the closed-form α for the uniform reference. For non-uniform references, LCOT still requires binary search for α, yielding O(K²N log(1/ε)) — the same asymptotic complexity as COT. Figure 5 confirms that the non-uniform reference case (dash-dotted curves) is often comparable to or only marginally faster than COT. The paper should explicitly state which case the speed claim applies to, and the non-uniform-reference scenario should not be presented as similarly advantageous.

### Minor

1. **The "OT (fixed cutting point)" baseline in Experiment 1 is uninformative.** A fixed cutting point is not a principled baseline for circular OT, since the whole point of COT is to optimize over cutting points. Showing that LCOT beats an arbitrary fixed cut is not impressive. Fortunately, COT is also included as a baseline, so this does not invalidate the comparison — but the fixed-cut baseline adds nothing and could be dropped or replaced with a meaningful competitor (e.g., sliced Wasserstein on S¹).

2. **The claim that the explicit Monge map formula was "previously lacking" (line 176) is overstated.** Delon (2010) and Rabin (2011) characterize optimal couplings on the circle, and Bonet et al. (2022) give the closed-form for the uniform-to-ν case. While consolidating these into a clean unified formula with explicit notation is valuable, presenting it as entirely novel risks overclaiming. The genuine novelty is the linearization framework (LCOT), not the Monge map formula itself.

3. **Rotation invariance of the expectation-based formula should be explicitly discussed.** When μ = Unif(S¹), the closed-form α = E(ν) − 1/2 depends on the expectation under a fixed parametrization (North Pole = 0). A reader may reasonably wonder whether the LCOT distance is invariant under simultaneous rotation of both ν₁ and ν₂. (It is: the min_{k∈ℤ} in the integrand combined with the translation invariance of Lebesgue measure ensures invariance, but the paper does not address this.) A brief remark would resolve the concern.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Quantitative experiments on real circular data** (e.g., hue histograms from images, orientation histograms from geology). The paper names these applications in the introduction but never evaluates on them. Even one such experiment would substantially strengthen the practical relevance.
- **Comparison to alternative fast OT distances on the circle**, such as sliced Wasserstein on S¹ (by projecting onto rotations) or the energy distance. These could be cheaper baselines that help contextualize LCOT's speed/accuracy trade-off.
- **Analysis of the approximation error of linearization.** Since LCOT is a linearization of COT at the uniform measure, a bound on |LCOT(ν₁,ν₂) − COT(ν₁,ν₂)| in terms of how far ν₁, ν₂ are from uniform would give users guidance on when LCOT is a faithful proxy.
