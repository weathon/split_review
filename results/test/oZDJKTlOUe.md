## Summary

This paper proposes LURE (LVLM Hallucination Revisor), a post-hoc method for reducing object hallucination in large vision-language models. The key idea is to train a separate revisor model on synthetically corrupted captions generated by GPT-3.5, where the corruption is guided by three identified factors: object co-occurrence, token-level uncertainty during decoding, and object position in generated text. The revisor takes potentially hallucinatory descriptions from any LVLM, masks high-uncertainty or late-position objects with `[IDK]` placeholders, and reconstructs accurate descriptions. Experiments across six open-source LVLMs show consistent reductions in hallucination under CHAIR metrics, GPT evaluation, and human evaluation, with substantial margins over several baselines.

## Strengths

- **Consistent and substantial improvements across diverse LVLMs and multiple evaluation protocols.** On all six evaluated LVLMs, LURE achieves the lowest CHAIR$_S$ and CHAIR$_I$ scores (Table 1), with reductions often exceeding 50% relative to the original descriptions (e.g., mPLUG-Owl: CHAIR$_S$ from 71.2 to 18.8; LLaVa: from 54.0 to 27.1). These gains are corroborated by human evaluation (Table 2), where LURE ranks first across every LVLM by wide margins (e.g., 1.25 vs. next best 2.16 for mPLUG-Owl), and by GPT evaluation.

- **Lightweight post-hoc design that decouples hallucination correction from base model retraining.** Unlike prior work requiring fine-tuning the full LVLM or constructing high-quality training datasets from scratch, LURE trains a separate revisor that can be deployed alongside any LVLM without modifying the base model. The paper demonstrates this by evaluating on six different LVLMs and testing three different revisor backbones (Table 5), showing consistent improvements across all configurations.

- **Empirical statistical analysis identifying three key hallucination factors.** The paper provides systematic empirical evidence (co-occurrence scores, uncertainty distributions, position distributions shown in Figure 1) linking co-occurrence, token-level uncertainty, and object position to hallucination. This analysis is grounded in real data from multiple LVLMs and provides a principled basis for the revisor design.

- **Ablation studies confirm each factor's independent contribution.** Removing any one of the three factors during training degrades performance (Table 4), supporting the claim that all three identified factors are actively beneficial and not redundant. For instance, removing the position factor raises CHAIR$_I$ from 4.9 to 5.8, and removing uncertainty raises it to 5.4.

### Major

- **The training pipeline in Algorithm 1 is underspecified regarding which LVLM generates the descriptions and uncertainty estimates used to create training data.** Algorithm 1 takes an LVLM $\mathcal{M}$ as input and uses $\mathcal{M}$ to generate descriptions $s$ and compute token-level uncertainties for masking. The paper never states which specific LVLM plays this role during training. If $\mathcal{M}$ is always the same model (e.g., MiniGPT-4), then the uncertainty-based masking during training is conditioned on that specific model's uncertainty distribution, raising a question about how well the revisor generalizes to LVLMs with substantially different uncertainty patterns at inference. The paper's claim of "seamless integration with any LVLMs" would be strengthened by clarifying this choice and explaining why the revisor's performance does not depend on a particular $\mathcal{M}$ during training. The empirical results show it does work across models, but the mechanism is not explained, and the current lack of specification harms reproducibility. This is the most significant issue — it does not invalidate the results but needs to be addressed in a revision.

- **The theoretical analysis (Section 2.4) provides only weak support for the paper's contributions.** The theorems adopt strong simplifying assumptions (linear predictors, Gaussian conditional features, equally balanced binary classes) and essentially formalize well-known facts: reducing spurious correlations lowers error, and sampling more certain examples lowers error. The connection between this theoretical setup and the actual revisor training is loose — the theorems analyze data sampling distributions, not the revisor's denoising training or architecture. The object position discussion merely cites existing work on autoregressive error accumulation without deriving a novel result. While this theoretical framing does not detract from the paper, it also does not provide actionable insight into *why* the revisor training strategy works. Consider condensing this section into a brief intuitive discussion and focusing the paper's rigor claims on the empirical statistical analysis, which is stronger.

### Minor

- **The ablation study lacks a "no-factor" baseline where synthetic corruption is performed without any of the three identified factors** (e.g., random object insertion and random token masking). The ablation removes one factor at a time (Table 4), but does not test whether the three factors collectively outperform a naive corruption strategy. This would help distinguish whether the specific factors drive the gains, or whether any plausible corruption-based denoising training would produce similar improvements. Given that the gap between the full model and the best 2-of-3 ablation is modest (CHAIR$_S$ 19.7 vs. 21.2–22.6), this baseline would quantitatively strengthen the paper's central claim about the factors' importance.

- **Threshold values $\gamma$ (uncertainty) and $\eta$ (position) are not reported.** The paper states they are selected via cross-validation but provides no specific values, range searched, or sensitivity analysis in the main paper. Reporting these values and a brief sensitivity table would improve reproducibility.

- **The revisor is itself an LVLM (e.g., MiniGPT-4), not lightweight in an absolute sense.** The paper should be more explicit about the revisor's parameter count and inference cost relative to the models it corrects, to help readers assess practical deployment trade-offs.
