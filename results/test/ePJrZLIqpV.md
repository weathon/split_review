## Summary

This paper studies audio-visual adversarial robustness from temporal and modality perspectives. The authors propose two novel attacks—Temporal Invariance-based Attack (TIA), which minimizes feature variance across frames to exploit temporal redundancy, and Modality Misalignment-based Attack (MMA), which disrupts cross-modal semantic alignment to improve transferability. They also propose an adversarial training defense that uses efficient sampling-based perturbation crafting and a curriculum learning strategy. On Kinetics-Sounds, the integrated TMA attack achieves 95.2% success rate across 8 audio-visual models, substantially outperforming prior baselines.

---

## Strengths

1. **Novel attack methods with clear empirical superiority.** TIA and MMA are the first audio-visual attacks explicitly designed to exploit temporal consistency and cross-modal correlation. The combined TMA attack achieves 95.2% success rate across 8 models, exceeding the best prior baseline (PAM) by a large margin (Figure 4). This is a genuine advance in audio-visual adversarial attack methodology.

2. **Systematic empirical analysis of audio-visual adversarial transferability.** Section 3 provides a thorough diagnostic study showing that temporal masking improves adversarial transferability (up to 13.8%), that modality redundancy behaves differently under synchronous vs. asynchronous perturbation, and that adversarial transferability exists across diverse audio-visual model combinations. This analysis is a valuable contribution in itself and motivates the attack design.

3. **Extensive evaluation across diverse model configurations.** Experiments cover 8 model combinations (2 visual backbones × 2 audio backbones × 2 fusion layers) under white-box, black-box, ensemble, and defended settings. The ensemble attack achieving 100% success rate for TMA on all victim models (Section 6.3) convincingly demonstrates the attack's effectiveness.

---

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Defense evaluated only against the authors' own attack (TMA).** Section 6.4 tests the defense exclusively under white-box attacks generated by TMA, which is the same attack used during adversarial training. This design cannot rule out overfitting to TMA-style perturbations; the defense's robustness against other attack types (standard PGD, FGSM, MI-FGSM) is unknown. Since defending against a diverse threat model is the standard expectation for adversarial training papers, this gap significantly weakens the defense claims.

2. **Efficiency claims are unsubstantiated.** The paper frames computational efficiency as a central challenge (Section 5.2) and proposes sampling-based perturbation crafting as the solution. However, Table 2 only reports an internal ablation of sampling ratio vs. time within the proposed method. There is no comparison of training time per epoch (or total training time) against vanilla adversarial training or any baseline defense (AT, CRMT-AT). Without this comparison, the reader cannot tell whether the method actually saves time relative to standard approaches, or whether reported time savings come at a non-trivial robustness cost.

3. **Curriculum strategy is not ablated.** The defense proposes a randomized adversarial curriculum with both data-level (cyclic sampling ratio) and model-level (dropout) components. However, no experiment isolates the curriculum's contribution: there is no comparison against a non-curriculum variant (fixed sampling ratio, no dropout). The model-level dropout strategy is never evaluated separately. Table 2 varies only the sampling ratio, not the curriculum aspect per se. It is therefore impossible to attribute any robustness gain to the curriculum design rather than the sampling-based perturbation crafting.

4. **Single-dataset evaluation limits generality.** All results are on Kinetics-Sounds (27 classes). The paper mentions MIT-MUSIC in Section 6.1 but reports no results on it. Given the paper's self-description as a "comprehensive study" of audio-visual adversarial robustness, evaluation on at least one additional dataset (AVE, MIT-MUSIC, or similar) is needed to assess whether the attack and defense findings generalize beyond the specific properties of Kinetics-Sounds.

### Minor

1. **Clean accuracy on benign data not reported after adversarial training.** Adversarial training is known to degrade natural performance. The paper reports only robustness to TMA after defense (Figure 6) without reporting benign classification accuracy for any defense method (AT, DCFL, CRMT-AT, or the proposed method). This makes it impossible to assess the natural accuracy trade-off.

2. **Input transformation details are underspecified.** The paper references "input transformation methods (scaling, masking, blurring, mix-up)" for temporal diversification (Section 4.1) but does not specify which transformations are used in practice, their parameters, or whether they are applied synchronously or asynchronously. This hinders reproducibility.

### Trivial
None.

## Removed Points

- **"Statements about concat fusion are contradictory"** (Harsh Critic, Other Observations): The paper states concat is relatively robust against single-model attacks, then reports that ensemble attacks achieve 79.9% success against it. This is not contradictory—ensemble attacks are stronger and overcome concat's relative robustness. This is a standard finding, not an inconsistency.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting insight emerging from the reviews is that while the attack contributions are clearly novel and empirically validated (95.2% success rate is a strong result), the defense side of the paper exposes a recurring pattern in the adversarial robustness literature: proposing a defense alongside a new attack and evaluating the defense only against that same attack. The defense's claimed efficiency also lacks the most basic comparative measurement. This makes the paper's two halves (attack vs. defense) very uneven in evidentiary support, which is a structural issue that a revision should fix by either substantially strengthening the defense evaluation or re-scoping the defense claims.

---

## Suggestions

1. **Add cross-attack defense evaluation.** Evaluate the proposed defense against at least FGSM, PGD-10, and MI-FGSM alongside TMA. If the defense is genuinely robust, this should hold across attack types. If it only works against TMA, this should be acknowledged and discussed.
2. **Report training time comparison.** Provide training time per epoch (or total) for vanilla AT, CRMT-AT, and the proposed method alongside final robustness. This is the minimum needed to support the efficiency claim.
3. **Ablate the curriculum.** Compare: (a) no curriculum (fixed sampling ratio, no dropout), (b) curriculum with only data-level strategy, (c) curriculum with only model-level strategy, and (d) both strategies. This is the only way to attribute robustness gains to the curriculum.
4. **Add a second dataset.** Report at least one result on MIT-MUSIC (already mentioned in Section 6.1) or AVE to support generality. Even a subset of the main experiments on a second dataset would substantially strengthen the claims.
5. **Report clean accuracy** for all defense methods to allow assessment of the natural performance trade-off.

---

## Score and Decision

This paper makes a solid contribution on the attack side—TIA and MMA are genuinely novel, well-motivated, and achieve clear SOTA on Kinetics-Sounds. The defense contribution, however, is not adequately validated: it lacks cross-attack evaluation, efficiency comparison, curriculum ablation, and a second dataset. The paper is not fatally flawed (the attacks alone are publishable), but the defense claims need substantial additional evidence.
