## Summary

This paper introduces Collaborative Speculative Decoding (CoSD), a decoding-time algorithm that fuses knowledge from two LLMs by repurposing the speculative decoding framework. A draft model generates tokens; an assistant model verifies them in parallel; and when the tokens differ, a simple confidence-based rule (or a trained decision tree on the two confidence scores) decides whether to replace the draft token. The method requires no additional training beyond an optional small decision tree, operates on token probabilities (not hidden states), and is evaluated on MMLU, GSM8K, and HumanEval across six model pairs spanning complementary knowledge, catastrophic forgetting recovery, capacity imbalance, and different-tokenizer scenarios.

## Strengths

- **Novel reframing of speculative decoding for knowledge fusion.** Prior work treated speculative decoding purely as an efficiency technique (reproducing the target model's distribution). CoSD is the first to explicitly use the draft-assistant disagreement signal and confidence scores as a decision mechanism for token-level knowledge fusion. This is a genuine conceptual advance over prior fusion methods that require training routers, averaging probabilities, or accessing hidden states (Section 2, Section 3).

- **Consistent accuracy gains across diverse fusion scenarios.** CoSD-Rule (the simple threshold-based variant) outperforms Speculative Decoding, Average Decoding, and Co-LLM on complementary knowledge fusion (e.g., Pair 1 MMLU: CoSD-Rule 56.97 vs. Co-LLM 55.21), catastrophic forgetting recovery, and different-tokenizer settings where it substantially outperforms CharED (Table 3). The method does not simply interpolate — it typically matches or exceeds the better model on each benchmark in complementary settings, demonstrating genuine knowledge integration.

- **Interpretable and resource-light.** Rule-Based CoSD uses two interpretable thresholds (α=0.5, β=0.5) and requires no training. Tree-Based CoSD trains on as few as 3–10 samples. The method only requires token-level probabilities (not hidden states), making it applicable through API access where probabilities are exposed.

### Major

- **Factual error in the catastrophic forgetting recovery claim.** The paper states (Section 4.2): "It not only recovers from forgetting across all benchmarks but also outperforms both the draft and assistant models on MMLU *and GSM8K*." According to the specific numbers the harsh critic reports from Table 2 (Pair 3 GSM8K: Draft 31.02, Assistant 56.86, CoSD-Rule 56.10), CoSD-Rule falls *below* the assistant model on GSM8K — directly contradicting the paper's own claim. The authors must either correct the text or provide alternative evidence that the claim holds.

- **Decision tree evaluation uses implausibly small training sets.** The paper trains decision trees on 3 samples from AlpacaEval (Section 4.1) and 10 samples of MMLU / 3 samples of other datasets (Table 4). A binary classifier with two input features trained on 3–10 samples has essentially no statistical reliability — no cross-validation, no variance estimation, and high risk of trivial/arbitrary splits. The paper treats Tree-Based CoSD as a major variant and draws conclusions about domain adaptation and transferability from it (e.g., "the decision tree trained on different datasets performs relatively consistently"). Without justification for why such tiny training sets yield meaningful trees, or results with larger, properly validated training sets, the tree-based experiments cannot be taken as reliable evidence. This is particularly concerning because the paper uses tree results to support conclusions about domain specificity vs. transferability.

### Minor

- **Overstated efficiency claim.** The paper states "the increase in total latency is almost negligible" (Section 4.2). According to the latency numbers reported in Table 5 (which I cannot independently verify from the parsed text but which the harsh critic provides as CoSD-Rule 1.57, CoSD-Tree 1.92 vs. Speculative Decoding 1.40), CoSD-Tree may incur a substantially larger overhead than SD — in the range of 30%+. Calling a 30%+ increase "almost negligible" is misleading. The authors should qualify which variant they refer to and temper the language.

- **No uncertainty quantification.** The paper uses tinyBenchmarks for MMLU and GSM8K, which are specifically designed for efficiency precisely because they use very small sample sizes. Yet no confidence intervals, standard errors, or bootstrap intervals are reported for any result. Given the small evaluation sets and the small decision tree training sets, readers cannot assess result stability. Single-point scores without error bars are a significant omission for an empirical paper.

- **No analysis of the relationship between confidence and correctness.** The core decision rule replaces tokens when the draft is uncertain and the assistant is relatively confident — yet the paper never analyzes whether this heuristic actually tracks correctness across the tested models. Does higher confidence correlate with correctness on these benchmarks? How often does the rule pick correctly vs. incorrectly? Providing even a simple breakdown would substantially strengthen the paper's justification for the rule design.

- **Practical limitation about API probability availability not acknowledged.** The conclusion claims CoSD enables fusion "with only the API queries," but many commercial APIs do not expose per-token probabilities (or do so only for specific tiers/models). This practical constraint is not mentioned in the Limitations section (Section 6), which only covers two other limitations.

- **No efficiency analysis for the different-tokenizer setting.** Table 3 shows accuracy improvements over CharED for different-tokenizer fusion, but no latency/throughput numbers are reported for this setting. The decode-encode step across tokenizers introduces non-trivial overhead. The paper should either report this cost or acknowledge the gap.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation from the reviews is the tension between the paper's two verification strategies: the Rule-Based variant (simple confidence thresholds) consistently outperforms the Tree-Based variant on math and coding tasks, while the Tree-Based variant (trained on 3 AlpacaEval samples) only shows an edge on MMLU (general QA). This pattern — if the tree results can be trusted — suggests that the decision tree's limited training data may actually be a liability for domain-specific tasks, and the general-purpose human-readable rules are more robust. This is an unusual finding that flips the typical expectation that learned models outperform fixed rules, and it merits explicit discussion in the paper.
