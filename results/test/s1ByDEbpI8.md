## Summary

This paper introduces Skills-in-Context (SKiC), a prompting strategy that teaches LLMs to explicitly ground each reasoning step onto foundational skills by providing both a skill list and compositionally grounded exemplars within the same prompt context. On compositional generalization benchmarks (last letter concatenation, addition, dynamic programming), SKiC achieves near-perfect accuracy on out-of-distribution harder instances where existing prompting methods (CoT, Least-to-Most, decomposed prompting) degrade sharply. On complex math reasoning (MATH, GSM8K), SKiC provides consistent 6–9% absolute improvements over strong baselines. The paper further demonstrates that SKiC can activate internal pre-trained skills not present in the prompt, is robust to exemplar choices, and that fine-tuning with SKiC-style data can improve weak-to-strong generalization.

## Strengths

- **Near-perfect systematic generalization on compositional tasks**: Across last letter concatenation, addition, and dynamic programming, SKiC achieves near-100% accuracy on increasingly harder OOD instances with ChatGPT and GPT-4, while CoT, Least-to-Most, and decomposed prompting all degrade quickly (Figure 3, lines 93–97). This is the paper's strongest evidence and directly supports the central claim about unlocking compositionality.

- **Consistent and meaningful gains on complex math reasoning (MATH)**: SKiC outperforms the strongest comparable baselines (ComplexCoT, CoT) by 6–9 absolute percentage points on both ChatGPT (40.6% vs. 34.1% for ComplexCoT) and GPT-4 (56.4% vs. 50.3%), with improvements across all seven sub-domains (Table 1, lines 129–141). These gains hold without ensemble methods, demonstrating that the prompt structure itself drives improvement.

- **Robustness to exemplar choices and orders**: SKiC maintains 100% accuracy across three different exemplar sets on last letter concatenation (including non-word tokens like 'ashc, edhoh'), while CoT fluctuates between 90.8% and 92.5% (Table 5, lines 222–228). On GSM8K, SKiC is stable across three random exemplar orders (86.9–87.8%) while CoT varies from 73.0% to 74.4% (Table 6, lines 230–239).

- **Cross-task transferability**: Prompts designed for GSM8K under SKiC improve performance on unseen tasks MATH (31.3% vs. 28.2%) and FOLIO (72.5% vs. 68.8%) compared to the same prompts with CoT structure (Table 4, lines 193–200). This shows the benefit generalizes beyond the task for which skills were constructed.

- **Ablation confirms both skills and grounding are essential**: Removing either the skill list or the explicit skill grounding from the SKiC prompt causes a drop of 4–16 points in dynamic programming accuracy (Table 2, lines 205–214), validating the core design principle.

- **Skills can be discovered automatically by LLMs**: On RTE and last letter tasks, SKiC using skills automatically generated by ChatGPT achieves performance identical to hand-crafted skills (89.8% and 100%, Table 3, lines 159–169), reducing the human effort requirement.

- **Self-consistency in skill source improves performance**: Using skills generated by ChatGPT yields higher accuracy (40.6%) and internal skill activation rate (14.9%) than using skills from the stronger GPT-4 (38.9%, 12.5%) (Table 7, lines 172–191), revealing an alignment benefit between the skill-generating and answer-generating models.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
None that threaten the core claims. The paper's primary evidence—near-perfect OOD generalization on compositional tasks—is clean and well-supported.

### Minor

- **"Near-perfect systematic generalization" framing is slightly broader than what the evidence supports.** The abstract states "near-perfect systematic generalization across a broad range of tasks." The paper correctly limits the near-perfect claim to last letter concatenation, addition, and dynamic programming in the body (line 95), and explicitly acknowledges that multiplication, QA, GSM8K, and MATH are "still far away from (nearly) perfect generalization" (line 253). However, a reader encountering only the abstract could reasonably expect near-perfect results across all evaluated tasks, including MATH (where best accuracy is 56.4%). The paper would benefit from qualifying this claim more precisely in the abstract.

- **The internal skill activation rate detection method is not described.** The paper defines this metric as "the percentage of skills utilized in the generated reasoning steps that originates from pre-trained knowledge (rather than being introduced in the SKiC prompt)" (line 152) and provides concrete examples (Figure 2, line 48). However, the detection protocol—how skills are identified in generated text, whether by string matching, semantic matching, or human annotation—is never specified. This is a gap for a metric used to support one of the paper's most distinctive claims (that SKiC unlocks latent pre-trained skills). The detection method likely belongs in the appendix; the main text should at minimum reference the protocol.

- **The fine-tuning experiment (Section 4) is under-reported.** While the paper correctly specifies that both CoT and SKiC training data come from GPT-4 ("also by GPT4," line 275), it does not report training hyperparameters, model sizes used, dataset sizes, or whether the improvement is statistically significant. The figure (Figure 6, line 263) is a bar chart without numerical labels in the text. This experiment is positioned as a key "beyond ICL" contribution but currently provides only suggestive evidence.

- **MATH results could be presented with single-run alongside ensemble numbers for clearer interpretation.** Table 1 includes both non-ensemble results (SKiC, CoT, ComplexCoT) and ensemble results (SC, CSV, PHP, maj1@k) in the same table, which can be confusing. The non-ensemble comparisons are the cleanest evidence of SKiC's effect and should be foregrounded more clearly.

- **The robustness evaluation is conducted on only two tasks (last letter and GSM8K) with three variations each** (Tables 5–6, lines 222–239). While results are consistent, this is a narrow test for a general claim about robustness.

### Trivial
- The Elaborative Rehearsal reference (line 43) is mentioned only once as a brief analogy and is not developed further; it could be removed or expanded.
- The error analysis (Section 3.4) is based on 50 sampled errors per task, which is a reasonable qualitative sample but should be noted as illustrative rather than statistically rigorous.

## Nice-to-Haves
- A controlled ablation that compares SKiC against a prompt with the same skill list but standard CoT-style exemplars (no tags, no explicit grounding in the reasoning) would further isolate the grounding mechanism—though the existing "- skill grounding" ablation (98% → 82%) already strongly suggests grounding is the active ingredient.
- Providing human evaluation or inter-annotator agreement for the internal skill classification would strengthen confidence in the activation rate metric.
- A small-scale experiment on a domain with a larger skill set (e.g., a subset of BIG-bench requiring diverse procedural skills) would test whether SKiC scales gracefully.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting finding beyond the paper's own contributions is the "self-consistency in skill source" result (Table 7): using skills generated by ChatGPT itself outperforms using skills from the stronger GPT-4 (40.6% vs. 38.9%, with higher internal activation 14.9% vs. 12.5%). This suggests an alignment or "shared ontology" effect — a model can more effectively ground reasoning steps onto skills expressed in its own conceptual vocabulary than onto skills expressed by a different (even stronger) model. This has practical implications for automated prompt engineering pipelines and points toward a deeper principle about the match between how skills are specified and how they are retrieved during generation.

## Suggestions

- **Qualify the abstract's "near-perfect" claim** to explicitly note that this applies to the compositional generalization tasks and does not extend to complex math reasoning (MATH/GSM8K), where SKiC provides meaningful but bounded improvements.
- **Document the internal skill activation detection protocol** (string matching? semantic parsing? human annotation?) in the main paper or clearly reference the appendix section that describes it. Include a few examples of detected vs. rejected skill references to validate the metric.
- **Add training details to the fine-tuning experiment**: model sizes, training hyperparameters, dataset sizes, and ideally confidence intervals or multiple seeds. A length-controlled baseline (matching token counts between CoT and SKiC training data) would rule out trivial explanations.
- **Move ensemble rows in Table 1 to a separate table** or clearly separate them, so the non-ensemble SKiC vs. baseline comparisons are immediately legible.
- **Expand robustness testing** to at least one more diverse task (e.g., a semantic parsing or logical reasoning benchmark) to strengthen the generality claims.

## Score and Decision

The paper presents a well-motivated prompting strategy with strong empirical evidence on compositional generalization tasks. The core finding—that explicit grounding of reasoning steps onto skills, demonstrated in context, enables near-perfect OOD generalization where existing methods fail—is clearly supported by Figure 3 and the ablations. The MATH and GSM8K improvements, while not near-perfect, are consistent and meaningful. The paper's weaknesses are limited to framing imprecision in the abstract, one under-described metric, and a thin fine-tuning section—none of which undermine the central contribution. The approach is simple, practical, and likely to be adopted.
