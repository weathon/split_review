## Summary

This paper proposes PromptAttack, a method that converts adversarial textual attacks into a structured prompt (Original Input + Attack Objective + Attack Guidance) that causes a victim LLM to generate its own adversarial examples — effectively having the LLM "fool itself." The authors evaluate on six GLUE tasks across GPT-3.5, Llama2-7B, and Llama2-13B, reporting substantially higher attack success rates (ASR) than the transfer-based AdvGLUE and AdvGLUE++ datasets. Additional contributions include a fidelity filter (word modification ratio + BERTScore), few-shot and ensemble strategies to boost attack power, and transferability analysis between models.

## Strengths

1. **Novel self-attack paradigm via prompt engineering.** The core idea — repurposing the victim LLM to generate its own adversarial examples through a structured prompt — is genuinely novel and practically useful. The three-component prompt (OI, AO, AG) is clearly formalized in Section 3.1 with templates and perturbation instructions (Table 2). This eliminates the need for surrogate model transfer and enables direct adaptive evaluation.

2. **Consistent and large-margin improvements over transfer-based baselines.** Across all six GLUE tasks and three victim LLMs, PromptAttack (especially with ensemble) achieves substantially higher ASR than AdvGLUE and AdvGLUE++. For example, on GPT-3.5 the average ASR rises from 25.51% (AdvGLUE) to 48.34% (PromptAttack-FS-EN), and on SST-2 from 33.04% to 75.23% (Table 1). This validates the claim that adaptive attacks are more effective at uncovering LLM vulnerabilities than static transfer datasets.

3. **Rigorous fidelity analysis.** The paper provides a thorough investigation of ASR across varying BERTScore thresholds τ₂ (Figure 3), showing PromptAttack maintains high ASR even at strict fidelity requirements (e.g., τ₂=0.95 on QNLI) where baselines drop below 10%. This confirms the attack produces semantically-preserving adversarial examples.

4. **Systematic ablation by perturbation level and transferability.** The breakdown of ASR by character/word/sentence level perturbations (Table 3) shows sentence-level attacks (e.g., S3 with few-shot) are most effective, and the transferability analysis (Tables 5–6) demonstrates that PromptAttack-generated examples transfer to other LLMs — adversarial samples against GPT-3.5 achieve 73.84% average ASR on Llama2-13B — validating broader utility.

5. **Evaluation across multiple task descriptions.** By testing zero-shot/few-shot and task-oriented/role-oriented prompts (Table 4 on MNLI-mm), the paper provides evidence that PromptAttack robustly outperforms baselines regardless of prompt format, and identifies that role-oriented descriptions can serve as a defense — an actionable insight.

### Minor

1. **The BERTScore threshold τ₂ for PromptAttack in the main results (Table 1) is not specified.** The configuration paragraph (Section 4, line 254) states τ₁ values for PromptAttack (15% for char/word level, 1.0 for sentence level) but does not report τ₂. Both baselines have τ₂ explicitly stated (AdvGLUE: τ₂=0.0, AdvGLUE++: τ₂=0.0). Since τ₂ directly controls semantic fidelity and affects ASR, omitting it for the paper's primary results undermines reproducibility. This is the most significant experimental detail gap. (Note: the paper does analyze ASR vs. τ₂ in Figure 3, but those results use τ₁=1.0, not the τ₁ values from Table 1, so they do not resolve the ambiguity.)

2. **The few-shot example generation procedure is not described.** The paper states (Section 3.4) that it uses m=5 examples {(eⁱ, ẽⁱ)} per perturbation instruction, where each pair consists of an original sentence and its perturbed version "that fits the corresponding perturbation instruction." The paper does not explain whether these examples were manually written, automatically generated by an algorithm, or produced by the LLM itself in a preparatory step. This missing detail affects reproducibility and makes it impossible to isolate the contribution of the few-shot component.

3. **No comparison against simpler adaptive baselines or ablation of the prompt components.** The paper compares only against transfer-based datasets (AdvGLUE, AdvGLUE++). It does not benchmark against a simpler adaptive baseline — e.g., a prompt like "Generate a new sentence with the same meaning that will be misclassified" without the structured OI/AO/AG design, or a non-prompt-based black-box attack (such as repeated querying with TextFooler-style perturbations). Without such comparisons, it is unclear whether the specific three-component prompt architecture contributes beyond the general observation that LLMs can be prompted to modify text. Similarly, ablating the components (e.g., removing OI, AO, or AG individually) would directly test their necessity. These are the most impactful missing experiments.

4. **The emoji finding is supported by only a single example.** The paper reports (Figure 2, Section 1) that appending "':)'" flips GPT-3.5's prediction from negative to positive on SST-2. The abstract and introduction present this as an "interesting finding." However, no systematic statistics are provided — e.g., what fraction of SST-2 samples this works on, how it varies across tasks or models. Without quantification, this remains an anecdotal curiosity rather than a systematic finding.

5. **Query cost is not quantified.** The paper claims PromptAttack "only requires a few queries through the victim LLM" (Section 1), but does not report the exact per-data-point query budget. The ensemble strategy queries the LLM for each of nine perturbation instructions and then runs a forward pass on each candidate — roughly 9 generation + 9 classification queries per point. Reporting this explicitly and showing the trade-off between number of perturbation instructions and ASR would strengthen the efficiency claims.

6. **The transferability asymmetry is noted but not discussed.** Attacks generated on GPT-3.5 transfer to Llama2-13B with 73.84% ASR (Table 5), while attacks generated on Llama2-7B transfer to GPT-3.5 with only 38.15% ASR (Table 6). This suggests model-capability-dependent specificity in the generated examples. The paper does not discuss this asymmetry, which is relevant to the claim that PromptAttack serves as a general auditing tool.

### Trivial

- The paper could report the fraction of generated samples rejected by the fidelity filter, to help readers assess the cost of enforcing fidelity.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Component ablation.** Testing what happens when OI, AO, or AG is individually removed from the attack prompt would directly validate the necessity of the three-part design.
- **Comparison against a simple adaptive baseline.** A prompt like "Generate a new sentence with the same meaning that will be misclassified. Only output the new sentence" (without the structured AG) would test whether the elaborate prompt structure is needed.
- **Quantifying the diminishing returns of the ensemble strategy** (ASR vs. number of perturbation instructions tried) would be informative.

## Novel Insights

The reviews surface one genuinely underexplored finding: the pronounced asymmetry in transferability (GPT-3.5→Llama2: 73.84% vs. Llama2→GPT-3.5: 38.15%) suggests that adversarial examples generated by stronger models expose vulnerabilities in weaker ones far more effectively than vice versa. This has practical implications for auditing — if the goal is to surface failure modes across a model family, generating adversarial examples from the strongest available model may be more productive. The paper currently notes the numbers but does not extract this implication.
