## Summary

MetaPhysiCa proposes a meta-learning framework that combines causal structure discovery over a library of basis functions with an IRM-type (V-REx) penalty to improve out-of-distribution (OOD) robustness in forecasting parametric ODE dynamical systems. The method learns a shared sparse causal graph (which basis functions determine each derivative component) across training tasks with different parameters and initial conditions, then adapts only task-specific coefficients at test time. Empirical results on three synthetic OOD forecasting tasks show 2× to 28× lower OOD normalized RMSE compared to five baselines (NeuralODE, DyAd, CoDA, APHYNITY, SINDy).

## Strengths

- **Large and consistent OOD error reduction across multiple systems**: MetaPhysiCa achieves 2× to 28× lower OOD NRMSE than the best baseline on damped pendulum, predator-prey, and epidemic models, under both OOD-initial-condition and OOD-initial-condition-plus-parameter scenarios (Table 1; Figures 3, 6, 7; line 211). This directly substantiates the core claim.

- **Novel synthesis of causal structure discovery, meta-learning, and invariant risk minimization**: The bi-level optimization objective (Equation 4, lines 162-165) jointly learns a sparse global causal structure (Φ) via ℓ₁ regularization and a V-REx variance penalty across tasks, while adapting task-specific coefficients (W⁽ⁱ⁾). The paper provides empirical evidence that omitting either the ℓ₁ sparsity or the V-REx penalty substantially degrades OOD performance (e.g., without V-REx, OOD NRMSE is 24× worse; line 213-214). This combined approach goes beyond prior work that applies meta-learning or causal discovery separately.

- **Clear diagnosis of why existing PIML methods fail OOD**: Sections 3.1–3.2 and Figures 2–3 provide specific evidence and reasoning for the OOD failure of neural networks (linear extrapolation), transductive PIML methods like SINDy (inability to transfer across tasks with different parameters), and inductive PIML methods like APHYNITY (incorrect parameter estimation, neural network extrapolation issues). This problem analysis strengthens the motivation.

- **Theoretical guarantee for causal structure identification**: Theorem 1 (line 170) states that under the assumed SCM in Figure 4 and certain identifiability conditions, MetaPhysiCa recovers the true causal graph. This provides a theoretical foundation distinguishing the approach from purely empirical methods.

- **Principled test-time adaptation that preserves the learned structure**: During test, only task-specific parameters W⁽ᵗᵉ⁾ are adapted using initial observations, while the learned causal structure Φ̂ and basis-function parameters ξ remain fixed (Equation 5, lines 181-183). This design explicitly targets OOD shifts in both initial conditions and ODE parameters.

### Major

- **Theorem 1 assumptions are not stated in the main text**: The paper claims the proposed approach "learns the true causal structure … under certain identifiability conditions" (line 29) but never enumerates what those conditions are in the main body. Line 172 says Theorem 1 holds "only for SCM in Figure 4," and line 120 mentions linear independence of basis functions, but the critical assumptions (e.g., sufficient variability in initial conditions and task-specific parameters across training tasks, the data actually being generated by the SCM in Figure 4 without misspecification) are left unspecified. While not fatal — many papers defer proofs to appendices — this gap makes it difficult for a reader to judge whether the identifiability conditions are satisfied in the experimental setup (noisy observations, finite samples, discrete time steps).

### Minor

- **No error bars or confidence intervals reported**: The paper reports single NRMSE values for all methods across all conditions without variability estimates. With 200 test trajectories per condition (line 203), standard errors or interquartile ranges would be inexpensive to compute and would substantially increase confidence in the reported 2×–28× improvement claims, especially given that baselines show high variance in OOD predictions (visible in Figures 3, 6, 7).

- **Synthetic evaluation with known basis functions provided to the method**: The paper's experimental design (standard in the PIML literature, e.g., SINDy) assumes the library of candidate basis functions is given and contains the true functions (line 125). While the method does not know _which_ basis functions are relevant and must discover this, the paper does not examine sensitivity to an imperfect library — e.g., missing a relevant basis function or including many irrelevant ones. Since the claimed improvement over baselines (2×–28×) is large, it would strengthen the paper to probe whether similar advantages hold when the library is imperfect.

- **Ablation results are mostly deferred to the appendix**: The main text states key ablation findings qualitatively (e.g., "without V-REx, OOD NRMSE is 24× worse," lines 213-214), which is helpful, but detailed ablation tables and the fully-neural (no basis functions) variant appear only in the appendix. Including a concise summary table (even one column) of the main ablation conditions in the main body would make the paper more self-contained and directly support the claim that each component is necessary.

- **Baseline hyperparameter tuning not discussed**: The paper does not state whether baseline hyperparameters were tuned for in-distribution or OOD performance, or whether they were tuned separately per dynamical system. This is a minor transparency issue.
