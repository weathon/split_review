## Summary

This paper proposes GazeReward, a framework that integrates synthetic eye-tracking (ET) features — generated by frozen off-the-shelf ET prediction models — into reward modeling for LLM alignment. The ET features are projected via a learned MLP and combined with text embeddings through either concatenation (GazeConcat) or elementwise addition (GazeAdd). Evaluated on OASST1, HelpSteer2, and RewardBench across Llama-3-8B, Llama-3-8B-Instruct, and Mistral-7B, the method reports consistent accuracy improvements over QLoRA-trained baselines, with many configurations achieving double-digit relative gains.

## Strengths

1. **First integration of eye-tracking as implicit feedback into reward modeling.** The paper explicitly notes in Related Work (line 264) that "no research has explored the integration of ET or other implicit feedback signals into RM," establishing novelty. The GazeReward framework is clearly described (Section 3, Figures 1 and 2), and this represents a genuinely new direction for improving reward models using cognitive signals rather than additional annotation.

2. **Extensive ablation across multiple dimensions.** The evaluation covers 3 LLMs, 2 ET prediction models, 3 feature combinations, and 2 integration methods on 2 training datasets plus a held-out benchmark (RewardBench). Tables 3, 4, and 5 substantiate this breadth. This goes well beyond a single proof-of-concept experiment and gives a nuanced picture of when and how ET features help.

3. **Consistent improvements across nearly all configurations.** The gains are large (many in the 15–25% relative range) and appear consistently across datasets and model initializations. Importantly, some ET configurations (notably GazeAdd with `fcomb_{2.5}`) actually *hurt* performance (Table 3: 51.9% vs 64.6% baseline), which provides internal evidence that the improvements are tied to the specific ET signal rather than merely the additional capacity — a meaningful sanity check.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Missing control for the extra-parameter confound.** The GazeReward models add a multi-layer MLP projector, two learned special tokens (GazeConcat), and additional operations not present in the baseline. The paper compares "with vs. without ET embeddings" but this confounds adding ET features with adding trainable capacity. A critical ablation — feeding random/shuffled/null ET features through the same projector and integration pipeline — is absent. Without it, the claim that *ET information itself* drives the gains is not fully disentangled from "more parameters help." **Why this is major**: It is the single largest threat to the paper's central claim. The paper's internal evidence (some ET configurations underperform baseline despite having more parameters) mitigates this concern but does not eliminate it. A random-features control would decisively settle the question.

2. **Viability of synthetic ET features on LLM-generated text is unvalidated.** Both ET prediction models were trained on standard reading corpora (Dundee, GECO, ZuCo, PROVO) containing natural text. The input domain here is prompt–LLM-response pairs, which differ in genre, structure, and likely reading patterns. No validation (qualitative or quantitative) is provided to show the predicted gaze features are meaningful on this domain. The paper mentions collecting real ET data on LLM responses as future work (limitations), but this is a limitation that constrains the current claims. **Why this is major (but partially addressed)**: The authors acknowledge this gap in the Limitations section, but it remains unresolved in the present work. If the predicted features are noisy or capture spurious correlations (e.g., word length patterns), the claimed mechanism weakens.

### Minor

3. **Low baselines inflate relative improvement percentages.** The baselines (trained with QLoRA for 2 epochs) achieve only 53.2–58.6% on HelpSteer2 and 58.6–64.6% on OASST1. These are substantially below what full fine-tuning would achieve. The paper's claim of "accuracy gains of over 20%" is in *relative* terms, and the absolute gains are 6–15 percentage points. While the controlled comparison (same training setup with/without ET) is internally valid, the framing is somewhat aggressive — the benefit on a properly trained baseline remains unknown. The paper should be more forthright about this limitation in its headline claims.

4. **No confidence intervals or statistical significance.** Given test set sizes of only 364–416 pairs, accuracy estimates could shift by several points due to random variation. Standard practice for RM training is to report results across multiple seeds. This is not fatal but weakens the precision of the reported numbers.

5. **Mistral-7B excluded from GazeAdd without explanation.** Tables 2 and 3 show dashes for Mistral-7B in all GazeAdd rows, but the paper never explains why. This could be due to tokenizer incompatibility, computational constraints, or poor results — any of which would be worth stating for completeness.

6. **Chosen hyperparameter values per model are not reported.** The paper describes the grid search ranges (batch size {8,16,32}, LR {1,5,10,50}e-6, schedulers constant/linear/cosine) but does not state which values were selected for each model. This is a reproducibility gap, though the ranges are clearly documented.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting signal emerging from the reviews is the internal evidence that *some* ET feature combinations *hurt* performance (GazeAdd + fcomb_{2.5} on OASST1 drops from 64.6% to 51.9%), while others with the same architecture but different features improve it. This asymmetric pattern — if robust — is actually a stronger argument for the ET signal being meaningful than uniform gains would be, because it suggests the features themselves carry task-relevant (or irrelevant) information. This observation is implicit in the paper's results but not exploited as evidence against the capacity confound.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a control: train the same GazeReward architecture with randomly shuffled ET features (or all-zeros) and compare.** This directly addresses the most serious weakness and would either confirm or refute the core claim.
2. **Report multi-seed runs (at least 3) with mean and std for the main comparisons.**
3. **Explain the Mistral-7B GazeAdd exclusion.**
4. **Report selected hyperparameters in a brief table or footnote.**
5. **Soften the "over 20% improvement" framing to clarify it is relative improvement over a QLoRA-trained baseline** (e.g., "relative gains of 20%+ over comparable low-resource baselines").

## Score and Decision

The paper proposes a genuinely novel and interesting direction. The core idea — injecting cognitive signals into reward modeling — is well-motivated, the ablation is impressively broad, and the results are consistent. However, the most serious weakness (the missing control for the capacity confound) prevents the central claim from being fully supported in the current form. This is addressable with additional experiments, and the paper provides partial internal evidence that mitigates the concern (some ET configurations with extra parameters *underperform* the baseline). With the control experiment added and the minor issues cleaned up, this could be a solid contribution.
