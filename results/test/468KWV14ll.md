## Summary

This paper identifies "bonus inconsistency" in Random Network Distillation (RND) and proposes Distributional RND (DRND), which distills a distribution of multiple random target networks rather than a single one. DRND derives two bonus terms: a squared-error bonus b₁ (reducing initial inconsistency) and a pseudo-count bonus b₂ (better aligning with visitation frequency after training). Theoretical analysis (Lemmas 1–3) shows that increasing the number of target networks N reduces initial bonus variance, and that a statistic of the predictor is an unbiased estimator of 1/n. Experiments span online exploration (Atari, Adroit, Fetch) and offline anti-exploration (D4RL MuJoCo + Antmaze).

## Strengths

- **Novel identification of RND's bonus inconsistency and a principled remedy**: The paper clearly articulates both initial and final bonus inconsistency in RND and shows that using multiple target networks reduces the initial bonus variance (Lemma 1–2). The toy experiment (Figure 3) provides clean empirical support for this theoretical insight.

- **Novel derivation of pseudo-counts from the predictor statistics (Lemma 3)**: Showing that y = (f_*² − μ²)/(B₂ − μ²) is an unbiased estimator of 1/n is a genuine theoretical contribution. It connects RND-style curiosity bonuses with count-based exploration without requiring an auxiliary density model.

- **Quantitative evidence of better bonus alignment (Tables 1–2)**: DRND achieves substantially lower KL divergence against both the uniform distribution (0.0070 vs. 0.0377) and the 1/√n distribution (0.0476 vs. 0.0946) compared to RND. This directly supports the claim that DRND produces better-calibrated intrinsic rewards.

- **Broad empirical scope**: The paper evaluates DRND in both online exploration (Atari, Adroit, Fetch) and offline anti-exploration (D4RL), demonstrating versatility. In offline tasks, SAC-DRND achieves the highest average score (86.0) among ensemble-free methods compared to SAC-RND (82.6).

- **Clean connections to prior methods as special cases**: DRND reduces to standard RND when α=1, N=1, and connects to the pseudo-count method CFN when α=0 with Rademacher targets, providing a unifying perspective.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **The pseudo-count bonus b₂ uses an uncharacterized nonlinear transformation.** Lemma 3 shows that y is an unbiased estimator of 1/n. However, the actual bonus term b₂ = √y estimates √(1/n), and by Jensen's inequality, E[√y] ≠ √(1/n) = √E[y]. The bias is largest when n is small — exactly the regime where the paper acknowledges high variance but does not address bias. Without theoretical or empirical characterization of this bias, the claim that b₂ "recovers" or "estimates" √(1/n) is unsubstantiated in the sense of statistical estimation.

- **No ablation separating b₁ and b₂; no sensitivity analysis for α and N; specific hyperparameter values not stated in the main text.** The paper combines b₁ and b₂ with a fixed α based on the observation that b₁ decays faster than b₂ during training. This is an empirical observation, not a derived guarantee — the relative decay rates depend on architecture, optimization, and environment. The specific values of α and N used in the experiments are not stated in the main text. No ablation study is provided to attribute improvements to b₁ vs. b₂, and no sensitivity analysis is given for α or N. Without this, the improvement over RND could be driven by a specific choice of α tuned per environment rather than by the method's intrinsic merit.

- **Limited bridge from linear theory to deep networks.** Lemmas 1–2 assume linear functions f_θ(x) = θᵀx. The paper concludes that "the expected maximal difference is modulated by N" and that this "substantiates that DRND exhibits lower bonus inconsistency." No bridge argument (e.g., relating to the neural tangent kernel or wide-network limits) is provided. The toy one-hot experiment (Figure 3) supports the trend but does not validate the mechanism on the high-dimensional image spaces used in the online Atari experiments.

### Minor

- **No statistical significance reported for online experiments.** The Atari, Adroit, and Fetch results (Figures 4–6) show mean curves over only 5 seeds with overlapping confidence bands. No formal statistical tests (e.g., Mann-Whitney U, matched pairs) are provided, making it difficult to assess whether the improvements are reliable.

- **KL divergence experiments are on synthetic data, not actual training state distributions.** Tables 1 and 2 report KL divergence on randomly generated dataset distributions, not on the state distributions the agent actually encounters during online training. While informative as a diagnostic, this does not directly demonstrate that the improved bonus alignment translates to better exploration behavior.

- **Computational cost not reported.** DRND requires N forward passes per state through the frozen target networks (vs. 1 for RND). The paper states "no additional backpropagation" and "comparable" efficiency, but no wall-clock time, FLOP, or throughput comparison is provided. For large N, this overhead could be non-trivial.

### Trivial

- The bonus inconsistency concept is described qualitatively rather than given a formal mathematical definition, making precise replication of the diagnostic harder.
- The CFN connection is stated as an "alignment" but the mapping is approximate (b₂ becomes |f_θ| rather than the prediction error used in CFN).

## Suggestions

1. **Characterize the bias of b₂ = √y as an estimator of √(1/n).** A simple empirical plot of E[√y] vs. √(1/n) across n values, or a second-order Taylor expansion of the bias, would either validate or delimit the b₂ design. This is the most technically novel claim and needs stronger support.

2. **Add ablations and hyperparameter sensitivity as described above.** This is essential to demonstrate that the method's improvement is not driven by a hyperparameter choice.

3. **State the specific α and N values used in each experiment in the main text.** Even a brief sentence — e.g., "We use α = 0.5 and N = 10 for all experiments" — would resolve a serious reproducibility gap.

4. **Report statistical significance** (e.g., Mann-Whitney U p-values or effect sizes) for the online results, or increase the number of seeds.
