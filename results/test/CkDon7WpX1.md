## Summary

This paper introduces CLML (Consistent Lebesgue Measure-based Multi-label Learner), a method that directly optimizes multiple non-convex and discontinuous multi-label loss functions (Hamming loss, 1−label ranking average precision, 1−micro F1) using the Lebesgue measure (hypervolume) from multi-objective optimization, avoiding surrogate loss functions. The paper claims a theoretical proof that maximizing the Lebesgue measure is consistent with converging to the Bayes risk for each loss, and provides empirical results on nine tabular multi-label datasets showing that a simple feedforward network trained with this objective achieves competitive or state-of-the-art performance against methods that use more complex architectures with explicit label-interaction modeling.

## Strengths

- **Novel application of the Lebesgue measure to multi-label learning without surrogates.** The idea of treating multiple multi-label loss functions as objectives in a multi-objective framework and directly optimizing them via the hypervolume (Lebesgue measure) is a creative and well-motivated approach to the known inconsistency problem between surrogate and target losses in multi-label learning. The paper correctly identifies this gap and proposes a principled alternative.

- **Competitive empirical performance with a simpler architecture.** CLML achieves the best aggregate critical difference ranking (2.50) across all measures and 9 datasets, outperforming DELA (2.90), CLIF (3.02), and other methods by margins of 13.79%–58.33% in rank improvement. These results are obtained with a simple feedforward network without label graphs, perturbation-based conditioning, or semantic embeddings—architectural components that state-of-the-art competitors rely on—which supports the paper's claim that the Lebesgue measure itself drives performance.

- **Empirical demonstration of surrogate-target loss discrepancy.** The training curves (Figure 4) show that CLML minimizes all three target losses in tandem while the binary cross-entropy loss (a standard surrogate) increases near convergence, providing concrete evidence that surrogate minimization does not align with target loss minimization—a key motivation for the paper's approach.

### Fatal

### Major

- **The proof of consistency (Theorem 2) is incomplete and does not establish the claimed result.** The proof shows that the Pareto optimal set P^B contains Bayes predictors for each individual loss (an existence claim), then asserts that maximizing the Lebesgue measure is "eventually consistent" with minimizing the individual losses. This misses several critical steps: (1) it does not establish that maximizing λ(H(F^(n),R)) drives F^(n) toward P^B in the first place; (2) it does not connect convergence of the Lebesgue measure value to convergence of individual loss functions toward their Bayes risks; (3) it says nothing about the limiting behavior of the sequence F^(n) generated by CMA-ES. The proof conflates the static concept of Pareto optimality (all Bayes predictors are in P^B) with the dynamic concept of convergence (maximizing λ drives losses toward Bayes risks) without bridging this gap. This is the paper's centrally advertised contribution—appearing in the title, abstract, and contributions list—and it is not supported by the presented argument. The authors should either provide a rigorous proof or honestly downgrade the theoretical claim.

- **The empirical evaluation lacks the controlled baseline needed to isolate the Lebesgue measure's effect.** The paper claims that "the primary performance factor is the Lebesgue measure design" and attributes CLML's gains to consistency rather than model complexity. However, there is no experiment that trains the same feedforward architecture with a standard surrogate loss (e.g., binary cross-entropy) and gradient-based optimization. Without this control, the observed gains could plausibly come from CMA-ES (a non-gradient optimizer with very different search behavior), the matrix-based batch processing, or the specific architecture choices—not necessarily from the Lebesgue measure itself. This weakens the paper's central empirical claim about why CLML works, though it does not invalidate the results themselves.

### Minor

- **The reference set R is initialized to {1}^3 and its impact on optimization is not discussed.** In hypervolume-based methods, the reference point critically affects search behavior. As losses improve, the relative Lebesgue contributions may become very small if the reference point stays fixed at {1}^3. The paper does not discuss whether or how this affects convergence, or whether dynamic reference-point adaptation was considered.

- **The aggregated ranking of 2.50 is reported without sufficient explanation of how rankings from different CD plots are combined.** The paper states CLML achieves "the best aggregate rank of 2.50 (aggregated among all measures and datasets presented in Figure 1)" but does not specify whether these are averaged, weighted, or otherwise aggregated, nor whether averaging ranks across different performance measures (ℒ₁, ℒ₂, ℒ₃, λ(P(f)), geometric mean) is statistically justified.
