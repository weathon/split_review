## Summary

This paper presents a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models expressive piano performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT) by disentangling note-level score content from global performance style. The framework uses a Seq2Seq formulation that avoids note-level alignment, and introduces a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone. The model achieves competitive results on both tasks on the ASAP benchmark, with clear evidence for disentanglement and reasonable subjective EPR quality.

## Strengths

1. **Novel problem formulation — first unified framework for joint EPR and APT.** Prior work treats these as separate tasks despite their inherent duality. The paper correctly identifies this gap and proposes a joint architecture with shared content representations and separate style encodings. The parallel to ASR/TTS is well-motivated.

2. **Competitive APT results without note-level alignment.** Table 1 shows that the alignment-free Seq2Seq model achieves the best scores on several MUSTER and ScoreSimilarity sub-metrics (E_extra, E_onset, E_offset, E_avg, E_dur, E_pitch), outperforming or matching alignment-dependent systems (Shibata et al. 2021, Liu et al. 2022). This is a direct demonstration that the framework can recover accurate score content without strict note-level supervision.

3. **Seq2Seq EPR matches or exceeds alignment-based baselines.** Table 3 shows Ours (PSR) achieving the highest alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%) among all EPR systems, including DExter and VirtuosoNet which require aligned data. Subjective listening tests (Figure 2) show Ours (Target) receiving the highest ratings across all attributes, confirming that sequence-level supervision alone can produce perceptually natural performances.

4. **Disentanglement of content and style is empirically validated.** Table 4 shows that classifiers using the style representation achieve substantially higher performer/composer identification accuracy (42.07% vs. 9.94% for performer; 77.46% vs. 29.99% for composer) compared to those using content. Figure 3 visualizes clear clustering by both performer and composer in the style space. This is strong evidence that the disentanglement objective is effective.

5. **PSR module generates stylistically coherent embeddings.** Figure 4 shows that 2D projections of PSR-generated style vectors from the held-out ATEPP dataset closely mirror those extracted from real performances, with similar era-wise clustering and centroid locations. Subjective results (Figure 2) further show that Ours (PSR) outperforms both DExter and VirtuosoNet on all evaluated attributes, demonstrating that automated style generation from score content alone yields perceptually competitive renderings.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **The claimed benefit of joint modeling over separate models is not demonstrated.** The paper's central contribution is a unified framework where "joint modeling... leverages the duality between the two tasks for mutual supervision." However, the ablation evidence is insufficient to separate the effect of joint modeling from additional training data. The EPR-Only baseline is trained solely on ASAP (967 performances), while the full joint model additionally trains on 75k+ unpaired scores and an unpaired performance dataset of unspecified size. The paper's own wording — "joint modeling, **together with** training on additional unpaired data, leads to better EPR performance" — acknowledges this confound but does not resolve it. There is also no APT-Only baseline trained in isolation to assess whether joint modeling helps or hurts transcription. Without a controlled ablation where EPR-Only is trained on the same total data, or where the unpaired data is removed from the joint model, the mutual-benefit narrative remains a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated result. This does not undermine the paper's other contributions (Seq2Seq EPR, disentanglement, PSR) but means the strongest advertised claim is unsupported.

2. **Style transfer evaluation is too thin to be meaningful.** Figure 5 reports subjective ratings for only three individual samples (not three pieces — three samples total). The paper does not specify the number of listeners, whether all listeners rated all samples, or the total number of responses collected. No confidence intervals or significance tests are reported for these subjective ratings. With such a small evidence base, the conclusion that "the model supports flexible style transfer" (Finding c in Conclusion) is not adequately supported. This is especially problematic since the style transfer evaluation directly supports one of the paper's four claimed findings.

### Minor

3. **Subjective EPR evaluation is on the small side.** The main listening test uses 11 participants rating 5 pieces, which is acceptable as a pilot but does not support strong conclusions — particularly for the per-composer breakdown in Figure 2b, where each cell averages ratings from at most 11 listeners. No inter-rater agreement metric (e.g., Cohen's kappa) is reported, and no significance tests accompany the subjective ratings. For a paper making claims about perceptual quality, this is a weakness, though not a fatal one given the supporting objective evidence.

4. **Several useful experimental details are missing.** These do not threaten the paper's conclusions but hurt reproducibility and thoroughness: (a) The weight of the KL term (Eq. 5) is not reported — is there a β scaling factor, and how was it chosen? (b) The size of the unpaired performance dataset (YouTube-sourced and transcribed) is not specified, while the unpaired score dataset size (75,913) is. (c) The evaluation of EPR alignment/insertion/missing rates (Table 3) requires note-level alignment, but the alignment tool or method used for this evaluation is not described. (d) The paper claims PSR generates "diverse style embeddings" but provides no explicit diversity metric (e.g., intra-score variance across multiple PSR samples).

5. **Style transfer evaluation lacks self-vs.-cross control.** The style transfer test (Figure 5) compares Original, Mean, and Target conditions. However, Sample 2 shows Target rated *lower* on similarity than Mean, and the paper offers no discussion of why this failure case occurs. Including both successful and failed cases is good practice, but the paper attributes the finding only to success. A brief analysis of failure modes would strengthen the presentation.

### Trivial

6. Minor writing and presentation issues: the paper uses "generates diverse style embeddings" in the abstract for the PSR module but does not operationally define or measure diversity. The term "sequence-aligned data" in the abstract could be clarified to avoid confusing readers about what supervision is required.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Train an EPR-Only model on the same total data (paired + unpaired) as the joint model to isolate the effect of joint modeling. Also train an APT-Only variant.
- Expand the style transfer listening test to more samples (e.g., 10-20) with clearly reported listener counts and significance testing.
- Report the KL loss weight and show a brief sensitivity analysis.
- Measure PSR diversity explicitly: for a single score, generate multiple style embeddings and report pairwise distances or resulting performance variance.
- Include a dedicated limitations section (e.g., classical piano only, style space limited to seen performers/composers).

## Removed Points

- **PSR evaluation is "circular"** (Harsh Critic Critical Issue 2): Removed because this criticism is factually incorrect. The PSR evaluation in Section 5.3 uses the held-out ATEPP dataset (5,003 performances) — the PSR module was trained on ASAP, so the evaluation tests generalization to unseen scores. Comparing PSR-generated embeddings to real performance embeddings on held-out data is a valid generalization test, not circular.
- **PSR "cannot discover new styles" / "limited to reproducing training pair statistics"**: Removed because this is speculative and misunderstands the PSR's purpose. The module is designed to generate *appropriate* styles for given scores, not to discover entirely novel style spaces. The diffusion process can sample from the learned conditional distribution, providing diversity within the learned manifold.
- **"Sequence-aligned data" terminology concern**: Removed. The paper clearly defines this in Section 3.2.
- **Absence of APT-Only baseline as fatal flaw**: Downgraded to Major (merged into weakness 1). The missing APT-Only baseline is a real gap, but it doesn't invalidate the paper — it weakens one specific claim.
- **Mask rates intuition question**: Removed. The paper justifies these as following Beyer & Dai (2024).
- **Pitch edit distance (E_p) being best by Liu et al.**: Kept as context but not a standalone weakness. The paper claims "comparable" performance, which is accurate since it leads on many other metrics.
- **Strength Finder strength #5 ("joint training with unpaired data improves EPR")**: Removed because it conflicts with verified weakness 1 — the improvement is confounded with data size and cannot be attributed specifically to joint training.

## Novel Insights

The meta-review reveals that the paper's strongest empirical contribution is not the mutual-benefit claim it advertises most prominently, but rather the demonstration that a Seq2Seq formulation with disentangled representations can match or exceed alignment-dependent EPR and APT systems on standard benchmarks. The disentanglement validation (Table 4, Figure 3) is particularly clean and well-executed — the content vs. style classification gap is striking. A secondary insight is that the PSR module's era-level clustering alignment (Figure 4) provides a template for evaluating automated style generation that avoids subjective-only evaluation. The paper would be stronger if it leaned into these demonstrated strengths rather than overclaiming the joint-modeling synergy.

## Suggestions

1. Add a controlled ablation: train the EPR-Only model with access to the same unpaired score and performance data (via masked reconstruction) but without the APT task and shared APT encoder. This would isolate whether joint modeling itself, versus simply more data, drives the improvement seen in Table 2. Similarly, add an APT-Only variant.

2. Expand the style transfer evaluation to at least 10-15 samples, report the number of listeners, compute confidence intervals, and include a brief analysis of failure cases (e.g., why Sample 2's Target rating dropped).

3. Report the KL regularization weight and consider a brief ablation on its value.

4. Report the size of the unpaired performance dataset and the estimated transcription accuracy.

5. Measure PSR diversity explicitly: sample multiple PSR outputs per score and report the mean pairwise distance in style space.

6. Add a limitations section discussing scope (classical piano only), style space coverage, and the reliance on an external transcription model for the unpaired performance dataset.
