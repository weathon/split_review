## Summary

This paper introduces the problem of *reliability scoring* for datasets whose ground truth is unobserved, but where an auxiliary observation generated by an unknown experiment is available. The authors propose the Gram determinant score — the determinant of the Gram matrix of observation distributions conditional on reported labels. They show it preserves several ground-truth-based reliability orderings (exact-match, Blackwell, approximate Hamming) under nearly tight conditions, is uniquely experiment-agnostic up to scaling, and admits plug-in and kernelized estimators. Experiments on synthetic categorical data, CIFAR-10 image embeddings, and CES employment data demonstrate monotonic relationships with corruption metrics.

## Strengths

1. **Novel problem formulation with clean formal foundation.** Sections 2.2–2.3 provide a rigorous framework for reliability scoring: misreport matrices Q capture the report–truth relationship, and three ground-truth-based partial orders (exact-match, Blackwell, dist/Hamming) serve as principled benchmarks. This allows both impossibility results and positive guarantees to be stated precisely, making the paper's theoretical contributions well-scoped.

2. **Experiment-agnosticism and uniqueness result (Proposition 4.3).** The Gram determinant score satisfies Γ(PQ) = det(PᵀP) det(Q)², which factors out the unknown experiment P. This means the reliability ranking depends only on the misreport matrix Q, not on the specific observation process. Proposition 4.3 further shows this is the unique score with this property (up to scaling) under mild continuity and homogeneity assumptions — a foundational theoretical contribution.

3. **Nearly tight preservation guarantees (Theorem 4.2).** The score preserves exact-match ordering under P_indep and Q_nonperm, Blackwell ordering under P_indep and Q_reg, and a (1/4LΔ)-dist ordering under P_indep and Q_{L,δ}. The paper explicitly connects these to the impossibility results (Proposition 3.1), showing the conditions are close to necessary — the score cannot be extended to larger classes of misreport matrices or non-independent experiments. This disciplined framing strengthens the theoretical narrative.

4. **Practical extensions.** The plug-in estimator (Definition 4.4) with asymptotic consistency and the kernel extension (Definition 4.6) for continuous/structured observation spaces make the method applicable to real-world problems beyond finite categorical data.

### Minor
1. **The claimed contradiction between Proposition 3.1 and Theorem 4.2 does not hold, but the notation is imprecise and invites confusion.** Proposition 3.1 states that for all 𝒬 ⊇ 𝒬_nonperm, no score preserves exact-match ordering on P_indep and 𝒬. Theorem 4.2 claims preservation under P_indep and 𝒬_nonperm. Since 𝒬_nonperm excludes permutation matrices (including the identity I), and exact-match ordering requires comparing a perfect report (Q=I) with an imperfect one (Q'≠I), there are *no valid pairs* (Q, Q') both in 𝒬_nonperm — the preservation condition is vacuous. The impossibility results apply to proper supersets (𝒬_nonperm ∪ {I}), not to 𝒬_nonperm itself. The paper's text on line 245 ("no score preserves exact ordering for any superset of 𝒬_nonperm") and Proposition 3.1's use of ⊇ (which includes equality) are inconsistent with each other. This is a presentation flaw — the claims do not actually conflict — but the paper should clarify the notation (use ⊃ or explicitly note the vacuity) to avoid misleading readers.

2. **Experiments do not directly test the claimed orderings.** The synthetic and CIFAR-10 experiments show that the Gram determinant score correlates monotonically with corruption probability p, Hamming distance, and ℓ₂ error. This is sensible but weaker than directly verifying the theory: e.g., constructing pairs of datasets satisfying the Blackwell ordering (one obtained by stochastic post-processing of another) and confirming that the score orders them correctly. The Kendall-tau analysis in Figure 2d tests rank correlation with error metrics, not preservation of the specific strict inequalities required by the orderings in Section 2.3.

3. **No baseline comparisons.** The experiments compare the score only against itself across corruption levels, not against alternative scoring methods. The paper discusses mutual information, KL divergence, and determinant mutual information (Kong 2024) in related work, any of which could serve as a natural baseline. Showing that the Gram determinant matches or outperforms these on ranking tasks would substantially strengthen the empirical case.

4. **Employment experiment is underpowered.** The real-world CES data experiment uses N=209 observations, reports no measures of uncertainty (error bars, confidence intervals), and has no baseline comparison. The bar chart (Figure 3d) showing increasing scores across revisions is suggestive but not statistically rigorous. Additionally, the discretization into four quantile buckets is not justified.

5. **No discussion of limitations.** The paper does not acknowledge that the model assumes the auxiliary observation y depends only on the true data x and is conditionally independent of the reported data given x. In strategic settings, agents might manipulate y as well, violating this assumption. Other unstated limitations include reliance on the finiteness of 𝒳 (the label space is assumed to be of size d) and the requirement that d is not too large for determinant computation (O(d³)).
