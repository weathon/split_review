## Summary

This paper identifies a training-inference mismatch in tree-based speculative decoding: existing methods (EAGLE, HASS) train draft models on token *sequences* from the target model, yet inference uses *tree structures* built by the draft model. The authors propose two complementary solutions: (1) TALF, a tree-aware loss function that aggregates cross-entropy over all nodes in a target-generated tree during training, and (2) SALF, a stopping criterion for dynamic tree construction that terminates drafting when the aggregate probability gain falls below a threshold. Experiments across three LLMs (Llama2-7B, Llama3-8B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B) and five benchmarks show consistent end-to-end speedups over HASS and EAGLE-2.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated problem with empirical quantification.** Section 3.1 and Figure 2 provide clear evidence that (a) lower-ranked tokens constitute a non-negligible fraction (~12% for ≥5th rank) of draft tree nodes, and (b) prior training methods (EAGLE, HASS) underperform on these tokens in terms of accuracy and calibration. This concretely establishes the mismatch that TALF addresses.

- **TALF is cleanly validated through controlled ablation.** Table 2 evaluates all 9 combinations of three tree-construction methods × three loss functions on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B (where all methods are trained with equal wall-clock time). TALF improves τ over HASS by 7.2% (optimal tree search) and 3.5% (SALF), with consistent gains across all five benchmarks. This is the cleanest evidence for TALF's core claim.

- **SALF has a provable property and practical demonstrated value.** Theorem 1 proves the sum of expanded-node probabilities decreases monotonically, making the stopping criterion principled rather than heuristic. Table 4 shows SALF increases end-to-end speedup by 14.4% over optimal tree search (TALF loss) while τ drops only 6.3% — confirming that reduced drafting overhead more than compensates for slightly smaller trees.

- **Comprehensive evaluation coverage.** Experiments span 3 models (one per major family), 5 tasks (dialogue, code, math, instruction-following, summarization), both greedy and non-greedy decoding, and systematic sensitivity analysis of the SALF threshold (th=0.0 to th=0.9) and TALF training width (top-k=1,2,4).

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Unfair training budget for the EAGLE-2 baseline in Table 1 (Llama2/3 results).** For Llama2-7B and Llama3-8B, the EAGLE-2 baseline is trained for 10 epochs, while HASS and TALF receive 3 additional epochs (13 total). The headline speedup claims of 15.6–39.4% over EAGLE-2 in Table 1 are thus confounded: the extra training, not the method, could account for part of the gain. This does *not* affect the core HASS-vs-TALF comparison (both get equal extra training) or the DeepSeek experiments (time-controlled, 24h each). However, the paper's most prominent comparative claim against EAGLE-2 is overstated as presented. The authors should either rerun the affected experiments with controlled budgets or add a clear caveat.

### Minor

- **Tree-structure mismatch between training and inference is acknowledged but not analyzed.** TALF is trained on trees generated by the *target* model (fixed across epochs), but during inference the *draft* model builds its own tree dynamically, which may differ in branching structure. The paper notes this design choice (Section 3.2, "Making the draft model dynamically construct the tree at training time would generate a different tree structure for each training epoch… incur prohibitively high computational cost") but does not quantify the discrepancy. Measuring the overlap (e.g., Jaccard similarity or rank correlation) between target-generated and draft-generated trees would strengthen the thesis.

- **SpecExec overhead is asserted but unquantified.** The paper motivates SALF by stating that SpecExec's optimal tree search has "prohibitively high" drafting overhead (Section 3.3), but provides no runtime numbers or overhead breakdown. A direct comparison of drafting time for beam search, optimal tree search, and SALF would make the motivation more concrete.

- **No variance or statistical significance reported.** All speedup numbers in Tables 1–4 appear to be single-run point estimates. Reporting variance across multiple training seeds or inference runs would increase confidence, especially given the sensitivity to the SALF threshold shown in Table 4.

### Trivial

- **Input feature handling in Algorithm 1 is omitted ("for brevity").** The exact feature specification (whether target or draft features are used at training time) should be included for reproducibility.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A breakdown of wall-clock time spent in drafting vs. verification for each tree-construction method would isolate SALF's benefit more cleanly.
- Table 2 (ablation on DeepSeek) isolates TALF's benefit convincingly. Repeating a subset of those controlled experiments on Llama2/3 (even two tasks) would address the training-budget concern definitively.
- The SALF threshold could be dynamically adapted during inference; the paper notes this as future work.

## Suggestions

1. **Rerun or caveat the Llama2/3 EAGLE-2 comparison.** Either run EAGLE-2 for 3 additional epochs on the same schedule as HASS/TALF, or add a clear paragraph explaining that the EAGLE-2 baseline received fewer training epochs and that the relative gains over HASS (which is the controlled comparison) are the paper's main evidence.
2. **Quantify the tree-structure gap.** Measure overlap (e.g., node Jaccard similarity or tree edit distance) between trees generated by the target model (used during TALF training) and trees generated by the draft model during inference. This would either confirm that the gap is negligible or bound a limitation.
3. **Add drafting overhead breakdowns.** Report end-to-end time as (drafting time + verification time) for beam search, optimal tree search, and SALF, across at least one representative benchmark.
4. **Report variance.** Even 2–3 seeds for training the draft model would assess stability, especially for the SALF threshold sensitivity in Table 4.
