## Summary

This paper uses electrocorticography (ECoG) data from participants listening to a spoken narrative and compares it to all 48 layers of GPT2-XL. The central finding is that within high-order language areas (IFG, aSTG, TP), the DLM's spatial layer hierarchy maps onto a temporal sequence of neural activity: earlier layers predict brain activity at earlier time lags relative to word onset, later layers at later lags. The core evidence is a Pearson correlation of r=0.85 between layer index and peak-encoding lag in the IFG (permutation p<10e-5), supported by mixed-effects models and a control ruling out linear interpolation as an explanation. The result is novel and made possible by the temporal resolution of ECoG, which prior fMRI work could not provide.

## Strengths

- **Quantitative lag-layer correlation in IFG (r=0.85, p<10e-13)**: Section 4 demonstrates a strong correlation between layer index and the time of peak encoding performance in the IFG, with permutation p<10e-5 and Spearman r=0.80. This is the paper's most important finding — it directly shows that the spatial layer hierarchy of GPT2-XL maps onto a temporal sequence in a high-level language area, a result inaccessible to prior fMRI work.

- **Control for linear interpolation alternative**: Section 5 and Supp. Fig. 9 show that the actual GPT2-XL nonlinear layer-wise transformations yield significantly higher lag-layer correlations than pseudo-layers generated by linear interpolation between the first and last layers (p<.01). This rules out the trivial explanation that the effect merely reflects a linear mix of previous and current word representations.

- **Generalization across electrodes via mixed-effects models**: Sections 4 and 5 fit linear mixed-effects models with electrode as a random effect, finding significant fixed effects of layer index across IFG electrodes (p<10e-15) and across ROIs (p<.001). This demonstrates the effect is not driven by a single outlier electrode.

- **Replication of intermediate-layer best-fit alongside the new temporal result**: Section 4 confirms the well-known inverted-U shape of encoding performance peaking at intermediate layers (layer 22 in IFG), consistent with prior fMRI studies. The paper then shows this aggregate pattern does not contradict the new temporal-lag finding — a useful disambiguation.

- **Demonstration of increasing temporal receptive window along the ventral hierarchy**: Section 5 uses Levene's test to show significant differences in the spread of peak lags across ROIs (mSTG vs aSTG: F=48.1, p<.01; aSTG vs TP: F=5.8, p<.02), with TP showing >500 ms separation between layer 1 and layer 48 peaks. This extends the core finding from a single region to a broader cortical hierarchy.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Unresolved statistical inconsistency for mSTG (the paper's main negative control)**: The paper claims that mSTG does *not* show the temporal structure and uses this to argue that the effect is regionally specific to higher-order areas ("does not occur in the early stages of the language processing hierarchy"). However, the reported statistics are contradictory: the Spearman correlation for mSTG gives r=−0.24, p=.09 (not significant at α=.05), while the permutation test immediately following gives p<.02 (significant at α=.05). The paper never acknowledges or explains this discrepancy. If the permutation test result is correct, then mSTG *does* show a significant (if weak) lag-layer correlation, directly undermining the claim of regional specificity. If the Spearman result is the correct test, then the permutation p-value and the claim of non-significance need to be reconciled. As written, a reader cannot determine which conclusion to trust. This is the most consequential weakness in the paper because it affects a central interpretive claim.

- **Main results presented only for "predictable" words; generality unclear**: The primary figures (Figs 2 and 3) and all quantitative claims about lag-layer correlations in Sections 4 and 5 are based exclusively on words where GPT2-XL assigned the highest probability to the correct next word (top-1 predictable, 1709 words). The paper mentions that results for unpredictable words and all words exist in supplementary figures (Supp Figs 4–7), and the Discussion notes qualitatively that the sequence is "maintained" for unpredictable words but with a lag shift. However, the central narrative — that DLM layers map onto temporal brain dynamics — should be evaluated and presented on the full stimulus set to justify claims about "general language processing." If the effect changes character or weakens for unpredictable words (which constitute roughly half the data), that is important conditioning information that belongs in the main body, not only the supplement.

### Minor

- **Mixed model omits subject as a random effect**: The linear mixed model across ROIs (lag ~ 1 + layer + ROI + (1 + layer | electrode)) includes electrode as a random effect but not subject. Given that multiple electrodes come from the same subjects (9 participants), electrode-level non-independence across subjects is not fully controlled. This could inflate the significance of the reported fixed effects. A model with random intercepts for subject (and possibly subject×layer) would be more appropriate. This does not undermine the core IFG result (which is tested independently) but weakens confidence in the cross-ROI generalization claim.

- **Scatter plots do not show electrode-level variability**: Fig. 2F and the corresponding panels in Fig. 3 show only mean points (one per layer) without any measure of electrode-level variance. Given that 46 electrodes contributed to the IFG result, plotting individual electrode data or confidence bands would allow readers to assess whether the layer ordering is consistent across electrodes. The mixed model partially addresses this, but the figure should visually support the claim.

- **Asymmetric definition of predictable vs. unpredictable words**: The paper defines predictable words as top-1 correct (n=1709) and unpredictable words as top-5 all incorrect (n=1808). This asymmetry (top-1 vs. top-5) creates an imbalance in the strength of the conditions. A cleaner split (e.g., top-1 correct vs. top-1 incorrect) would be more straightforward. The paper briefly justifies matching statistical power, but the asymmetry merits discussion.

### Trivial

- The phrase "binned at 50 ms resolution" (line 84) appears in the discussion of nonlinearities, while the methods describe "incremental shifts of 25 ms" (line 69). These may refer to different quantities (effective resolution vs. sliding window step), but they should be harmonized or explicitly distinguished to avoid confusion.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Move the "all words" or combined results into a main figure**: Consider adding a panel to Fig. 2 or 3 showing the lag-layer scatter for all words alongside the predictable-words results. This would strengthen the generality claim without requiring a major restructure.

- **Report per-electrode lag-layer correlations or confidence intervals**: A leave-one-electrode-out or leave-one-subject-out analysis would demonstrate robustness and give readers a sense of effect size variability.

- **Briefly summarize the unpredictable-words result in the main text**: The Discussion mentions the result qualitatively but providing the quantitative lag-layer correlation and significance for unpredictable words in the main body (not just the supplement) would help the reader evaluate the generality of the claim without flipping to supplementary figures.

## Novel Insights

The Harsh Critic identified a genuine statistical contradiction in the mSTG analysis that neither reviewer resolved — the Spearman and permutation tests point in opposite directions. This is a non-obvious flaw that the authors likely did not notice, and fixing it could either strengthen the paper (if addressed by clarifying one-tailed vs. two-tailed testing) or weaken it (if mSTG does in fact show a significant effect, requiring a reframing of the regional-specificity claim). Beyond this, the reviews converge on the view that the core IFG finding (r=0.85) is robust, novel, and the main contribution. The secondary findings about regional hierarchy are interesting but rest on shakier statistical ground, and the reviews effectively highlight this asymmetry in evidential quality across the paper's claims.

## Suggestions

1. **Resolve the mSTG statistical contradiction**: Align the Spearman and permutation tests (e.g., report both as one-tailed or both as two-tailed). If one test is two-tailed and the other is one-tailed, state this explicitly. Then either (a) if mSTG truly shows a significant but weak effect, reframe the discussion to acknowledge that even early auditory regions show a mild lag-layer correlation, with the effect growing stronger along the hierarchy; or (b) if mSTG is genuinely non-significant, explain why the permutation p<.02 is misleading (e.g., a different null hypothesis).

2. **Bring the "all words" or "unpredictable words" analysis into the main body**: At minimum, report the lag-layer correlation and significance for unpredictable words in the main text, and consider adding the all-words scatter plot as a panel in Fig. 2 or 3. The claim is about general language processing, not only about predictable words.

3. **Add electrode-level variability to the lag-layer scatter plots**: Either overlay individual electrode points, add bootstrapped confidence bands, or include a leave-one-electrode-out analysis. This would let readers directly assess consistency.

4. **Re-run the cross-ROI mixed model with subject as a random effect**: Add (1 | subject) or (1 + layer | subject) to the model specification to control for subject-level dependencies, and report whether the fixed effects remain significant.
