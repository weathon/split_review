## Summary

This paper derives sufficient mathematical conditions under which offline reactivation (diffusive sampling of waking activity patterns during quiescence) emerges in task-optimized recurrent neural networks. The authors show that noisy RNNs trained to estimate environmental state variables from change-based sensory inputs naturally develop dynamics that decompose into denoising (score-function-based) and state-tracking terms. During quiescence, with doubled noise, these denoising dynamics implement Langevin sampling of the active state distribution, producing statistically faithful reactivation. Numerical experiments on spatial position estimation and head direction estimation tasks validate the key predictions.

## Strengths

- **Clean mathematical derivation of sufficient conditions for reactivation**: Section 2 derives closed-form greedily optimal dynamics (Eq. 14) that decompose into a denoising term (score function of the neural state distribution) and a state-tracking term. This provides a rigorous analytical foundation linking task optimization to reactivation, going beyond prior work that relied on empirical observation without formal guarantees.

- **Prediction and quantitative validation that quiescent distribution equals active distribution**: Section 2.4 shows that with doubled noise, the quiescent dynamics become Langevin sampling of the active state distribution. Numerical experiments validate this via KL divergence comparisons (Fig. 2e) and kernel density estimates (Fig. 1h–k, 2c–d), with explicit baselines including random networks and uniform distributions.

- **Biased behavioral statistics are faithfully reflected in reactivation**: The paper demonstrates that training under a spatially biased policy produces quiescent trajectories that occupy the same biased region (Fig. 2a–d), showing that the *statistics* of waking behavior, not just its qualitative structure, are recapitulated offline.

- **Noisy training is necessary for stable exploratory reactivation**: Networks trained without noise produce quiescent trajectories with low variance (limited exploration; Fig. 2f), and adding noise at test time to noiseless networks yields erratic trajectories (Suppl. Fig. C.3–C.4). This experimentally vindicates the theory's prediction about the role of noise in enabling broad exploration of the task manifold.

- **Generality across two canonical neuroscience tasks and network architectures**: The same reactivation phenomena are demonstrated in both spatial position estimation (2D, Fig. 1) and head direction estimation (1D circular, Fig. 3), with ring attractor structure emerging in the latter. Results also generalize to continuous-time GRUs (Suppl. Fig. C.2), showing the findings are not architecture-specific.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **The distribution matching predicted by the theory also occurs in noiseless networks, weakening the causal claim for the denoising mechanism.** The paper explicitly reports (lines 181–182) that "even trajectories generated by networks that were not trained in the presence of noise, and also were not driven by noise in the quiescent phase, still generated quiescent activity distributions that corresponded well to the active phase distributions." The paper explains this as due to learned attractive task manifolds. While the paper frames its contribution as *sufficient* (not necessary) conditions, this result means the theory's central mechanism — denoising dynamics producing Langevin sampling of the active distribution — is not the primary causal driver of the observed distribution matching. The attractor structure alone, learned through task optimization, produces similar results. The theory's distinctive added value over existing attractor-based accounts of reactivation is therefore less clear than the framing suggests. The paper partially addresses this by showing that noiseless networks have reduced exploration (lower variance), but this shifts the explanatory burden from "why distribution matching occurs" to "why exploration is more thorough," which is a qualitatively different claim than the core theoretical narrative.

### Minor

- **KL divergence evaluation lacks statistical rigor.** The primary quantitative evidence for distribution matching is KL divergence reported as point estimates averaged over five networks (Fig. 2e) without confidence intervals, error bars, or significance tests. The statement that "unbiased quiescent outputs were almost as close to unbiased active outputs as a true uniform distribution" is not accompanied by a statistical test of whether active and quiescent distributions are distinguishable. A permutation test, energy distance, or bootstrapped confidence intervals would substantially strengthen the claims.

- **The doubled-noise assumption is theoretically elegant but biologically unmotivated.** The Langevin sampling result (Section 2.4) depends on exactly doubling the noise variance during quiescence. While the paper notes that "deviating from this factor is not catastrophic" and that different noise variances yield different temperatures, no biological mechanism or justification is offered for why neural circuits would double their noise specifically during quiescence. The noiseless control networks achieve distribution matching without any such manipulation, further reducing the force of this assumption.

- **Head direction reactivation results are purely qualitative.** The head direction results (Fig. 3) show distribution matching through KDEs and PC projections, but the claim about moment-to-moment transition structure (Suppl. Fig. C.5) is described as qualitative ("partially recapitulated," "less clearly than during waking") without statistical measures of trajectory autocorrelation or transition similarity. The paper honestly acknowledges (Section 5) that the theory does not explain moment-to-moment dynamics, which limits the explanatory scope for sequential reactivation phenomena.

- **The stationarity assumption on p(s(t)) is restrictive.** The derivation assumes the stimulus distribution is stationary (line 30), which the paper acknowledges "amounts to ignoring the effects of initial conditions on an animal's state occupancy statistics." For a theory claiming sufficient conditions, violations of this assumption in naturalistic settings (e.g., changing behavioral policies or environmental boundaries) could substantially affect the predictions, and this is not discussed.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation emerging from the synthesis of reviews is the tension between the paper's theoretical framing and its own control experiments. The harsh critic correctly identifies that the noiseless networks produce the same distribution matching as noisy ones, which would be a fatal flaw for a claim that the denoising mechanism *causes* distribution matching. However, read charitably, the paper instead demonstrates two distinct routes to the same phenomenon: (1) the theory's denoising+Langevin route (noisy networks with broad exploration), and (2) a simpler attractor-diffusion route (noiseless networks with limited exploration). What the theory uniquely adds is an explanation of *why* the attractor structure supports distribution matching in the first place (the attractor density reflects p(r) because of the task objective), plus a mechanism for increasing exploration beyond the attractor's local structure. This reframing — that the theory explains *why* attractor-based reactivation is statistically faithful, not that it's necessary for it — would better reflect the empirical evidence.

## Suggestions

1. **Reframe the narrative** to explicitly position the theory as providing a *mechanistic explanation* for why attractor-based reactivation preserves task statistics, rather than as the unique causal pathway. The noiseless results strengthen rather than weaken the paper when framed as showing that the attractor structure learned through task optimization already encodes the relevant statistics, and noise enhances exploration of this structure.

2. **Add statistical tests** for distribution equality (e.g., a two-sample test or bootstrapped confidence intervals on the KL divergence) to move beyond point estimates.

3. **Provide quantitative measures of transition structure** for the head direction biased-velocity experiment (e.g., angular velocity autocorrelation or transition matrix similarity) to substantiate the qualitative claims about sequential reactivation.
