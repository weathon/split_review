The paper presents LangProp, a framework for iteratively optimizing code generated by LLMs using data-driven training loops. It draws an explicit analogy to ML training (IL, DAgger, RL) and applies this to code optimization across Sudoku, CartPole, and autonomous driving in CARLA. The framework is designed to be task-agnostic (mirroring PyTorch's interface), generates interpretable policies, and shows a clear progression in performance from offline IL → DAgger → Online IL/RL in CARLA.

## Strengths

- **Successful transfer of ML training paradigms to code optimization**: Table 1 shows a clean monotonic improvement from offline IL (0.07 driving score) → DAgger IL (36.2) → DAgger IL/RL (64.2) → Online IL/RL (70.3) on CARLA training routes, and similarly on testing routes (0.00 → 41.3 → 61.2 → 80.9). This directly supports the claim that standard training paradigms transfer to code optimization with LangProp.

- **Interpretable failure analysis via inspectable policy code**: Section 4.3 identifies a concrete instance of causal confusion — the learned policy stopping when speed is near zero (Listing with causal confusion code). The paper explicitly diagnoses this failure mode at the code level, which is a genuine advantage over black-box neural policies and is noted as the first reported instance of causal confusion using LLMs for policy optimization.

- **Competitive performance against strong baselines in a complex domain**: The best LangProp agent (Online IL/RL) achieves 80.9 on CARLA testing routes, outperforming the Roach expert (63.4), TCP expert (72.9), TransFuser expert (73.1), and InterFuser expert (78.6). The comparison against six published expert agents provides meaningful validation in a challenging domain.

- **Substantial improvement over zero-shot generation**: In CartPole, zero-shot code scored 9.9/500, while LangProp achieved 500.0 after training (9/10 seeds converged within 10 updates and ~10k environment steps), demonstrating that the optimization loop adds significant value beyond the base LLM's capabilities.

- **Training dynamics analysis**: Figure 4 and the discussion in Section 4.3 show that RL-based training on infractions introduces spikes in the replay buffer scores, enabling immediate policy correction, while offline IL fails to address causal confusion. This analysis offers mechanistic insight into why the training paradigm choices matter, which is rare in code-generation papers.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **CARLA results lack any measure of variance**: The central empirical results (Table 1) report single numbers per method with no standard deviations, confidence intervals, or number of runs/seeds. While the magnitude of improvement (e.g., 0.07→70.3 on training routes) makes it unlikely that the core findings are noise, the absence of repeated-trial information prevents the reader from assessing the reliability of finer-grained comparisons (e.g., whether LangProp Online IL/RL at 80.9 is meaningfully different from TransFuser at 73.1 or InterFuser at 78.6 on test routes). This is the single most important omission in the empirical section.

### Minor

- **Sudoku experiment is too shallow to support the generality claim**: The Sudoku section reports only that zero-shot "occasionally failed" and LangProp "identified a fully working solution." No quantitative metrics are given — no success rate, number of updates needed, number of solutions evaluated, or comparison to baselines. As presented, this is anecdotal evidence for a task that could have provided clean quantitative validation.

- **The "RL" component is a substantial simplification of standard RL, and the terminology overclaims**: The paper's RL mechanism (Section 3.3) assigns an infraction penalty when the current policy's action matches a behavioral policy that previously caused an infraction. There is no discounted return, value function, policy gradient, or sequential credit assignment. The paper acknowledges the simplification ("While RL typically optimizes for maximum returns... we simplify the setting"), but still draws a strong terminological parallel to standard RL methods (e.g., the CartPole comparison to PPO). A more precise label like "cost-sensitive IL correction" or "infraction-weighted IL" would better represent what is actually implemented. The onus is on the paper to be explicit about this gap, especially since the term "RL" carries specific technical expectations.

- **No ablation separating online data from the infraction penalty**: The paper reports DAgger IL (36.2 training/41.3 test), DAgger IL/RL (64.2/61.2), and Online IL/RL (70.3/80.9). Since DAgger IL/RL already includes both online data collection AND the infraction penalty, and Online IL/RL modifies both the data distribution (pure online vs. aggregated) AND the penalty, it is impossible to determine how much of the improvement comes from the changed data distribution versus the penalty signal itself. An ablation comparing (a) Online IL without penalties, (b) Online IL with penalties, and (c) DAgger IL with penalties would isolate these effects.

- **Novelty claim is somewhat overstated relative to prior work**: The paper states it is "the first to completely translate and apply the training paradigm used in machine learning for iterative code generation." Prior work such as CLAIRify (iterative error correction with feedback until resolved) and Voyager (skill library with iterative improvement from environment feedback) are acknowledged, but the paper's distinguishing features (priority reranking, evolutionary selection, dataset splits, checkpoints) are useful engineering contributions rather than a conceptual paradigm shift. The framing would benefit from a more precise statement of what is genuinely new (e.g., the formalized training loop with dataset splits, checkpoints, and parallel policies applied to driving).

- **The backpropagation analogy is overextended**: The paper states the execution feedback is "equivalent to gradient assignment" and draws parallels between the forward pass/backward pass in neural networks and code evaluation/LLM feedback. While rhetorically appealing, this is not technically accurate — gradient assignment is a specific mathematical operation (chain rule applied to differentiable functions), whereas LangProp uses error-driven prompting. This doesn't harm the science but invites unnecessary scrutiny from readers familiar with optimization theory.

- **PPO comparison in CartPole is not directly informative**: PPO is a general-purpose RL algorithm that does not leverage pre-trained knowledge, while LangProp uses GPT-3.5's extensive prior training (including knowledge of PID controllers and physics). The comparison shows LangProp converges in fewer environment steps, which is not surprising given that GPT-3.5 brings substantial prior knowledge while PPO starts from scratch. A comparison against a few-shot prompting baseline (an LLM asked to iteratively self-correct without the LangProp loop) would be more informative for disentangling the value of the framework from the LLM's inherent capabilities.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Quantify LLM query cost and training runtime**: The paper notes that LLM calls are "both expensive and slow" and that inference does not need the LLM (a real strength), but does not report the number of LLM calls per training run, total API cost, or wall-clock time for training. For practitioners considering adoption, these practical costs matter.

- **Comparison to a simple self-reflection baseline**: The paper does not compare LangProp against a simpler baseline where the same LLM is asked to iteratively self-correct its code given error messages (without the LangProp training loop with dataset splits, priority ranking, and multiple parallel policies). Such a comparison would disentangle whether the framework's complexity adds value over straightforward iterative prompting.

- **Use checkpoint analysis to study learning dynamics**: The paper highlights that checkpoints are small (hundreds of KB) and interpretable, but does not perform any analysis using this property (e.g., tracking how the policy code evolves across updates, measuring which code changes correlate with performance improvements).

## Suggestions

1. **Report variance for CARLA experiments**: Re-run the CARLA experiments with at least 3–5 seeds and report means and standard deviations for the driving score, route completion, and infraction factor. Even a small number of seeds would substantially strengthen the empirical claims.

2. **Add an ablation study for the CARLA experiments**: Include conditions for (a) Online IL without RL penalties, (b) Online IL with RL penalties, and (c) DAgger IL with RL penalties, to isolate the effect of online data from the infraction penalty signal.

3. **Strengthen the Sudoku experiment with quantitative results**: Report success rate, number of updates to convergence, and the proportion of candidates that were valid solutions, to match the rigor of the CartPole and CARLA sections.

4. **Rename or explicitly qualify the "RL" component**: Use a term like "infraction-weighted IL" or "cost-sensitive correction," or at minimum include a one-sentence disclaimer in the main text that this is not policy-gradient RL but rather a simplified penalty-augmented imitation objective.

5. **Replace or supplement the CartPole PPO comparison with a self-reflection baseline**: Show what happens when the same LLM is prompted to self-correct given only the error/score feedback, without the LangProp framework's priority-ranking, multiple policies, and evolutionary selection. This would directly demonstrate the value added by the framework.

## Score and Decision

The paper makes a solid contribution: it proposes a well-motivated framework for iterative code optimization using LLMs, applies it to a challenging real-world domain (autonomous driving in CARLA), demonstrates a clear improvement pattern across training paradigms, and showcases the interpretability advantage of code-based policies. The weaknesses — primarily the lack of variance reporting for CARLA and the thin Sudoku experiments — are addressable and do not invalidate the core claims. The paper is a meaningful step toward integrating ML training methodologies with LLM-based code generation.
