## Summary

This paper proposes "neural phishing," a data poisoning attack that teaches an LLM to memorize and later reveal sensitive secrets (e.g., credit card numbers) through benign-appearing poisoned training data. The attacker inserts only tens of short poison sentences with a structure like "credit card number is not: 123456" before the actual secret appears in training, then extracts the secret by querying the model. The attack achieves 10% secret extraction rate (SER) on 12-digit secrets with just 50 random GPT-generated poisons — 10¹¹× better than random guessing — and the paper's standout finding is that the poisoned behavior persists for thousands of clean training steps (30% SER after 10k steps). The attack works with only vague priors (e.g., a GPT-generated bio of Alexander Hamilton as a poison prefix yields 40% SER) and a novel randomized inference strategy enables extraction without knowing the exact secret prefix.

## Strengths

1. **Attack succeeds with minimal attacker knowledge** (lines 110-119, Fig. 1): The baseline uses random GPT-generated sentences as poisons (no knowledge of the secret prefix or digits) and achieves 10% SER on 12-digit secrets with just 50 poisons. This is 10¹¹× higher than random guessing, and the paper verifies that poisoning-free models yield 0% SER. This directly supports the claim that the attack is practical under weak assumptions.

2. **Poisoning during pretraining yields surprisingly durable memorization** (lines 249-257, Fig. 5a): When poisons are inserted during pretraining (before the model ever sees the secret), the attack still achieves ~30% SER even after 10,000 steps of training on clean data. Even in the worst-case scenario (poisons inserted near the end of pretraining), SER is ~10% after 10k clean steps. This is a genuinely novel and practically alarming result — prior work has not demonstrated poisoned behavior persisting for this many steps.

3. **Simple "not" trick elegantly prevents overfitting to poisons** (lines 121-128, Fig. 1 orange line): Appending "not" before the poison digits eliminates the concavity in SER and enables scaling to 500 poisons with monotonic improvement. This practical insight improves attack success at negligible cost.

4. **Vague priors multiply SER dramatically** (lines 213-216, Fig. 3): Using a poison prefix generated by asking GPT-4 to "write a biography of Alexander Hamilton" (a prior with no actual knowledge of the target user) raises SER to 40%, compared to ~10% with random prefixes. This shows that even extremely weak structural knowledge amplifies the attack.

5. **Randomized inference strategy extracts secrets without knowing the exact prefix** (lines 225-227, Fig. 4): Perturbing proper nouns in the secret prefix during model queries yields higher SER than using the true prefix, enabling extraction even when the exact prefix is unknown. This validates the core intuition that the attack teaches generalized memorization rather than a fixed prefix–suffix mapping.

### Minor

1. **No statistical variance or multiple-seed reporting**: All SER results appear as point estimates from single experimental runs. Given the stochasticity of training, results could vary across random seeds. This is particularly relevant for the durability results (Figs. 5a, 5b) where a single seed could produce a spurious local optimum. Multiple seeds with confidence intervals would substantially strengthen confidence in the findings.

2. **Limited analysis of the attack's detectability**: The paper asserts that poisons are "benign-appearing," but a dataset curator could flag repeated patterns around "credit card number," "social security number," or "not: [digits]." While the paper shows poisons can be varied (randomized prompts, Fig. 4), it does not test whether existing data sanitization or anomaly detection filters would catch them. A brief empirical check or even a discussion of how poisons could be made more natural (e.g., embedded in longer sentences) would strengthen the practical claims.

3. **The durability experiments use out-of-domain clean data between poison and secret**: The paper uses Wikitext as intervening clean data but acknowledges the finetuning domain is Enron Emails. The model might retain the poisoned behavior longer when intervening data comes from a different domain (Wikitext) than from the same domain (more emails). This does not invalidate the durability result but limits generalizability — the paper could discuss whether in-domain intervening data would accelerate forgetting.

### Trivial

1. **Model sizes limited to 1.4B–6.9B parameters**: The paper acknowledges this (line 163) and plausibly speculates larger models would be more vulnerable. This is a computational constraint, not a flaw, but it means the strong claims about scaling to "truly large models" remain speculative.

2. **"Memorization" and "extraction" used interchangeably despite explicit definition**: The paper states (line 45) that they use the terms interchangeably because extractability implies memorization and vice versa under their definition. This is fine, but a cleaner separation would help readers unfamiliar with the literature.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting finding across the reviews is the durability result: the poisoned behavior persists for 10,000 clean steps even when the attack was inserted during pretraining (30% SER for undertrained models, ~10% for fully pretrained models). This is genuinely surprising because prior poisoning work on LLMs has not demonstrated persistence at this scale. It suggests that the attack is not merely exploiting a transient overfitting artifact but is actually "teaching" a durable association. The randomized inference result (extraction improves when the secret prefix is perturbed) further supports this interpretation — the model learns a robust, generalizable mapping from semantically related prefixes to the secret, rather than a brittle exact-match memorization. If the paper were extended, a mechanistic analysis of *how* the model represents this mapping (e.g., via attention head analysis or probing classifiers on hidden states) would be a natural next step.

## Suggestions

1. **Add variance reporting**: Rerun the key experiments (baseline SER, durability at key checkpoints) with at least 3 random seeds and report mean ± std. This single change would substantially increase confidence in the results.
2. **Test detectability empirically**: Take the poison templates from the paper and run them through a simple perplexity filter or existing data sanitization pipeline. Report how many would be flagged. If few are flagged, this directly strengthens the practical claim.
3. **Address the domain mismatch in durability**: Either add a control experiment with in-domain intervening data (e.g., more Enron emails between poison and secret) or at minimum discuss why the observed trend is expected to hold in-domain.
4. **Weaken the "practical" framing slightly** in the abstract and introduction, or add a paragraph concretizing one threat model scenario end-to-end (e.g., a specific company's finetuning API workflow). The current framing is defensible but vulnerable to the criticism that it overclaims.
