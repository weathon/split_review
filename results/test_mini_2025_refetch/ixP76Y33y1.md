Let me write the final review now.## Summary

This paper studies how intrinsic dataset properties (intrinsic dimension $d_{\text{data}}$ and a proposed "label sharpness" $K_{\mathcal{F}}$) relate to generalization error and adversarial robustness, focusing on explaining differences between natural and medical image domains. It derives scaling laws connecting these properties, introduces label sharpness as a measurable dataset characteristic, and provides empirical results across 11 datasets and 6 architectures. The core idea—that a dataset's label Lipschitz constant can help explain cross-domain generalization gaps—is well-motivated and the experiments are broad in scope.

---

## Strengths

1. **Introduction of label sharpness ($K_{\mathcal{F}}$) as a measurable dataset property.** The paper defines $K_{\mathcal{F}}$ in Section 3.2 (Equation 1) and shows in Figure 1 that medical datasets have consistently higher $\hat{K}_{\mathcal{F}} \sim 2.5\times10^{-4}$ than natural datasets $\sim 1\times10^{-4}$. This is a concrete, interpretable quantity that existing work (Konz et al., 2022; Bahri et al., 2021) treated as an unknown constant.

2. **Broad empirical scope.** The evaluation covers 11 datasets (7 medical, 4 natural), 6 architectures (ResNet-18/34/50, VGG-13/16/19), 6 training set sizes (500–1750), and multiple attack budgets ($\epsilon \in \{1/255, 2/255, 4/255, 8/255\}$). Figure 2 shows that medical points lie systematically above natural points at comparable $d_{\text{data}}$, and the pattern is consistent across all six models.

3. **Theoretical scaffolding linking dataset properties to generalization and robustness.** Theorems 1–5 form a coherent chain: Theorem 1 bounds generalization via $d_{\text{data}}$ and $K_{\mathcal{F}}$; Theorem 2 shows $K_f \to K_{\mathcal{F}}$ as $N\to\infty$, making the bound model-independent; Theorem 3 connects robustness to $K_{\mathcal{F}}$; Theorems 4–5 extend to representation intrinsic dimension. While the proofs are deferred, the overall formal framework is a genuine contribution.

4. **Demonstration of a negative correlation between label sharpness and adversarial robustness.** Table 1 reports Pearson correlations between loss penalty under FGSM attack and $\hat{K}_{\mathcal{F}}$ across 24 model–$\epsilon$ combinations. The correlations are positive across all settings (range 0.18–0.77), consistent with the theoretical prediction that higher $K_{\mathcal{F}}$ reduces robustness.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **No quantitative goodness-of-fit for the central scaling law claim.** The paper states that "generalization error typically increases with $d_{\text{data}}$, as predicted" and that Eq. (2) holds, but provides no $R^2$, no confidence intervals on the exponent $-1/d_{\text{data}}$, and no comparison to a null model without $d_{\text{data}}$. The likelihood analysis that "increases the likelihood of the scaling law" is relegated to Appendix C.1. Figure 2 shows substantial scatter and overlapping points across training set sizes; without quantitative fit metrics, the claim that the scaling law is "supported" rests on visual inspection. This is a significant gap in empirical validation for a paper whose central contribution is an empirically tested scaling law.

2. **The $K_f \approx K_{\mathcal{F}}$ approximation is asserted but unverified.** Theorem 2 states that $K_f \to K_{\mathcal{F}}$ in probability as $N \to \infty$, but the experiments use $N$ up to only 1750, and training is stopped once the model fits the training set—not when $K_f$ has converged. The paper provides no direct measurement of $K_f$ (e.g., via Fazlyab et al., 2019) to verify that the approximation holds in practice. The adversarial robustness analysis (Section 6) relies entirely on this approximation to connect $\hat{K}_{\mathcal{F}}$ to robustness radii, making the empirical link indirect.

3. **Adversarial robustness correlations are moderate and lack statistical testing.** The Pearson correlations in Table 1 range from 0.18 to 0.77, with 11 of 24 below 0.5. The paper does not report p-values or confidence intervals for these correlations. While the overall positive trend is consistent with the theory, several individual values (e.g., RN-34 at 0.18 for $\epsilon=8/255$) are weak, and the reader cannot assess whether they are statistically significant.

### Minor

1. **Table–text inconsistency for the Prostate dataset.** The table (line 94) labels "prostate" as a "natural" dataset with $\hat{K}_{\mathcal{F}} \sim 1.8 \times 10^{-4}$, but Section 4 (line 73) correctly lists "Prostate MRI cancer risk scoring" under **Medical Image Datasets**. This is a clear labeling error. Notably, correcting the label (moving prostate to medical) would *increase* the separation between domain means, so the error does not undermine the paper's claims—but it does affect any figure that uses domain color-coding and needs to be fixed.

2. **The label sharpness estimator's properties are unexplored.** Equation (1) uses $\max_{j,k} |y_j - y_k| / \|x_j - x_k\|$ over 1000 random samples. This is sensitive to outliers (a single near-duplicate pair with different labels dominates the estimate). The paper states that $M=1000$ is "more than sufficient for a converging estimate" but provides no convergence analysis (e.g., varying $M = 500, 2000$) and no comparison to alternatives like the mean of top-$k$ ratios.

3. **Asymmetric experimental design for class pairing.** Natural image datasets use binary classification tasks averaged over 5 random class pairs, while medical datasets use a single fixed clinical task. This asymmetry means the variance of the natural-domain measurements reflects class-pair variability, while the medical measurements reflect only sampling variance—potentially inflating the apparent domain difference or introducing systematic differences in task difficulty.

4. **Causal claims are slightly stronger than the evidence warrants.** The paper mostly uses hedged language ("may be at least partially attributed to," "we posit"), and the Discussion explicitly acknowledges the causal limitation. However, some phrasings (e.g., "this difference is *explained* by medical datasets typically having much higher label sharpness" in Section 5.2) imply a stronger explanatory claim than the correlational evidence supports. The paper does not attempt to control for $d_{\text{data}}$ when assessing the effect of $K_{\mathcal{F}}$, nor does it vary $K_{\mathcal{F}}$ independently (e.g., with synthetic data).
