## Summary

This paper addresses continuous space-time physics simulation from sparse observations by formulating it as a double dynamical system. System 1 is a discrete-time latent GNN that auto-regressively predicts "anchor states" in a latent space; System 2 is a learned state observer (cross-attention + GRU) that maps those anchor states to the solution at arbitrary spatial and temporal query points. The method is evaluated on three fluid dynamics datasets (Navier, Shallow Water, Eagle) against DINO, Interpolated MeshGraphNet, and MAGNet, achieving substantial improvements particularly on spatial extrapolation (Ext-𝒳).

## Strengths

1. **Clear, well-motivated problem formulation that identifies a genuine gap.** Section 1 explicitly defines three requirements (R1–R3) that few existing methods satisfy simultaneously, providing a principled benchmark for the community. This framing gives the paper a strong intellectual foundation.

2. **Novel and principled architecture grounded in control theory.** The double dynamical system design — separating latent discrete-time forecasting (System 1) from continuous state estimation (System 2) — is well-motivated by observability considerations. The cross-attention + GRU state estimator is a concrete instantiation that goes beyond handcrafted interpolation by leveraging temporal context. Proposition 1 formalizes why maintaining state in latent space rather than projecting to physical space at each step can yield better error bounds.

3. **Strong empirical results on challenging benchmarks.** On spatial extrapolation (Ext-𝒳, Table 1), the method achieves 0.2029 MSE on Navier High (25% spatial coverage), outperforming DINO (1.600) by ~7.9×, Interpolated MGN (2.694) by ~13×, and MAGNet (35.73) by ~176×. Similar order-of-magnitude improvements hold across Shallow Water and Eagle. On time continuity (Table 2), the method dominates on Navier and Shallow Water and is competitive on Eagle. These results convincingly demonstrate the value of the approach for its targeted task.

4. **Clever training strategy for spatiotemporal generalization.** The artificial generalization technique (Section 3.4) — using a coarser time step Δ > Δ* and spatially sub-sampling anchor nodes during training — is a practical and well-designed mechanism to force the state estimator to handle query points that do not align with anchor nodes. This directly targets the continuous evaluation setting and is likely a key contributor to the method's success.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **No reported variance or confidence intervals across any metric.** All results in Tables 1 and 2 are point estimates without error bars. Given the random sub-sampling during training (Section 3.4) and the stochasticity in neural network training, it is impossible to judge whether the reported improvements are statistically significant. This is a significant gap that should be addressed — even reporting results over 3–5 random seeds would substantially increase confidence in the claims.

2. **Key ablations and implementation details are deferred to an appendix that was not available in the reviewed manuscript.** The paper repeatedly references Appendix G for ablation studies (including comparison with attentive neural processes, attention map analysis, computational cost), Appendix E for architectural details, and Appendix F for baseline training details. While conference papers routinely use appendices, the main paper should include at least a summary of the most critical ablation (e.g., replacing the learned state estimator with bicubic interpolation as a control) to allow readers to assess the contribution without the appendix.

### Minor

1. **The theoretical bounds (Propositions 1 & 2) are motivational rather than directly supportive of the experimental claims.** The bounds depend on Lipschitz constants (L_f, L_h, L_e, L_{s_2}) and an observability function α(q) that are not estimated or bounded for the actual systems. Proposition 1's comparison with the "classic AR" scheme (Equation 3) also does not directly match the behavior of the actual baselines (e.g., Interpolated MGN does not strictly follow the encode-process-decode pattern assumed). The paper acknowledges the theory as providing an "upper bound" and intuition, but the introduction's claim of "strong theoretical results" (end of Section 1) is somewhat overstated. The theory is best framed as insightful motivation.

2. **The performance advantage is dataset-dependent and not uniform.** On Eagle High In-𝒳 (Table 1), Interpolated MGN achieves lower MSE than the proposed method (64.44 vs. 70.02). On Eagle time continuity at 1/4 temporal resolution (Table 2), MAGNet outperforms the proposed method on both in-domain (225.8 vs. 278.6) and out-of-domain (230.6 vs. 306.9). The paper acknowledges these results in the tables but could discuss more explicitly where and why the method underperforms, and what characteristics of a dataset make the approach more or less suitable.

3. **Fairness of baseline comparisons is not fully established in the main text.** The paper describes adaptations made to baselines (e.g., splitting MAGNet queries into chunks of 10 nodes), and acknowledges that MAGNet's original setup assumed knowledge of the target graph during training. However, it does not explicitly state whether all baselines were retrained from scratch on identical data splits with comparable hyperparameter budgets. This information is referenced to Appendix F, which was not accessible in the reviewed version.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting insight emerging across the reviews is the tension between the paper's control-theoretic framing and its practical execution. The double dynamical system formulation (Sections 3.1–3.2) is genuinely elegant and brings fresh intellectual grounding to a field dominated by ad-hoc architectural choices. However, the translation from theory to practice reveals the gap: Proposition 2's observability condition (A2) is invoked but never empirically verified, and the Lipschitz constants in Proposition 1 are not estimated. This is not a fatal flaw — nearly all ML-for-physics papers have this gap — but it is a pattern worth noting. The paper's actual strength lies less in its theoretical guarantees and more in the well-designed training strategy (Section 3.4) and the empirically effective cross-attention + GRU architecture. The most novel architectural contribution is arguably the temporal aggregation via GRU over cross-attention outputs, which differs from conventional interpolation approaches by explicitly modeling temporal sequence structure rather than treating each time step independently.

## Suggestions

1. **Add variance estimates.** Report results over at least 3 random seeds for all main metrics (Tables 1 & 2). This is the single highest-impact improvement.
2. **Move the state estimator ablation into the main paper.** Showing a comparison where ψ_q is replaced by bicubic/spline interpolation (keeping System 1 fixed) would directly validate the paper's central architectural claim.
3. **Tone down the "strong theoretical results" claim.** Frame Proposition 1 and 2 as "motivational error bounds" or "theoretical insight" rather than "strong theoretical results" to match what the analysis actually delivers.
4. **Add a paragraph discussing when the method might not work.** Based on the Eagle 1/4 time results, discuss whether the approach struggles in high-turbulence regimes or when temporal observability degrades. This honesty would strengthen the paper.
