## Summary

This paper introduces Meta-Rewarding, an extension to the Self-Rewarding framework where the LLM plays three roles (actor, judge, meta-judge). The meta-judge evaluates the judge's own judgments to create preference pairs for training the judge's judging ability, while the judge's scores are used to train the actor. On Llama-3-8B-Instruct, the method achieves a 39.44% length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval 2 (up from 22.9% and surpassing Self-Rewarding+LC at 35.49%) and a 29.1% score on Arena-Hard (up from 20.6%). The paper also introduces a length-control mechanism to prevent response length explosion during iterative DPO training.

## Strengths

1. **Novel and well-motivated extension to Self-Rewarding.** The meta-judge idea — training the model to evaluate its own judgments to improve judging skill — directly addresses a known limitation of Self-Rewarding (saturation of the judge). The three-role framing (actor, judge, meta-judge) is conceptually clean and clearly explained, and it provides a principled way to include judge-improvement data in the DPO training loop.

2. **Strong empirical results on AlpacaEval 2.** Table 1 shows monotonic improvement across four iterations, reaching 39.44% LC win rate for an 8B model. This surpasses the Self-Rewarding+LC baseline by ~4 percentage points at Iteration 4 and even exceeds GPT-4-0314 (35.5%). The gap between Meta-Rewarding and Self-Rewarding+LC widens in later iterations (35.45% vs 34.87% at Iter 3, then 39.44% vs 35.49% at Iter 4), supporting the claim that training the judge via meta-judging yields a sustained advantage.

3. **Effective length-control mechanism.** The ρ-based quality-tier selection is a simple but well-ablated fix for length explosion. Table 4 shows that disabling length control (ρ=0) degrades LC win rate to 34.68% and increases response length to 2063, while ρ=0.3 gives 35.83% LC win rate at length 1806. The ablation clearly isolates the mechanism's contribution.

4. **Transparent analysis of meta-judge biases.** Table 5 and Figure 5 honestly document that meta-judge training induces score saturation (97.68% preference for higher-score judgments by Iter 2) and positional bias (87.75%). This candid reporting, while revealing a limitation, is a valuable signal to the community about where the method breaks down.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The meta-judge training is unstable and unsustained: the central claim of simultaneous actor-judge improvement holds for only two iterations.** After Iteration 2, judge training is abandoned because the meta-judge develops severe score and positional biases (Table 5: 97.68% score bias, 87.75% positional bias). Iterations 3 and 4 train only the actor. The paper acknowledges this in the Limitations section but does not fully grapple with how it undermines the framing. The method does not demonstrate a *sustainable* mechanism for jointly improving both capabilities — the judge improves measurably over two iterations but then the training loop for it must be stopped. This is a structural limitation of the approach, not just a scope issue.

2. **The abstract's claim of "without human supervision" is overstated.** The method requires an initial SFT stage on the Evaluation Fine-Tuning (EFT) dataset, which is built from Open Assistant — a dataset of *human-ranked responses* (Section 3.1: "This dataset is built from Open Assistant... and provides initial LLM-as-a-Judge training data of ranked human responses"). The paper later clarifies (Section 3.3) that "our training did not utilize any extra human data beyond the seed model (except the EFT dataset used in the SFT stage)," which is a more honest framing. The abstract and introduction should adopt this qualified language. The contribution is not diminished by this correction — Self-Rewarding also required the EFT SFT stage — but the framing should be accurate.

3. **The judge evaluation has a favorable asymmetry that is not discussed.** Table 3 reports two settings for judge agreement with GPT-4. In "Self-Chosen Pairs" (where the judge picks which pairs to rank), Meta-Rewarding shows large gains (e.g., Iter 3: 76.80% vs Self-Rewarding+LC 72.68% in agree-without-ties). In the more rigorous "GPT-4 Chosen Pairs" setting (where pairs are fixed by GPT-4), improvements are modest (Iter 3: 61.24% vs 55.90%). The Self-Chosen Pairs setting inflates agreement because the judge selects the easiest comparisons from its own scoring distribution. The paper does not discuss this discrepancy or its implications for the headline claim about judge improvement. It reports both numbers, which is helpful, but the text emphasizes the Self-Chosen results without caveat.

### Minor

1. **The Arena-Hard results are more modest than AlpacaEval results.** Table 2 shows Meta-Rewarding reaching 29.1% at Iter 4 (from 20.6%), but Self-Rewarding+LC also improves (27.3%), and the 95% confidence intervals (±2%) overlap substantially between methods and iterations. The gap at Iter 4 is only 1.8 percentage points, and there is no monotonic advantage at every iteration (Self-Rewarding+LC Iter 3, 28.2%, numerically exceeds Meta-Rewarding Iter 3, 27.6%). The paper's claim that Meta-Rewarding significantly improves on harder questions is weaker here.

2. **The judge evaluation on human preferences (Appendix Table 7) is mentioned but the main results are deferred to the appendix.** Section 3.4 references Appendix Table 7 for human correlation but provides no main-text summary. Given that human correlation is a key validation of judge quality, some headline numbers should appear in the main paper.

3. **AlpacaEval results lack confidence intervals.** Only Arena-Hard reports CIs. While the gains are large and likely significant, the omission of variance estimates is a missing standard.

### Trivial
None.

## Novel Insights

The harsh critic's observation about the asymmetry between "Self-Chosen Pairs" and "GPT-4 Chosen Pairs" in Table 3 is insightful: the paper's strongest evidence for judge improvement comes from a protocol where the judge selects the pairs it ranks, which inflates agreement. This is not a fatal flaw — the GPT-4 Chosen Pairs setting still shows improvements — but it suggests that the judge may be improving more at identifying extreme (easy-to-distinguish) cases than at fine-grained discrimination. The calm reviewer's analysis of this point usefully reframes what the judge evaluation actually measures.

Additionally, the narrowing gap between Self-Rewarding+LC and Meta-Rewarding on Arena-Hard (Table 2) is worth noting: on harder questions, the advantage of explicit judge training is less pronounced, which may indicate that the actor's improvement on complex tasks is driven more by additional DPO data quantity than by judge quality per se.

## Suggestions

1. Revise the abstract and introduction to say "without *additional* human supervision beyond a one-time SFT on existing human preference data" or equivalent, to accurately represent the method's data requirements.
2. Add a discussion of why the Self-Chosen Pairs setting shows larger gains than GPT-4 Chosen Pairs, and consider reporting the latter as the primary metric or at minimum caveating the former.
3. Move the judge-human correlation results from the appendix into the main paper as a table or summary statistic.
4. Add confidence intervals or variance estimates to the AlpacaEval results.
