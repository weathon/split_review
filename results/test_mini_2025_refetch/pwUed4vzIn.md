## Summary

This paper proposes a computational model where intermittent Intrinsic Behavioral Variability (IBV) — inspired by prenatal myoclonic twitches — promotes flexible motor representations. A 4-joint simulated robotic arm with a small feedforward neural network is trained to reach targets under three conditions: no IBV (H0), IBV before training only (H1), and IBV intermittently throughout training (H2). Across three experiments (novel skill learning, amputation, and neural network knockout), H2 consistently outperforms H0 and H1 in both behavioral efficiency (fewer timesteps to reach targets) and neural weight variability (higher PCA-derived distances). The paper argues this supports the hypothesis that intermittent, non-goal-oriented variability facilitates adaptation.

## Strengths

1. **Well-motivated biological premise, tested systematically across three distinct challenges.** The paper draws on an interesting and timely question from developmental neuroscience (the function of prenatal myoclonic twitches and postnatal SMAs) and operationalizes it as three clearly defined scheduling hypotheses (H0, H1, H2). Testing adaptation to an external task change (Experiment 1), morphological change (Experiment 2), and neurological damage (Experiment 3) provides breadth — a single result could be task-specific, but the pattern across all three strengthens the case.

2. **Consistent advantage of the intermittent IBV condition (H2).** In Experiment 1, H2 significantly outperforms both H0 and H1 (ANOVA F(2,2997)=555.86, p≈4.7×10⁻²⁰⁶, with all pairwise Tukey HSD differences significant); in Experiment 2, H2 outperforms H1 (F(1,2400)=116.76, p≈1.3×10⁻²⁶); in Experiment 3, H2 outperforms H1 (F(1,7198)=56.97, p≈5.0×10⁻¹⁴). The behavioral advantage is reproducible across different perturbation types and is paralleled by significantly higher neural weight variability at measured timepoints (Mann-Whitney U tests, all p<0.001 in Experiment 1), providing convergent evidence.

3. **Connection to catastrophic interference.** The paper identifies a specific mechanism — intermittent IBV may prevent overtraining on context-specific actions — and notes that H2 recovers previously learned skills faster after the novel-target phase in Experiment 1 (Figure 2). This is a concrete computational hypothesis that connects the results to a well-known problem in neural network learning.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **The IBV mechanism as described does not guarantee the variable behavior it claims to produce (Section 2.4, Algorithm 1).** The IBV model trains the network to minimize MSE between its *input* (current joint angles + velocities) and its *output*, then applies that output as the action. This is an identity-mapping (autoencoder) objective: as the network converges, output ≈ input ≈ current state, meaning the applied action would instruct the joints to stay in place — the opposite of variable behavior. At the start of each IBV epoch, weights inherited from reaching training would produce non-identity outputs (hence some movement), but this would extinguish as training progresses. The paper provides **no behavioral analysis of what happens during IBV epochs** — no joint-angle traces, no output distributions, no loss curves — to empirically verify that the model actually generates "variable behavior" rather than a transient burst followed by convergence to stasis. This is a disconnect between the claimed mechanism and its operational definition. The biological grounding (tying IBV to spontaneous muscle twitches) is also loose: twitches are generated by spontaneous motoneuron firing with specific spatiotemporal structure, not by training a network to reproduce its own state.

2. **The H2 advantage is confounded by unequal total training (Experiments 1–3).** H2 receives additional IBV training epochs every 100 reaching epochs — these are extra gradient updates that H0 and H1 do not get. The paper never controls for total number of training steps or timesteps. The observed advantage could therefore be explained by *more* training rather than a *different kind* of training. The authors acknowledge a noise-injection control in the appendix (Figure 6), but it is not described in the main text, so its adequacy cannot be evaluated from the paper as presented. A proper control would give H0/H1 an equivalent number of extra gradient updates (e.g., continued reaching training) to isolate whether the type of IBV training matters.

3. **Inconsistent justification for dropping H0 in Experiments 2 and 3 (Section 4.2).** The paper states that "Experiment 1 gave strong indication that H1 would mirror H0's results" as the rationale for excluding H0 from the amputation and stroke experiments. However, Experiment 1's behavioral analysis found **significant differences between all three agents** (Tukey HSD: "significant differences between all agents when compared to one another"). The neural analysis did find no significant difference between H0 and H1 in weight variability, but this is not the stated basis. The claim that H1 and H0 are interchangeable is therefore contradicted by the paper's own main behavioral result. This selective baseline exclusion weakens the conclusions of Experiments 2 and 3, since those experiments cannot distinguish whether the advantage comes from intermittent IBV specifically, or simply from having *any* IBV at all (since H1, the only non-intermittent baseline retained, performed significantly worse than H2 in all three experiments — but what about H0?).

### Minor

4. **Neural variability analysis is under-specified (Section 3.2).** The paper describes "averaged the agents' weight matrices at each epoch of training" followed by PCA, but it is unclear what "averaged" means — across the 25 runs? Across hidden units? The "mean distance" in PC space used in Mann-Whitney U tests is not clearly defined. Higher weight-space variance could indicate beneficial exploration, or it could equally indicate training instability or failure to converge in H2. Without a clear definition and validation (e.g., showing that H2's weight variability correlates with behavioral flexibility rather than noise), the link between weight variability and adaptation is asserted, not demonstrated.

5. **Statistical reporting lacks effect sizes (Experiments 1–3).** The reported F-statistics and p-values are highly significant, but this is expected given the large number of observations (e.g., 30,000 data points in Experiment 1 with 1200 epochs × 25 runs). Without effect sizes (Cohen's d, η², or confidence intervals), the practical significance of a ~50–300 timestep improvement cannot be assessed. The very small p-values are likely inflated by the large sample and do not quantify the magnitude of the advantage.

### Trivial

6. The paper does not specify several details needed for reproducibility: exact target coordinates, joint link lengths, learning rate, optimizer parameters beyond "Adam," batch size, or random seed ranges. The hidden layer size varies per experiment (implied by "manually changed depending on the complexity") without justification.

7. The H0 hypothesis is framed as "learning across a diverse array of environments," but H0 is trained on exactly the same reaching tasks as H1 and H2 — no environmental diversity is added. This framing is misleading.

## Suggestions

1. **Replace or re-verify the IBV mechanism.** Either (a) replace the identity-mapping objective with one that demonstrably generates sustained variable motor commands (e.g., injecting random perturbations to the output, maximizing prediction error, or using a noise-driven exploration policy), or (b) add an empirical analysis showing that the current IBV model actually produces variable joint angles during IBV epochs and quantifying how long the variability persists before converging toward stasis.

2. **Control for total training.** Add a baseline where H0 or H1 receives the same number of additional gradient updates as H2, but using continued reaching training rather than IBV. This would isolate whether H2's advantage stems from the type (self-modeling) or merely the quantity of extra training.

3. **Include H0 in Experiments 2 and 3**, or provide a rigorous statistical justification for its exclusion based on the neural (not behavioral) results of Experiment 1, clearly acknowledging that behavioral performance differed significantly between H0 and H1.

4. **Clarify the neural variability metric.** Define precisely how weight matrices are aggregated, how PCA is applied, and what "mean distance" represents. Show that higher weight variability in H2 correlates with behavioral exploration (e.g., wider variance in end-effector positions during IBV epochs) rather than training instability.

5. **Report effect sizes** (Cohen's d or η²) for the behavioral and neural comparisons to complement the p-values.
