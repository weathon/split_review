## Summary

This paper proposes Direct Judgement Preference Optimization (DJPO), a training framework that applies DPO with three types of preference data (chain-of-thought critiques, standard judgements, and a novel response deduction task) to train generative judge models. The authors train models at 8B, 12B, and 70B scales, evaluating on 13 benchmarks across pairwise comparison, single rating, and classification tasks. Their 70B model achieves best performance on 10/13 benchmarks, outperforming GPT-4o and specialized judge models, while also demonstrating reduced positional bias, flexible prompting, and utility for downstream model alignment.

## Strengths

1. **Novel application of preference optimization to generative judge training with a well-motivated multi-task data design.** The paper introduces a principled framework that constructs three types of preference pairs (CoT Critique, Standard Judgement, Response Deduction) and optimizes with a DPO+SFT hybrid loss (§3). This is a clear departure from prior SFT-only judge models (Prometheus, Auto-J, FLAMe). The ablation study (§5.3, Fig. "training_task_ablation") confirms all three tasks contribute, directly supporting the claim that the design improves evaluation capabilities.

2. **State-of-the-art results on a comprehensive evaluation suite.** The 70B model achieves best performance on 5/7 pairwise, 2/4 single rating, and 2/2 classification benchmarks (Tables 1–3), including exceeding GPT-4o on RewardBench (92.4 vs 87.1) and top scores on HHH, InstruSum, and LLM-AggreFact. All three model sizes rank among the top four generative judges on RewardBench. The evaluation is unusually thorough (13 benchmarks across 3 task types), lending credibility to the claims.

3. **Introduction of a novel Response Deduction auxiliary task.** The paper proposes an original task where the judge reconstructs the original response from the evaluation protocol and critique (§3.3). The ablation confirms this task contributes to pairwise and classification performance — a conceptually new direction for improving judge understanding of response quality that prior work has not explored.

4. **Demonstrated robustness to evaluation biases.** The models achieve the highest positional consistency (91.41% for 70B) across six non-RewardBench pairwise benchmarks, outperforming GPT-4o-mini and Llama-3-OffsetBias by substantial margins (§5.3). On EvalBiasBench, results trail only the bias-specialized Llama-3-OffsetBias overall and match or exceed it on length- and concreteness-bias categories.

5. **Validated flexibility across prompting strategies and downstream utility.** The performance is stable across customized and generic prompts (§5.4), and the judge's scores and CoT critiques demonstrably improve downstream model alignment on AlpacaEval-2 beyond classifier-based reward models (§5.5).

## Weaknesses

### Major
None.

### Minor

1. **Missing ablation comparing loss function variants.** The training objective combines DPO and SFT losses (Eq. 1), motivated by the claim that positive examples are "nearly-gold." However, no experiment compares DPO-only, SFT-only, and DPO+SFT on the same data. The ablation in Figure 5 removes entire training tasks, not loss components. Since the paper's central claim is about preference optimization driving gains, showing that the DPO+SFT combination beats the alternatives would cleanly attribute the results to preference optimization rather than simply more supervised data.

2. **No verification of negative example quality in the Response Deduction task.** The negative examples for Response Deduction (§3.3) are generated by Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct under the assumption that a weaker model will produce *incorrect* deductions. The paper provides no human or heuristic check on whether these negatives are actually wrong. If some are correct (or partially correct), the DPO signal is noisy. A small-scale quality check (e.g., 50–100 samples judged by a human or a stronger model) would substantially strengthen confidence in the training signal.

3. **Contamination risk not discussed for benchmarks beyond LLM-AggreFact.** The paper carefully handles the LLM-AggreFact/RagTruth overlap (line 129), but does not discuss potential overlap with other evaluation benchmarks. The training data uses synthetic data "similar to those used by" Prometheus, OffsetBias, and others. Two evaluation benchmarks (PreferenceBench, FeedbackBench) are in-domain test sets for Prometheus models. A brief statement about why overlap is unlikely (or a check that none exists) would close this gap.

4. **Teacher-model comparison is relegated to the appendix.** The paper demonstrates the trained models improve over their base model counterparts (which is the same Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct used as the teacher) in the appendix (§app:basemodelablation, §app:instruct_models). While the comparison exists, its absence from the main paper weakens the narrative: the reader cannot immediately tell whether preference-optimization training improves upon the same model used to generate the supervision. Moving a summary of this comparison — especially showing the teacher's performance on the same 13 benchmarks — into the main paper would make the contribution self-contained.

5. **Training data ratio (70/15/15) reported without sensitivity analysis.** The 70% CoT, 15% Std, 15% Ded split is stated as a fixed choice with no exploration of how robust the recipe is to variation. The ablation shows all three tasks matter, but does the 70/15/15 split matter, or would 60/20/20 or 80/10/10 work equally well? A few points of variation would clarify the recipe's sensitivity.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- The bias analysis in §5.3 could discuss whether the Response Deduction task might inadvertently introduce certain biases (e.g., "familiar knowledge" or "empty reference") seen in the results.
- Reporting confidence intervals or variance across runs for benchmarks with small test sets (e.g., HHH) would help calibrate the significance of observed differences.
- A discussion of why the human-annotated FLAMe datasets were filtered for "modern (2023+) responses" and what this implies for benchmark relevance would address a natural reader question.

## Removed Points

- **Teacher-model comparison is "never reported."** The paper explicitly states this comparison exists in the appendix (\Cref{app:basemodelablation}, \Cref{app:instruct_models}), where the trained models are compared against their base model counterparts. Since the base model (Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct) is the same model used as the teacher, this criticism is factually incorrect — the comparison does exist. The point is downgraded to a Minor weakness about prominence rather than a major gap.

## Novel Insights

The reviewers' strongest convergence is that the paper is basically sound and well-executed but would benefit from one additional ablation (loss function components) to definitively attribute gains to preference optimization over data volume. The core methodological contributions — especially the Response Deduction task and the three-task preference data design — are genuine, and the evaluation is among the most thorough in the generative judge literature. The most insightful observation across the reviews is that the paper is one clean ablation away from being very strong: a DPO-only vs SFT-only vs DPO+SFT comparison on the full dataset would simultaneously address the main methodological concern and strengthen the novelty claim about preference optimization.

## Suggestions

1. Add an ablation comparing DPO-only, SFT-only, and DPO+SFT on the full training data using the 8B model to directly attribute gains to the proposed loss combination.
2. Include a summary of the base model (teacher) comparison on the 13 evaluation benchmarks in the main paper, not only the appendix.
3. Add a brief human or automated check on the quality of negative examples generated for the Response Deduction task.
4. Include a statement about dataset overlap checks for all evaluation benchmarks beyond the acknowledged LLM-AggreFact case.

## Score and Decision

The paper presents a genuinely novel application of DPO to generative judge training with a carefully designed multi-task preference data pipeline. The evaluation is unusually comprehensive (13 benchmarks, 3 task types, 3 model sizes), the results are strong (outperforming GPT-4o on most benchmarks), and the additional analyses (bias, prompting flexibility, downstream utility) add value. The identified weaknesses — missing loss ablation, unverified negative examples, contamination discussion gaps — are all addressable and do not undermine the core contribution. This is a solid paper that would be strengthened by the suggested ablations but is already above the acceptance threshold.
