## Summary

This paper proposes GOOD-AT, an adversarial training paradigm for graph neural networks that reframes graph structural attacks as an OOD detection problem. It trains an ensemble of binary edge classifiers on adversarial edges (generated by PGD) as OOD samples versus clean edges as in-distribution samples, then filters out detected OOD edges at inference to defend against evasion attacks. For poisoning attacks, it adapts a self-training strategy from prior work. The paper also hypothesizes a trade-off between attack effectiveness and defensibility when facing OOD-based defenses. Experiments on the adversarial unit test of Mujkanovic et al. (2022) over Cora and Citeseer show RAUC improvements over several prior defenses.

## Strengths

- **Novel OOD-detection-based defense with strong empirical performance on the standard unit test**: The core idea — treating adversarial edges as OOD samples and training an ensemble detector — is well-motivated and different from prior property-based defenses. The results in Figure 3 show GOOD-AT achieving RAUC close to the upper bound on Cora for evasion attacks and outperforming all seven prior defenses (RGCN, JaccardGCN, GNNGuard, ProGNN, SVDGCN, GRAND, Soft-Median-GDC). The self-training variant for poisoning attacks shows RAUC values 3–5× higher than other methods.

- **Evaluation against a diverse, standardized attack suite**: The paper adopts the adversarial unit test framework (Mujkanovic et al. 2022) with 7 adaptive attack types, multiple perturbation budgets (0–15%), 5 data splits, and the budget-agnostic RAUC metric — totaling ~25,000 perturbed graphs. The transferability analysis (Figure 4) further shows GOOD-AT achieves the highest RAUC on 6 out of 7 adaptive attack types when attacks are transferred across defenses.

- **Generality checks with different backbones**: Table 1 and the inductive experiment show that the approach works with different GNN backbones and that increasing the number of detectors improves performance (up to ~10 detectors).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Adaptive robustness against bespoke attacks targeting GOOD-AT is not quantitatively evaluated.** Section 6 describes two adaptive evasion attacks specifically designed to fool the OOD detectors (generating perturbations undetectable by the detectors, and incorporating the detector's output as a regularization term). However, these attacks are *not* included in the experimental evaluation in Section 7.2. The unit test evaluates attacks designed for *other* defenses' categories, not attacks tailored to GOOD-AT's OOD detectors. The paper provides only an anecdotal observation that these adaptive attacks "make the attack ineffective on the vanilla GCN." This leaves a significant gap between the claim in the abstract ("our method could still maintain good robustness against both adaptive and non-adaptive attacks") and the evidence provided. The bespoke adaptive attacks must be run and reported to support this core claim. The trade-off hypothesis does not substitute for experimental evidence.

- **The claimed "failure of traditional adversarial training" is not rigorously substantiated.** Section 4 states that graph adversarial training leads to learning "incorrect (feature, structure)–label mapping" and the paper refers to this as a "theoretical analysis" and claims "We theoretically show it will lead to the model learning incorrect mapping relationships." In reality, the paper provides only a brief informal argument (lines 66–68) with no formal proof, theorem, or illustrative experiment. Since this observation motivates the entire approach, the framing overstates certainty. The paper would be equally strong (and more honest) if it simply presented GOOD-AT as a practical alternative without claiming to have proven a failure mode of adversarial training.

### Minor

- **The trade-off between attack effectiveness and defensibility is presented as a contribution (Contribution 2) but remains a speculative hypothesis.** The paper acknowledges in Section 8 that Hypothesis 1 is not rigorously proven, but the abstract and introduction frame it as a "pivotal insight" and a main contribution. Listing an untested hypothesis as a core contribution without experimental validation (e.g., systematically varying adaptivity and measuring both attack success on the defense and on an undefended model) weakens the paper's novelty claims. The observation is interesting and worth stating, but the framing should be proportionate to the evidence.

- **The OOD detector is trained exclusively on PGD-generated edges, yet generalization to other attack distributions is not analyzed.** The paper trains detectors only on adversarial edges from PGD with tanh logit margin loss. While the unit test results show good empirical performance against diverse attack types, the paper offers no analysis of whether PGD-generated edges sufficiently cover the distribution of adversarial edges from other attacks (e.g., Metattack, the 7 adaptive attacks). A diagnostic experiment or coverage analysis would strengthen confidence that the detector has not overfit to PGD-specific patterns.

- **Threshold selection for OOD detection is unspecified.** The detector uses a threshold *t* (line 80) to decide whether an edge is OOD, but the paper does not state how this threshold is chosen — whether it is fixed, tuned on a validation set, or determined via some heuristic. If tuned on clean validation graphs, the paper should discuss whether the threshold remains appropriate under attack. This is a simple missing detail that affects reproducibility.

- **No adversarial training baselines are included.** Since the paper's motivation is that adversarial training "fails" for graph structure, a direct comparison against an adversarial-training-based GNN (e.g., the AT-GNN baseline from Xu et al. 2019 or a simple PGD adversarial training variant) should be included in the main experiments. Currently, none of the seven baselines are adversarial training methods, making it difficult to assess the claimed advantage over adversarial training specifically.

### Trivial

- **The experiments use only two small, homophilous datasets (Cora, Citeseer).** This is consistent with the unit test framework and prior work in the area, but it limits claims about generality to larger or heterophilous graphs. Not a barrier to publication given the field standard, but worth noting.
- **The self-training strategy for poisoning attacks is acknowledged as a minor modification of Li et al. (2023),** yet the abstract gives equal billing to both evasion and poisoning robustness. The novelty of the paper clearly lies in the evasion defense (GOOD-AT); the framing slightly overstates the contribution on the poisoning side.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, the most interesting synthetic insight across the reviews is that GOOD-AT's approach — treating the OOD detection problem on *edges* rather than nodes — naturally avoids the label-mapping issue that plagues standard adversarial training on graphs, because it decouples detection from classification. This structural insight is not fully articulated by the paper itself but emerges as the key design advantage. However, the paper's evaluation stops short of demonstrating that this advantage holds under attacks specifically designed to exploit the detector, which would be the most stringent test.

## Suggestions

1. **Run the two bespoke adaptive evasion attacks from Section 6 against GOOD-AT and report RAUC**, along with the performance on a vanilla GCN. This is the single most important gap to close.
2. **Add an adversarial training baseline** (e.g., PGD adversarial training on a GCN) to the comparison set in Figure 3 so readers can directly assess the claimed limitation of adversarial training versus the proposed OOD approach.
3. **Tone down the framing** of both the "theoretical analysis" of adversarial training failure and the trade-off hypothesis. The former should be presented as an informal observation/motivation; the latter should be clearly labeled as a hypothesis for future validation rather than a core contribution.
4. **Report the threshold selection strategy** for the OOD detector and include a brief sensitivity analysis.

## Score and Decision

The paper proposes a genuinely novel direction — using OOD detection on edges for graph defense — and achieves strong empirical results against the standard unit test benchmark. However, the evaluation has a critical gap: the paper claims adaptive robustness but does not quantitatively test against the bespoke adaptive attacks it designs in Section 6. Combined with overclaimed framing of the "theoretical analysis" and the trade-off hypothesis, these issues prevent acceptance in the current form. The core idea is promising and the results on the unit test are encouraging, but the paper requires major revisions — especially adding the missing adaptive attack experiments — before it meets the bar for publication.
