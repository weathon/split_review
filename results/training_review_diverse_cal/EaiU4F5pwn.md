## Summary

This paper studies the realistic but under-explored setting of reconstructing high-fidelity CFD data from *solver-generated* low-fidelity inputs (where low-res simulations are run on coarser grids) rather than from artificially downsampled data. The authors propose PG-Diff, a diffusion model with two complementary components: (1) a DWT-based Importance Weight during training that emphasizes high-frequency flow structures in the loss, and (2) a training-free Residual Correction module during inference that enforces PDE constraints via gradient descent. Experiments on four 2D turbulent flow datasets (4× and 8× upsampling) show consistent improvements over several baselines, with 3.5–7.7% relative L2 gains and significantly lower PDE residuals.

## Strengths

1. **Addresses a practically important data-generation gap.** The paper clearly distinguishes between "integrate then downsample" (artificial, the common assumption) and "downsample then integrate" (solver-generated, the real-world scenario). Figure 1 visually demonstrates that solver-generated low-fidelity data loses more fine-grained information, and the paper's experimental setup consistently uses solver-generated data, making the evaluation directly relevant to real CFD workflows.

2. **Two-component design is well-motivated and ablated.** Both the Importance Weight (high-frequency emphasis via DWT during training) and Residual Correction (PDE-enforcing gradient descent during inference) are directly motivated by the distribution-shift challenge. The ablations (PG-Diff w/o IW, PG-Diff w/o Cor) confirm both components contribute meaningfully, with the full model consistently outperforming the ablations across all datasets.

3. **Consistent empirical gains across diverse datasets and two upsampling factors.** Table 1 shows PG-Diff outperforms CNN, GAN, vanilla diffusion, and conditional diffusion baselines on Taylor Green Vortex, Decaying Turbulence, Kolmogorov Flow, and McWilliams Flow — the latter being the most challenging. The 3.5–7.7% L2 improvement and substantially lower PDE residuals are demonstrated with reported mean and standard deviation.

4. **Multi-scale evaluation via DWT decomposition** provides deeper insight into where the model excels (LL, LH, HL subdomains), going beyond aggregate pointwise error to show PG-Diff captures both large-scale features and fine turbulent structures.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Residual computation details are underspecified, creating a reproducibility gap.** The PDE residual ℛ(·) is central to both the correction procedure (Algorithm 1) and the evaluation metric, yet the paper never specifies how it is discretized on the 256×256 grid. The governing equations (Eq. 7) are continuous, the data is generated by a pseudo-spectral solver on 2048×2048 grids, but the residual correction operates on 256×256 reconstructions. The paper does not state: what discretization scheme is used (finite-difference, spectral, or the solver's own discretization)? How are spatial derivatives (∇ω, ∇²ω) computed at this resolution? This matters because if the same discretization is used for both correction and evaluation, the PDE residual metric becomes circular. This is the single most significant weakness — it must be resolved for the results to be independently verifiable.

2. **Novelty of the problem framing is somewhat overstated.** The paper argues that existing work "assumes low-fidelity data is artificially downsampled" and that the solver-generated vs. downsampled distinction is a new problem formulation. However, the core observation — that coarser-grid solver outputs differ from downsampled data in meaningful ways — is a natural consequence of numerical discretization, and several cited works (e.g., Erichson et al., 2020; Pradhan & Duraisamy, 2021; Gao et al., 2021) already work with solver-generated or physically-noisy low-fidelity data. While the paper is correct that *most* benchmark setups use downsampled data and that PG-Diff demonstrates advantages in the solver-generated setting, the framing as a "novel problem" is stronger than the evidence warrants. A more precise characterization of *which specific failure modes* prior methods exhibit on solver-generated data (beyond "distribution shift") would strengthen the contribution.

### Minor

1. **No hyperparameter sensitivity analysis for the Importance Weight.** The DWT-based weighting (Eq. 4) introduces three free parameters: quantile threshold θ, min/max weights α and β. The ablation (w/o IW) only removes the entire module, so the reader has no sense of how sensitive performance is to θ, α, or β. Are the chosen values brittle across datasets? A sensitivity study on one dataset would suffice.

2. **The generalization experiments are informative but limited.** Table 3 shows that PG-Diff trained on one configuration generalizes comparably to models trained directly on new configurations. This is a useful self-consistency check, but it does not compare PG-Diff's generalization to that of a standard diffusion model under the same distribution shifts. Without such a comparison, it is unclear whether PG-Diff generalizes *better* than alternatives or simply does not catastrophically fail — a lower bar.

3. **Missing training details for reproducibility.** The paper uses a U-net (Ho et al., 2020) but provides no layer counts, channel dimensions, learning rate, batch size, number of training steps, or optimizer settings. While some space constraints are understandable, these details are essential for reproducing the results.

4. **Computational cost not discussed.** The Residual Correction involves multiple Adam steps at selected diffusion steps. The paper does not report inference time or training time relative to baselines. Since this cost is a practical concern for real-world CFD workflows (where efficiency is a key motivation), it should be quantified.

5. **Residual correction schedule analysis is only on Kolmogorov Flow.** The optimal schedule (Start 2, End 2) and the study of correction-step count are evaluated only on one dataset. Showing similar analysis on at least one other dataset (e.g., McWilliams Flow, the most challenging) would strengthen the claim of generality.

### Trivial

- **Notation inconsistency in the problem setup (Section 2).** The test distribution for high-fidelity data is inconsistently referenced: it is written as $p_{\mathcal{X}}^{\mathrm{test}}$ (which conflicts with the low-fidelity domain notation) and then $\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{test1}}$ appears without definition. This does not affect the technical content but is confusing.

## Score and Decision

The paper addresses a genuine practical problem, proposes a well-motivated method, and provides solid empirical support across diverse datasets. The main concerns — missing residual discretization details and overclaimed novelty — are substantive but addressable. The residual computation detail is the most serious issue and must be resolved for reproducibility, but it does not invalidate the core claims. The paper would be strengthened by adding hyperparameter sensitivity analysis, computational cost reporting, and a broader generalization comparison, but these are not fatal. Overall, this is a solid contribution to the CFD-ML community.
