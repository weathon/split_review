## Summary

This paper studies providing multi-step recourse paths (sequences of feasible transitions) to individuals who receive negative decisions from a classifier. The authors introduce a PAC-learning framework that learns an optimal distance function and threshold from labeled transition data, then propose a data augmentation algorithm (Algorithm 2) that generates synthetic intermediate points to ensure every negatively-classified individual can find a valid recourse path. Experiments on synthetic, PIMA, Adult, and HELOC datasets show the method achieves 100% validity (every individual gets a recourse path satisfying the learned constraints), while FACE and nearest-neighbor counterfactual baselines do not.

## Strengths

1. **First formal PAC-learning framework for transition feasibility in multi-step recourse.** The paper proves VC-dimension bounds and efficient ERM learnability for learning distance functions and thresholds from labeled transition data, for both bounded hypothesis classes (Theorem 2.2, 2.3) and structured weighted-feature classes (Theorem 2.5, 2.6). This is a genuine theoretical contribution explicitly framed as novel in the literature (lines 44–45).

2. **Data augmentation algorithm that guarantees recourse for all individuals.** Algorithm 2 empirically achieves validity = 1 across all four datasets (Figure 2), directly addressing the failure case illustrated in Figure 1(b) where existing path-based methods leave some individuals without recourse when the distance threshold is stringent. The approach is principled: the objective in lines 6 and 8 of Algorithm 2 balances transition ease ($\lambda/w(x',y)$) against progress toward the decision boundary ($f(y)-f(x')$).

3. **Model-agnostic design.** The method requires only classifier probability outputs (lines 40, 186), making it applicable to black-box models commonly used in high-stakes settings.

4. **Ablation on the $\lambda$ trade-off parameter** (Figure 3) demonstrates the expected behavior: larger $\lambda$ produces paths with smaller per-step distance/weight but longer paths, providing practical guidance for hyperparameter selection.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Evaluation of path feasibility is partially circular.** The validity metric (lines 198–199) checks whether consecutive path steps satisfy $d(z,w) \leq \tau$ — where $d$ and $\tau$ are *the ones learned by the method*. The ground-truth feasibility labels are themselves generated by simple distance-based rules (per-feature L1 < standard deviation, monotonicity). The path evaluation therefore measures internal consistency with the learned approximation rather than directly verifying that generated paths satisfy the ground-truth rules. While Table 1 reports the 0–1 error of the learned model against the ground-truth labels (showing the learned model is reasonably accurate), the paper does not compute the proportion of path steps that would be labeled feasible by the ground-truth rules themselves. This gap weakens the claim that the paths are "feasible" in the sense promised by the title and abstract. The paper is transparent about how labels are generated and acknowledges this as a limitation (line 236), but the evaluation as presented conflates "satisfies our learned criterion" with "is feasible in practice."

### Minor

2. **Baseline comparison uses only the learned threshold.** FACE is evaluated using exactly the $d$ and $\tau$ learned by the proposed method (line 211). While this is a reasonable comparison to isolate the effect of augmentation (both methods operate under the same feasibility constraints), it does not explore whether FACE could achieve higher validity under a different threshold that still respects the ground-truth feasibility labels (e.g., one that maximizes coverage rather than minimizing classification error). The paper's claim that "existing methods fail to provide recourse for some individuals" is only demonstrated under one specific learned criterion. A sweep over thresholds would clarify whether the gap is due to the particular $\tau$ or a fundamental limitation of graph-search approaches.

3. **Convergence guarantee (Theorem 2.8) requires strong, unverifiable conditions.** The theorem requires that *for every* $x \in \mathcal{Z}$, there exists a $y \in \mathcal{Z} \setminus V$ such that $f(y)-f(x) > \lambda / \min_{a,b} w(a,b)$, and that the algorithm only picks fresh points. The first condition essentially demands that the classifier's probability can increase arbitrarily quickly relative to the smallest possible transition weight — a strong assumption that may not hold for many realistic classifiers. The paper appropriately calls this a "formal convergence scenario" (line 175) rather than a practical guarantee, and the empirical results provide the real support. Still, the gap between theoretical support and empirical claims should be acknowledged more explicitly.

4. **Weighting function for augmented-point transitions is not discussed.** The weight function $w(x,y) = d(x,y) / f_\rho((x+y)/2)$ uses a density estimate $f_\rho$ computed from the original data *without* augmented points (line 192). For transitions involving synthetic points, the density at $(x+y)/2$ may fall outside the support of the original data, requiring extrapolation. The paper does not discuss how the density estimator handles these out-of-support queries, nor how this affects the quality of the weight estimates used in Algorithm 2's optimization objective.

### Trivial

5. **The distance function chosen by ERM is not reported.** The paper notes that "the chosen distance was consistent across all 5 runs" (line 206) for each dataset but does not say *which* distance was chosen (L1, L2, Mahalanobis, Cosine, or Jensen-Shannon). Reporting this would strengthen reproducibility confidence.

6. **The structured/unbounded hypothesis class (weighted feature-wise distances, Theorem 2.5–2.7) is not tested experimentally.** All experiments use the bounded set of five fixed distance functions. The paper could note explicitly that the more general theory was not implemented.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting tension revealed across the reviews is the gap between the paper's strong formal learning-theoretic contributions (VC-dimension bounds, PAC guarantees for learning transition feasibility from labeled pairs) and the relatively simple, ad-hoc rules used to generate those labels in practice. The theory assumes access to a ground-truth feasibility oracle; the experiments approximate it with heuristic rules. This mirrors a broader challenge in algorithmic recourse: learning models of feasibility requires feasibility data, which is expensive or impossible to obtain at scale without resorting to domain-expert rules or causal models that are themselves difficult to validate. The paper's data augmentation technique partially sidesteps this issue by making the best use of whatever feasibility model is learned, but the fundamental bottleneck of obtaining reliable feasibility labels remains underexplored.

## Suggestions

1. **Validate against ground-truth feasibility rules directly.** Compute the proportion of steps in each generated path that would be labeled feasible by the *same ground-truth rules* used to produce the training labels (e.g., per-feature L1 < std). This breaks the circularity and would strongly support the claim that the learned model is a good proxy.

2. **Sweep $\tau$ for FACE.** Run FACE across a range of thresholds and report the smallest $\tau$ at which it achieves validity = 1 (or the maximum validity it can attain). This would clarify whether the baseline gap is due to augmentation or to threshold selection.

3. **Report which distance function was selected by ERM** for each dataset.

4. **Acknowledge the density extrapolation issue** for weights involving augmented points, or explain why it is not a concern (e.g., if Bayesian optimization naturally proposes points near the data manifold).

5. **Explicitly note the gap between Theorem 2.8's conditions and practice**, perhaps adding a sentence that the theorem is meant to provide intuition for convergence rather than a verifiable guarantee.

## Score and Decision

The paper introduces a well-motivated PAC-learning framework for feasibility models and a practical augmentation algorithm that empirically resolves a real failure mode of path-based recourse. The theoretical results are sound, the algorithm is clearly described, and the core idea — learning feasibility from labeled transitions and augmenting to fill gaps — is novel and useful. The main weakness is that the evaluation validates paths against the *learned* model rather than against the ground-truth feasibility rules; this weakens but does not invalidate the contribution. With additional validation against ground truth, the paper would make a stronger case. The paper represents a solid contribution to the algorithmic recourse literature and merits acceptance.
