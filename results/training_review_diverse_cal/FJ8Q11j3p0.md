## Summary

This paper proposes Ego-Foresight (EF), a self-supervised method for agent-environment disentanglement that predicts future visual frames from proprioceptive sequences. The key idea is that an agent can learn to distinguish itself from the environment by predicting its own visual appearance changes given its proprioceptive states — removing the need for supervisory agent masks required by prior work (e.g., SEAR). The method is integrated with DDPG as an auxiliary loss, and evaluated on DMC, Distracting DMC, and Meta-World benchmarks against DrQ-v2, SEAR, Dreamer-v3, and TD-MPC2. Results show competitive or better performance on several tasks, particularly on high-degree-of-freedom agents like Quadruped.

## Strengths

- **Self-supervised agent-environment disentanglement without mask supervision**: EF learns to separate agent from environment using only motion and proprioception, removing the need for segmentation masks or CAD models. This is demonstrated by matching or exceeding SEAR (which requires ground-truth masks) on multiple tasks, including outperforming it on the Quadruped task (16 DoF). The paper is explicit about building on Denton & Birodkar (2017) for the similarity loss and identifying the novelty in the proprioception-conditioned prediction as the self-supervisory signal — a clean contribution.

- **Competitive results on high-degree-of-freedom and distracting environments**: On the Quadruped (16 DoF) in DMC, EF achieves better relative performance than all baselines including the supervised SEAR. On Distracting DMC Cheetah with changing backgrounds, EF outperforms DrQ-v2, supporting the claim that learning self-prediction helps the agent ignore visual distractors.

- **Qualitative evidence of agent-body prediction and tool integration**: Figure 7 shows that the model accurately predicts the agent's arm motion while keeping static scene elements (e.g., a door) fixed, and also predicts the hammer's motion once grasped — indicating the model learns to treat tools as part of the agent's body schema. This concretely illustrates the claimed disentanglement.

- **Integration with model-free RL without full dynamics learning**: EF augments DDPG with a prediction auxiliary loss that only requires predicting the agent's future visual appearance, not the entire environment transition. This makes it computationally lighter than full world models like Dreamer while still providing representation benefits. The motor babbling stage is a pragmatic fix to a real exploration issue.

- **Broad evaluation across multiple benchmarks and agent morphologies**: The method is tested on DMC (4 agents with 2–16 DoF), Distracting DMC (2 tasks), and Meta-World (10 manipulation tasks), demonstrating generality across locomotion and manipulation. The paper also reports BAIR dataset experiments (appendix) showing the visuomotor prediction model works on real-world video data.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Motor babbling confound undermines sample-efficiency claims.** EF uses a 50k-step motor babbling phase during which the agent executes random actions and collects data for the visuomotor prediction model. The baselines (DrQ-v2, SEAR, Dreamer-v3, TD-MPC2) do not receive this free exploratory warm-start. On a 100k-step experiment, this 50k advantage is substantial — it is unclear whether EF's sample-efficiency gains come from the disentanglement objective or simply from having 50k steps of diverse exploration data before RL begins. The paper acknowledges this as a limitation but does not control for it experimentally (e.g., by giving baselines the same 50k random steps, or by pre-training the prediction model and transferring it across tasks). Without such a control, the central claim that "visuomotor prediction of the agent provides good feature representations" is confounded with the babbling data advantage.

- **Inconsistent and underpowered baseline comparisons.** The paper relies on published numbers for Dreamer-v3 and TD-MPC2 while re-running DrQ-v2 and SEAR. The number of random seeds varies across experiments, and the paper explicitly states they "failed to match the results reported" for SEAR. The Meta-World results show EF winning in 3/10 tasks, tying in 3, and underperforming in 4 — a mixed picture that does not clearly establish superiority. The claim "plainly achieves the best success rate in 3 out of the 10 tasks" is accurate but the overall pattern is one of comparable performance, not dominance. The inconsistent evaluation protocol (different seeds across setups, failed reproduction of SEAR) makes cross-baseline comparisons unreliable.

### Minor

- **Limited ablation scope.** The prediction horizon H ablation (Figure 8) tests only one task with three values; the similarity loss weight α ablation (Figure 9) tests only two tasks; the motor babbling duration (fixed at 50k) receives no sensitivity analysis. While the ablations that exist are informative, their narrow scope limits confidence in the generality of the design choices. A sensitivity analysis on babbling duration (e.g., 0, 10k, 50k, 100k) would be particularly valuable given the centrality of this component.

- **The motor babbling phase is underanalyzed.** Beyond the fairness issue, the paper does not investigate how much babbling is needed, whether it can be made adaptive, or how representation quality depends on the diversity of babbling data. The 50k fixed budget appears arbitrarily chosen. The paper itself notes that "in the Door Close and Button Press Wall tasks, our method starts being successful immediately after the 50k babbling steps, hinting that in those cases this stage could have been shorter" — suggesting adaptive stopping would be feasible.

- **Statistical rigor gaps.** The paper reports that greater variability in DMC results is "due to a lower number repetitions with different random seeds" and that "not all baselines and experiments use the same number of random seeds." While seed counts are deferred to the appendix, the fact that EF's own curves sometimes show larger variance than baselines makes comparisons less conclusive. The shading in the plots is described as standard deviation, but without consistent seed counts across all conditions, the uncertainty quantification is incomplete.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation in this review dialogue is that the reviewer correctly identifies the motor babbling phase as both the method's pragmatic enabler AND its greatest empirical weakness — the paper needs this phase to collect training data for the visuomotor prediction model, but the same phase also makes it impossible to cleanly attribute downstream RL improvements to the disentanglement rather than to simply having more exploratory data. This tension is inherent to methods that learn representations online: the data collection process for representation learning is itself an intervention that changes the training distribution. A promising future direction would be pretraining the prediction model on babbling data collected once per robot morphology, then fine-tuning on tasks — which the paper mentions as a possibility but does not evaluate. If such transfer learning works, it would simultaneously address the fairness concern and demonstrate practical value.

## Suggestions

- **Control for the babbling confound**: Run at least one experiment (e.g., Quadruped in DMC) where baselines also receive 50k random exploration steps, to isolate the effect of the disentanglement objective from the extra data. This is the single most impactful experiment that could strengthen the paper.
- **Standardize the evaluation protocol**: Use the same number of random seeds (at least 5) across all methods and all tasks. Report results with confidence intervals rather than mere standard deviation shading.
- **Add babbling duration ablation**: A simple experiment varying babbling length (0, 10k, 25k, 50k, 100k) on one or two tasks would substantially justify the design choice and help readers understand the method's data requirements.
- **Tone down claims**: The paper currently says EF "plainly achieves the best success rate in 3 out of the 10 tasks" in Meta-World, but the overall picture (3 best, 3 tied, 4 worse) supports a weaker claim closer to "competitive performance." Align the language with the evidence.
