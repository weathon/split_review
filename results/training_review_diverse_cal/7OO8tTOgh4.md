## Summary

This paper proposes MIE (Maximizing Information Entropy), a non-targeted white-box attack method for vision-language models (VLMs) that requires no ground-truth captions. The attack maximizes entropy at three levels of the Transformer decoder — logits, attention weights, and hidden states — and uses PGD optimization to generate adversarial perturbations. The method is evaluated on five open-source VLMs (BLIP, BLIP-2, InstructBLIP, MiniGPT-4, LLaVA) using 1000 ImageNet images, with results reported via CLIP Score and manual assessment.

## Strengths

1. **First non-targeted white-box attack for VLMs without ground-truth captions.** The paper correctly identifies that prior non-targeted attacks (e.g., Schlarmann & Hein 2023) rely on true image descriptions, while MIE uses only the model's own outputs. This is a genuine gap in the literature and the paper fills it cleanly (Section 2.2, line 60–62; Section 1, line 21).

2. **Architecture-agnostic design targeting universal Transformer components.** By attacking logits, attention weights, and hidden states — which are shared across Transformer decoders regardless of vision-language integration scheme (Image as Key-Value vs. Image as Token) — the method generalizes across diverse VLM architectures without modification (Section 2.1, lines 47–51; Figure 2). The paper validates this on five architecturally distinct models.

3. **Consistent attack effectiveness demonstrated across multiple models.** The CLIP Score results (Table 1) show a consistent drop from approximately 30 to approximately 20 across all five models under MIE, with MIE outperforming comparison methods. The visualization (Figure 4) provides supporting evidence that the attack indeed disperses attention and hidden state distributions, directly verifying the entropy rationale at the representational level.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Manual evaluation protocol lacks critical transparency, and BLIP's anomalously low clean accuracy is unexplained.** The paper reports a 96.88% average attack success rate based on "manual assessments" (line 219) but provides no information about: the number of human judges, whether they were blinded to condition, the sample size per model, the exact annotation criteria beyond a one-sentence description, or inter-rater agreement. The statement that "with the exception of BLIP, all other models achieve 100% accuracy on clean images" (Table 2 caption) is itself a red flag: it is implausible that every generated caption for 1000 ImageNet images is factually correct for four different models unless the evaluation standard is extremely lenient or the sample is much smaller than 1000. Conversely, BLIP's reported clean accuracy (27.2%) is drastically lower than all others, which the paper acknowledges but never explains — this suggests either a different evaluation standard was applied or the model was used in a configuration that does not reflect its intended use. Because these two problems (opaque protocol and unexplained BLIP baseline) compound each other, the headline 96.88% success rate cannot be taken at face value. The automated CLIP Score results provide separate evidence, but the manual evaluation — which is the paper's most striking quantitative claim — is not credibly supported as reported.

2. **CLIP Score metric is not fully specified.** The paper states that CLIP Score is "cosine similarity between vectors generated by CLIP's image encoder and text encoder" (line 200), which yields values in [-1, 1]. Yet Table 1 reports scores in the range of approximately 19–33. No scaling or transformation (e.g., ×100 or the Hessel et al. 2021 formulation) is described. While the *relative* within-model comparisons remain meaningful and the direction ("higher = better alignment") is clear, the missing specification makes exact reproduction difficult and prevents a reader from interpreting the absolute values. This is not fatal — the attack's relative effectiveness is still evident — but it is a gap in experimental reporting.

### Minor

3. **Hidden-state entropy maximization lacks theoretical grounding.** The paper applies a softmax transformation to hidden state vectors (line 155: "where $\mathcal{F}$ is the softmax function as $h_i$ is not a normalized probability") before computing entropy. Hidden states are not logits, and softmax produces a valid probability distribution regardless of whether the variation in hidden states is semantically meaningful. The paper provides no formal argument linking hidden-state entropy to output-level uncertainty. The ablation (Figure 3) empirically validates that the joint loss works best, but it does not disentangle the individual contributions of the attention and hidden-state terms from the logits term alone. This makes it unclear whether the hidden-state objective adds value beyond L1 (logits entropy) or whether any two-term combination would perform similarly.

4. **Ablation study is conducted only on BLIP.** The ablation of loss coefficients, perturbation size, and iteration count (Figure 3) uses only BLIP, which the paper itself identifies as the least robust model with an unusually low clean-accuracy baseline (27.2%). The optimal hyperparameters (e.g., the 8:1:1 ratio found in Figure 3-a) may not transfer to other architectures, especially larger models using different integration schemes.

5. **Comparison methods lack detailed implementation specifics.** The paper describes how each baseline was adapted (line 198: Carlini et al. using random targets for untargeted attack, Schlarmann & Hein using clean descriptions as labels, Aafaq et al. as a GAN-based method) but provides no details on hyperparameters, loss formulations, or how the GAN method was retrained or repurposed per model. While this level of description is common in many conference papers, the brevity limits reproducibility of the comparative results.

6. **BLIP's low clean accuracy distorts aggregate claims.** The paper reports BLIP's clean accuracy as 27.2% while all other models achieve 100%. For a model that already fails on roughly 73% of clean images, a 94.7% attack success rate does not convey the same information as it does for models with 100% clean baselines. Averaging across all five models to obtain 96.88% conflates these very different baselines.
