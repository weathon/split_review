## Score and Decision## Summary

This paper introduces Bisection Projection (BP), a framework to enforce feasibility of NN-predicted solutions for constrained optimization over **general compact sets with non-empty interior** — going beyond prior work that only handled ball-homeomorphic sets. BP projects infeasible NN predictions onto the constraint boundary along a line segment toward learned interior points (IPs). The paper introduces an **eccentricity measure** that bounds the bisection-induced optimality loss, trains a secondary NN (IPNN) to predict low-eccentricity IPs efficiently at inference, and provides theoretical guarantees (feasibility, bounded optimality loss, O(mKG) runtime). Experiments on convex (QCQP, SOCP) and non-convex (AC-OPF, JCCIM) problems show 100% feasibility with substantial speedups over prior methods.

## Strengths

1. **First framework to handle general compact sets beyond ball-homeomorphic assumption.** The paper clearly positions itself relative to existing work (Table 1), and the bisection procedure works for any compact set with non-empty interior (Assumption 1). Prior methods like LCL23/LCL24 required ball-homeomorphism, which excludes many practical constraint geometries. This is a genuine advance.

2. **Clean theoretical connection between eccentricity and projection distance.** Proposition 4.1 establishes an explicit bound: worst-case projection distance ≤ ε_pre + ℰ(X°, Γ_θ). This insight is novel and directly motivates the eccentricity-minimization objective used in IPNN training. The smoothed eccentricity loss (Proposition 4.2) with controlled approximation gap is practically useful.

3. **Empirically validated with strong results.** Table 2 shows BP achieves 100% feasibility across four problems (QCQP, SOCP, AC-OPF, JCCIM) with the fastest runtime (e.g., 0.39s for AC-OPF vs 7.12s for H-Proj, ~18× faster) while maintaining comparable optimality loss. Table 3 confirms that the eccentricity loss meaningfully reduces projection distance and that multi-IP strategies improve performance as predicted by Proposition 5.1.

4. **Rigorous treatment of multiple IPs and covering complexity.** Proposition 5.1 provides an explicit bound on minimum eccentricity scaling as O(m^{-1/(n-1)}), connecting the method to covering arguments. The paper acknowledges both the theoretical limits and the practical observation that 1-2 IPs suffice empirically.

## Weaknesses

### Minor

1. **The IPNN training bootstrapping is under-specified, though not circular.** The paper states (line 182) that the eccentricity loss "becomes active once IPNN outputs IPs under the first penalty loss," and that boundary samples are generated by bisecting infeasible NN predictions. But the paper never explicitly states how the **very first feasible IP** is obtained to initiate this process. The intended answer is that the penalty loss alone trains IPNN to produce feasible outputs via gradient descent (standard), and/or that the solver-generated training data (MOSEK/PYPOWER solutions, line 240) provides initial feasible points. However, this bootstrapping is left implicit. The paper would benefit from a brief clarification of the training initialization to avoid ambiguity. This is **not** a "chicken-and-egg problem" — the paper describes a sequential, not circular, process — but the description could be more explicit.

2. **Theorem 1's sufficient conditions are acknowledged to be hard to verify in practice, which the paper itself admits.** The sample-based condition requires Lipschitz constants C₀, C₁, C₂ that are generally unknown; the verification-based condition is NP-hard for ReLU networks. The paper states these limitations at lines 208-210 and in the conclusion (lines 266-267). This honesty is commendable, but it means the "universally valid IPNN" guarantee is more of a structural insight than a practical tool. The core contribution of the paper does **not** depend on Theorem 1 being actionable — the empirical results (Tables 2, 3) demonstrate that IPNN successfully generalizes in practice — but the framing of "universal validity" slightly overstates what is delivered.

3. **Scalability to high dimensions is unexplored.** The paper's largest problem has decision dimension n=13 (AC-OPF). The worst-case boundary sampling complexity scales as O((1/δ)^{n-1}) for covering the full boundary, and while the paper uses stochastic training to mitigate this (line 226), the method's behavior in substantially higher dimensions (n ~ 100+) is unknown. The paper advertises "large-scale networks" in the abstract but does not demonstrate or discuss when the method would break down due to dimension. A discussion of expected scaling limitations would be valuable.

4. **Mismatch between worst-case theory and average-case reporting.** Proposition 4.1 and Theorem 2 bound worst-case optimality loss, but Table 2 reports only average metrics. While average-case reporting is standard, the paper's theoretical selling point is a worst-case bound. Reporting the maximum (worst-case) optimality loss over the test set would better connect theory to experiment and is a small ask.
