## Summary

This paper introduces *prompt diffusion*, a method that uses a diffusion model in prompt space to generate sample-specific soft prompts for vision-language classifiers. Given a pre-trained prompt learning baseline (textual, visual, or multi-modal), the authors first overfit prompts per training sample via a few gradient descent steps, then train a diffusion transformer to learn the mapping from noise + image features to these overfitted prompts. At test time, the diffusion model generates a customized prompt from random noise conditioned only on image features. The method is evaluated as a plug-in to five baselines across 15 datasets in three settings (base-to-new, cross-dataset, domain generalization), showing consistent improvements of 1–3.5%.

## Strengths

1. **First application of diffusion models to prompt learning.** The paper proposes a genuinely novel approach: applying iterative refinement via diffusion in the prompt embedding space, replacing static prompts with sample-specific ones. This is a clear conceptual departure from existing deterministic prompt learning methods.

2. **Modality-agnostic plug-and-play design.** Prompt diffusion is shown to integrate as a plugin into five diverse baselines spanning textual (CoCoOp), visual (VPT), and multi-modal (MaPLe, PromptSRC, CoPrompt) prompt learning. The consistent improvements across all baselines and modalities support the claim of generality.

3. **Broad experimental validation.** The method is evaluated across 15 datasets in three challenging generalization settings (base-to-new, cross-dataset, domain generalization). The improvements are consistent — every baseline at every setting shows gains — which is strong evidence that the benefit is real, not an artifact of a specific setup.

4. **Ablation demonstrating diffusion's advantage over alternatives.** Table 4 compares diffusion against GAN, VAE, and normalizing flows under the same per-sample overfitting framework. Diffusion (78.02 harmonic mean) clearly outperforms GAN (75.99), VAE (76.17), and normalizing flows (76.32), showing that the choice of generative model matters beyond the overfitting pipeline.

5. **Efficient inference via ODE-based sampling.** Using the AMED-Solver, prompts are generated in just 5 function evaluations with only 0.045s added inference time per sample over CoCoOp, making the method practical for deployment despite using a diffusion model.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Conceptual asymmetry between training and test-time prompt generation is not analyzed.** The per-sample overfitting step produces targets V* using the ground-truth label (via CE loss). The diffusion model is then trained to predict V* from image features π. The paper does not analyze whether V* encodes class-discriminative information that is not present in π, nor whether the diffusion model is effectively learning a refined classifier through the prompt-generation pipeline. While the comparison against MLP/Transformer (Table 4) shows diffusion outperforms deterministic alternatives trained on the same V* targets, the paper provides no analysis of *what* the diffusion model learns — e.g., whether prompts generated for different classes with similar visual features are meaningfully different, or whether the gains come from the generative process or the noise injection acting as test-time regularization. Until this asymmetry is better understood, it is unclear whether the method's benefit stems from principled diffusion dynamics or from a more complex (and expensive) classification pipeline.

### Minor

2. **Missing standard deviations for all main results.** The paper reports that results are averaged over three random seeds (line 189), but no standard deviations or confidence intervals are provided. Given that the reported gains are typically 1–3% and the training regime is 16-shot (high variance), the reader cannot assess whether the improvements are statistically significant. This is especially important for domain generalization results (Table 3), where improvements on individual datasets are often <2%.

3. **Notation in Section 3.2 is confusing and underspecified.** The text introduces V_t*, Ṽ_t, Ṽ_t*, and V* with insufficiently clear definitions. The forward process on prompts and the distinction between the clean overfitted prompt V* and the noised version used as input are not crisply separated. The training loss in Eq. (6) uses an x₀-prediction objective (predicting V* directly) rather than the more standard noise-prediction objective — this is a valid choice but should be stated explicitly, and the connection to standard diffusion should be clarified. The paper references the appendix for a computational graph (line 157), but the main text should be self-contained on this point.

4. **Training cost of per-sample overfitting is not discussed.** The method requires running gradient descent for each training sample (5 iterations per sample). In the 16-shot settings evaluated, this is manageable (~80K total gradient steps for a 1000-class dataset). However, the paper presents prompt diffusion as a general "plug-and-play" module without quantifying the preprocessing cost in GPU-hours or discussing scalability to larger training sets. Readers cannot assess whether the method is practical beyond the few-shot regime without this information.

5. **Diffusion vs. generative baseline ablation is limited to one setting.** Table 4 compares diffusion against GAN, VAE, and normalizing flows only for CoCoOp on base-to-new generalization. Showing this ablation across additional baselines (e.g., VPT or MaPLe) would strengthen the claim that diffusion specifically — not any generative model of prompts — is responsible for the gains.

### Trivial

6. **The choice of 5 overfitting iterations as optimal is reported but not analyzed.** Figure 2 (right) shows accuracy peaking at 5 iterations. The paper does not discuss what happens at higher iterations (overfitting collapse? plateau?), leaving the reader to wonder why such a small number is optimal. A brief explanation would be helpful.

7. **The diffusion loss in Eq. (6) predicts V* (x₀-prediction) rather than noise (ε-prediction).** While this is a valid denoising objective, the paper never states this design choice or explains why x₀-prediction was chosen over the standard ε-prediction used in the preliminary section (Eq. 3).

## Nice-to-Haves

- An analysis of failure cases: Are there datasets or classes where prompt diffusion degrades performance relative to the baseline? The current results show improvements across the board — if truly universal, this is remarkable and deserves discussion; if there are exceptions, they should be reported.
- An experiment isolating whether the diffusion advantage comes from the generative process vs. test-time stochasticity (e.g., comparing diffusion with multiple noise samples against simple MC dropout or test-time augmentation).
- Visualization of the prompt embedding space to show whether diffused prompts meaningfully capture domain characteristics beyond what image-conditional CoCoOp already captures.

## Suggestions

1. Provide standard deviations for all main results, particularly the domain generalization table where gains are smallest.
2. Add an analysis section examining whether the diffusion model simply learns a more powerful classifier: compare the prompt embeddings generated for different classes with similar visual features, or compare the diffusion trajectory for same-class vs. different-class images.
3. Clarify the diffusion training objective: explicitly state that you use x₀-prediction (not ε-prediction) and justify this design choice.
4. Clean up the notation in Section 3.2 — a single consistent notation for clean prompts, noised prompts, and diffused prompts would greatly improve readability.
5. Report the wall-clock time for the per-sample overfitting preprocessing step for at least one representative dataset.
6. Extend the generative model ablation (diffusion vs. GAN/VAE/flow) to at least one additional baseline/setting beyond CoCoOp base-to-new.

## Score and Decision

This paper makes a novel contribution (diffusion in prompt space) and backs it with unusually broad empirical evaluation (5 baselines, 15 datasets, 3 settings). The consistent improvements provide credible evidence that the method works. However, the paper has a significant conceptual gap: it does not analyze whether the per-sample overfitting step (which uses labels) makes the diffusion model approximate a classifier rather than a genuine prompt refiner, and it provides no apparatus for the reader to assess statistical significance of the small gains. These are addressable issues, but they prevent the paper from being accepted as-is.
