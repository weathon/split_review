## Summary

This paper makes two main contributions: (1) **DeepFakeVox-HQ**, a large-scale deepfake audio dataset with 1.3M samples (270k high-quality deepfake from 14 sources), and (2) **F-SAT** (Frequency-Selective Adversarial Training), a method that applies adversarial perturbations only to the high-frequency magnitude components (4–8 kHz), motivated by an observed vulnerability of detectors in that range. The method is augmented with a RandAugment adaptation for audio. Results show strong performance on clean, corrupted, and attacked audio across multiple benchmarks.

## Strengths

1. **DeepFakeVox-HQ is the largest and most diverse public deepfake audio dataset to date.** The dataset includes 1.3M total samples, 270k high-quality deepfake samples from 14 TTS/VC sources, with both clean and noisy real audio. Table 1 shows it substantially exceeds prior datasets in scale, recency, and diversity. This alone is a valuable community resource if released.

2. **F-SAT demonstrably improves robustness without degrading clean accuracy.** Table 2 shows F-SAT+RandAug achieves 97.5%/98.4% real/fake on origin data, versus RawNet3's 96.1%/84.4%, and dramatically outperforms time-domain adversarial training (which drops to 94.7%/83.1% on origin). The method also shows strong gains on attacked data — 87.0%/92.8% on time/frequency attacks vs. RawNet3's 6.5%/17.9% on fake audio.

3. **Systematic ablation studies across attack types, frequency ranges, and hyperparameters.** The paper explores which attack domain (magnitude vs. phase) works best, identifies 4–8 kHz as the optimal range for F-SAT (Table 3), and analyzes the clean/robust loss trade-off (γ = 0.1 optimal). This provides actionable guidance for practitioners.

4. **Comprehensive robustness evaluation across 24 corruptions and multiple attack types.** Figure 5a shows the method outperforms baselines on every corruption (average +15.3% points), and Figure 5b demonstrates gains against both time-domain and frequency-domain attacks. The breadth of evaluation strengthens claims of practical robustness.

### Major

1. **Adversarial attack specifications for evaluation are completely omitted.** The paper reports robustness against "Attack (Time)" and "Attack (Frequency)" in Tables 2–3 and Figures 5b/6, but never states: the attack algorithm (PGD? 2. **The high-frequency vulnerability experiment (Figure 2) — the primary motivation for F-SAT — is critically underspecified.** The experiment is described in a single caption sentence. A different architecture?), (b) what kind of high-pass filter was applied (order, design, roll-off characteristic), (c) whether the filter was applied to training data, test data, or both. If applied only at test time, the observed "reliance" could reflect training distribution bias rather than an inherent property of the detection task. Figure 3 shows a confusion matrix comparing training sets, but no numerical accuracy values are reported in the text or a dedicated table. Table 1 compares "Ours" (which includes F-SAT + RandAug) against baselines, not the dataset in isolation. This claim should be directly verifiable.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation emerging from this review is the tension between the paper's core methodological insight (frequency-selective vulnerability) and the underspecification of the experiment that establishes it. The frequency-domain analysis in Figures 2 and 5b and Table 3 is internally consistent — fake audio features concentrate higher than real audio features, attacks in 4–8 kHz are most damaging, and training adversarially in that range works best. However, because the motivating experiment (Figure 2) lacks details about the model, filter design, and whether filtering was applied at train or test time, it is impossible to tell whether this is a genuine property of the deepfake detection task or a byproduct of the particular training distribution. This is precisely the kind of question that arises when a paper's strongest empirical contribution (the dataset) and its strongest methodological contribution (F-SAT) are connected by an analysis that is described too briefly to be properly evaluated.
