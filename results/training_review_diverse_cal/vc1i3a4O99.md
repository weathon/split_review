## Summary

This paper proposes a mutual-information-based method for explaining features learned by sparse autoencoders (SAEs) trained on LLM hidden states, along with two steering strategies (Amplification and Calibration) for modifying LLM behavior at inference time. The core idea is to explain SAE features using words from a fixed vocabulary selected by a mutual-information objective (rather than raw activation spans), which the authors argue mitigates a "frequency bias" that causes existing explanation methods to capture linguistic patterns rather than discourse-level semantics. Experiments on Mistral-7B-Instruct show higher explanation rates and improved jailbreak defense compared to baselines like TopAct and N2G.

## Strengths

- **Novel approach combining fixed-vocabulary explanation with mutual information for SAE interpretability**: The idea of replacing raw activation spans with a fixed vocabulary selected via a mutual-information objective is well-motivated and distinct from prior work (TopAct, N2G). The mutual-information normalization (Eq. 5), which penalizes words that activate many features nonspecifically, has a principled basis and is a clear improvement over a naive top-K dot-product ranking.

- **Identifies and formalizes the frequency-bias problem in SAE explanations**: Section 3.1 provides a topic-model-based formalization (Eq. 1–2) that articulates why discourse-level features may be harder to capture than surface-level linguistic patterns. This framing gives the paper a clear diagnostic story and motivates the methodological choices that follow.

- **Demonstrates practical utility for jailbreak defense**: The steering experiments (Table 3, Figure 3) show that the proposed method achieves a meaningful reduction in attack success rate (81.6 → 72.8 on Salad-Bench) with only a minor reduction in general helpfulness (MT-Bench: 6.5 → 6.0), and that both TopAct and N2G explanations fail to produce comparable steering results. This is a practically relevant demonstration.

- **Qualitative examples are informative**: Table 1 and the example in Section 4.3.2 (e.g., "contact, phone, unit, accuracy, exact...") concretely illustrate the claimed qualitative difference between word-set and text-span explanations, making the paper's central claim tangible.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Explanation evaluation confounded by output format asymmetry**. The evaluation in Section 4.2 asks a machine annotator (GPT-4o) to judge whether raw explanations are relevant to a generated summary. However, the raw explanations differ fundamentally across methods: TopAct and N2G produce short text spans (e.g., "used to," "CSV file"), while the proposed method produces sets of individual words (e.g., "contact, phone, unit, accuracy..."). A word set is trivially more likely to appear "relevant" to an abstract summary — each word can independently match different aspects of the topic — whereas a single short span either contains the topic or does not. The paper acknowledges the format difference (line 147) but does not treat it as a confound. Without calibration (e.g., having annotators produce summaries from both formats and then evaluating those summaries blind), the quantitative gap in Table 2 is uninterpretable as a measure of explanation quality. This undermines RQ1's primary quantitative evidence.

2. **Downstream experiment conflates explanation quality with feature selection**. In the steering experiment (Section 4.3), features are selected for steering based on their explanations: "We prompt the machine annotator to provide the harmfulness and safety labels for each learned feature by providing their explanations" (line 177). This means the comparison across explanation methods (Figure 3) uses *different feature sets* for each method — a method that produces poor explanations will also produce a poor feature selection, and the steering will fail regardless of whether the explanations *could* have been useful if the features had been correctly identified. The experiment therefore tests the full pipeline (explanation + selection + steering), not whether the explanations themselves are more useful for a practitioner who already knows which features matter. The claim that "baseline explanations fail" overreaches: it conflates the quality of the explanations with the quality of the selection they enable.

3. **Word embedding source is not specified, leaving a key component underspecified**. Equations 5 use dot products between word embeddings $\mathbf{e}_w$ and feature vectors $\mathbf{W}_c$ to estimate conditional probabilities. The paper states "By leveraging word embedding $\mathbf{e}_w$ of word $w$" (line 93) but never states where these embeddings come from — whether they are the LLM's own embedding layer, a separate embedding model (e.g., GloVe, word2vec), or something else, nor why the embedding space and the SAE feature space are assumed to be compatible (i.e., why dot products between them are meaningful). This omission makes a core technical component of the method incompletely specified and non-reproducible.

### Minor

4. **Limited empirical scope**. The experiments use a single model (Mistral-7B-Instruct), a single SAE instance, and a single layer (the 8th, chosen based on a heuristic from Lieberum et al. (2024) applied to Gemma2-9B). No evidence is given that this layer choice is optimal for either interpretation or steering on Mistral-7B. Generalizability across models, layers, and SAE configurations is unknown.

5. **Theoretical analysis of frequency bias is not empirically validated**. Section 3.1 derives a topic-model random walk and concludes that "discourse topics are rarer than linguistic patterns." While this provides useful intuition, the paper does not provide empirical evidence (e.g., activation statistics of learned features, analysis of what fraction of features correspond to discourse vs. linguistic patterns) that this frequency bias actually occurs in practice for trained SAEs. The analysis is motivating but remains unsubstantiated by data from the actual system.

6. **Vocabulary construction choices are not analyzed for sensitivity**. The vocabulary is constructed by tokenizing training data with regular expressions and keeping the 5000 most common words (line 147). No sensitivity analysis is provided for vocabulary size, tokenization rules, or the choice of 5000 as the threshold. It is unclear whether results would hold under different vocabulary construction choices.

### Trivial

- The method uses 10 words per explanation for the proposed method vs. 5 text spans for baselines (line 147). While different output modalities make this an apples-to-oranges comparison anyway (see Major #1), the asymmetry in quantity is an additional confound that should at least be acknowledged and ideally controlled.
- Table numbers and Figure captions in the extracted text appear as image references (likely due to PDF extraction), but the qualitative analysis in Table 1 and quantitative results in Table 2 are discussed substantively in the text.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Decouple explanation evaluation from feature selection** in the steering experiment: either (i) use a fixed, independently-identified feature set for all methods and compare how well each method's explanations allow practitioners to identify the relevant subset, or (ii) at minimum, explicitly decompose the steering experiment to show what fraction of the improvement comes from better feature selection vs. better explanation quality.
- **Specify the word embedding source** and justify the compatibility between the embedding space and the SAE feature space. Alternatively, compare against a simpler top-K dot-product baseline to isolate the effect of the mutual-information normalization.
- **Add an ablation study** comparing the full MI-based selection against a naive top-K word selection (without cross-feature normalization) to isolate whether the normalization in Eq. 5 matters.
- **Analyze vocabulary sensitivity**: test at least one alternative vocabulary size (e.g., 1000, 10000) or a different vocabulary construction method.
- **Provide quantitative operationalization of "discourse-level"** — for example, topical coherence scores, or human judgments comparing the explanation type generated by each method.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting takeaway from this set of reviews is the tension between evaluating a method "fairly" (by isolating individual components) versus evaluating it "practically" (by testing the end-to-end pipeline the authors actually intend practitioners to use). The harsh critic demands the former, but the paper's contribution is arguably the latter — a complete system for extracting and using SAE feature explanations. The confounds identified (output format asymmetry, conflation of selection with explanation quality) are real, but they stem from comparing fundamentally different explanation *representations* (word sets vs. text spans). This points to a broader challenge in the SAE interpretability literature: as methods diverge in the *kind* of explanations they produce (words, spans, graphs, etc.), standard evaluation methodologies have not kept pace. The paper's evaluation limitations are partly symptoms of an underdeveloped evaluation infrastructure in the field, not just author oversight. Future work would benefit from a benchmark that normalizes explanation format before comparing quality.

## Suggestions

1. **For the explanation evaluation**: Conduct a controlled experiment where the *format* is held constant — for example, use the proposed method's word sets as the explanation format for all methods (for baselines, extract the top-K most distinctive words from their activation spans rather than the spans themselves). Then re-run the machine annotator evaluation.
2. **For the steering experiment**: Either (a) identify a fixed set of safety-relevant features via an independent procedure (e.g., human annotation of raw activations on a small set of examples) and then test how well each explanation method allows a classifier to identify the safety-relevant subset, or (b) acknowledge the confound explicitly and provide an ablation that decomposes the pipeline.
3. **Specify the word embedding source** used in Eq. 5 and add a brief justification for why dot products between word embeddings and SAE feature vectors can be interpreted as (un-normalized) log-probabilities.
4. **Soften the theoretical claims** — replace "a key challenge ... revealed" with "a hypothesized challenge motivated by a topic-model analogy" and add empirical validation (e.g., show that features with high-frequency activations correspond to linguistic patterns) or acknowledge the absence thereof.
5. **Add a sensitivity analysis** for the vocabulary size (e.g., 1000, 5000, 10000) and the number of selected words K (e.g., 5, 10, 20) to demonstrate robustness.

## Score and Decision

The paper proposes a genuinely interesting and well-motivated approach to SAE feature explanation. The core methodological idea — using a fixed vocabulary with mutual-information-based selection — is novel and practically appealing. The jailbreak defense results are promising and suggest real utility. However, the evaluation has two structural confounds (output format asymmetry in the explanation comparison, and conflation of explanation quality with feature selection in the steering comparison) that prevent the paper from convincingly demonstrating its central claims. These are not fatal in principle — the method could still be useful even with imperfect evaluation — but in their current form, the experiments do not cleanly establish that the proposed explanations are superior for either interpretation or steering, because the comparisons are not fair tests. The underspecified word embedding source is a reproducibility concern. Addressing these would require non-trivial additional experiments.

I recommend rejection of the current manuscript, but a significantly revised version that addresses the evaluation confounds could be considered for acceptance at a future venue.
