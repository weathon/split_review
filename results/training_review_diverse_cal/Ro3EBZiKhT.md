## Summary

This paper proposes HiLoRL, a hierarchical reinforcement learning framework that uses a Differentiable Logic Machine (DLM) as a trainable high-level logical planner, combined with a pool of low-level action policies for continuous control. The high-level planner learns logical predicates from continuous MDP states, selects low-level policies, and the whole system can be jointly trained. The paper also demonstrates automaton synthesis for interpretability and expert knowledge injection via special predicates. Experiments are conducted on highway driving and Fetch Pick&Place tasks.

## Strengths

- **Adaptive logical planner without fixed specifications.** Unlike prior work such as DiRL (Jothimurugan et al., 2021), which requires a human-coded logical specification upfront, HiLoRL learns high-level decision rules autonomously. The fetch experiments show that when a low-level policy fails (e.g., to grasp an object), the high-level planner can revert and restart — a capability not present in fixed-specification approaches. Table 3 reports >90% success rates on Pick&Place tasks, competitive with DiRL.

- **Interpretability via automaton synthesis.** The paper introduces a method to extract a Deterministic Finite Automaton from the trained high-level planner by tracing weight paths backward through the DLM, tracking focused input predicates, and applying Hopcroft minimization. Figure 2 shows a concrete 7-state automaton for an overtaking task with natural-language state descriptions, going beyond what prior ILP-based RL methods (DLM, NLM) provide.

- **Expert knowledge injection demonstrably improves performance.** Section 4.4 shows that adding a single expert-designed predicate (L1-norm distance between gripper and object) reduces the failure rate by more than 50% and requires fewer training epochs. This demonstrates a practical mechanism for incorporating human insight without altering the core algorithm.

- **Joint training provides clear gains.** The comparison between HiLoRL and HiLoRL\* (without joint training, Tables 1 and 3) shows substantial improvements from joint training — e.g., on highway, velocity increases from 16.5 to 27.1; on Pick&Place, success rate jumps from ~70% to ~97%. This supports the claim that the symbiotic training loop is effective.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None. The paper's core contributions (adaptive logical planner, automaton synthesis, expert knowledge injection) are real and demonstrated, even if with limited rigor.

### Major

- **Predicate generation from MDP states is critically underspecified.** The paper states that predicates are generated by "going through the combinations of the transformation functions, the indices, and the activation intervals" (Section 3.1). For a state space ℝ²⁵, transformation functions up to arity 3, and continuous intervals (u,v), the combinatorial space is enormous. The paper provides no details on: (a) how this search is pruned, (b) how activation intervals are initialized or learned, (c) whether intervals are fixed or optimized, (d) how many predicates are retained per dimension, or (e) the exact predicate generation parameters used in each environment. Without this, the method cannot be reproduced, and it is unclear whether the input space to the high-level planner is feasibly sized or essentially hand-crafted through unreported engineering.

- **Low-level action policies are unspecified.** The paper "assume[s] access to a pool of pre-selected and pre-trained action policies" (Section 3.2) but never specifies: what these policies are (neural networks? PID controllers?), how many exist, how they were pre-trained, their architecture or input space, or whether separate policies exist for each sub-task (approach, grasp, lift, place) in the fetch experiments. The entire experimental evaluation depends on this unspecified component. Even if the assumption follows prior work (DiRL, Yang et al., 2020), the paper must state what policies were actually used in these specific experiments.

- **No statistical reliability in reported results.** All tables report point estimates (single success rates, velocities, crash rates) without standard deviations, confidence intervals, or number of random seeds. For RL experiments, this is a structural weakness: the gap between HiLoRL (94.21%) and DiRL (91.23%) on PickLiftPlace could easily be within noise. Given that HiLoRL involves multiple interacting training stages, variance is likely higher than flat baselines. The paper explicitly claims "superior performance" but never demonstrates statistical significance.

- **Joint training procedure is described at an aspirational level, not an algorithmic one.** Section 3.2.3 states that "parameters for both the high-level and low-level models are diligently updated" and "each layer informs and refines the other," but provides no concrete optimization schedule, loss function interaction, update frequency, or convergence criteria. Joint training is central to the method's reported performance gains, yet the mechanism by which it improves the high-level planner beyond sequential training is never explained or ablated. This makes it impossible to tell whether the improvement comes from the surrogate reward (Eq. 4), from better low-level policies alone, or from an unaccounted interaction.

- **No hyperparameters provided for any experiment.** The paper does not report learning rates, batch sizes, number of training epochs, volley length, PPO clipping parameter, DLM depth D and breadth B, the hyperparameter α in Eq. (4), or any other setting. This is a fundamental reproducibility gap.

### Minor

- **The "logically uncertain tasks" claim is only partially evaluated.** The paper emphasizes that HiLoRL can handle tasks "where the number and execution sequence of all sub-tasks may vary in every execution." However, there is no experiment that directly manipulates task uncertainty — no comparison of performance when sub-task sequences are fixed vs. variable, no measurement of how often the high-level planner replans, and no demonstration of adaptation to out-of-distribution logical structures. The highway environment does involve varying traffic, and the fetch experiments show failure recovery, so the claim is partially supported, but an explicit ablation would substantially strengthen it.

- **The automaton synthesis evaluation relies on human interpretation of extracted predicates.** The synthesized automaton (Figure 2) has states labeled with human-assigned semantic descriptions (e.g., q₁ ← ∃(car ahead)). While the focused predicate extraction procedure is described, the paper does not formally verify that the extracted predicates correspond to the claimed environmental conditions relative to the ℝ²⁵ state vector, nor does it compare the synthesized automaton to ground-truth task structure. The interpretability claim is therefore partially based on manual interpretation.

- **Some baseline comparisons are not informative.** Tables 1 and 2 compare HiLoRL against SAC, PPO (flat RL methods), DLM, and NLM. SAC and PPO are well-known to struggle with sparse-reward continuous control tasks, and the paper itself notes DLM and NLM "are not even designed to support continuous control scenarios." These comparisons do not strengthen the paper's case. The meaningful comparison is Table 3 against DiRL, which is appropriate.

- **Expert knowledge injection experiment is limited.** Section 4.4 tests only one type of expert predicate (L1-norm distance) in one environment. While the results are positive, this is insufficient to support the general "instructable" claim made in the contributions.

- **No computational cost analysis.** The paper presents training time advantages from expert knowledge but reports no wall-clock training time or comparison with DiRL's computational cost.

## Suggestions

3. Add standard deviations and number of random seeds to all tables (at least 5 seeds; 10 is better).
4. Provide full hyperparameter tables for all experiments.
5. Include an explicit ablation or experiment that varies sub-task uncertainty and measures HiLoRL's adaptive capability vs. a fixed-specification baseline.

The core ideas have merit and the experimental results, while lacking statistical rigor, are directionally positive. However, the paper has major flaws in its current form: the method is critically underspecified in several key components (predicate generation, low-level policies, joint training), no hyperparameters are provided, and the experimental evaluation lacks the statistical rigor expected for RL papers.
