## Summary

This paper studies offline preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL) and proposes Adversarial Preference-based Policy Optimization (APPO). The key idea is to formulate PbRL as a two-player Stackelberg game between a policy and a reward/value model, using a novel reparameterization (via the performance difference lemma) to eliminate the need for online rollouts. The paper provides a sample complexity bound (Theorem 4.1) under standard function approximation and trajectory concentrability assumptions, and presents a practical deep-RL implementation evaluated on Meta-world continuous control tasks.

## Strengths

1. **Novel reparameterization that enables fully offline adversarial training for PbRL.** The use of the performance difference lemma (Section 3.3, equation 6) to rewrite the adversarial objective purely in terms of value functions and offline data is technically clever and is the core innovation that allows Algorithm 2 to avoid online rollouts entirely.

2. **First computationally efficient offline PbRL algorithm with provable sample complexity.** Unlike FREEHAND-transition (Zhan et al., 2024a) and Sim-OPRL (Pace et al., 2024), which require intractable confidence-set constructions or uncertainty penalties, Algorithm 2 relies only on standard gradient-based optimization. Theorem 4.1 provides a finite-sample bound, and the paper correctly contrasts its tractability against prior work (Section 3.3, lines 176-182).

3. **Practical implementation demonstrates feasibility on continuous control tasks.** On Meta-world medium-replay tasks (Table 1), the practical version of APPO achieves success rates comparable to or exceeding strong baselines (MR, PT, DPPO, IPL), and even outperforms the ground-truth-oracle-trained policy on two tasks. Figure 2 shows robustness to preference dataset size, with strong performance at as few as 100 labels.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The theoretical analysis and the implemented algorithm operate at different levels of approximation, and the gap is unaddressed in the main text.** The sub-optimality decomposition in the proof sketch (Section 4, lines 222-231) defines the induced reward $r^t = r_{P^\star, f^t}^{\pi^t}$ and bounds $\mathcal{E}(f^t; P^\star, \hat{r})$ — using the true transition $P^\star$ throughout. However, Algorithm 2 (line 120) optimizes $\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{traj}}}(f; \hat{P}, \hat{r})$ using the **estimated** transition model $\hat{P}$ and finite-sample expectations. The paper acknowledges using $\hat{P}$ "in place of $P^\star$" (line 174) and calls this an "approximation" (line 171), but the main-text analysis never quantifies how this substitution — or the finite-sample error — affects the guarantee. The practical implementation (Section 5) goes further, replacing even $\hat{P}$ with a one-step sample approximation $(Q_\phi \circ \pi_\theta)(s_{l+1})$ (line 251). The overall presentation treats "APPO" as a single entity, but three different variants (analyzed with $P^\star$, implemented in Algorithm 2 with $\hat{P}$, deployed in experiments with sample approximations) are conflated without analyzing the propagation of errors. This weakens the central claim that APPO offers "both statistical efficiency and practical applicability" as a unified method.

2. **Experimental evaluation is too narrow to support the claim of "practical applicability."** The experiments are limited to five tasks on Meta-world medium-replay datasets, with all baseline numbers taken verbatim from a prior paper (Choi et al., 2024) rather than re-run in a controlled setting. Only success rate is reported — no average returns, confidence intervals for baselines, or computational efficiency metrics (wall-clock time, gradient steps). The absence of any comparison on standard offline RL benchmarks (e.g., D4RL MuJoCo locomotion) or an ablation isolating the effect of the $\ell_1$ trajectory-pair loss versus standard MLE-only reward learning makes it difficult to assess what drives APPO's empirical performance. The paper would benefit from a within-codebase comparison where all methods share the same infrastructure, seeds, and evaluation protocol.

### Minor

3. **Assumption 3 (value function realizability) is strong and undiscussed.** The assumption requires that *every* $Q^\pi_{h,r}$ for *any* policy $\pi$ and *any* reward $r$ belongs to a prescribed function class $\mathcal{F}$. For neural network parameterizations of fixed architecture, this is extremely demanding and unlikely to hold exactly. The paper does not comment on the plausibility of this assumption or what happens when it is violated.

4. **The claim of "only one algorithmic hyperparameter" (line 293) is slightly overstated.** The practical implementation (Section 5) also introduces the entropy regularization coefficient $\alpha$ in the policy loss (line 277), which is an additional algorithmic choice. While this does not undermine the paper's overall simplicity, the claim as written is imprecise.

5. **The proof sketch introduces an "approximation error $\epsilon$" (line 231) without clarifying its dependence on problem parameters.** The sketch asserts that $\epsilon$ is "small" and that term (II) is bounded by it, but does not indicate how $\epsilon$ scales with $N$, $H$, $|\mathcal{F}|$, or the transition estimation accuracy — even at a high level. This makes the sketch less informative than it could be.

## Suggestions

1. **Align the theory with the practice.** Either modify the theoretical analysis to explicitly account for the $\hat{P}$-for-$P^\star$ substitution (via standard MLE concentration) and present that analysis in the main text, or adjust Algorithm 2 to match what is analyzed. 2. **Expand the experimental evaluation.** Add at minimum 2-3 tasks from a second benchmark (e.g., D4RL MuJoCo) to demonstrate broader applicability. Re-run all baselines in the same codebase with controlled seeds to enable fair comparison with confidence intervals.

3. **Discuss the value function realizability assumption** (Assumption 3) and what theoretical relaxations are possible (e.g., misspecification bounds).

4. **Clarify the hyperparameter count** — distinguish between the primary algorithmic parameter $\lambda$ and auxiliary parameters ($\alpha$, learning rates) that also require tuning.

## Score and Decision

The paper presents a genuine theoretical contribution: the reparameterization-based adversarial framework for offline PbRL is novel, and Algorithm 2 is indeed more computationally tractable than prior confidence-set-based approaches. The theoretical analysis is structurally coherent and the practical experiments demonstrate feasibility.

However, the gap between the algorithm analyzed (with $P^\star$ in the sub-optimality decomposition) and the algorithm actually deployed (using $\hat{P}$ and further sample approximations) is too wide for the central claim — that APPO offers *both* statistical efficiency *and* practical applicability as a unified method — to be fully supported as written. The experimental evaluation, limited to five tasks on a single benchmark with externally-sourced baseline numbers, does not provide the breadth of validation that such a claim requires. A revision that bridges the theory-practice gap (either by analyzing the approximations or redesigning the algorithm to match the analysis) and substantially expands the experimental scope could bring the paper to an acceptable standard.
