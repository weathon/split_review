## Summary

This paper introduces Shot2Story, a benchmark of ~20K multi-shot web videos (10–40s, 2–8 shots each) with shot-level visual captions, narration captions (linking speech to visual referents), and GPT-4-generated video summaries (averaging 201.8 words), all human-verified. The benchmark includes three tasks: single-shot captioning, multi-shot summarization, and retrieval with shot descriptions. The most compelling result is that a summarization model trained purely on Shot2Story, combined with a frozen Vicuna, achieves **56.8% on MSRVTT-QA** and **47.4% on ActivityNet-QA** (vs. Video-ChatGPT's 53.7% and 37.4%) without any QA-specific instruction tuning, demonstrating the transfer value of the dataset's comprehensive summaries.

## Strengths

- **Fine-grained shot-level annotations with separate visual and narration captions.** Unlike prior benchmarks (MSRVTT, ActivityNet Caps, VAST) that provide holistic or coarsely segmented descriptions, Shot2Story annotates separate visual captions (avg. 35.3 words) and narration captions (avg. 17.8 words) per shot. This granularity enables explicit modeling of multi-shot structure and audio-visual alignment — a genuine gap in existing benchmarks.

- **Summarization models trained on Shot2Story transfer to out-of-domain QA without instruction tuning.** The zero-shot QA results (Table 5) are the paper's strongest evidence of practical value: SUM-shot+Vicuna outperforms Video-ChatGPT's text-only baseline by +3.1 on MSRVTT-QA and +10.0 on ActivityNet-QA. ActivityNet videos are much longer than Shot2Story's 10–40s range, and MSRVTT covers out-of-domain topics (TV shows, food), so this transfer is nontrivial.

- **Controlled ablations isolating the contributions of shot structure and ASR.** SUM-shot (explicit shot structure) consistently outperforms SUM-holistic (no shot structure) by +0.8 BLEU, +1.4 METEOR, +2.3 CIDEr. SUM-shot w/o ASR underperforms SUM-shot (CIDEr 4.7 vs. 8.6). These ablations provide clear evidence that both shot-level segmentation and narration information contribute to summarization quality, supporting the benchmark's design rationale.

- **Novel retrieval benchmark with shot-description queries.** The three retrieval settings (T2V, T2S, V2T) using shot-level descriptions are a natural extension of the dataset, and evaluation of strong baselines (Alpro, CLIP4CLIP, UMT) establishes initial reference points.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **No quantitative validation of annotation reliability.** The paper provides no inter-annotator agreement scores, no measurement of the fraction of GPT-4 summaries that required correction, no analysis of correction types or severity, and no description of annotator training or qualification. For a benchmark dataset that aspires to be a community resource, this is the paper's most significant gap. The reader cannot assess whether the shot captions and summaries are consistently accurate, or whether the LLM-driven pipeline introduces systematic biases (e.g., stylistic uniformity, hallucination residues that annotators missed). This undermines confidence in the dataset as a ground-truth benchmark for all downstream tasks.

### Minor
- **No human baselines for any task.** The paper does not provide human performance estimates for single-shot captioning, multi-shot summarization, or narration captioning. Without calibration against human-level performance, it is impossible to tell how much room for improvement remains and whether the automatic metrics (BLEU, METEOR, ROUGE, CIDEr) are meaningful for these long-text settings. This weakens the paper's ability to characterize task difficulty.

- **No ablation of the aggressive filtering pipeline.** From 1.1M clips, only ~20K (1.8%) survive the filtering criteria (CLIP correlation >0.25, inter-shot similarity <0.9, static-content removal). The paper provides no ablation varying these thresholds, no analysis of topic diversity or shot-length distribution in the surviving set, and no evidence that the filters do not introduce systematic biases. The concern is not that the filtering is wrong, but that its effect on dataset representativeness is uncharacterized.

- **No ASR-only baseline for the zero-shot QA experiments.** The QA experiments (Table 5) use summaries generated by SUM-shot (which consumes both video and ASR) fed to Vicuna. The paper does not compare against a baseline where ASR text alone is fed to Vicuna for QA. Without this, it is unclear how much of the QA improvement comes from the summary capturing visual information versus simply repackaging the ASR content. A simple ablation (ASR → Vicuna directly) would address this.

- **Missing dataset release, license, and annotator details.** The paper does not state whether/how the dataset will be released, under what license, or provide annotator background, count, per-video cost, or annotation time. These are standard expectations for a benchmark dataset paper.

- **No human evaluation of summary quality.** Beyond automated captioning metrics (B, M, R, C) and the indirect QA transfer, there is no human evaluation of summary fluency, factual consistency, or coverage. Given that summaries are the paper's marquee feature (201.8 words), direct human assessment would strengthen claims about their coherence and comprehensiveness.

### Trivial
- **The comparison between VALOR (raw audio) and the ASR-text model in narration captioning (Table 2)** is comparing fundamentally different input modalities — VALOR processes raw audio waveforms, while the ASR-text model receives transcribed speech. The CIDEr gap (13.5 vs. 130.9) is unsurprising and does not constitute a meaningful comparison. The paper should clarify that these baselines operate on different input representations and simply report both for completeness.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A human evaluation of generated summaries on a 100–200 video sample (fluency, factual consistency, coverage) and/or human-written summaries on a held-out subset to establish an upper bound for the summarization task.
- Training a simple retrieval model (e.g., fine-tuning CLIP) on the shot-caption pairs to make the retrieval benchmark a genuine Shot2Story-trained evaluation rather than only off-the-shelf model evaluation.

## Removed Points

Benchmark papers routinely evaluate existing models to establish reference scores; training new models on the dataset is follow-up work. The retrieval section is a benchmark, not a method contribution.
- *"Video QA is not zero-shot in any meaningful sense"* — The paper explicitly defines its zero-shot setting: the summarization model is trained on Shot2Story, the Vicuna model is frozen, and no QA-specific training data is used. This is a standard and defensible use of "zero-shot" for transfer from summarization to QA. The paper is clear about this.
- *"Exposition mixes motivational claims with speculative benefits"* — This is normal for a dataset paper's conclusion and not a substantive weakness. The claim about being "ready to be extended" is appropriate scoping language.

Demanding SOTA-level comparisons on a brand-new benchmark task before the task is even established is scope creep.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation from the reviews is that the two-stage pipeline (SUM-text) outperforms the end-to-end shot-structured model (SUM-shot) on summarization. This is a nontrivial finding: it suggests that the bottleneck in end-to-end multi-shot video summarization is not the availability of shot-level supervision, but rather the ability of the multimodal encoder to compress shot information into tokens without losing the temporal and referential structure that a separate captioning stage preserves. The fact that SUM-shot still outperforms SUM-holistic (+0.8–2.3 across metrics) confirms that shot structure is useful, but the gap between SUM-text and SUM-shot points to a specific architectural challenge — how to give an end-to-end model access to shot boundaries and within-shot frame diversity without overwhelming the LLM with token budget. This tension is precisely the kind of benchmark-driven research question Shot2Story aims to enable.

## Suggestions

1. **Add inter-annotator agreement** for a sample of videos (e.g., 200 videos, 2–3 annotators) for both shot captions and summaries. Report pairwise CIDEr/Pyramid scores and the fraction/nature of corrections to MiniGPT-4 and GPT-4 outputs. This single addition would address the most serious weakness.

2. **Include at least one human baseline** — either human-written summaries for a 100-video subset or human performance on the single-shot captioning task — to calibrate task difficulty and validate the automatic metrics.

3. **Add an ASR-only Vicuna baseline to the QA experiments** (Table 5) to isolate the value added by the visual summary beyond the already-available ASR text.

4. **Add a simple ablation of one filtering threshold** (e.g., vary the CLIP correlation threshold from 0.15 to 0.35 and report dataset size and a diversity metric) to address representativeness concerns.

5. **State dataset release plans, license, and annotator details** (count, background, per-video annotation time/cost).
