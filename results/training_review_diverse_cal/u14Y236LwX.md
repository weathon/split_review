## Summary

OpenWaves is a large-scale dataset of over 16 million frequency-domain wave simulations for ultrasound computed tomography, constructed using anatomically realistic breast phantoms from the FDA VICTRE project and system parameters from a real annular USCT array (256 transducers, 8 frequencies, 8,000 phantoms across four density categories). The paper benchmarks five forward neural operators (UNet, FNO, BFNO, AFNO, MgNO) and four inverse imaging methods (DeepONet, InversionNet, NIO, gradient-based optimization with learned surrogates), finding that MgNO consistently achieves the lowest forward errors and that optimization-based inversion with learned forward operators substantially outperforms direct inversion methods. The dataset's primary contribution is bridging the gap between oversimplified wave-equation benchmarks and the complexity of real biomedical imaging.

## Strengths

- **Anatomically realistic, application-driven dataset construction.** OpenWaves uses phantoms from a validated medical simulation tool (FDA VICTRE) and USCT system parameters (256 transducers, 220mm ring, 8 frequencies 300–650 kHz), directly addressing the oversimplified media (layered structures, Gaussian random fields, ROIs <40 wavenumbers) in prior datasets like OpenFWI and WaveBench. This is a genuine advance for the community (Sections 2.2, 3.2.1, Table 1).

- **Massive scale that enables scaling-law analysis.** With 16M+ frequency-domain simulations (8,000 phantoms × 256 sources × 8 frequencies), the dataset is orders of magnitude larger than existing wave-equation datasets. Figure 4b validates that model performance improves with training data size, supporting the claim that large-scale realistic data is necessary for studying neural operator scaling behavior (Section 4.3.2, Fig. 4b).

- **Multi-faceted benchmarking revealing meaningful architectural insights.** The paper evaluates five forward operators and four inverse methods across four breast types and multiple frequencies, with additional analyses of OOD generalization (unseen breast types, frequencies, source locations). Key findings — MgNO consistently lowest error (Table 3), gradient-based optimization with learned surrogates achieving visibly higher resolution than direct inversion (Table 4, Fig. 3), and inverse models generalizing poorly OOD while forward models are more robust (Table 5, Fig. 5) — directly demonstrate the dataset's utility for surfacing practically relevant trade-offs (Sections 4.1–4.3).

- **Physically rigorous data generation.** The Convergent Born Series (CBS) algorithm is used for wavefield simulation, which guarantees convergence for strong scattering media — important for realistic breast tissue where simpler Born approximations would fail (Section 3.2.2).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **The optimization vs. direct inversion comparison conflates compute budget with architectural insight.** The paper states that the gradient-based optimization approach "revealed significantly higher resolution than all direct inversion methods" and "suggests that the forward operators better capture the underlying wave physics" (Section 4.2). However, the comparison is between an iterative method (many forward passes + gradient descent) and single-pass amortized networks. The performance gap could simply reflect the extra inference-time computation rather than any fundamental superiority of the forward models' representation of physics. The paper acknowledges the higher computational cost in passing, but the interpretive claim about "better capturing physics" goes beyond what this experimental design can support. A fairer comparison would control for inference budget (e.g., allow direct inversion methods multiple refinement iterations) or explicitly separate architecture quality from inference procedure.

### Minor

- **Main benchmark results cover only a subset of the dataset's full complexity.** The forward simulation benchmarks use 3 of 8 frequencies (300, 400, 500 kHz) and 64 of 256 source locations; the inverse imaging benchmarks use the same 3 frequencies. The paper itself notes that higher frequencies (550–650 kHz) define "a more challenging operator learning task" (Section 4.3.1) and performance degrades with frequency (Fig. 4a). While the paper does analyze frequency dependence in Figure 4a, the main benchmark tables — which drive the core comparative claims about which model is "best" — exclude the hardest portion of the dataset. For a paper whose abstract claims the dataset "enables comprehensive benchmarking," this gap weakens the evidence base for the benchmark conclusions.

- **No uncertainty quantification.** Tables 3, 4, and 5 report point estimates of RRMSE, Max Error, PSNR, and SSIM without error bars, standard deviations, or multi-seed results. Since metric differences between models can be small (e.g., MgNO vs. FNO in Table 3), and the dataset spans substantial phantom variability, the reader cannot assess whether these differences are statistically meaningful. The scaling (Fig. 4b) and generalization (Fig. 4c) analyses also lack error measures.

- **No quantitative comparison of neural operator speed vs. CBS solver time.** Table 2 reports neural operator inference times, and the text asserts optimization with neural operators is "still much faster than traditional iterative reconstruction with numerical solvers" (Section 4.2), but no CBS solve times are provided. For a dataset whose motivation is accelerating PDE solving, quantifying this speedup would be valuable.

### Trivial
- The "mixture-of-experts" framing in Section 3.1.1 is unnecessary — each frequency is handled by a separate independently trained model, not a learned gating MoE. This does not affect any results.
- The dataset's ROI size improvement over prior work (50–100 wavenumbers vs. <40) is stated but the practical significance of this difference is not discussed.

## Novel Insights

The reviews surface a tension that the paper does not fully resolve: the dataset itself is a clear, substantial contribution, but the paper's interpretive claims about neural operator architectures partially overreach the experimental design. In particular, conflating the iterative-optimization vs. single-pass comparison with a claim about "better capturing physics" is an interpretive leap that the experiment cannot support. The most valuable use of this dataset going forward would be controlled studies that disentangle architecture quality, inference budget, and physics fidelity — the dataset's scale and realism make it ideal for precisely such work, and the paper would be stronger if it scoped its claims to match what the experiments can actually show.

## Suggestions

1. In Section 4.2, reframe the comparison between optimization-based and direct inversion: explicitly state that the former uses substantially more inference-time computation, and either (a) soften the claim that forward operators "better capture physics" to acknowledge this confound, or (b) add a controlled baseline where a direct inversion method is given comparable compute (e.g., iterative refinement or ensembling).

2. Extend the forward simulation benchmarks to include at least one higher frequency (e.g., 600 kHz) on a representative subset of phantoms to verify that the main conclusions about model ranking (MgNO best, FNO variants > UNet) hold across the full frequency range.

3. Add error bars or standard deviations to key metrics (especially Table 3 differences between MgNO and FNO) using multiple runs or bootstrapping.

4. Include a brief comparison table of CBS solver vs. neural operator wall-clock time for a matched task to quantify the speed-accuracy trade-off that motivates the dataset.
