## Summary

This paper conducts a large-scale empirical study investigating whether soft prompt embeddings that encode visual concepts (e.g., `<orange-cat>`) are transferable across multimodal models. Across 4,800 training runs covering 40 concepts, 4 datasets, and 3 models (Stable Diffusion, OWL-v2, DFN-CLIP) spanning generation, detection, and classification, the paper finds that these embeddings are generally model-specific and non-transferable under a linear mapping. The key novel finding is "fracturing": performant solutions exist within small l2-balls around arbitrary anchor words, including those unrelated to the target concept, and performance saturates at δ=0.5 regardless of anchor choice, suggesting initialization matters little.

## Strengths

- **Large-scale, systematic empirical study**: The paper conducts 4,800 training runs spanning 40 diverse visual concepts across 4 standard datasets (ImageNet, DreamBooth, COCO, PASCAL) and 3 models covering generation, detection, and classification. This provides robust support for the paper's empirical claims. Results are reported with 95% confidence intervals over 100 randomized trials.

- **Novel discovery of "fracturing" in embedding space**: The constrained optimization experiment (Section 5.1, Equation 4) is the paper's strongest contribution. By constraining solutions to l2-balls around anchor words — including unrelated anchors like "vase" and "candle" for a "duck" concept — the paper demonstrates that performant solutions exist near virtually any initialization, and in-domain performance saturates at δ=0.5. This finding has practical implications for understanding what soft prompt-tuning actually learns.

- **Transfer metric with closed-form linear mapping**: The paper formalizes a clean, reproducible evaluation protocol (Equation 2: linear least squares transfer function between embedding spaces) and evaluates all six ordered model pairs. This methodology enables direct comparison and benchmarking.

- **Cross-task coverage**: The paper studies the same soft prompts across three fundamentally different tasks (generation, detection, classification), which is rare and provides a richer picture than a single-task study would.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **The linear transfer assumption is a significant limitation that tempers the non-transferability claim**: The paper restricts its transfer function to linear least squares (Equation 2) "to simplify the investigation" (Section 3.1). However, the central claim that embeddings are "non-transferable" is evaluated only under this linear mapping. Given that the models have different embedding dimensions (768 vs 1024) and training objectives, the relationship between their embedding spaces need not be linear. A nonlinear transfer function (e.g., a small MLP) could potentially succeed where the linear estimator fails. The paper does not test any nonlinear transfer and does not adequately discuss this gap. A more precise framing would be: *under linear transfer, embeddings do not transfer, suggesting the spaces are not linearly aligned*. The fracturing finding (Section 5) is independent of this limitation and remains a distinct contribution.

- **The "fracturing" claim extrapolates beyond the tested evidence**: The abstract and Section 5.1 claim that perturbations exist "within an ε-ball to **any** prior embedding" (emphasis added). However, the evidence comes from 10 anchor words per concept across 40 concepts — while substantial for an empirical study, this does not warrant the universal quantifier "any." The paper's own more precise phrasing ("every anchor word tested") better matches the evidence. Additionally, the claim that "the set of word vectors that encode an arbitrary visual concept is disconnected" (Section 5.1) is presented as a central finding, but the paper does not quantify what fraction of randomly sampled vocabulary words yield performant solutions under a fixed δ, nor does it show the distribution of distances between solutions found from different anchors. The qualitative examples (e.g., near-identical duck images from vase vs. candle anchors) are suggestive but not systematically quantified.

### Minor

- **Generation evaluation metric relies on CLIP, creating partial circularity**: The metric for generation quality uses "OpenAI's pretrained CLIP L-14 model" with the prompt "a photo of {visual_concept_name}" (Section 3.2). For the classification model (DFN-CLIP), this means both training and evaluation use CLIP-based representations, potentially inflating classification→generation transfer results (up to 84% on PASCAL) and underestimating detection→generation transfer. While the paper uses a different CLIP variant for evaluation than for training, a fully independent evaluator (e.g., a non-CLIP object detector or human judgments on a subset) would substantially increase confidence.

- **Activation mismatch analysis is based on a single example**: Section 5.2 presents truncated generation and TSNE visualizations for one example (sombrero vs. strawberry anchor). The paper claims perturbations "generally target the final layers" but does not report how often this pattern occurs across concepts, anchors, or models, nor does it quantify layer-wise agreement. This observation is suggestive but not conclusive without systematic quantification.

- **No random/untrained embedding baseline**: The paper does not compare transferred embeddings to a simple baseline such as an untrained (random) embedding or a transferred but unoptimized anchor word. Such a baseline would contextualize the transfer results — e.g., does a transferred random embedding produce better-than-chance detection or classification? This would clarify whether the transfer results reflect genuine information loss or simply the difficulty of the evaluation task.

- **Only one model per task**: The paper uses one model for generation (Stable Diffusion 2.1), one for detection (OWL-v2), and one for classification (DFN-CLIP). While this is practical, it leaves open the possibility that findings are specific to these particular model choices rather than general properties of multimodal models. The paper acknowledges this implicitly but should be more prominent.

### Trivial

- The paper uses "fracturing" to describe a phenomenon where performant solutions exist near many anchors — this could be interpreted as a *connected* rather than *disconnected* property of the solution space (solutions from different anchors appear to converge to similar functional behavior). The term is catchy but the conceptual framing could be clarified.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Nonlinear transfer experiment**: Even a negative result (nonlinear transfer also fails) would substantially strengthen the claim that embeddings are genuinely non-transferable, not just non-linearly related.
- **Quantification of fracturing**: For each concept, reporting the fraction of randomly sampled vocabulary words that yield performant solutions under a fixed δ, and measuring distances between solutions found from different anchors, would turn qualitative observations into quantitative statements.
- **Independent generation metric**: Supplementing CLIP with a non-CLIP detector (e.g., Grounding DINO) or a human evaluation subset would break the circularity concern.
- **Investigation of why classification→generation transfer works well (84% on PASCAL) but poorly on fine-grained concepts**: The paper notes this result but does not analyze why some concepts transfer and others do not.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed; treat them with caution:

1. **"δ=0.5 may be quite large in absolute terms"** (Harsh Critic Point 2): The constraint normalizes by the minimum distance between words (Equation 4 denominator), so δ=0.5 means the solution is at most half that minimum distance — this is well-defined and not "quite large" as implied. The paper clearly defines the normalization.

2. **"All anchors are semantically related to the concept (e.g., 'hat' for sombrero)"** (Harsh Critic, initialization criticism): This conflates the Section 4 initialization scheme (which does use semantically related words) with the Section 5 fracturing analysis (which explicitly tests unrelated anchors like "vase" and "candle" for "duck"). Section 5.1 clearly states solutions are found "near words unrelated to the visual concept" and Figure 3 shows unrelated anchors.

3. **Strength Finder's "activation analysis" strength**: While the activation analysis is interesting, it is based on a single example and lacks systematic quantification, making it a weaker strength than the other two claimed. Keeping it but downgraded from "direct support" to "suggestive evidence."

## Novel Insights

The most interesting insight emerging from the reviews is that the paper's two main claims — non-transferability and fracturing — have different evidential bases. The non-transferability claim depends on the linear transfer assumption and is partly contradicted by the paper's own finding that classification→generation transfer reaches 84% on PASCAL. The fracturing finding, by contrast, is independent of the transfer function and is arguably the paper's stronger and more novel contribution, but it lacks quantification (what fraction of words work as anchors? how far apart are solutions from different anchors?). The conjunction of these two findings — that solutions are pervasive yet model-specific — is what most needs explanation, and neither reviewer satisfactorily resolves this tension.

## Suggestions

1. Tone down universal quantifiers ("any," "every") to match the tested evidence ("all tested anchors").
2. Explicitly acknowledge in the abstract and conclusion that the non-transferability conclusion is about *linear* transfer.
3. Add a random/untrained embedding baseline to the transfer evaluation to contextualize the results.
4. Quantify the fracturing phenomenon: report the success rate across randomly sampled vocabulary words as anchors, and measure distances between solutions found from different anchors.
5. Extend the activation mismatch analysis beyond the single sombrero/strawberry example to at least a few more concept-anchor pairs.
