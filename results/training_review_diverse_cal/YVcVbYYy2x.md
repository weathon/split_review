## Summary

This paper introduces MeCo, a method that trains linear probes on LLM internal representations (via Representation Engineering) to detect when the model should invoke external tools versus relying on its own knowledge. The approach is fine-tuning-free, requiring only a lightweight probe trained on contrastive pairs labeled by GPT-4-turbo. The authors also introduce a new benchmark (MeCa) for evaluating adaptive tool use and adaptive RAG. Experiments on Metatool, MeCa-Tool, and MeCa-RAG across Llama-3-8b, Llama-3-70b, and Mistral-7b show substantial aggregate accuracy improvements over Naive and P_Yes baselines.

## Strengths

- **Substantial accuracy gains on aggregate across benchmarks**: On Metatool without context, MeCo improves Llama-3-8b from 61.50% (Naive) to 79.01%, and from 66.30% (P_Yes) to 79.01%. These improvements hold across multiple model families, sizes, and with/without context settings. The gains on the independent Metatool benchmark are particularly compelling evidence of effectiveness.

- **Fine-tuning-free and lightweight**: The method requires training only a linear probe (PCA on pairwise difference vectors in representation space), without modifying the LLM's weights. This is genuinely lightweight and orthogonal to fine-tuning — the paper shows MeCo further improves already fine-tuned models (e.g., Llama-3-8b-sft on Metatool without context: from 83.49% Naive to 88.19% MeCo).

- **Extension to adaptive RAG**: The same probe-based approach transfers to retrieval decisions in adaptive RAG (Table 3), demonstrating generality beyond tool use. On Llama-3-8b, MeCo achieves 74.26% accuracy vs. 45.50% for Naive and 68.18% for P_Yes.

- **Threshold transferability**: Decision thresholds learned on Metatool transfer to MeCa tasks (Task1, Task4) with minimal degradation, suggesting the internal signal is model-dependent and task-independent, which is a non-trivial finding.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Inflated "meta-cognition" framing**: The paper defines meta-cognition as "the model's ability to self-assess its own capabilities and limitations" (Section 3), but the probe is trained on labels generated by GPT-4-turbo, not on any signal of the *target model's own awareness*. The probe learns to map the target model's hidden states to an external decision criterion. This conflates "internal state that correlates with an external oracle's judgment" with "self-assessment." The method is still valid as a tool-use trigger, but the cognitive-psychology framing is unsupported and misleading. The paper would be stronger with an honest description: the probe detects whether the model's representations encode features predictive of correct tool-use necessity, as judged by a stronger model.

- **Unfair baseline comparison**: MeCo requires labeled training data from GPT-4-turbo to train its probe, whereas the Naive and P_Yes baselines use *no additional training data*. The performance gap could largely reflect the value of the supervised signal rather than anything unique about representation-space probing. A fair comparison would require either (a) giving baselines access to the same training data (e.g., a simple classifier trained on token-probability features using the same labels) or (b) evaluating an unsupervised variant of MeCo (e.g., using the model's own uncertainty as the signal). As presented, the comparison conflates the probe architecture with the presence of supervised training.

- **No statistical significance testing**: The paper reports point estimates with no confidence intervals, standard deviations, or significance tests. The harsh critic correctly identifies several individual tasks where MeCo underperforms baselines (e.g., Table 2, Llama-3-8b without context, Task5: Naive 65.6, P_Yes 58.3, MeCo 63.9; Task6: Naive 57.0, P_Yes 54.8, MeCo 53.9). The paper's claim that MeCo "significantly enhances" and "consistently" outperforms baselines is too strong given that some individual comparisons contradict this. Aggregate trends favor MeCo, but without significance testing, the reliability of individual wins is unclear.

### Minor

- **Potential benchmark confound**: MeCo's probe is trained on GPT-4-turbo labels, and the MeCa benchmark queries are also generated by GPT-4-turbo (with human verification). This shared supervision source could create an alignment that overstates MeCo's performance on MeCa relative to other benchmarks. The concern is partially mitigated by strong results on the independent Metatool benchmark, but the paper's strongest claims about MeCa should be tempered.

- **Probe layer selection may be data-dependent**: The probe is selected from layers -5 to -2 based on classification accuracy on the training distribution. Since this selection is made on the training data, there is a risk of overfitting to that distribution. The paper does not validate whether the chosen layer generalizes across benchmarks, beyond the threshold transfer experiment.

- **Key implementation details missing**: The paper does not specify (a) the exact number of queries used to train the probe, (b) the training/validation split for threshold fitting, (c) the number of GPT-4-turbo API calls required. Without these numbers, the claim of "minimal cost" and "small number of queries" is unverifiable. These are critical for both reproducibility and assessing practical feasibility.

- **Multi-turn interactions not evaluated**: The MeCa benchmark includes a multi-turn interaction category, but the experiments appear to evaluate only single-turn tasks (the 6 tasks listed). The paper would be stronger by either evaluating multi-turn or explicitly scoping it out.

- **Figure 3 probe comparison is not apples-to-apples**: The meta-cognition probe is compared against honesty and confidence probes, but the classification tasks differ fundamentally. Distinguishing tool-required vs. not-required queries is likely an easier binary classification than detecting honesty or confidence, so the higher accuracy is expected. The comparison does not support a claim that meta-cognition is a "stronger" internal signal in any meaningful sense.

## Suggestions

1. **Reframe the narrative**: Replace the "meta-cognition as self-assessment" language with an honest description: the method trains a probe to detect whether the model's internal representations encode features that predict correct tool-use necessity (using a stronger model as the oracle). The method stands on its own merits without the inflated framing.

2. **Add a supervised baseline**: Train a classifier on the same GPT-4-turbo labels using token-probability features (e.g., logistic regression on P(Yes) and P(No) and their ratio) as a direct baseline. This would disentangle the value of the supervision from the value of representation-space probing.

3. **Report confidence intervals or significance tests**: Without statistical rigor, the claimed superiority over baselines on individual tasks is not fully supported given visible counterexamples.

4. **Specify training data size and cost**: The exact number of queries used for probe training, the train/validation split, and the number of GPT-4-turbo calls are essential for reproducibility and assessing the "minimal cost" claim.

## Score and Decision

The paper introduces a practical adaptive tool-use method with promising aggregate results across multiple models and benchmarks, and contributes a new benchmark. However, the inflated conceptual framing ("meta-cognition"), the unfair baseline comparison (MeCo uses supervised labels that baselines lack), and the absence of significance testing substantially weaken the empirical case. The core technical contribution — using representation-space probes for tool-use decisions — is legitimate and interesting, but the evaluation does not convincingly demonstrate that the probe's internal representations are superior to a simple supervised classifier on output probabilities. The paper needs a major revision to fairly characterize its contribution before it can be accepted.
