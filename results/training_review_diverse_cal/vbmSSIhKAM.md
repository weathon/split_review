## Summary

VoxDialogue introduces a synthetic spoken dialogue benchmark covering 12 acoustic attributes (speaker information, paralinguistic features, and environmental sounds) across 4.5K multi-turn samples. The paper constructs attribute-specific test sets using a tailored synthesis pipeline (LLM script generation + conditional TTS + post-processing), then evaluates several existing spoken dialogue systems. The key empirical finding is a complementary weakness pattern: ASR-based systems handle context-driven attributes (emotion, gender) well but fail on acoustic-dependent ones (speed, fidelity, audio events), while direct audio-language models reverse this pattern. The benchmark addresses an underexplored dimension — acoustic understanding beyond semantic content in spoken dialogue — and the systematic comparative evaluation reveals genuine blind spots in current systems.

## Strengths

1. **Comprehensive attribute coverage.** VoxDialogue integrates 12 acoustic dimensions across speaker, paralinguistic, and environmental categories — substantially more than existing benchmarks (Table 1 shows SUPERB, AirBench, SpokenWOZ, and SD-Eval each cover only subsets). The balanced distribution across 35 categories (Figure 1d, Table 3) supports fine-grained diagnosis of model capabilities.

2. **Attribute-specific synthesis pipeline.** The paper develops distinct generation methods per attribute (Section 3.2, Stage 2): conditional TTS for gender/emotion/speed, post-processing for volume/fidelity/audio/music, reference speaker selection for age, and industrial TTS for accent. This controlled approach, combined with automatic verification (Whisper WER < 5%, speaker diarization for timbre consistency) and human annotation checks (Stage 5), enables isolated evaluation of individual acoustic cues — something not achievable with naturalistic corpora where attributes co-occur and confound each other.

3. **Empirically demonstrated complementary model limitations.** The comparative evaluation (Section 4.4, Table 4, Figure 2) reveals a principled pattern: ASR-based systems (GPT-4+ASR) outperform direct dialogue systems on context-driven attributes (emotion, gender, age, accent) but score poorly on acoustic-dependent attributes (speed, fidelity, audio events, music), while direct systems like Qwen2-Audio reverse this. This finding directly supports the paper's central claim — that acoustic cues beyond text are critical and current systems have opposing weaknesses — and provides actionable evidence for the field.

4. **Open release commitment.** The paper commits to open-sourcing all data, code, and model weights, which enables independent verification and community-driven extensions.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **N-gram evaluation metrics are poorly suited to the task, and the GPT-based metric is unvalidated.** The paper uses BLEU, ROUGE-L, METEOR, and BERTScore to assess response "content" quality (Section 4.2). These metrics measure n-gram and token-level overlap against a single reference — but the task of attribute-conditioned dialogue generation admits multiple appropriate responses that differ in wording. A model that correctly detects female gender and responds with "Yes, madam" instead of "Yes, ma'am" (or vice versa) may score lower than a model that ignores the attribute but produces a lexically generic response. This is not merely a noise issue; the metrics actively conflate lexical similarity with task correctness. The paper's main qualitative metric (GPT-based scoring) attempts to address attribute relevance, and the paper draws its core conclusions from it (Table 4, Section 4.4). However, this GPT metric is presented without any calibration against human judgment — no correlation study, no agreement analysis, no validation that the GPT scores correspond to what a human evaluator would consider correct. For a benchmark whose raison d'être is evaluating attribute-aware responses, the combination of (a) mismatched n-gram metrics that can produce misleading rankings and (b) an unvalidated central metric that may have its own biases means the experimental conclusions rest on uncertain foundations.

2. **Insufficient human validation of the benchmark and its evaluation.** The paper mentions "human annotators for additional quality checks" (Stage 5) in a single sentence with zero operational details: no number of annotators, no inter-annotator agreement, no description of the protocol, no rejection or modification rate. The reference responses are generated by GPT-4o and assumed correct without human verification that the intended acoustic signal is perceptible and that the reference response is indeed the natural one. The GPT-based evaluation metric lacks any human calibration. For a benchmark intended as a community resource — one whose scores will be used to rank models — the absence of human grounding is a structural gap. Without demonstrating that test items and scoring align with human intuition, the benchmark's validity as an evaluation instrument is unsubstantiated.

3. **Synthetic data raises concerns about ecological validity that are not addressed.** The data is produced entirely through controlled generation: LLM script synthesis + TTS with explicit attribute prompting + post-processing (Section 3.2). Each sample is designed to *clearly* exhibit the target attribute in a categorical, often stereotyped way. The paper provides no evidence (e.g., human listening studies, correlation with natural data benchmarks) that performance on VoxDialogue generalizes to real-world spoken dialogue, where acoustic variation is continuous, subtle, and ambiguous. The synthesis pipeline may also introduce artifacts — poor TTS quality on certain accents, splicing discontinuities for audio events — that models could exploit as spurious features. While synthetic data is a pragmatic and widely used choice for controlled evaluation, the paper's central claim ("evaluating the ability of spoken dialogue systems to understand multi-modal information beyond words") implies ecological validity that is not demonstrated.

### Minor

3. **No uncertainty quantification for experimental results.** Tables 3 and 4 (and Figure 2) report scores as point estimates with no confidence intervals, standard deviations, or statistical significance tests. Many reported differences are small (e.g., 0.1–0.2 in GPT-based scores), and without uncertainty estimates the reader cannot distinguish systematic advantages from noise. This is especially important given that the synthetic data may introduce systematic but spurious differences.

4. **No analysis of automatic verification biases.** The paper uses Whisper WER < 5% as a quality filter and speaker diarization for timbre consistency (Stage 3), but does not analyze whether the WER threshold disproportionately filters out non-native, accented, emotional, or low-fidelity speech — precisely the acoustic conditions the benchmark is designed to test. If so, the benchmark may be biased toward easier acoustic conditions, undermining its diagnostic power for those attributes.

5. **Style evaluation metric is underspecified.** The paper mentions "weighted F1 score of speech sentiment" for style evaluation (Section 4.2) but does not define which sentiment model is used, how sentiment categories are mapped to the 12 attributes, or why this metric is relevant. This metric does not appear in the main results, so it is not a fatal omission, but as written it is non-reproducible and unnecessary noise.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Human validation study:** A human listening experiment where annotators listen to test samples and (a) identify the intended attribute and (b) rate the appropriateness of reference responses would substantially strengthen the benchmark's validity. Reporting agreement metrics would allow the community to trust the test items.
- **GPT metric calibration:** Collecting human ratings on a sample of model outputs and reporting correlation (Spearman) between human and GPT scores would validate the main evaluation instrument.
- **Attribute-specific accuracy metrics:** Supplementary evaluations using targeted classifiers (e.g., gender-appropriate salutation detection, emotion-consistency classifiers) would be more interpretable than n-gram overlap for a benchmark focused on attribute understanding.
- **Error analysis breakdown:** Providing per-attribute performance breakdowns and concrete failure examples (e.g., "ASR-based systems fail on speed because...") would substantially strengthen the insights.
- **Acknowledgment of data contamination risk:** The models evaluated may have been exposed to similar synthetic data during training. A brief discussion of this limitation would improve the paper's completeness.

## Suggestions

1. **Address the metric mismatch** by either (a) supplementing or replacing n-gram metrics with attribute-specific accuracy measures (e.g., for gender: does the response use the correct honorific? for emotion: is the response tone-appropriate?) or (b) at minimum, explicitly discussing the limitations of n-gram metrics for this task.
2. **Provide human validation details** for Stage 5: number of annotators, inter-annotator agreement (e.g., Cohen's κ or Fleiss' κ), rejection rate, and examples of rejected/modified samples.
3. **Calibrate the GPT-based metric** against human ratings on a sample of outputs, even a small one (100–200 examples).
4. **Report confidence intervals or bootstrap estimates** for key comparisons in Table 4, especially for the GPT-based scores where small differences are reported.
5. **Analyze WER filtering bias** by reporting WER distributions across attributes and checking whether accented, emotional, or low-fidelity samples are disproportionately removed.

## Score and Decision

The paper identifies a genuine gap — evaluating acoustic understanding in spoken dialogue — and offers a systematic, multi-attribute benchmark with an informative comparative evaluation. The core contribution is real and the empirical finding (complementary model weaknesses) is interesting and actionable. However, the evaluation methodology has significant gaps: n-gram metrics are poorly suited to the task, the GPT-based metric is unvalidated, human validation of the benchmark is absent in any meaningful detail, and results lack uncertainty quantification. These issues are addressable in a revision but materially weaken the paper's claims as presented. The paper falls between acceptance and rejection: the idea and construction are solid, but the experimental conclusions are not yet on firm ground.
