## Summary

This paper proposes GIE (Growth Inhibitors for Erasure), a fine-tuning-free method for suppressing inappropriate concepts (NSFW content, artistic styles, objects) in text-to-image diffusion models. The key idea is to extract cross-attention features for a target concept, re-weight them into "growth inhibitors," and inject these into the prompt's attention map group during inference. An MLP adapter trained with GPT-4o labels predicts the optimal suppression scale. Experiments show strong nudity removal (outperforming eight baselines), high object removal rates with minimal side effects, and preserved image quality/semantics.

## Strengths

- **Superior NSFW erasure validated quantitatively.** GIE achieves the highest NSFW removal rate (NRR) among all eight baselines on the I2P dataset (Figure 4), demonstrating effective nudity suppression.

- **Preserves image quality and semantic alignment.** On COCO-30K, GIE obtains the best CLIP score (0.309) and a competitive FID (17.85) among all methods (Table 1), showing that erasure does not degrade semantic alignment or overall quality.

- **Precise object erasure with minimal side effects.** GIE achieves object removal rates (ORR) above 0.90 for target concepts (car, airplane, cat) while keeping ORR for non-target objects at or below 0.05 (Table 2), demonstrating highly specific suppression.

- **Addresses implicit unsafe prompts.** The paper tackles the practical problem of associative prompts (e.g., "Japan body") generating inappropriate content — a scenario that explicit-word filtering cannot handle — and shows GIE works on such prompts.

- **Lightweight and practical.** The adapter trains in seconds with a few dozen images; the method requires no fine-tuning and is plug-and-play with different diffusion models.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The adapter component lacks proper validation.** The MLP adapter that infers the suppression scale is central to GIE, yet the paper provides no comparison against simpler alternatives (e.g., a fixed suppression scale or a grid search over candidate scales). Without this, it is unclear whether the adapter's learned scale actually improves results over a trivial heuristic — the added complexity and GPT-4o dependency may be unnecessary. Additionally, the GPT-4o scoring labels are not validated against human judgments or any established metric; the paper gives no examples of GPT-4o's scoring behavior, no inter-sample consistency analysis, and no evidence of label reliability.

2. **Quantitative evaluation is narrower than the paper's scope.** The paper claims to suppress NSFW content broadly (hate, violence, self-harm, sexual content, etc.) and artistic styles, but: (a) the NSFW metric (NRR) uses NudeNet and only measures nudity — no quantitative results are reported for other I2P categories (e.g., violence, gore, hate symbols), though qualitative examples are shown in Figure 5; (b) style erasure (van Gogh, Monet, Hokusai, Figure 6) is demonstrated only qualitatively, with no artist classifier accuracy, CLIP-based style similarity, or user study. These gaps do not invalidate the nudity/object results but mean the claims of generality are less substantiated than the text suggests.

### Minor

3. **Cross-concept generalization claim is not clearly verified.** Section 4.2 claims that training the adapter on "cat" generalizes to "dog" and "airplane" — a strong claim — but the experimental setup (Section 5.1) does not clarify whether the adapter used for Table 2 was trained per-concept or on one concept and tested on others. The paper should explicitly state the training regime for the adapter in each experiment and, if cross-concept, provide a dedicated table or figure.

4. **No ablation study.** The paper does not ablate key design choices: (a) injection position (before [EOT] vs. other locations), (b) comparison between the full GIE and a version without the adapter (fixed scale), (c) comparison of the injection strategy to alternative approaches (e.g., subtractive re-weighting). An ablation would clarify which components contribute most to the method's success.

5. **Adapter training details are underspecified for reproducibility.** The paper does not report the exact number of training samples, the range of suppression scales sampled, the prompt set used for training, or the scoring prompt given to GPT-4o. While the general approach is described, the missing details hinder reproduction.

6. **Multi-concept merging uses a heuristic with unexamined consequences.** When target concepts are semantically similar (e.g., "nude" and "naked"), the method discards all but the one with the highest suppression scale (Section 4.3). Whether this yields sufficient erasure for near-synonyms is not evaluated.

### Trivial
- The qualitative figures (5–8) use Realistic Vision v6.0 while quantitative experiments use SD v1.4. The paper acknowledges this (Section 5.2, line 171), but the inconsistency is a minor confound.
- The biological analogy of "growth inhibitors" is briefly defined mechanically (line 95: "We refer to the weighted result I as the growth inhibitor") but the analogy is not elaborated.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation emerging from this review — one the paper itself does not fully articulate — is the tension between the method's core mechanism and its evaluation. The attention-injection idea (extracting target-concept features and replacing portions of the prompt's attention map) is clever, model-agnostic, and fundamentally different from fine-tuning approaches. Its main advantage is the ability to erase implicit concepts that have no corresponding token in the prompt. Yet the quantitative evaluation relies almost entirely on explicit-concept scenarios (measuring nudity or object presence). The paper would be significantly stronger if it designed experiments that specifically isolate and quantify the method's advantage on *implicit* prompt scenarios — e.g., measuring how often "Japan body" generates nudity before vs. after erasure, over a large sample. The current evaluation defaults to the standard I2P-based NRR metric, which does not foreground what makes GIE distinctive.

## Suggestions

1. **Validate the adapter.** Add an experiment comparing GIE with the adapter against GIE with a fixed suppression scale (sweep over a range) on a held-out set. Show that the adapter's chosen scale is within one step of the best grid-search value on most samples. Also report the adapter's prediction error (MSE) against the GPT-4o labels.

2. **Clarify the experimental setup for generalization.** Explicitly state, for each experiment involving the adapter, whether the adapter was trained on each target concept independently or on a single concept and tested on others. If the latter, present a dedicated table of cross-concept performance.

3. **Add quantitative metrics for style and at least one non-nudity NSFW category.** Even a simple classifier-based metric (e.g., CLIP similarity to artist names, or an off-the-shelf violence classifier) would significantly strengthen the generality claims without requiring a large human study.

4. **Add an ablation study.** Compare: (a) GIE without adapter (fixed scale), (b) GIE with adapter, (c) the naive token-weakening baseline from Section 4.1, and (d) alternative injection positions, on a consistent set of prompts.

5. **Disclose adapter training details.** Include the number of training samples, range of suppression scales, prompt set, and GPT-4o scoring prompt in the paper or supplement.

## Score and Decision

The paper proposes a genuinely novel and well-motivated attention-injection approach for concept erasure. The core idea is clean, avoids catastrophic forgetting, and handles implicit unsafe prompts in a principled way. The quantitative results for nudity erasure and object erasure are strong and compare favorably against eight baselines. However, the adapter component — which the paper presents as a key contribution — is not validated against any simpler alternative, and the evaluation does not fully cover the claimed scope (style erasure lacks quantitative support, NSFW evaluation focuses only on nudity). These gaps are addressable in revision but reduce confidence in the method's generality as presented.
