## Summary

This paper presents the first systematic benchmark evaluating adversarial defenses for no-reference Image Quality Assessment (IQA) metrics. It tests 25 defense strategies (adversarial purification, adversarial training, and certified robustness) against 14 white-box and black-box attacks under both non-adaptive and adaptive settings, using 7 NR IQA metrics and IQA-specific evaluation metrics (D_score, SROCC_adv, Q_score). The benchmark includes a dataset of over 215,000 adversarial images and an online leaderboard for community submissions.

## Strengths

- **First systematic benchmark for IQA defenses**: The paper evaluates 25 defense mechanisms spanning purification, adversarial training, and certified robustness methods against 14 attacks across 7 IQA metrics. This is the first work to provide a unified framework for comparing IQA defenses, filling a clear gap (Section 1, Tables 1–3).

- **Dual evaluation setting (non-adaptive vs. adaptive)**: The paper explicitly distinguishes between attacks unaware of the defense and attacks with access to the defense's gradients. This distinction is critical and reveals that simple defenses like Flip perform well non-adaptively (SROCC_adv=0.710) but degrade drastically under adaptive attacks (SROCC_adv=0.356) (Table 1).

- **IQA-specific evaluation metrics**: The proposed metrics—D_score, D_score^(D), Q_score (combining SSIM+PSNR), and SROCC variants—are tailored to the IQA domain where both score preservation and perceptual quality matter, going beyond classification-focused metrics (Equations 3–6).

- **Public dataset and leaderboard**: The benchmark provides an adversarially perturbed dataset of over 215,000 images and an online leaderboard for community submissions, enabling reproducibility and future extensions (Section 3.5).

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Large confidence intervals reduce the precision of rankings**. 
   Table 1 reports confidence intervals that are wide enough that many defenses are statistically indistinguishable on D_score and SROCC_adv. For example, JPEG (D_score=10.62±2.18), Rotate (10.73±3.44), and DISCO (8.59±1.84) have overlapping intervals. The paper acknowledges this (Section 4, paragraph 1) and provides per-attack breakdowns in Figure 2 showing that the large variance stems from attack-type heterogeneity. However, the benchmark's primary function—guiding defense selection—is weakened when uncertainty drowns out differences between most methods. The paper selects the best defense parameters by SROCC_adv, but if variance is high, the selected parameter may be an artifact. The paper itself notes that "a more rigorous statistical framework is needed" (Limitations), which is a significant gap for a benchmark.

2. **Certified defenses evaluated on only 10 images**. 
   Table 2 reports results for certified defenses on only 10 images (Section 3.4, line 186). This sample is far too small to draw reliable conclusions about certified radius, abstention rates, or correlations. The paper presents these results alongside purification defenses as if they are part of the same benchmark, but the experimental conditions are not comparable. While the paper notes computational constraints, 10 images makes these results exploratory at best. The certified defense findings (e.g., "DDRS and DP show better results") are not supported at this sample size.

### Minor

3. **Definition of adversarial purification (Eq. 2) does not match the evaluated methods**. 
   Equation 2 defines purification as solving an optimization problem that requires access to the original clean image x and the distance function ρ—neither of which is available to a real-world defender. The actual methods evaluated (JPEG compression, blur, flipping, etc.) are fixed transformations, not optimization-based solutions. This mismatch between the formal definition and the implemented methods is confusing. The paper should either adopt a simpler definition consistent with the implemented methods or explain why the theoretical formulation is useful despite not matching the experiments.

4. **Adversarial training results are presented as a main category but are not integrated into the main comparison**. 
   The paper lists adversarial training as one of three major defense categories (Section 3), yet the main results (Table 1) include only purification defenses. Adversarial training experiments cover only 2 of the 7 IQA metrics (LINEARITY, KONCEPT), and detailed results are relegated to the supplementary material. For a benchmark claiming to compare 25 defense methods, readers cannot assess how adversarial training stacks up against purification defenses. The paper is transparent about this scope limitation (line 186), but the structural imbalance weakens the "comprehensive" claim.

### Trivial

- The paper's Limitations section (line 312) mentions that "the current ranking methodology is straightforward and might not capture the complexity of different evaluation metrics." This is appropriately placed but underscores that the statistical framework is acknowledged as a weakness by the authors themselves.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Per-attack or per-metric breakdowns** in the main paper for more than the selected few defenses shown in Figure 2(a). The current aggregated tables hide structure that matters — e.g., whether a defense works well for one metric but poorly for another. Even showing a top-5 defense breakdown by metric would add diagnostic value.
- **Statistical significance tests for rankings** (e.g., non-parametric pairwise comparisons) rather than relying on point estimates with overlapping confidence intervals.
- **Specific parameter values** for attacks and defenses in the main text (or a concise table), not just in supplementary material. Example values (e.g., "JPEG quality levels: 10, 30, 50, 70, 90") would help readers interpret what "weak/medium/strong" means without digging into supplementary.

## Novel Insights

The reviews collectively identify a tension that the paper itself recognizes but does not fully resolve: the benchmark's value proposition is enabling defense comparison, yet the high variance across attacks and metrics means few comparative statements are statistically robust. The paper's strongest findings are thus at the qualitative pattern level (e.g., "spatial transforms work well non-adaptively but fail under adaptive attacks; randomness helps adaptively") rather than fine-grained rankings. This is a characteristic challenge for comprehensive benchmarks in high-variance domains, and future work on IQA defense benchmarking would benefit from variance-aware experimental design (e.g., more images per condition, fewer conditions) or Bayesian ranking methods from the outset.

## Suggestions

1. Release per-attack, per-metric, per-defense disaggregated results as a companion table or interactive visualization so the community can perform their own comparisons, circumventing the aggregation problem.
2. Either expand the certified defense evaluation to a sample size comparable to the main experiments, or explicitly reframe it as a pilot study with appropriate caveats in the main text and table.
3. Bring key adversarial training results (at least a summary row) into the main comparison table so readers can compare across all three defense categories, or clearly delimit the benchmark scope to purification + certified defenses.
4. Add a simple non-parametric bootstrap test (e.g., "defense A beats defense B with probability p") to give readers a rigorous basis for comparison despite the high variance.
5. Clarify the relationship between the formal definition of purification (Eq. 2) and the actual fixed-transformation methods evaluated, to avoid misleading readers about what was tested.

## Score and Decision

The paper makes a real and timely contribution: it is the first systematic benchmark of adversarial defenses for IQA metrics, with substantial experimental effort (~5000 GPU hours), a reasonable range of attacks and defenses, and a public leaderboard. The evaluation methodology is thoughtfully designed with IQA-specific metrics and the non-adaptive/adaptive distinction. 

However, the paper has two weaknesses that meaningfully reduce what the benchmark can claim. The large confidence intervals mean the fine-grained rankings are not reliable, and the 10-image evaluation for certified defenses is too thin to support the comparison. These do not invalidate the benchmark's existence or its qualitative findings, but they mean the paper's primary deliverable—enabling defense comparison—is partially compromised by insufficient precision in the main results.

The paper is well within the range of a reasonable acceptance if these limitations are clearly acknowledged and if disaggregated results are released to enable community-driven comparisons. The core contribution (first benchmark, systematic protocol, public leaderboard) is solid; the weaknesses are in the precision of the claims, not their validity.

**Overall assessment**: Accept. The paper delivers on its promise of a first systematic benchmark, with appropriate caveats, and the public leaderboard enables future improvement.
