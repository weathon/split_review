## Summary

This paper introduces Re-TASK, a prompting framework that applies Bloom's Taxonomy and Knowledge Space Theory to decompose LLM tasks into "capability items" (knowledge + skill combinations), then injects these items into prompts via a structured Chain-of-Learning ordering. The method is evaluated on legal sentencing (CAIL), financial course exams (FinanceIQ), and mathematical reasoning (MMLU-Math) across multiple LLM families. The key empirical result is a 33.33% average improvement over Zero-shot CoT on the legal task, with more modest gains on finance (14.61%) and math (7.61%).

## Strengths

1. **Large and consistent performance gains on the legal domain.** Re-TASK (Full) achieves a 44.42% improvement over Zero-shot CoT with Yi-1.5-9B and 33.08% with Llama3-Chinese-8B on legal sentencing (Table 1). These are substantial absolute gains on a realistic domain-specific task.

2. **Token efficiency.** Re-TASK (Full) uses slightly fewer tokens than 3-shot CoT while achieving much higher accuracy (Table 3), suggesting the structured capability-item demonstrations are more informative per token than random examples.

3. **Ablation study showing the contribution of different capability items.** The ablation (Table 2) compares different capability-item combinations and shows they contribute differently (gains range from 24.17% to 32.17% over Zero-shot CoT), supporting the claim that the content/selection of items matters.

4. **Consistent benefit across model scales.** Figure 2 shows Re-TASK outperforms all baselines across Qwen1.5 models from 7B to 32B, indicating the benefit is not limited to small models and addresses gaps not resolved by scaling alone.

5. **Cross-domain and cross-model generality.** Improvements are demonstrated on three distinct domains (law, finance, math) and four model families (Qwen1.5, Llama3/Llama3-Chinese, Yi-1.5, Mistral), which strengthens the generality claims despite varying effect sizes.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

1. **The experimental design does not fully isolate the framework's structural contribution from the effect of simply providing relevant domain knowledge in context.** The baselines are Zero/Few-shot CoT with randomly selected examples, which may not contain the relevant legal knowledge (Article 234). Re-TASK injects this knowledge as part of its capability items. Without a control that provides the same domain knowledge in a flat, unstructured form (e.g., "Here is Article 234. Now solve the case using CoT") without the Chain-of-Learning capability-item decomposition, the observed gains cannot be cleanly attributed to the framework's structure vs. the mere presence of task-relevant knowledge. The ablation study partially addresses this by showing different capability-item combinations yield different results (Table 2), but it does not include the critical flat-knowledge control. This is the most significant limitation of the current experimental design.

2. **The theoretical framing is substantially overstated relative to the actual contribution.** The paper presents Re-TASK as "a novel theoretical model" and claims that it "reveals" that CoT failures stem from insufficient knowledge or inadequate skill adaptation. In practice, the framework is a design methodology that applies existing educational theories (Bloom's Taxonomy, KST) to prompt construction. The paper does not use the framework to derive any non-trivial prediction, conduct a failure analysis to validate its claimed diagnostic power (e.g., categorizing CoT failures by knowledge vs. skill deficits), or demonstrate predictive utility beyond what common sense would suggest. The framework's value is as an organizational heuristic for prompt design rather than as a scientific theory. Reframing the contribution as a *principled prompting methodology* rather than a novel theoretical model would better match the evidence.

### Minor

3. **No evaluation of the quality of automatically generated capability items.** In the finance and math domains, items are generated by larger LLMs, and the gains are considerably smaller (14.61%, 7.61%) than manually crafted items (33.33%). The paper acknowledges the items "may not be fully optimized" but does not evaluate their quality (e.g., via human judges, automatic metrics, or variance across generation seeds). Without quality assessment, it is unclear whether the smaller gains reflect framework limitations or poor item generation.

4. **Claimed diagnostic value is asserted, not demonstrated.** The paper states that the framework "reveals that many CoT failures in domain-specific tasks stem from insufficient knowledge or inadequate skill adaptation" (contribution 2, Section 3), but no empirical analysis of CoT failures is performed. No attempt is made to take cases where CoT fails and categorize whether the failure was due to missing knowledge vs. inability to apply known knowledge. This claim is a design assumption, not a finding.

5. **No statistical reliability measures.** Results are reported from single runs without confidence intervals or error bars. For the smaller finance and math datasets (178 and 276 instances), the gains are modest enough that statistical significance is not obvious. This is standard practice for prompting papers but worth noting given the strength of some claims.

6. **Data leakage concern not addressed.** For the auto-generation experiments, larger LLMs generate capability items while the same model families are evaluated on test sets (FinanceIQ, MMLU-Math). The paper does not describe how test instances were kept separate from item generation, which matters because LLM-generated items could inadvertently encode information from test-set-like examples seen during pretraining.

### Trivial

- The ablation study always retains C01 (knowledge) and C03 (overall applying), meaning the most informative ablation — removing the knowledge foundation entirely — is not tested, limiting what can be concluded about item dependencies.
- The y-axis range in Figure 2 makes it somewhat difficult to visually assess whether the gap narrows at 32B.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A failure analysis where CoT errors are categorized as knowledge-deficit vs. skill-deficit, showing that Re-TASK disproportionately fixes one category. This would turn the framework's diagnostic framing from assertion into evidence.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting finding is the token efficiency result: Re-TASK (Full) uses *fewer* tokens than 3-shot CoT yet achieves much higher accuracy. This suggests that structured, relevant demonstrations (even if longer individually) can be more efficient than multiple random examples because each token carries more task-relevant signal. The reviewer's suggestion that the ablation should test removing C01 (knowledge) entirely is a genuine methodological contribution that could reveal whether knowledge injection or skill adaptation drives the bulk of the gain. Additionally, the contrast between the large manual-item gains in law (33.33%) vs. smaller auto-generated gains in finance (14.61%) and math (7.61%) raises a practical question for the field: to what extent does the success of knowledge-enhanced prompting depend on domain-specific item engineering, and can this gap be closed by better automatic generation methods?
