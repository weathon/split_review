## Summary

This paper introduces ProxyBench, a benchmark for evaluating the robustness of neural-network proxy reward models against optimization pressure (proxy gaming) in the text domain. The benchmark includes three optimization methods (best-of-n, RLHF/PPO, and white-box adversarial attacks), DeBERTa-v3 proxies of varying sizes, and a gold reward model trained as an ensemble from three human-preference datasets. Through experiments, the paper uncovers that proxy gaming can be pervasive in subsets of examples even when aggregate reward curves look healthy, that larger proxy models are consistently harder to game, that larger policies and more optimization exposure increase gaming, and that existing robustness techniques (adversarial training, SAM) provide at best limited and at worst negative benefits outside white-box settings.

## Strengths

- **Discovery that proxy gaming is hidden in aggregate metrics and is statistically systematic.** Section 5.1 and Figures 2–3 show that even when mean gold reward improves, a subset of examples is gamed (proxy reward increases while gold reward decreases). Over 45% of gamed examples overlap across independently trained proxies of different sizes — a rate that rejects the randomness null hypothesis with near-zero probability. This is a non-obvious finding that justifies the benchmark's existence and motivates instance-level evaluation.

- **Comprehensive benchmark with diverse optimization methods and multiple complementary metrics.** The paper includes BoN, RL (PPO), and white-box (GBDA, PEZ) optimization, and evaluates with Final Gold Reward, RMS divergence, Mean/Max Absolute Error, and correlation-based gaming counts (Section 4.4, Table 2). This breadth allows the benchmark to distinguish between qualitatively different failure modes (Section 5.3).

- **Scaling analyses revealing asymmetric trends.** Larger proxy models are monotonically harder to game across all three optimization methods (Table 1, Figure 5). Conversely, larger policy models and increased optimization exposure (more data, more steps) increase gaming (Figures 5–6). These results are empirically clean and provide actionable guidance: scale up proxies, but beware that scaling policies will worsen the problem.

- **Identification of phase shifts in reward curves as emergent exploits.** During RLHF, the paper documents sudden divergence points between proxy and gold rewards corresponding to learned exploits (e.g., generating lists of tips), which are not captured by the functional forms of prior work (Section 5.4, Figure 6). This provides a concrete diagnostic signal for when gaming begins.

- **Honest evaluation of existing robustness methods.** Adversarial training (PGD, PEZ) reduces gaming under white-box attacks but does not help — and even hurts — in BoN and RL settings. SAM provides no benefit (Section 5.5, Table 3). The paper does not overclaim these results and correctly concludes that new methods are needed.

- **Gold model trained as an ensemble from three datasets.** Training the gold reward model on HH, SHP, and OA datasets as an ensemble (Section 4.1) reduces the risk of model-specific quirks propagating through the benchmark, strengthening the ground-truth signal.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
None. The weaknesses below are addressable and do not undermine the paper's core contributions.

### Minor

- **RMS metric lacks interpretability anchoring.** The RMS formula (line 120: RMS = 100 × sqrt(1/T Σ (P'ₜ − G'ₜ)²)) includes a scale factor of 100 with no motivation. The paper never explains what a change from RMS=5 to RMS=10 means in practical terms — e.g., what fraction of examples experience gold reward decrease, or how large the average gold reward drop is on the gamed subset. While the paper also uses correlation-based gaming metrics (Table 2), RMS is the primary metric in the scaling analysis (Figures 5–6) and main results (Table 1), so its interpretability gap is consequential. The paper would be strengthened by calibrating RMS against a concrete outcome (e.g., "RMS>X corresponds to >Y% of examples showing gold reward decrease") or by supplementing it with a directly interpretable measure.

- **Policy size scaling mixes model families.** The policy models are GPT-2 variants (0.1B–1.5B) and LLaMA-7B (Section 4.1 — "Policy Models"). The jump from 1.5B (GPT-2 XL) to 7B (LLaMA) changes architecture, training data, and base capabilities simultaneously. The paper claims "larger policies are easier to game" (Section 5.2), and the trend is visible within GPT-2 alone (0.1B→1.5B), but the 7B data point is confounded. This should be acknowledged or validated within a single model family.

- **Gold model quality is not assessed.** The gold model defines the benchmark's ground truth, but the paper provides no evaluation of its quality — e.g., its accuracy on held-out human preference data, its agreement with human raters, or its own reliability. If the gold model has systematic biases, the observed gaming could partly reflect gold-model pathologies rather than proxy failures. The paper acknowledges this caveat (Section 4.1: "This choice disregards how well the model captures the underlying objective of human raters") but does not quantify how much signal is trustworthy.

- **Only one proxy architecture family (DeBERTa-v3) is used.** The scaling result that larger proxies are harder to game is valuable but architecture-specific. Including at least one additional proxy family (e.g., a GPT-2-based reward model) would strengthen the generality of the claim. Without it, the results could reflect properties of DeBERTa-v3 specifically.

- **White-box setting: some tension between omitting Final Gold Reward and reporting RMS.** The paper states "We omit the gold reward in the white-box setting" because adversarial outputs are gibberish and "incomparable to other optimizers" (Section 4.4). Yet Table 1 reports white-box RMS, which requires gold reward to compute. While measuring divergence between proxy and gold curves on gibberish is conceptually different from comparing absolute gold reward values, the paper does not explain why RMS is comparable when Final Gold Reward is not. A brief clarification would resolve this.

- **Adversarial training failure on BoN/RL is noted but not analyzed.** Section 5.5 reports that adversarial training reduces gold reward in BoN and RL settings. This negative result is potentially important (it suggests adversarial training degrades the proxy's in-distribution tracking ability), but the paper does not analyze why — e.g., by comparing the proxy's loss landscape before/after adversarial training, or examining whether the proxy loses discriminatory power on in-distribution examples. A brief analysis would turn a descriptive result into a mechanistic insight.

### Trivial

- **The "exposure" experiment confounds two dimensions simultaneously.** The exposure increase in Figure 6 combines more training examples and more iterations per example. It is unclear which dimension drives the effect. A simple ablation (vary one at a time) or explicit acknowledgment would clarify.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Validate RMS against instance-level gaming (e.g., show correlation between RMS and the fraction of examples with negative gold reward change).
- Characterize what distinguishes consistently gamed examples — are they from specific domains, do they have low gold reward variance, are they in low-density regions of proxy training data?
- Provide more transparency on the gold model architecture, ensemble size, and training details so others can replicate.
- Include qualitative examples of gamed vs. non-gamed generations beyond the one phase-shift example (Section 5.4) to build intuition.
- Broaden proxy architecture families in future iterations of the benchmark.

## Novel Insights

The insight that carries the most value beyond the paper's own claims is the observation that **gamed examples are not noise — they are systematic, reproducible, and correlated across independently trained proxies.** This shifts the proxy gaming problem from "some proxies are imperfect" to "certain inputs are inherently vulnerable to gaming regardless of the proxy." If this finding generalizes, it implies that improving proxy robustness requires not just better training but also identifying and handling these intrinsically fragile inputs. The phase-shift phenomenon (Section 5.4) further suggests that gaming can emerge abruptly during optimization rather than gradually, which has implications for monitoring and early stopping strategies.

## Suggestions

1. Re-center the RMS metric by either calibrating it to a concrete interpretable threshold (e.g., "above RMS=X, >Y% of examples are gamed") or elevating a directly interpretable metric (fraction of gamed examples, average gold reward drop on the gamed subset) to primary status alongside RMS.
2. Acknowledge the GPT-2 / LLaMA confound in the policy scaling analysis and either validate the trend within a single family or state the limitation explicitly.
3. Add a brief analysis of gold model quality (accuracy on held-out preference pairs, or agreement with a subset of human judgments) so readers can calibrate how much of the gold signal is reliable.
