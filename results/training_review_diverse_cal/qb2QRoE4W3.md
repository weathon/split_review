## Summary

The paper proposes LLM-Cite, a fact verification method that replaces expensive search-based retrieval by directly prompting an LLM to generate candidate citation URLs, fetching their content on-the-fly, and using NLI to verify the claim. The core insight—that LLMs memorize URLs during pretraining and can exploit their semantic structure to guess unseen URLs—is both clever and non-obvious. The method achieves a >45× cost reduction over Google Search–based verification while providing attribution and handling fresh claims that static-index methods cannot. Evaluation spans three datasets covering human-written claims (Biographies), model-generated claims (ASQA), and fresh claims (FreshQA).

## Strengths

- **Novel and conceptually clean approach to eliminating search from fact verification.** Replacing the expensive search/retrieval step with LLM-generated URLs is a genuine conceptual contribution. The paper grounds this in two concrete mechanisms (URL memorization during pretraining and semantic URL structure) and provides real examples where the LLM generates valid URLs for post-cutoff claims (Figure 1, right). This differs fundamentally from prior work that either requires an external index or uses parametric knowledge without attribution.

- **Dramatic and well-documented cost advantage.** Section 4.5 and Table 2 present a detailed cost breakdown using published API prices: URL generation with Gemini 1.5 Flash is >90× cheaper than a single Google Search API call, and the full pipeline is >45× cheaper than Google Search + NLI. The paper also correctly notes the avoided upfront engineering costs (indexing, embedding, retrieval, ranking infrastructure).

- **Demonstrated ability to verify fresh claims that static methods cannot.** Figure 3 shows FACTSCORE at only 20% accuracy on FreshQA (unsurprising, given its static Wikipedia index), while LLM-Cite with GPT-4o and rejection sampling reaches 87%, approaching the Google Search baseline. The concrete example of verifying the November 2024 US election winner—a claim outside the LLM's training cutoff—effectively illustrates the practical value.

- **Empirical analysis of URL validity robustness.** Figure 4 (left) shows that increasing the number of generated URLs from 1 to 6 drives the fraction of claims with zero valid URLs to nearly zero, and even at 6 URLs fewer than 25% of generated URLs are invalid. This analysis supports the method's practical viability despite imperfect URL generation.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **FreshQA evaluation rests on only 30 manually written claims, making the quantitative comparison with Google Search unreliable.** The paper itself states that the dataset's answers were "sometimes outdated" and "often short," necessitating manual rewriting, which limited the evaluation to 30 claims (Section 3.1). On this tiny sample, the gap between LLM-Cite (GPT-4o RS: 87%) and Google Search (90%) is 3 percentage points—well within likely noise from a sample of 30. The paper's takeaway that LLM-Cite "gets very close to the performance of Google Search" is not supported by an evaluation of this size. The qualitative demonstration that LLM-Cite *can* verify fresh claims is not in doubt, but the headline quantitative comparison rests on a fragile foundation.

- **ASQA evaluation filters to only known-factual claims, turning the task into a recall-only test.** The paper filters model-generated answers to only those entailed by human-written reference answers (Section 3.1). This means every claim evaluated is *known to be factual by construction*. The evaluation tests only whether methods can find supporting evidence—i.e., recall. It provides no information about precision (would the method mistakenly verify a false claim?). The paper frames the results as "verification accuracy" (Section 4.2, Figure 2 right) and concludes it "can verify claims generated by diverse models better than FACTSCORE and P(TRUE)," but the evaluation design cannot support claims about discriminative verification ability on model-generated text. The relative ranking of methods on this recall-focused test is informative, but the absolute framing as verification accuracy is misleading.

### Minor

- **NLI component relies on commercial APIs, limiting reproducibility.** The primary NLI model (Check Grounding API) and the LLM-based NLI variant (Gemini 1.5 Flash) are both commercial services. While the paper correctly notes that LLM-Cite "can work with any NLI model" and provides a cost/performance trade-off analysis, the default configuration depends on paid APIs with no open-source fallback evaluated. This raises the practical barrier for reproduction and extension.

- **Generalizability claim to non-Wikipedia domains is unsupported.** Section 6 states the method "can be easily extended to any other reliable domains appearing on the Web (e.g., nytimes.com)," but no experiments or even qualitative examples are provided outside Wikipedia. While this is presented as a forward-looking discussion, the phrasing overstates what has been demonstrated. The paper would benefit from at least a pilot experiment on one non-Wikipedia domain or a softened claim.

- **No analysis of *why* generated URLs are invalid.** Section 4.4 reports that ~23% of generated URLs are invalid but does not categorize failure modes (e.g., wrong domain, non-existent page, redirect mismatch). Such analysis would help readers understand the method's failure patterns and guide future improvements.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, the reviews surface an interesting tension: the very property that makes LLM-Cite work (LLMs memorizing URLs from pretraining) also constrains its natural domain to publicly indexed web pages seen during training. This creates a clear capability boundary—the method excels on Wikipedia and similar public knowledge bases but would require finetuning or proxy identifiers for private corpora. The paper's discussion of this boundary is honest, but the reviews highlight that the community would benefit from a clearer quantification of where the method's URL generation works well versus breaks down (e.g., by entity popularity, URL structure type, or domain). This is a productive direction for follow-up work.

## Suggestions

1. **Expand the fresh-claims evaluation to at least 100–150 claims.** The manual annotation effort is justified by the importance of this claim. Alternatively, use existing FreshQA answers with careful verification of timeliness rather than writing new answers from scratch for only 30 questions.

2. **Restore precision information to the ASQA evaluation.** Either (a) report recall and precision on the unfiltered set, or (b) use the ground-truth ASQA answers and supporting Wikipedia URLs to measure whether the correct source is retrieved. This would turn the evaluation from a recall-only test into a full verification assessment.

3. **Provide a categorized breakdown of URL generation failures** (e.g., wrong domain, non-existent page, URL exists but content mismatches the claim) to help the community understand and improve the method.

4. **Evaluate one non-Wikipedia domain** (e.g., a curated subset of news articles with known URLs) to support the generalizability claim, or soften the language to make clear this is a hypothesis.

## Score and Decision

This paper makes a genuine and non-obvious contribution to fact verification. The core idea—offloading search to URL generation—is simple, cheap, and surprisingly effective. The cost analysis is rigorous and the main qualitative finding (that LLMs can generate valid URLs for post-cutoff claims) is compelling. However, the two major evaluation issues (30-sample fresh-claims comparison and the recall-only ASQA setup) mean the paper's quantitative performance claims are weaker than the presentation suggests. These issues are fixable, and the paper would be a solid contribution after addressing them.
