## Summary

This paper introduces ELM (Embedding Language Model), a framework that trains an adapter layer to map arbitrary domain embedding vectors (e.g., movie or user embeddings from a recommender system) into the token-embedding space of a pretrained LLM, enabling natural-language queries and descriptions of those abstract vectors. The approach is evaluated on 24 movie-interpretation tasks and a user-profile task using the MovieLens 25M dataset, with human ratings and two proposed consistency metrics (semantic and behavioral). ELM is also demonstrated on interpolated/extrapolated (hypothetical) embeddings and on generalizing Concept Activation Vectors to language.

## Strengths

1. **Novel and timely framework for making domain embeddings interpretable via LLMs.** The paper proposes a general architecture — adapter mapping from a domain embedding space (ℝⁿ) into an LLM's token-embedding space — that is agnostic to the type of domain embedding. This cleanly separates the problem of "understanding what an embedding encodes" from training or modifying the downstream model that produced the embedding. The framework is formally defined (Section 2) and applicable to behavioral, semantic, or other embedding types.

2. **Comprehensive evaluation with 25 tasks, two embedding types, and 100 human raters per task.** The paper constructs 24 movie tasks (summarization, review writing, comparison, etc.) and a user-profile generation task, tests on both behavioral (matrix factorization) and semantic (Sentence-T5-style) embeddings, and obtains human ratings from 100 raters per task on consistency, comprehensiveness, and quality. Many tasks score ≥0.85 on human ratings (Table 1), demonstrating that ELM's outputs are plausible and relevant.

3. **Demonstration of interpretation on hypothetical/novel embedding vectors.** ELM is tested on linearly interpolated movie and user embeddings (Figure 2) and on CAV-direction extrapolations (Figures 3, 4) — vectors that correspond to no real entity and for which no ground-truth text exists. The ability to generate coherent descriptions of these novel points is a genuine capability that text-only LLMs cannot replicate, because there is no natural-language way to direct a text-only LLM to an arbitrary point in a domain embedding space. This is the paper's most interesting result.

4. **Extension of CAVs from a directional sensitivity measure to actionable language generation.** By decoding embedding shifts along CAV directions (e.g., "funnier," "thought-provoking"), ELM produces natural-language attribute manipulations (Figures 3, 4), demonstrating that the framework can operationalize CAV-based interpretability into human-readable outputs.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The comparison to text-only LLMs (PaLM 2-L, GPT-4) is misaligned with the paper's claims of "outperforming."** The setup (Section 4) gives text-only LLMs a ground-truth task example but **no information about which specific movie or user to describe** — they are asked to generate task output without knowing the entity. ELM, by contrast, receives the domain embedding of the interpolated movie/user. The text-only LLMs are guaranteed to fail because they lack the relevant input. The paper's conclusion (Section 5) that ELM has "better semantic and behavioral consistency... compared to state-of-the-art text-only LLMs" is misleading. The comparison should be reframed as a **capability demonstration** (ELM can interpret embedding vectors; text-only LLMs fundamentally cannot because there is no text to give them) rather than a head-to-head performance comparison. The current framing inflates the results and undermines trust in the paper's claims.

2. **Synthetic training data from PaLM 2-L is a confound that is not analyzed or controlled.** All training targets for ELM's movie tasks are generated by prompting PaLM 2-L with movie titles and task descriptions (Section 3.1). ELM is trained to reproduce these outputs from the *embedding* alone. The paper provides no analysis of the quality, diversity, or potential hallucination rate of these synthetic targets. Without experiments showing that ELM can extract information *not present in the synthetic training targets* (e.g., by testing on a held-out attribute that is embeddable but not described in the PaLM-generated data), it is unclear whether ELM genuinely interprets the embedding or has learned to produce text that is plausible *in general* (i.e., mining the LLM head's own prior). The interpolation experiments help, but the evaluation of interpolations relies on SC (see Weakness 3), creating a partial circularity.

3. **The claimed generality to "arbitrary embedding spaces" is unsupported.** All experiments use a single dataset (MovieLens 25M) with two embedding types derived from it. The paper repeatedly claims the method is agnostic to embedding source (Section 2: "our technique is agnostic to the type and source of (W, d)"; Related Work: "supports interpretation of *arbitrary* embedding spaces"), but provides no evidence on other domains (e.g., protein sequences, product embeddings, graph embeddings, image features). A single-domain demonstration, while acceptable for a new method, does not support claims of "arbitrary" generality. The claims should be tempered to match the evidence.

### Minor

1. **Semantic Consistency (SC) metric has validity limitations.** SC measures cosine distance between the re-embedded output text and the original embedding. Since the DLM used for re-embedding is the same model used to create the original semantic embeddings, high SC primarily confirms that the output is in-distribution for the DLM's embedding space — it does not directly validate that ELM is extracting task-specific information from the embedding. The paper acknowledges SC's utility for hypothetical embeddings (where no alternative exists), which is reasonable, but the metric should be interpreted with appropriate caution. The paper would benefit from a complementary evaluation (e.g., probing whether specific attributes can be recovered from ELM's outputs with higher accuracy than from the embedding directly).

2. **No ablation studies.** The two-stage training procedure (freeze LLM → fine-tune all) is motivated as avoiding local minima (Section 2), but no experiment compares against alternatives: (a) joint training from scratch, (b) single-stage adapter-only training, or (c) alternative adapter architectures beyond the two-layer MLP. Similarly, the choice of a two-layer MLP adapter is not compared to linear projections or transformer bottlenecks. These are standard expectations for a systems paper proposing a new training methodology.

3. **Human evaluation details are incomplete.** The paper reports 100 raters per task and three rating dimensions (consistency with plot, language comprehensiveness, task quality) on a 0–1 scale, but does not report inter-rater agreement (e.g., Krippendorff's alpha, Fleiss' kappa), rater instructions, or whether raters saw ground-truth movie information. Without agreement statistics, it is unclear whether the near-perfect scores (many ≥0.85) reflect genuinely strong outputs or lenient rating criteria. Examples of low-scoring outputs to illustrate failure modes would also strengthen the evaluation.

4. **Behavioral Consistency (BC) metric depends on an unvalidated DLM ranker.** BC uses a dual-encoder DLM to rank movies by similarity to ELM-generated user profiles. The paper does not validate that this DLM ranker correlates with actual user ratings before using it as an evaluation tool. The metric thus conflates the quality of the DLM as a preference predictor with the quality of ELM's interpretation.

## Novel Insights

The reviews surface a tension that the paper does not fully address: the synthetic training paradigm (LLM → text targets → train ELM to reproduce from embedding) creates a potential auto-correlation between ELM's training signal and its evaluation metrics — if the re-embedding DLM, the PaLM teacher, and the training data all derive from similar text sources, high SC scores may partially reflect this shared provenance rather than genuine embedding interpretation. A secondary insight is that the paper's most compelling result (interpolation/extrapolation on hypothetical embeddings) partially sidesteps this issue because there is no ground-truth text for those novel vectors, but the evaluation still relies on SC/BC which inherit the same confounds. A stronger design would combine interpolation experiments with a held-out attribute prediction task that breaks the circularity.

## Suggestions

1. **Redesign the text-only LLM comparison.** Either remove the direct "outperformance" framing and present it as a capability demonstration, or provide the text-only LLMs with as much proxy information as possible (e.g., movie title, plot summary, or a nearest-neighbor text description of the interpolated point). Only compare ELM on the axis where the LLM has the same information.

2. **Address the synthetic data confound directly.** Design a held-out experiment where a specific attribute is known to be recoverable from the embedding (via linear probe) but is *not* explicitly described in the PaLM-generated training targets. Show that ELM can produce text from which that attribute can be inferred, breaking the circularity between synthetic targets and evaluation.

3. **Temper the generality claims.** Replace "arbitrary embedding spaces" with claims commensurate with the evidence (e.g., "our approach is demonstrated on semantic and behavioral embeddings from a recommender system domain").

4. **Add ablation studies** comparing two-stage training against joint end-to-end training, and comparing the two-layer MLP adapter against simpler (linear) or more complex alternatives.

5. **Report inter-rater agreement statistics** and provide examples of both high- and low-scoring ELM outputs to help readers calibrate the human evaluation results.

## Score and Decision

This paper proposes a genuinely novel framework — using LLM adapters to render domain embeddings interpretable via natural language — and backs it with a large-scale evaluation (25 tasks, human ratings, interpolation experiments). The core idea is sound and the technical execution is competent.

However, the paper has two significant weaknesses that directly affect the persuasiveness of its central claims. First, the comparison to text-only LLMs is framed as "outperforming" when the setup guarantees the text-only models cannot succeed (they receive no information about the entity to describe). This inflates the reported advantage and needs fundamental reframing. Second, the reliance on PaLM 2-L generated training targets creates an unanalyzed confound between what ELM learns from the embedding versus what it inherits from the teacher LLM's style and priors. On top of these, the claims of generality to "arbitrary embedding spaces" outpace the evidence from a single-domain demonstration.

These issues are **major but not fatal** — they can be addressed with evaluation redesign and tempered claims. The paper would benefit from a revised presentation (not claiming performance superiority where the comparison is inherently asymmetric) and additional controls (held-out attribute verification, domain diversity). In its current form, the paper is borderline: the contribution is real and interesting, but the evaluation does not fully support the claims as stated.
