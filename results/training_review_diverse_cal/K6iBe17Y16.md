## Summary

This paper provides a statistical grounding for supervised heuristic learning in planning, arguing via the maximum entropy principle that admissible heuristics (e.g., LMCUT) should be used as lower bounds of a Truncated Gaussian distribution rather than as regression targets for MSE. The resulting NLL loss function is tested on four classical planning domains with systematic ablations (learned vs. fixed sigma, residual vs. no residual, clipping vs. truncation), consistently showing faster convergence, lower heuristic error, and improved GBFS planning performance.

## Strengths

- **Principled theoretical justification via maximum entropy.** The paper derives the Truncated Gaussian as the max-ent distribution given finite mean, finite variance, and bounded support (Section 3, citing Dowson 1973). This goes beyond ad-hoc loss choices in prior work and gives a clean answer to the "why" question in heuristic learning.

- **Systematic ablations validate the theoretical distinction.** Table 1 compares learned vs. fixed sigma across 8 configurations; Truncated Gaussian improves with learned sigma in 7/8 cases, while Gaussian degrades in 5/8. This directly supports the paper's claim that sigma matters for the truncated model in a way it does not for standard MSE, because sigma affects the expected value of a truncated distribution.

- **Improved planning performance.** Table 2 shows that GBFS guided by Truncated Gaussian heuristics (learn/ff configuration) solves more instances (e.g., blocks: 0.88 vs. 0.84; ferry: 0.98 vs. 0.91) with fewer node evaluations than Gaussian or clipped-Gaussian variants. The pattern is consistent across all four domains, supporting the claim that the statistical modeling translates into search improvements.

- **Novel use of formally derived admissible bounds.** The paper is the first to combine admissible heuristics automatically derived by symbolic planning algorithms (LMCUT) as lower bounds in a Truncated Gaussian, contrasting with prior uses of arbitrary or manually specified bounds in other fields (Related Work, paragraphs 2–3).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
None. The paper's core claim — that the Truncated Gaussian loss is better than MSE-based Gaussian loss for supervised heuristic learning — is supported by consistent results across all tested configurations. No identified weakness invalidates this central result.

### Minor

- **Limited domain scope relative to the strength of the claims.** The experiments cover four classical planning domains (blocksworld-4ops, ferry, gripper, visitall), all relatively simple and small in scale. The conclusion states that results "confirm that it is the correct approach for utilizing admissible bounds in heuristic learning." While the theoretical derivation (max-ent → Truncated Gaussian) is domain-independent, the empirical evidence for its practical benefits is drawn from a narrow base. The paper mentions ablation studies with hmax and blind heuristics as alternative lower bounds (Section 4, "Lower bounds" paragraph) but does not report those results — the sentence is cut off at "Ablation studies with l=hmax(s) and l=blind(s) are" with no subsequent data. Demonstrating the method on at least 1–2 more challenging IPC domains (e.g., logistics, depots) and reporting the bound-quality ablations would substantially strengthen the generality claim.

- **Results for linear regression and HGN models not shown.** The paper states (line 601–604) that "our conclusions from the Linear and the HGN models were not substantially different" but provides no data, summary table, or even a brief quantitative statement (e.g., "TGN outperformed GN in 6/8 configurations for linear, 7/8 for HGN"). Since the paper claims implementation-agnostic validation (Section 4, "Model Configurations"), the absence of this evidence weakens that claim. Readers must take on faith that the pattern holds for non-NLM architectures.

- **Missing LMCUT baseline in planning evaluation.** Table 2 compares learned heuristics against FF and against other learned configurations, but does not include the off-the-shelf LMCUT heuristic in GBFS. Since LMCUT is the admissible bound used throughout the paper and the natural reference for the "bound-utilization" claim, including it would make the comparison more direct (e.g., does the learned heuristic consistently beat the raw admissible bound in search?). The MSE of LMCUT is reported in Table 1, but its planning performance is not.

- **The dismissal of learning from poly-time heuristics is somewhat overstated.** The paper says (lines 80–81) that "learning from poly-time admissible heuristics ... by minimizing mean square errors (MSE) does not provide any practical benefits, since its result is merely a noisy, inadmissible copy of a heuristic that is already efficient to compute." While the paper's target is using these heuristics as training targets (not as residual bases or bounds), the blanket wording could mislead readers. The paper later acknowledges residual learning from FF (a poly-time heuristic) as a beneficial component of its own method, which subtly undermines the absoluteness of the earlier statement.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation from the reviews is the interplay between the max-ent theoretical framework and the empirical finding that learning sigma helps the Truncated Gaussian but hurts the Gaussian. This is not just an implementation detail — it follows from the theory: sigma influences the expected value (used as the search heuristic) only when the distribution is truncated, which explains why Gaussian models (where the expected value is simply mu) cannot benefit from a learned sigma in the same way. This connection between the theoretical derivation and the specific empirical pattern is the paper's strongest contribution and deserves emphasis.
