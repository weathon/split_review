## Summary

This paper addresses driver dispatch in designated driving platforms, which differs from ride-hailing due to sparser orders, slower driver movement, and longer-lasting cross-agent effects. The authors propose RLD3, a MARL algorithm coupling three components: (1) group-sharing of parameters/experience across heterogeneous driver types, (2) a dense neighborhood potential reward to combat feedback sparsity, and (3) a window-lasting policy ensemble that averages idling actions over time to capture long-term interactions among agents. Experiments on a simulator built from real Hangzhou data show RLD3 outperforming several DRL and optimization baselines on completed orders, with ablation studies supporting each component's contribution.

## Strengths

- **Group-sharing mechanism validated by ablation.** The paper demonstrates that introducing group-sharing to MADDPG increases completed orders (Table 2) and that removing it from RLD3 degrades performance. This directly supports the claim that sharing parameters/experience among drivers of the same cost type helps under sparse-data conditions. The effect is shown both positively (adding to MADDPG) and negatively (removing from RLD3), providing complementary evidence.

- **Window-lasting interaction empirically justified.** The ablation shows removing the temporal averaging of idling actions substantially hurts RLD3's performance (25.24 → 21.31 completed orders). This confirms the paper's motivating insight that in a slow-movement setting, single-step action information is too noisy to capture cross-agent effects, and temporal smoothing matters.

- **Dense potential reward improves both performance and stability.** The ablation without the potential reward drops completed orders (25.24 → 23.28) and, as the paper notes, increases value-function instability. This validates the reward design's role in overcoming sparse immediate feedback.

- **Real-data simulator provides a grounded evaluation testbed.** The simulator is built from a real Hangzhou dataset (~3,000 drivers, ~13,000 orders/day), includes realistic order-cancellation dynamics, and supports both training and evaluation of RL algorithms. This grounds the experiments in a practical setting beyond synthetic benchmarks.

- **RLD3 outperforms a range of baselines including both DRL methods (DDPG, MADDPG, MAMFRL, RND) and optimization methods (Max-throughput, MPC).** The performance advantage on completed orders (25.24 vs. 23.06 for the next best, MPC) is nontrivial, and the method also maintains competitive moving distance.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **Baselines are not given the same potential reward, making the source of RLD3's advantage unclear.** The paper states that DRL baselines are "applied with the immediate match reward and move cost" (line 198) but not the neighborhood potential reward `nb_i^t`. Since this potential reward provides dense feedback at every timestep — a well-known technique for improving RL in sparse-reward settings — a substantial portion of the performance gap in Figure 4 and Table 1 could stem from this reward shaping alone rather than from the group-sharing or window-lasting mechanisms. The ablation shows RLD3 without potential reward still achieves 23.28 completed orders, which beats most baselines, partially mitigating this concern. However, without running the baselines *with* the same potential reward, the community cannot cleanly attribute the improvement to the claimed structural innovations.

- **Training environment is denser than the motivating real-world problem, weakening the sparsity claim.** The paper motivates sparsity with a real ratio of ~4.3 orders per driver (13,000 orders / 3,000 drivers). Training uses 500 orders and 50 drivers — a ratio of 10:1, or ~2.3× denser. The paper does not evaluate at the full scale or at any intermediate scale to show that RLD3's relative advantage grows with genuine sparsity. This does not invalidate the method (the absolute numbers 50 drivers, 500 orders are still small), but it leaves open whether the core thesis — that RLD3 specifically addresses the challenges of *extreme* sparsity — is supported by the evidence shown. The gap is not an "order of magnitude" (the critic's phrasing overstates it), but it is a meaningful real-vs-evaluated mismatch that the paper does not address.

### Minor

- **Group assignment is underspecified.** The paper says drivers are "classified into several types based on their cost conditions" (line 94) and that move cost depends on group index (line 102), but never specifies what cost conditions define the 5 groups used in experiments, how drivers are assigned to groups, or whether this classification is done using data, heuristics, or domain expertise. This makes the method irreproducible and raises the question of whether performance depends on a specific, potentially hand-tuned grouping that might not transfer to other cities.

- **Neighborhood definition for the potential reward is vague.** The formula for `nb_i^t` (line 119) sums over "neighbor order j" but never defines what distance threshold (if any) constitutes a "neighbor." It is unclear whether this is a fixed-radius neighborhood, a k-nearest-neighbor set, or all orders in the city. This affects both the reward magnitude and the computational cost, and again harms reproducibility.

- **IID generalization test is too weak to be informative.** The paper's own text notes that "all algorithms demonstrate good IID generalization performance" (line 220), which actually indicates the test is not discriminating — if all methods maintain performance, the test likely measures memorization of hotspot locations rather than genuine robustness. The paper claims robustness but the evidence is the opposite of discriminating.

- **Window size W=60 is not justified and no sensitivity analysis is provided.** The window size controls a core mechanism and corresponds to 30 minutes of simulated time, but the paper offers no analysis of how performance varies with W. Similarly, no sensitivity analysis is provided for the number of groups M (the ablation varies it for MADDPG but not for RLD3's own performance).

- **K-Means state encoder is used but not compared against alternatives.** The encoder is a fixed preprocessing step handling varying-dimensional state. The paper does not compare against learned encoders (e.g., attention-based) or simpler aggregation (e.g., mean/max pooling), leaving it unclear whether this choice is critical or incidental to performance.

- **Ablation comparisons would benefit from an "ablated-to-baseline" anchor.** The weakest ablated variant of RLD3 (Table 2) still includes the potential reward, move cost, and state encoder. The paper does not report a version stripped of all three RLD3-specific components (no group-sharing, no window, no potential reward) that would be directly comparable to the DRL baselines. While the existing ablations are informative for component contributions within RLD3, they do not fully bridge the comparison to the baselines.

- **No out-of-distribution or temporal shift evaluation.** The paper's generalization test is restricted to IID held-out samples. Real deployment would face distribution shifts (weekends, holidays, weather), and the current experiments do not test robustness to these.

- **Driver compliance assumption is acknowledged but not discussed in context of results.** The paper assumes full compliance with dispatch instructions (line 56) and flags this as future work (line 254). Given that real drivers often reject dispatches, the reported performance may be optimistic. This is an acknowledged limitation rather than a flaw, but its potential impact on the results deserves discussion.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting aspect of this paper is the interaction between the three sparsity types it identifies (dataset sparsity, feedback sparsity, interaction sparsity) and the three corresponding designs (group-sharing, potential reward, window-lasting). The ablation results show that removing *any one* of the three components degrades performance, but the degradation pattern is informative: window-lasting removal hurts the most (25.24 → 21.31), suggesting that the multi-agent interaction modeling challenge may be the most severe in this domain, even more than the individual feedback sparsity that potential rewards address. This hierarchy of which sparsity type matters most is not discussed by the paper but emerges from the numbers. None beyond the paper's own contributions.

## Suggestions

1. **Run baselines with the same potential reward.** This is the single most important control experiment. Even adding just one baseline (e.g., MADDPG + potential reward) would allow readers to separate the reward-shaping effect from the structural innovations.

2. **Test at multiple subsampling ratios to demonstrate the sparsity claim directly.** Show that RLD3's relative gain over baselines grows as the driver-to-order ratio moves from 1:10 (training) toward the real-world ~1:4.3, and ideally beyond into even sparser regimes. This would directly validate the paper's central thesis.

3. **Specify the group assignment protocol and the neighborhood threshold** in the potential reward formula. Without this, the paper is not reproducible.

4. **Add one "fully stripped" ablation variant** — group-sharing=False, window-lasting=False, potential-reward=False — that is the direct equivalent of a baseline DDPG. This would anchor the ablation study to the baseline experiments and show the cumulative contribution of all RLD3 components stacked together.

## Score and Decision

The paper tackles a real and practically important problem with a thoughtful combination of three mechanisms, each of which is validated by ablation. The weaknesses are significant — particularly the uncontrolled reward-shaping advantage in baseline comparisons and the training/reality density mismatch — but they are reparable and do not invalidate the core approach. The ablation study provides credible evidence that each component (group-sharing, window-lasting, potential reward) contributes meaningfully within RLD3's design space. However, the paper's strongest claims about "addressing sparsity" are not fully supported because the experiments operate at a different density regime than the motivating real-world problem, and the baseline comparisons cannot cleanly attribute gains to the claimed structural novelties versus the reward design.

This is a borderline paper: the technical insight is sound and the engineering contributions are real, but the experimental validation has gaps that prevent full confidence in the claimed contributions.
