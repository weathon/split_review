## Summary

This paper identifies two phenomena in time series forecasting models — **frequency confusion** (performance degradation when training on multiple frequencies) and **poor frequency generalization** (inability to handle unseen frequencies) — and proposes **Freq-Synth**, a synthetic data generator that creates training signals from harmonics of the target dataset's sampling rate. The key empirical finding is that training on only 5,000 synthetic samples generated by Freq-Synth outperforms training on ≈1,000× larger real-world datasets in zero-shot forecasting across 6/8 benchmarks and 6 different model architectures, while also reducing synthetic data generation time by orders of magnitude (0.1 seconds vs. up to 138 minutes for competing methods).

## Strengths

- **Clear empirical demonstration of frequency confusion and poor frequency generalization (Fig. 1).** The paper systematically shows that adding more frequencies to the training set degrades test performance across six models (TTM, Timer, UniTime, Moment, GPT4TS, PatchTST), and that models perform significantly worse when the target frequency is absent from training. This provides concrete grounding for the problem Freq-Synth addresses.

- **Periodogram alignment is shown to be more predictive of transfer performance than domain similarity (Fig. 2).** Datasets with high periodogram Pearson correlation (PCC ≥ 0.9) yield lower zero-shot MSE than same-sector datasets (Energy, Nature, Traffic) or even the same dataset at different sampling rates (ETT). This non-obvious result strongly supports the paper's central thesis that frequency similarity is the key factor for effective transfer.

- **Freq-Synth achieves superior zero-shot forecasting using orders of magnitude less training data (Table 1).** Training solely on Freq-Synth (5,000 samples, ≈1,000× smaller) outperforms training on real data across 6/8 datasets and all six models tested, with average MSE reductions (e.g., TTM: 0.602→0.454, GPT4TS: 0.606→0.426). The 60% MSE reduction on ETTm1 is particularly striking and is plausibly explained by the absence of the 15-minute sampling rate in the real training set, aligning with the frequency generalization argument.

- **Extremely efficient synthetic data generation (Section 5.2).** Generating 1 million time points takes 0.1 seconds for Freq-Synth vs. 3 s (TimesFM), 14.6 s (ForecastPFN), and 138.2 minutes (KernelSynth). This practical advantage is backed by measured generation times.

- **Freq-Synth also improves few-shot forecasting (Table 3).** Fine-tuning models pre-trained on Freq-Synth outperforms fine-tuning models pre-trained on real data, with average MSE reductions of 10.6% (TTM), 19.5% (Timer), and 17.3% (PatchTST), extending the method's utility beyond pure zero-shot.

### Minor

- **The synthetic-data comparison (Table 2) could be more explicit about the experimental protocol.** The columns are labeled with data-generation method names (TimesFM, ForecastPFN, KernelSynth), and the paper states results are "averaged on a forecasting horizon of 96 across all six models (see Sec. 5.1)." Clarifying this would remove an unnecessary obstacle for readers evaluating the central comparative claim.

- **The mapping from sampling rate to fundamental frequency is only given through examples, not as a general statement in the main text.** The paper says the fundamental frequency is "derived" from the sampling rate and references the appendix for details. While the appendix (stripped by the parser) presumably contains the full derivation, the main text would benefit from a short explicit statement (e.g., "for hourly data, the daily fundamental frequency is 1/24 cycles per hour; for 15-minute data, it is 1/96 cycles per 15-minute unit"). The intuition is recoverable from the experimental examples (ω=1/24 in Fig. 1; ETTm1/ETTm2 discussion), but a general statement would improve clarity, especially for practitioners applying the method to new datasets.

- **Standard deviations are not reported for main results (Tables 1 and 3).** Results are averaged over three random seeds, but no variance measure is provided. Since randomness enters both the synthetic data generation (amplitudes, phases, sampling from pool) and model training, variability could be non-negligible. Reporting standard deviations (or at least ranges) would help assess whether the reported improvements are robust.

- **The few-shot experiment (Table 3) contains a distributional confound that is not discussed.** Models pre-trained on real data (Monash+PEMS) are compared against models pre-trained on Freq-Synth (target-specific harmonics), then both are fine-tuned on 10% of target data. The real pre-training data spans many sampling rates, which the paper itself argues causes frequency confusion. This asymmetry tilts the comparison toward synthetic data. While the paper's own narrative explains why this happens, the conclusion should acknowledge that real pre-training with sampling-rate-matched data could be a stronger baseline, or discuss this confound explicitly.

- **The "robustness" claim in the conclusion is not specifically defined or measured.** The paper states that Freq-Synth "improves the performance and robustness" of models, but robustness (whether to seed variation, horizon, or dataset shift) is not explicitly evaluated. This is a minor overclaim that is easy to fix.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Ablation: isolate the effect of the harmonic assumption.** The strongest evidence for the core hypothesis would be to show that deviating from the harmonic structure degrades performance systematically — e.g., using the *wrong* fundamental frequency, varying the number of harmonics (1 vs. 3 vs. 5), or varying `l` (number of sines per variate).
- **Analysis of failure cases (Exchange, Weather) using periodograms.** The paper acknowledges these underperform but does not analyze why. Showing their periodograms and discussing why the harmonic assumption breaks down would strengthen the paper's honesty and guide future work.
- **Connect the frequency-confusion analysis directly to models trained on Freq-Synth.** Evaluating a Freq-Synth-trained model on the same sine-wave sweep used in Fig. 4 (i.e., measuring its frequency-response curve) would directly demonstrate that the method alleviates the frequency-specific overfitting observed in real-data-trained models.
- **Specify the exact Monash/PEMS dataset composition used for the "Real" training baseline.** The paper says it uses "a subset of datasets from Monash and PEMS" inspired by TTM but does not list which ones or the hyperparameters. Citing the TTM configuration or providing the list would improve reproducibility.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting insight that emerges across the reviews is that the converse framing of the standard scaling hypothesis may hold for time series: training on less data (5,000 synthetic samples) can outperform training on ≈1,000× more real data, provided the synthetic data is frequency-pure and task-aligned. This is not merely an efficiency result — it suggests that "more data" in time series can be actively harmful when it introduces frequency confusion, and that practitioners should consider frequency profiling of their training corpora rather than blindly accumulating volume. The synthetic-data comparison (Table 2) further shows that not any synthetic data works: methods that generate broad-spectrum or multi-frequency synthetic signals (KernelSynth, TimesFM, ForecastPFN) underperform the simple harmonic approach. This inverts the typical intuition that realistic or diverse synthetic data is better.
