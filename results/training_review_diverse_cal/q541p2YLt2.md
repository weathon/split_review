## Summary

This paper investigates why softmax-based self-attention in Transformers leads to training instability via attention entropy collapse. It demonstrates empirically that (1) the non-Lipschitz softmax function is highly sensitive to attention logit variance, producing near-one-hot distributions as variance grows, (2) this concentration increases the Frobenius norm of the attention probability matrix ‖P‖_F, which appears in a known upper bound on the gradient of the self-attention layer, and (3) Lipschitz kernel functions (ReLU, ELU+1) avoid entropy collapse and maintain stable gradients. The paper's core contribution is a proposed mechanistic explanation linking entropy collapse to gradient explosion through ‖P‖_F, supported by controlled experiments on an in-context linear regression task using only self-attention layers.

## Strengths

- **Empirical demonstration that Lipschitz kernel attention avoids entropy collapse while softmax does not.** Figures 1 and 2 show that softmax-based attention (including with QK-LayerNorm) drives attention entropy to zero and produces one-hot probability vectors, whereas ReLU and ELU+1 keep entropy high and stable throughout training. This directly supports the claim that the choice of re-weighting function determines whether entropy collapse occurs.
- **Controlled experiment isolating sensitivity to attention logit variance (Section 3.3, Figure 3).** By fixing the query and sampling keys with increasing variance σ² without any training, the paper cleanly isolates the effect of variance on entropy. Softmax produces sharply peaked, low-entropy distributions as σ² increases, while Lipschitz kernels maintain stable entropy. This experiment is well-designed to demonstrate the root cause of entropy collapse independently of training dynamics.
- **Systematic comparison of multiple attention variants under identical conditions.** The paper compares softmax, softmax with QK-LayerNorm, ReLU, and ELU+1 (Figures 1, 2, 4, 5). The finding that QK-LayerNorm still collapses is informative — it shows that normalizing the query/key magnitudes is insufficient, strengthening the case that the softmax exponential function itself is the key issue.
- **Clean experimental design using only self-attention layers (Section 3.1).** The paper deliberately strips away MLPs, layer normalization, and residual connections to isolate the influence of the attention mechanism on instability. This is a legitimate methodological choice for a mechanistic analysis and allows unambiguous attribution of gradient explosion to the attention function.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The central causal claim — that entropy collapse *causes* gradient explosion via increased ‖P‖_F — is supported only by an upper bound and correlation, not by a demonstrated causal mechanism.** The paper relies on Eq. (9) from Dasoulas et al. (2021), which gives an *upper bound* on the gradient norm that includes ‖P‖_F as one term. The reasoning is: entropy collapse → one-hot attention → ‖P‖_F grows from 1 to √N → the bound grows → gradient could be large. But an upper bound does not guarantee that the gradient *actually* becomes large — it only says the gradient is bounded by something that grows. The paper then shows empirically (Figure 5) that ‖P‖_F and the gradient norm rise together, but this is consistent with both being driven by a common underlying factor (e.g., growth in query/key weight norms). No intervention experiment is performed (e.g., artificially clamping ‖P‖_F to test whether gradient explosion is suppressed, or inducing one-hot distributions independently to test whether gradients explode). No lower bound on the gradient norm in terms of ‖P‖_F is derived. Since the paper's novelty hinges on explaining *why* entropy collapse causes instability (not just observing that it does), this gap is significant. The proposed mechanism remains a plausible hypothesis rather than a demonstrated result.

2. **The theoretical analysis of why Lipschitz kernel attention avoids instability is incomplete: it addresses only the kernel function φ, not the full normalized attention mechanism.** The paper claims (Section 3.1, Definition 3.1) that Lipschitz kernel attention is stable because φ is Lipschitz. However, the actual attention function in Eq. (5) includes a denominator: A_i' = φ(Q_i) Σ φ(K_j)^T V_j / φ(Q_i) Σ φ(K_j)^T. The normalized attention probabilities P_{i,j} = φ(Q_i)φ(K_j)^T / Σ_k φ(Q_i)φ(K_k)^T involve a ratio, and the Lipschitz property of φ does not automatically extend to this normalized composition. Small changes in φ outputs could produce large relative changes in the normalized probabilities if the denominator is small. The empirical results (Figure 3) show that the full mechanism works stably in practice, but the paper's theoretical argument for *why* it works is incomplete — it attributes stability to the Lipschitz property of φ without analyzing the effect of normalization. This weakens the paper's explanatory framework.

### Minor

1. **No loss or accuracy curves are reported.** The paper shows entropy, gradient norm, and ‖P‖_F, but never reports the actual loss or prediction error on the linear regression task. Figure 1 caption mentions "the loss diverges" for softmax, but a quantitative loss curve is never shown. If the model fails to converge with softmax but succeeds with Lipschitz kernels, showing the loss curves would add significant weight to the claim that entropy collapse → gradient explosion → training failure matters. Currently the reader must infer this from the gradient norm plots.

2. **Generalizability claims are broader than the evidence supports.** The paper states conclusions about "Transformer training instability" in general terms, but the evidence comes from a single task (in-context linear regression) using a model consisting only of self-attention layers (no MLPs, no layer normalization, no residual connections, no learning rate schedule, no weight decay). While the paper cites proxy-model precedents and justifies the simplicity for isolating the attention mechanism, the broader claims about instability in realistic transformers would benefit from a clearer acknowledgment of these limitations.

## Suggestions

1. **Strengthen the causal argument with an intervention experiment.** For example, artificially clamp the attention probabilities to be uniform (forcing ‖P‖_F = 1) during softmax-based training and check whether gradient explosion is suppressed; or independently induce one-hot distributions through a scaling factor on the logits and verify that gradients explode even without weight growth. This would directly test whether ‖P‖_F is the causal driver rather than a correlated symptom.

2. **Provide a theoretical analysis of the full normalized Lipschitz kernel attention function.** Show that the normalized attention probabilities P_{i,j} (including the denominator) have bounded gradients or a bounded Lipschitz constant, or state conditions under which this holds. This would directly parallel the softmax bound in Eq. (9) and complete the argument that Lipschitz kernels guarantee stability.

3. **Report loss curves alongside the diagnostic metrics** (entropy, ‖P‖_F, gradient norm) to make the connection between instability and task performance explicit.

4. **Add a limitations paragraph** in the conclusion acknowledging that the study uses a simplified proxy model and that the results may not fully transfer to full-scale transformers with residual connections, layer normalization, MLPs, and learning rate schedules.

## Score and Decision

The paper addresses an important question — why softmax-based attention leads to training instability — and provides clean, well-designed empirical demonstrations that Lipschitz kernel attentions avoid entropy collapse while softmax does not. The controlled experiment in Section 3.3 is particularly effective at isolating the role of variance sensitivity. However, the paper's core explanatory claim — that entropy collapse *causes* gradient explosion through increased ‖P‖_F — remains incompletely supported: the theoretical argument relies on an upper bound (not a lower bound or guaranteed mechanism), and no intervention experiment is performed to disentangle cause from correlation. Additionally, the theoretical analysis of Lipschitz kernel attention addresses only the kernel function and ignores the normalization denominator. These are structural gaps in the paper's main argument, not merely missing experiments or presentation issues. The paper has genuine strengths in its empirical demonstrations, but its central novel claim is not yet convincingly established.
