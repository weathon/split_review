## Summary

This paper introduces DITTO (Demonstration ITerated Task Optimization), a method for personalizing LLMs using fewer than 10 user demonstrations. DITTO generates online preference comparisons by treating demonstrations as preferred over model samples (including from earlier iterations), then optimizes with DPO in an online imitation learning framework. Across static author-attribution benchmarks (CMCC/CCAT50) and a user study (N=16, 320 pairwise judgments), DITTO achieves average win-rate improvements of ~19 percentage points over strong baselines including few-shot GPT-4, SFT, and self-play methods.

## Strengths

- **Consistent and substantial empirical gains across multiple evaluation modalities.** DITTO outperforms all baselines by an average of ~19 pp on static benchmarks (Table 1) and achieves a 68.8% win rate in a blinded user study against prompted GPT-4, SFT, and self-prompt baselines. These gains are statistically significant (ANOVA + Tukey, p<0.05) across nearly all conditions and are corroborated by both GPT-4 evaluation and human judgments.

- **Dramatic sample efficiency of demonstrations vs. pairwise preferences.** Section 5.3 empirically shows that DITTO with 4 demonstrations outperforms DPO trained on up to 500 synthetic pairwise preferences — even when the base model is first fine-tuned on the demonstrations (Figure 3). This directly supports the paper's central claim that demonstrations can substitute for large preference datasets in the individual-alignment setting.

- **Principled derivation from online imitation learning.** Section 3.3 derives DITTO as a solution to a minimax objective over a reparameterized reward function, establishing a theoretical connection to IRL and providing conditions (Lemma 3.1) under which DITTO can extrapolate beyond the demonstrator's performance.

- **Ablations validate key design choices.** The paper isolates the contributions of replay comparisons (6.5 pp drop when removed), inter-model comparisons (2 pp drop), and reference policy freezing (70.1% → 45.8% when unfrozen). These ablations convincingly show that each component of the algorithm is functional.

- **Realistic user study with blinded evaluation.** The user study (N=16) uses real participants defining their own email-writing tasks, with blinded head-to-head comparisons. A Fightin' Words analysis further confirms that DITTO avoids the cliché-ridden style of GPT-4 outputs, providing qualitative evidence of genuine stylistic alignment.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

None. The paper's core claims are well-supported by the evidence.

### Minor

- **The "order of magnitude more sample-efficient" claim (Section 5.3) is overgeneralized.** The experiment supporting this claim compares demonstrations against *synthetic* pairwise preferences generated by sampling from the base model or a demo-fine-tuned model. This does not test against human-provided pairwise preferences, which could be far more informative per sample. The conclusion as written ("an order of magnitude more sample-efficient for individuals than soliciting pairwise preferences") implies a general claim about feedback modalities, but the evidence only supports a comparison against model-generated synthetic preferences. The claim should be qualified to the specific setting where preferences are generated without human involvement.

- **DITTO is not significantly better than few-shot GPT-4 on the CMCC subset.** The paper reports (line 143) that significance holds "excluding few-shot GPT on CMCC." Given that few-shot GPT-4 is a realistic and important baseline — a user can put examples in context without any training — this gap weakens the uniformity of the static benchmark results. The user study does confirm DITTO's advantage, but the CMCC result remains a limitation worth noting.

- **The extrapolation claim (Lemma 3.1) is not empirically validated.** Lemma 3.1 provides conditions under which DITTO can outperform the demonstrations themselves. However, the paper does not test this — e.g., by having humans compare DITTO outputs directly against the provided demonstrations. The theoretical claim is interesting, but without empirical support its force is limited.

- **Normalized win rates in Section 5.2 are not clearly defined.** The paper reports "per-user normalized win rates" (line 176) but does not specify the normalization scheme or reference point. This makes the exact numbers in Figure 2 difficult to interpret without additional clarification.

### Trivial

- **Static benchmarks align to "authors" (public figures) rather than "users."** The paper frames the problem as aligning to an individual user, but the static benchmarks use known public authors from CMCC/CCAT. The paper is aware of this (it's an approximation) and the user study is the proper test. Not a real problem, but the framing could be tightened.

- **User study demonstrations start from GPT-4 outputs.** In the user study, participants were shown GPT-4 outputs to edit as demonstrations (line 151). This means the training demonstrations contain residual GPT-4 style, which could narrow the apparent gap between DITTO and GPT-4 baselines. However, this confound works *against* DITTO — making it harder to distinguish from GPT-4 — so DITTO's strong showing despite this is actually further evidence of its effectiveness. Worth noting as a design detail, not a weakness.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A human evaluation on at least one static benchmark task (CMCC or CCAT) would bridge the gap between the automated evaluation (known to have self-enhancement bias) and the user study (which uses a different domain). Even 100–200 human judgments on 2–3 authors would directly validate the GPT-4 evaluation proxy on the same data.

- An experiment comparing DITTO outputs directly against the demonstration set via human judgments would validate the Lemma 3.1 extrapolation claim.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, the most interesting observation from the reviews is that the confound in the user study (demonstrations derived from editing GPT-4 outputs) actually makes the experiment *conservative* with respect to DITTO's advantage. Starting from outputs that already resemble the baseline makes it harder to demonstrate differentiation, yet DITTO still achieves a 68.8% win rate. This lends indirect support to the claim that DITTO genuinely captures user-specific signals rather than merely amplifying its starting point. Conversely, the synthetic-preference experiment in Section 5.3 is *lenient* toward the baseline (DPO), yet DITTO still wins. Neither of these observations invalidates the results — they suggest the true effect size may be even larger than reported, but also underscore the importance of conducting the direct comparison (human preferences vs. demonstrations) that the paper does not include.
