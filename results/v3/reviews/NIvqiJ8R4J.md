## Summary

PELICAN proposes a two-stage LLM-based adaptive tutoring framework combining collaborative cognitive diagnosis with fast/slow-thinking strategy selection. The idea of diagnosing a student's knowledge state hierarchically and then using tree search over teaching strategies is conceptually interesting.

## Strengths

1. **Human evaluation with real students**: The paper includes an experiment with 169 high school students (1,335 tutoring reports) showing that PELICAN achieves higher R_coverage (70.04 vs. next-best 63.91), F_frequency (70.07 vs. 63.88), and subjective ratings (Appropriateness 4.23, Sentiment 4.42, Inspiration 4.33, Overall 4.39) compared to baselines. This provides real evidence that the framework can be deployed in classroom settings.

2. **Ablation confirms both stages contribute**: Table 3 shows that removing either the cognitive diagnosis module or the slow-thinking module degrades R_coverage and F_frequency. This supports the claim that the two-stage design and the slow-thinking algorithm are functional components.

3. **Clear framework presentation**: The two-stage architecture (collaborative cognitive diagnosis → adaptive tutoring with fast/slow thinking) is well-described, with clear figures illustrating the pipeline. The successor-first diagnostic strategy and the simulated teaching tree for strategy selection are sensible design choices.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Abstract makes untraceable numerical claims**: The abstract states "+18.7% critical thinking stimulation" and "+22.4% task completion rates compared to baseline models." These numbers cannot be found in any table or figure in the paper. The closest metric to "critical thinking" (Inspiration in Table 2) shows PELICAN at 4.21 vs. best baseline Socratic at 3.99 (≈5.5% relative), and the human experiment success rate (Table 6) shows 86.8% vs. 86.5% (0.3 pp). These numbers do not match 18.7% or 22.4%, and no calculation in the paper produces them. This is a serious misrepresentation.

2. **Primary experiments rely on an unvalidated simulated student**: The main evaluation (Tables 1–5) uses an LLM-simulated student (Appendix G, details stripped) whose responses are generated by GPT-4o as ground truth for both diagnostic accuracy and tutoring outcomes. The paper provides no validation that this simulated student behaves realistically. Without such validation, the quantitative results — though internally consistent — cannot be interpreted as evidence of real-world effectiveness. The system could simply be exploiting patterns in the simulator.

3. **GPT-based evaluation with potential self-preference**: The metrics Suitability, Logic, Inspiration, Reliability, and Overall are "GPT-based assessments" (Section 4.1). The paper states "all other base models in both stages are GPT-4o" (Section 4.1). Using the same model family (GPT-4o) as both the tutoring generator and the evaluator introduces a well-documented risk of inflated scores. The paper does not specify the evaluator model, discuss this risk, or provide any calibration against human judgments for these metrics.

4. **Weak baselines for the core contribution**: The tutoring baselines (Free-Prompt, Stepwise, Socratic, Bridge-Based, Cot-Bridge) are all simple prompt-based or template-based methods. For cognitive diagnosis (Table 1), baselines are only other prompting strategies — no comparison with established methods like NeuralCDM, Item Response Theory (IRT), or other cognitive diagnosis models that the paper itself discusses in Related Work (Section 2.2). This makes it impossible to assess whether the proposed method advances the state of the art or simply beats trivial baselines.

5. **Missing standard deviations for baselines and implausibly small stds**: Table 2 reports standard deviations only for PELICAN, not for any baselines. The reported stds for PELICAN are implausibly small (Suitability = 0.003, Inspiration = 0.002, Overall = 0.003), suggesting either multiple simulations of the same condition or a reporting error. Without stds for baselines, no statistical comparison is possible.

6. **Unexplained discrepancy between Table 2 and Table 3**: PELICAN's R_coverage drops from 72.36 in Table 2 (main results) to 54.84 in Table 3 (ablation). The caption says "Module ablation on stage-1 and slow-thinking" but does not explain why the values differ so dramatically between the two tables. This suggests the ablation was run under different experimental conditions, undermining comparability and the validity of the ablation conclusions.

### Minor

1. **Strategy adaptation is mostly static across cognitive levels**: Figure 4 shows that 7 out of 9 strategies (Suggestion, Confirmation, Correction, Open Question, Closed Question, Simplification, Decomposition) have identical percentages across all three cognitive levels (2%, 5%, 5%, 5%, 5%, 10%, 12% respectively). Only Explanation (30–33%) and Analogies (15–22%) vary. This weakens the claim that the system strongly adapts strategy selection to cognitive level.

2. **Human experiment success rate advantage is marginal**: PELICAN's 86.8% success rate is only 0.3 percentage points above Stepwise (86.5%) and 1.6 pp above Free-Prompt (85.2%). No statistical significance tests or confidence intervals are reported in the main text for these pairwise comparisons. While the R_coverage and subjective ratings show clearer advantages, the primary metric (success rate) shows at best marginal improvement.

3. **No hyperparameter sensitivity analysis**: The slow-thinking parameters (M=1, k=2, m=2, λ=0.4) are presented without justification or sensitivity analysis. The paper does not explore how varying these parameters affects the results.

4. **Binary knowledge state assumption**: The cognitive diagnosis uses binary "mastered/not mastered" values for each knowledge node, which may not capture partial knowledge, misconceptions, or varying degrees of proficiency.

### Trivial

- The assistant model is GPT-3.5 while all other models are GPT-4o — the paper does not discuss why a weaker model was chosen for the assistant role or whether this choice affects diagnostic accuracy.

## Removed Points

- **"Simulated student is also used for strategy selection (closed loop)"** — The slow-thinking algorithm simulates dialogue with a student model Φ_Sim_S, which is part of the method's design (a planning mechanism), not an evaluation loop. The concern conflates planning (simulating future steps to choose a strategy) with evaluation (measuring system performance). These are different uses of simulation, and the paper does not claim the planning simulation is a measure of real-world effectiveness. Removed as a misunderstanding.

- **"Evaluation uses GPT as judge for main tutoring metrics"** (weakened from Fatal to Major) — The paper mentions "GPT-based assessments" but does not explicitly state which model is the evaluator. - **"Table 5: simulated student's success rates lower than human rates"** — Simulated and real students could legitimately have different success rates; this is not evidence of simulator invalidity. Removed.
