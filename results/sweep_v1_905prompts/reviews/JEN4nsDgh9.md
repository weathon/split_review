## Summary

This paper introduces a benchmark for Taxonomy Image Generation — evaluating how well text-to-image models can generate images for WordNet synsets (taxonomy concepts). The benchmark comprises 9 metrics (including taxonomy-specific CLIP-based similarities grounded in KL divergence, ELO scores from human/GPT-4 pairwise comparisons, reward model scores, and FID/IS), three dataset splits (Easy Concepts, random WordNet, and LLM-predicted concepts), and 12 open-source models. Key findings: Playground and FLUX dominate preference-based evaluations, while SDXL-turbo leads taxonomy-similarity metrics. The paper also releases the generated image dataset.

## Strengths

- **First comprehensive benchmark for taxonomy-specific image generation.** The paper evaluates 12 models on three dataset splits using 9 metrics, establishing a new evaluation paradigm for a previously unaddressed task. The taxonomy-specific similarity metrics (Lemma, Hypernym, Cohyponym) are grounded in KL divergence and mutual information (Appendix D), and the Specificity ratio (hypernym/cohyponym) generalizes the In-Subtree Probability from Baryshnikov & Ryabinin (2023) without requiring an ImageNet-specific classifier.

- **Multi-faceted evaluation with human and automatic judges.** The benchmark includes human ELO evaluation (4 expert annotators, Spearman inter-annotator ρ=0.8), GPT-4 pairwise ELO evaluation (correlated with human rankings at ρ=0.92 with definitions), a reward model, and CLIP-based metrics. The paper documents GPT-4's first-position bias and reports Spearman correlations between human and GPT-4 rankings (0.88 overall, 0.92 with definitions), providing transparency about the automatic evaluator's behavior.

- **Partial validation of CLIP-based taxonomy metrics.** The paper reports Spearman correlations between model ranks from Hypernym CLIP-Score and human evaluation ranks (ρ≈0.911, p≤0.00004) and Cohyponym CLIP-Score (ρ≈0.871, p≤0.00022), suggesting these automatic metrics capture taxonomic relationships that humans recognize at the model-ranking level.

- **Practical resource for the community.** The paper releases the dataset of images generated by the best-performing model covering WordNet-3.0, extending ImageNet. The LLM Predictions dataset (1,685 items from TaxoLLaMA-3.1) tests robustness to AI-generated taxonomy concepts, demonstrating that top models maintain their lead across both ground-truth and predicted concepts.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Central claim of divergent rankings is stated without evidence.** The abstract and introduction assert that "the ranking of models differs significantly from standard T2I tasks" and that this "highlights the task importance," but no quantitative comparison is provided. The paper mentions GenAI Arena (Jiang et al., 2024a) as the standard benchmark but does not compute Spearman correlation or any other measure between the taxonomy benchmark's ELO ranking and GenAI Arena's ranking for the same models. Given that the top-performing models here (Playground, FLUX) are also strong in general T2I benchmarks, the claim may be overstated, and the reader cannot assess whether the taxonomy-specific evaluation produces genuinely new insights. **This claim should either be substantiated with a direct comparison or tempered.**

2. **CLIP-based taxonomy metrics validated only at the model-rank level.** The Spearman correlations reported (ρ≈0.911 for hypernym, ρ≈0.871 for cohyponym) are computed over 12 model ranks, not per-image judgments. Whether CLIP similarity to a hypernym string (e.g., "artifact") corresponds to a human's judgment that an image is a member of that class in the context of taxonomy is not demonstrated at the item level. The Specificity ratio (hypernym/cohyponym) is a plausible corrective but is not ablated against a simple lemma-only CLIP score to show it meaningfully improves sibling discrimination. Without per-item validation (e.g., human ratings of whether an image is a "good" depiction of the synset vs. a sibling), it is unclear what failure modes these metrics miss — for instance, images that score highly on hypernym CLIP by being generic but fail to capture the concept's distinctive features.

### Minor

3. **FID uses a non-standard reference distribution.** The paper computes FID against retrieved images (from Wikimedia Commons), not a canonical high-quality reference dataset. The paper is transparent about this ("FID reflects the 'realness' or closeness to retrieval rather than the semantic correctness"), but the retrieval process is underspecified — no details on query construction, pre-processing, or whether the same set was used across models. The reported FID values measure how well each model replicates a particular retrieval database rather than image quality in the standard sense, limiting interpretability and external comparability.

4. **No confidence intervals or uncertainty for similarity metrics.** Table 2 reports top-1 winners for Lemma, Hypernym, and Cohyponym Similarity across subsets with no confidence intervals or bootstrapping. SDXL-turbo wins every cell, but the margins could be trivial. The ELO scores and reward model use bootstrapped confidence intervals, but the similarity metrics — which are the paper's taxonomy-specific contribution — lack uncertainty quantification, making it hard to assess whether top-1 differences are meaningful.

5. **GPT-4 first-position bias is documented but not mitigated.** The paper identifies a strong first-option bias in GPT-4 preferences (Figure 5) and notes no correlation between GPT-4 and human raw scores for individual battles. However, presentation order is not randomized, and the bias is not accounted for in ELO computation. This weakens the reliability of GPT-4 as a pairwise judge.

6. **Retrieval baseline is underspecified.** The Wikimedia Commons retrieval baseline has no details on query construction, number of retrieved images, or selection criteria. This affects the fairness of comparisons: the poor retrieval performance could stem from suboptimal retrieval rather than fundamental limitations of retrieval-based approaches.

7. **Only one prompt template.** All generations use the template "An image of <CONCEPT> (<DEFINITION>)." Different models may be differentially sensitive to prompt phrasing, particularly since definitions are an atypical input for many T2I models. A small prompt-tuning experiment would help ensure results are not artifacts of the chosen template.

### Trivial

- The Spearman correlation in Figure 4 caption is reported as 0.92, while the main text (Section 5, ELO Scores paragraph) reports 0.88 for the same setting (with definitions). This small inconsistency should be reconciled.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A per-item human validation study for a subset of the taxonomy metrics (e.g., annotators rate whether a generated image is a good depiction of the synset) would substantially strengthen the claim that these metrics measure taxonomic understanding rather than generic text-image alignment.
- Statistical comparisons between the taxonomy benchmark's model rankings and standard T2I leaderboards (e.g., Spearman correlation with GenAI Arena) would substantiate or appropriately temper the claim of divergent rankings.
- An analysis of failure modes (partially present in Appendix I) would give practitioners concrete insight into where and why models fail on taxonomy concepts.

## Removed Points

- Criticism about Figure 1 "examples differ in subject, not just specificity": The figure's purpose is to illustrate differences in prompt style between DiffusionDB and WordNet, not to control for subject. This is a misunderstanding of the figure's intent. **Removed.**
- Criticism about sampling probability description being confusing: The paper clearly distinguishes training-sampling probabilities (0.1/0.1/0.8) from test-set occurrence probabilities (1e-5/0.05/0.1). The description is adequate. **Removed.**
- "Citation of Baryshnikov & Ryabinin and Liao et al. suggests incremental novelty": The paper explicitly differentiates its contribution — its metrics "do not depend on a specific ImageNet classifier and can be applied to any type of taxonomy node." This is a substantive differentiation. **Removed.**
- Strength finder claim that "model rankings diverge from standard T2I benchmarks": This conflicts with the verified weakness that this claim is unsubstantiated. **Removed.**
- Various nitpicks about formatting, missing appendix content, and reproducibility of trivial implementation details. **Removed per guidelines.**

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions.

## Suggestions

1. **Substantiate or drop the divergent-rankings claim**: Compute the Spearman correlation between the taxonomy benchmark's ELO rankings and GenAI Arena rankings for the overlapping model set. If the correlation is high, the claim should be tempered; if low, report the comparison as evidence.
2. **Add per-item validation of CLIP-based taxonomy metrics**: Conduct a small human study (50-100 concepts) where annotators rate whether generated images depict the precise synset vs. a sibling, and compute how well the taxonomy metrics (including Specificity) predict these ratings.
3. **Provide bootstrapped confidence intervals for similarity metrics** to let readers assess whether top-1 differences are meaningful.
4. **Randomize presentation order in GPT-4 pairwise battles** to eliminate first-position bias, or at minimum report results with balanced ordering.
5. **Clarify the FID reference**: Describe how retrieval images were obtained, how many per concept, and whether the same set was used for all models. Consider adding FID against a standard dataset for concepts that overlap.
