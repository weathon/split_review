## Summary

IncentRL proposes augmenting RL rewards with a KL divergence term between a predicted outcome distribution \(p(o|s,a)\) and a preferred outcome distribution \(q(o|s)\), controlled by a weight \(\beta\). The paper claims Bayesian online adaptation of \(\beta\) as its central novelty. Experiments on a toy MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid DoorKey show that fixed \(\beta > 0\) can improve over \(\beta = 0\) (standard RL) in sparse-reward settings.

## Strengths

- **KL-based preference-prediction shaping is a coherent idea.** The framework of aligning predicted outcomes with preferred outcomes via a KL penalty (Equation 1) is conceptually clean and well-motivated, connecting to reward shaping and cognitive science literatures. The paper positions this clearly against KL policy regularizers (TRPO, PPO) and active inference (Section 2).

- **The toy MDP experiment is fully specified and illustrative.** In the two-state MDP (Section 5.2), both \(p(o|s,a)\) and \(q(o|s)\) are concretely defined, and the results show that any \(\beta > 0\) substantially accelerates Q-learning and reduces variance (Table 1, ~70% improvement). This provides a proof-of-concept that the shaping mechanism can work as intended.

- **Fixed-\(\beta\) results show promising signal in two benchmark domains.** On MountainCar, \(\beta = 0.1\) yields an 18% improvement in goal reaches over \(\beta = 0\) (Table 2). On MiniGrid DoorKey, \(\beta = 0.01\) improves success rate from 90.5% to 98% and halves mean episode length (Figure 2). These results, while limited in baselines, suggest the KL shaping term can provide useful guidance in sparse-reward settings.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **The Bayesian adaptation of \(\beta\) — the paper's central claimed novelty — is never described.** The title, abstract, and introduction prominently advertise Bayesian online adaptation of the incentive weight as the key contribution. Yet nowhere in the paper is there any specification of the adaptation algorithm: no prior distribution, no likelihood, no update rule, no pseudocode. Section 3 (Methods) only defines the KL-shaped reward (Equation 1); it contains no Bayesian machinery whatsoever. Section 5.4 shows posterior mean evolution (Figure 3) and \(\beta\) sample distributions (Figure 4, Table 3), but the reader cannot determine how these were computed. The claim that "Bayesian adaptation eliminates manual trade-off tuning" is unverifiable — and effectively absent — without an algorithm. The paper further states that "In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of \(\beta\) achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value," which is hearsay, not evidence. A paper whose main contribution is an adaptation mechanism must at minimum state what that mechanism is.

### Major

- **The method is severely underspecified for the main experimental domains (MountainCar, MiniGrid).** The paper never explains how \(p(o|s,a)\) and \(q(o|s)\) are instantiated, represented, or optimized for these environments. What is the outcome space? Is \(p(o|s,a)\) a learned forward model, a hand-crafted distribution, or something else? How is the KL divergence computed — via discretization, a Gaussian assumption in a latent space, or otherwise? Section 5.1 lists "Predict Outcomes" and "Specify Preferences" as algorithmic steps but provides no concrete implementation. Without these details, the results are uninterpretable: an improved curve could arise from an arbitrary dense shaping signal that is not reproducible. The paper is transparent about the toy MDP (where \(p\) and \(q\) are hand-specified categorical distributions) but opaque where it matters most.

- **No comparison to existing intrinsic motivation or reward-shaping baselines.** The paper positions itself as addressing "a core limitation of intrinsic motivation methods" — the need for manual trade-off tuning — yet compares IncentRL only against \(\beta = 0\) (standard RL with no shaping). There are no comparisons to curiosity-driven exploration (ICM, RND), empowerment, VIME, potential-based reward shaping, or any other method designed for sparse-reward exploration. On MiniGrid, only two \(\beta\) values are tested (\(0\) and \(0.01\)). Without baselines, it is impossible to determine whether the observed gains are non-trivial or could be achieved by any reasonable intrinsic reward. This undermines the paper's empirical claims.

### Minor

- **The theoretical analysis is thin and does not constitute a meaningful contribution.** Proposition 1 (small \(\beta\) preserves optimality) is argued via a sketch that invokes a "positive reward gap" and continuity without a formal proof or explicit conditions. Proposition 2 (\(\beta \to \infty\) ignores external reward) is an immediate consequence of the objective and offers no insight. No convergence guarantees, regret bounds, or bias analysis are provided. The connections to dopamine and the Free Energy Principle remain at the level of metaphor. The theory section does not strengthen the paper beyond what intuition already provides.

- **The evidence for the fixed-\(\beta\) results is incomplete.** MountainCar reports only mean goal reaches per 5000 episodes (Table 2) with no learning curves over time, making it difficult to assess sample efficiency — the paper's stated goal. MiniGrid uses only \(\beta = 0.01\) with no sweep, so we cannot see how sensitive performance is to \(\beta\) in that domain. The MountainCar result at \(\beta = 0.3\) and \(1.0\) shows severe degradation (\(-\)53% and \(-\)75%), which the paper acknowledges but does not analyze.

## Suggestions

- **Define the Bayesian adaptation algorithm explicitly.** This is the single most critical missing piece. Specify the prior over \(\beta\), the likelihood (how observed data updates beliefs about \(\beta\)), and the inference procedure. Without this, the paper cannot claim Bayesian adaptation as a contribution.
- **Instantiate \(p\) and \(q\) concretely for each benchmark** with sufficient detail that a reader could reimplement them. Even a simple specification (e.g., "\(p(o|s,a)\) is the empirical next-state distribution from a learned ensemble of transition models; \(q(o|s)\) is a Dirac delta at the goal state") would be a major improvement.
- **Add at least one intrinsic motivation baseline** (e.g., RND or ICM) on the same environments with matched network capacity and training budget. This is essential for the paper's positioning.
- **Show learning curves for all experiments**, not just final aggregate statistics, so that sample efficiency can be evaluated.
