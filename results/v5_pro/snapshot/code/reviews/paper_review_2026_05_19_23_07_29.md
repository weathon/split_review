## Summary

FaceLinkGen identifies a critical blind spot in frequency-domain privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR) evaluation: the prevailing paradigm treats privacy as resistance to pixel-level reconstruction (PSNR, SSIM), but preventing pixel recovery does not prevent identity leakage. The paper demonstrates a simple distillation-based attack that extracts identity embeddings from protected templates and uses them for linkage and face regeneration, achieving over 98.5% matching accuracy and 96% regeneration success across three PPFR methods (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace). The attack is verified via commercial APIs (Face++, Amazon), costs under $1.60, and succeeds even under highly constrained assumptions (30 samples, no knowledge of the conversion process). The paper also extends the analysis to de-identification systems and soft biometric leakage.

## Strengths

1. **Clear identification and empirical demonstration of a fundamental evaluation gap.** The paper shows concretely (Table 1, Figure 2) that pixel-level metrics and identity-level metrics can diverge dramatically — a CanFG-protected face has high SSIM/PSNR with its original but near-zero identity similarity, while two different photos of the same person have low pixel similarity but high identity similarity. This directly challenges the reconstruction-centric evaluation paradigm used across recent PPFR work (DuetFace, PartialFace, MinusFace, FaceObfuscator, FracFace).

2. **Simple, low-cost attack achieving near-perfect identity extraction.** The student model trains in under two hours on a single A6000 GPU (~$0.80–1.60) and achieves >98.5% matching accuracy and >96% regeneration success on all three methods (Table 2). The simplicity is intentional and demonstrates that the vulnerability is in the representation, not in any complex optimization.

3. **Independent verification via commercial APIs.** Regeneration success is evaluated using Face++ (financial-grade security standard) and cross-validated with Amazon's face comparison API, avoiding circularity with the ArcFace embedding used for distillation. This significantly strengthens the practical relevance of the results.

4. **Attack succeeds under highly constrained assumptions.** With only 30 paired samples and a generic Gaussian-blur high-pass filter proxy (no knowledge of the conversion process), a single student model attacks all three methods with >92% matching and >94% regeneration success (Table 7). The high-pass proxy intuition is clever and demonstrates the shared structural vulnerability of frequency-domain PPFR.

5. **Threat model discussion returns to first principles.** Section 2 carefully argues why the service provider with oracle access is the correct adversary for PPFR, contrasting with external-attacker framings in recent work. This methodological grounding clarifies the security claims and directly informs the attack design.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

### Minor

1. **Student model architecture is underspecified for reproducibility.** The paper describes the student as "Antelopev2 with one additional 3x3 Conv2D layer prepended" but does not state which components are frozen vs. trained, learning rate, optimizer, batch size for the main setup, or data augmentations used during training. Training details are partially given for the minimal-resource experiment (weight decay, batch size) but not for the primary experiments. This is essential given the paper's contribution partly rests on the attack's simplicity.

2. **Constrained scenario (Section 6) lacks detail on how the 30 paired samples are obtained.** The paper states these pairs "can be obtained by a small number of leaked samples, known identities, or attacker-controlled accounts" but provides no experimental validation or discussion of potential biases in the selection of those 30 images. Given that this is a "bonus" result (the unconstrained threat model already covers the primary adversary), this is a gap in the evidence for this specific claim.

3. **No failure case analysis.** The attack achieves >90% regeneration success, which means some attempts fail. There is no analysis of what failures look like — whether they correlate with certain demographics, poses, or image qualities. A brief qualitative or quantitative analysis would increase trust and potentially reveal useful insights about the limits of the approach.

4. **Cross-method linkage results (Table 4) are underexplained.** Why is FracFace-to-Original linkage (0.8444) higher than FracFace-to-FracFace (0.7863)? Why does MinusFace have consistently lower linkage than the other two methods? Some of these patterns are notable but receive no commentary.

## Novel Insights
The two reviews largely converge on the paper's quality and significance, with neither identifying fatal flaws. The most interesting observation is the harsh critic's point about the constrained scenario: the high-pass filter proxy works remarkably well, but the paper does not fully explore *why* three methods with different algorithmic designs all share the property of preserving identity information in a way that a simple high-pass filter captures. This suggests a deeper structural property of frequency-domain PPFR — that identity information concentrates in high-frequency components — which the paper identifies intuitively but does not analyze formally. A theoretical characterization of this phenomenon (e.g., information-theoretic) would be a natural follow-up. No genuinely novel insight beyond the paper's own contributions emerges from synthesizing the two reviews.

## Suggestions
- Provide full training hyperparameters (optimizer, learning rate, batch size, number of steps, data augmentations) for the student model in the main experiments, not just the minimal-resource variant.
- Add a brief failure case analysis: show examples of the ~5-10% of images that fail regeneration and analyze whether failures correlate with specific demographics or image characteristics.
- In the constrained scenario, either provide a small experiment validating the 30-pair collection mechanism, or add a caveat about the assumed access model.
- Comment on the notable patterns in Table 4 (why cross-method linkage varies, why MinusFace underperforms) to help readers interpret the results.
- Consider adding a small-scale non-ArcFace teacher experiment (e.g., FaceNet) or a stronger argument about model-agnosticism.
