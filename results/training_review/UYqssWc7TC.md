## Summary

This paper proposes a unified framework for internal strategies to evaluate node embedding quality without labels, structured around two steps: building prior beliefs and quantifying them. It instantiates this framework from a spatial perspective (CSOR, which uses consensus-based pairwise Manhattan distances between embedding matrices) and a spectral perspective (SSOR, which computes a radar-chart area from normalized singular values). Experiments with 7 GNN models on 4 benchmark datasets show that both methods yield Spearman correlations often above 0.9 with downstream task performance and are competitive with existing internal strategies like RankMe and StableRank.

## Strengths

- **A clean, unifying conceptual framework**: The paper formalizes internal strategies for unsupervised HPO into two explicit steps — building prior beliefs and quantifying them. This structure is demonstrated by revisiting UDR (Section 3) and then used to derive CSOR (Section 4) and SSOR (Section 5). The framework provides a reusable lens for designing and comparing future methods.

- **Strong empirical correlation with downstream performance is well-documented**: Across 7 GNN models, 4 datasets, and 2 downstream tasks, CSOR and SSOR achieve average Spearman coefficients of 0.906 and 0.969 for link prediction (Table 7) and comparably high values for node classification (Table 8). SSOR achieves the best average rank (1.75 for node classification, 1.39 for link prediction) across all compared internal strategies (Section 6.2). The scale of the evaluation (7 models × 4 datasets × 1280 configurations each = 35,840 trained models) represents substantial empirical effort.

- **Observation-driven derivation of prior beliefs**: Rather than relying solely on opaque theoretical mechanism analysis, the paper uses PCA visualizations and empirical distance comparisons (Figure 1, Appendix figures) to motivate the hypothesis that better-performing embeddings are spatially or spectrally more distinct. This pragmatic approach makes the framework applicable to models whose inner workings are not fully understood.

- **Complementary spatial/spectral design**: The two proposed methods address the same problem from different angles — CSOR requires pairwise comparisons across configurations (useful when many candidates are available), while SSOR is a stand-alone method (useful when only a single embedding matrix is available). This design choice is explicitly motivated and extends the applicability of the framework.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **No variance or uncertainty information for headline quantitative results** (Section 6.2). The central numerical claims — average Spearman correlations of 0.906 (CSOR) and 0.969 (SSOR), best-selection counts ("SSOR excels 18 times"), and average ranks — are all reported as point estimates with no standard deviations, confidence intervals, or per-setting breakdowns. While single-run evaluation is common in this benchmark-heavy subfield, the paper's strongest claim is essentially quantitative (these methods rank configurations with near-perfect monotonicity), and the reader cannot tell whether the reported averages are tight across settings or driven by a few favorable cases. The paper states "the correlation coefficients are often around 0.9 and never fall below 0.6" (Abstract), which provides some range information, but this is insufficient for a method that claims average Spearman of 0.969. Addressing this does not require multiple random trials (which would be extremely expensive at 35,840 trained models) — a per-dataset/model breakdown table or a box plot of the 28 Spearman values per method would substantively address the concern.

2. **The hyperparameter configuration generation is underspecified** (Section 6.1). The paper states that HP configurations are generated by "varying the number of layers, hidden dimensions per layer, and the number of maximal training epochs" but does not specify: (a) whether the sampling is grid-based or random, (b) the numeric ranges or discrete values used for each hyperparameter, or (c) how many distinct values of each hyperparameter are explored. Without this information, the difficulty of the ranking task cannot be assessed. If the space includes many configurations that are trivially bad (e.g., 1 layer with tiny hidden dimension on a large graph), high Spearman correlations could be driven by easy separation of extremes rather than meaningful ranking among competitive configurations. The authors should state the exact HP search space and, ideally, report correlations restricted to the top-k configurations by true performance to demonstrate ranking fidelity where HPO actually matters.

### Minor

1. **The SSOR formula's interpretation as "area" is mathematically non-standard.** The paper distributes normalized singular values evenly across 360° in a radar chart and computes area = 0.5·|∑ σ̃_i·σ̃_{i+1}·sin(θ_{i+1}−θ_i)|. Since angles are evenly spaced, sin(θ_{i+1}−θ_i) is constant, and the metric reduces to a constant times ∑ σ̃_i·σ̃_{i+1} — the sum of products of consecutive normalized singular values, where the consecutive ordering follows an arbitrary angular sequence (the index order of sorted singular values). This is a valid metric, but calling it "area" is somewhat misleading since the polygon's vertex ordering (by sorted singular value index) does not correspond to a natural geometric neighborhood. The paper should either justify why this specific combination of products captures "spectral space occupancy" better than simpler alternatives (e.g., sum of squares, effective rank, or entropy of singular values) or clarify that SSOR is measuring something related to but distinct from geometric area.

2. **No justification for the choice of Manhattan distance in CSOR** (Section 4). The paper uses Manhattan distance to compute pairwise differences between embedding matrices but does not discuss why this is preferred over cosine similarity, Euclidean distance, or other metrics. Since CSOR aggregates distances across all dimensions of the embedding matrix, the choice of distance function can substantially affect results. A brief ablation or even a citation justifying the choice would strengthen the methodological presentation.

3. **Limited downstream tasks for validation.** The paper evaluates on only node classification and link prediction. The claim of measuring "general node embedding quality" would be strengthened by at least one additional task (e.g., node clustering or retrieval). The authors acknowledge this limitation in their discussion (Section 7). Not fatal, but it narrows the scope of the empirical claims.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation to emerge from the reviews — beyond the paper's own contributions — is the mathematical structure of SSOR. The reviewer correctly notes that with evenly spaced radar-chart angles, the "area" formula reduces to a constant times the sum of products of consecutive normalized singular values. This means SSOR is essentially measuring a form of smoothness-weighted magnitude of the singular spectrum. This perspective is not discussed in the paper and could be worth developing: SSOR can be understood as preferring embeddings whose singular values are both large (high magnitude) and smoothly varying in sorted order (the product terms penalize sharp drops between consecutive singular values, which would indicate dimensional collapse). This connects SSOR to known measures of effective rank and spectral decay, and a formal comparison (e.g., SSOR vs. log determinant of Z^T Z, or vs. the sum of squared singular values) would clarify when the product formulation is genuinely advantageous.
