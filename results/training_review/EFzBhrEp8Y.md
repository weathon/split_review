## Summary

This paper introduces MME-Finance, the first multimodal benchmark specifically designed for the financial domain. It contains 1,171 English and 1,103 Chinese open-ended VQA pairs sourced from realistic financial images (screenshots, mobile photos, candlestick charts, etc.) organized into a three-level ability hierarchy (perception, reasoning, cognition) plus a hallucination task. Questions are generated by GPT-4o and then manually reviewed and corrected by financial experts with 10+ years of experience. The paper evaluates 19 MLLMs and proposes an LLM-as-judge evaluation method that incorporates the image as additional input to the evaluator.

## Strengths

- **First multimodal finance benchmark with expert-validated content.** The paper fills a clear gap: no multimodal benchmark existed for finance. The annotation pipeline involves expert review at every stage, and complex subjective questions are adjudicated by a panel of three senior finance researchers (Section 3.3). The 1,171 English QA pairs covering 11 tasks across diverse real-world image types (mobile photos, screenshots, candlestick charts, etc.) constitute a genuinely useful resource for the community.

- **Extensive evaluation across 19 models revealing concrete failure modes.** The paper tests both open-source (LLaVA, Qwen2VL, InternVL2, etc.) and proprietary models (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini). The top overall score is only 65.69% (Qwen2VL-72B), and the analysis pinpoints specific weaknesses: Spatial Awareness (best 30.31%), Estimated Numerical Calculation (best 40.95%), poor performance on candlestick/technical indicator charts, and consistently worse results on mobile photographs. These findings are actionable for future model development.

- **Hallucination evaluation with a "Not Applicable" task.** Including 22 samples designed to test whether models can identify unanswerable questions is a thoughtful addition, and the variance across models (36.36%–100%) highlights a practically important capability gap.

- **Practical image collection reflecting real-world usage scenarios.** The inclusion of mobile photographs (40.5% of samples) and oblique-angle captures alongside computer screenshots is well-motivated by how users actually interact with financial content, and the finding that models perform worst on this style is directly useful.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Chinese portion of the benchmark is advertised but completely unevaluated.** The abstract and introduction repeatedly emphasize that MME-Finance is bilingual (1,171 English + 1,103 Chinese questions) and that the Chinese version "helps compare performance of MLLMs under a Chinese context" (abstract, line 5). However, every experiment, table, and analysis in Section 4 uses only the English subset. No Chinese results are reported whatsoever. This makes the "bilingual" claim hollow in the current submission. While the English benchmark is independently valuable, the paper overstates its contribution scope relative to what is actually demonstrated.

- **The LLM-as-judge evaluation strategy—claimed as a contribution—is validated on a very small sample with marginal gains and no statistical rigor.** The evaluator analysis (Section 4.3) uses only 100 samples from a single model (MiniCPM2.6). The gain from adding image input is 0.738 vs. 0.720 Spearman correlation—a 0.018 improvement with no significance test reported. No inter-annotator agreement metric (Fleiss' kappa, percentage agreement) is reported for the three human raters whose scores serve as ground truth. On objective questions, adding images *decreases* correlation (0.835 with image vs. 0.849 without; Table 5), yet the paper's headline claim is that visual information helps. The recommendation of Qwen2VL-72B as a "cost-effective alternative" evaluator is based on a single correlation comparison on the same 100 samples. For a claimed "novel evaluation strategy," the empirical support is thin.

- **Several per-task sample sizes are too small to support fine-grained comparative claims without statistical tests.** Reason Explanation (18 samples), Risk Warning (22), Not Applicable (22), Estimated Numerical Calculation (42), and Investment Advice (53) all have sample sizes where a few score changes could flip rankings. The paper makes claims such as "GPT-4o surpasses Qwen2VL-72B in all cognition-related tasks" (Section 4.2) without confidence intervals or significance tests. For example, on Reason Explanation (18 samples), GPT-4o scores 80.00 vs. Qwen2VL-72B at 77.78—a 2.22% difference that could easily arise from a single sample. The overall trends are meaningful, but the granular task-level comparisons are not robustly supported.

### Minor

- **The large performance gap between GPT-4o-05-13 (42.85) and GPT-4o (63.18) is not discussed.** This nearly 20-point difference likely reflects a major model version change, and understanding what drove the improvement could provide useful signal, but the paper offers no comment.

- **The hallucination modified-prompt experiment is described without any quantitative results.** The paper states (Section 4.2, last paragraph) that allowing "Not Applicable" across all tasks "led to a rise in false negatives in most MLLMs" but provides no numbers, tables, or comparisons. This is an interesting finding that deserves actual data.

- **Open-source evaluators with near-zero correlation are not discussed.** CogVLM2 achieves Spearman 0.049 and MiniCPM2.6 achieves 0.048 as evaluators—essentially random. The paper notes this in passing but does not analyze why these models fail as judges or discuss whether this pattern undermines confidence in LLM-as-judge approaches for this domain.

- **The objective/subjective split reveals that adding images slightly hurts objective evaluation (0.835 vs. 0.849),** but the paper only highlights the subjective improvement. This nuance is important for practitioners deciding whether to use image input and should be acknowledged.

### Trivial

- The 100-sample selection process for evaluator validation is not described (random? stratified by task?). This detail matters for assessing generalizability.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **GPT-4o-based QA generation introduces potential bias.** While the paper mitigates this through manual expert review (and the pipeline is well-documented), a discussion of how many generated questions/answers were corrected or rejected would strengthen confidence in the benchmark's independence from GPT-4o's blind spots.
- **Ablation on evaluation prompt design** (task-specific prompts, few-shot examples) would strengthen the evaluation methodology claim, but this is not standard practice for benchmark papers.
- **Reporting human performance** on a subset of the benchmark would anchor the results and clarify how far models are from expert-level understanding.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting finding — one the paper identifies correctly — is the interaction between task type and evaluation method: adding image information to the evaluator helps for subjective questions but marginally hurts for objective ones. This suggests that LLM-as-judge protocols for domain benchmarks may need to be task-adaptive rather than uniformly applied. Another insight that deserves deeper treatment: the near-zero correlation of open-source evaluators (CogVLM2 Spearman 0.049) raises the question of whether smaller/free models are fundamentally unsuited as judges for finance-specific content, which has practical implications for groups that cannot afford GPT-4o API costs. These observations are present in the data but not fully exploited in the paper's discussion.

## Suggestions

1. **Report Chinese subset results** in a table comparable to Table 2 (overall and per-task) and analyze cross-lingual differences. This is critical to fulfilling the paper's stated contribution.
2. **Add confidence intervals or bootstrap estimates** for per-task scores, especially for tasks with <50 samples. Report the sample-size-adjusted reliability of the claimed rankings.
3. **Expand evaluator validation** to outputs from at least one more model (e.g., a weak and a strong model) beyond MiniCPM2.6, and report inter-annotator agreement (Fleiss' kappa) for the human judges.
4. **Provide the quantitative results** for the modified-prompt hallucination experiment (the false negative rates across models) rather than just a qualitative description.
5. **Acknowledge the objective/subjective asymmetry** in the evaluator analysis more transparently — the current text implies uniform improvement from image input, which the data do not support.

## Score and Decision

**Originality:** Moderate — first multimodal finance benchmark, though the evaluation methodology contribution is incremental.  
**Importance of research question:** High — there is genuine demand for finance-specific MLLM evaluation.  
**Claims well supported:** Partially — the English benchmark results are useful and reasonably presented, but the bilingual claim is unfulfilled, and fine-grained comparisons lack statistical support.  
**Soundness of experiments:** Adequate with notable gaps — the evaluator validation is thin, and several tasks have sample sizes that weaken granular conclusions.  
**Clarity of writing:** Good — the paper is well-structured and the pipeline descriptions are clear.  
**Value to the research community:** High — the benchmark will be a useful resource, and the concrete failure mode analysis (SA, ENC, mobile photos) provides clear guidance for future work.

The paper makes a genuine contribution with the first multimodal finance benchmark and a solid evaluation of 19 models. However, the three major weaknesses (unevaluated Chinese portion, thin evaluator validation, and small per-task sample sizes for granular claims) prevent this from being a strong paper. The benchmark itself is valuable and the core English results are informative, but the paper overclaims relative to what is demonstrated. With a revision addressing these gaps, this would be a solid accept. As submitted, it is a borderline-to-weak accept.
