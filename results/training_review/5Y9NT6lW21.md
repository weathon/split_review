## Summary

The paper proposes APPO (Adversarial Preference-based Policy Optimization), an offline PbRL algorithm that avoids computationally intractable confidence set constructions by framing the problem as a two-player Stackelberg game between a policy and a reward model. Key innovations include: (1) a trajectory-pair ℓ₁ loss that enforces conservatism without explicit confidence sets, (2) a reparameterization via action-value functions (using the performance difference lemma) that eliminates the need for on-policy rollouts, enabling a fully offline algorithm, and (3) a sample complexity bound (Theorem 4.1) under standard function approximation and trajectory concentrability assumptions. Experiments on Meta-world medium-replay tasks show competitive performance against state-of-the-art offline PbRL baselines.

## Strengths

1. **Novel and well-motivated adversarial formulation for offline PbRL**: The two-player Stackelberg game with trajectory-pair ℓ₁ penalty cleanly addresses a real bottleneck in prior theoretical work (intractable confidence sets in Zhan et al. 2024a; Pace et al. 2024). The paper correctly identifies why a simple Lagrangian dual of the MLE constraint would not suffice and proposes a principled alternative. This is a genuine theoretical contribution.

2. **Clever reparameterization via the performance difference lemma (Section 3.3)**: Mapping reward optimization to action-value function optimization using induced reward models (Definition 1) is technically elegant. It allows the algorithm to operate without on-policy rollouts or policy evaluation oracles — a non-trivial advance over APPO-rollout (Algorithm 1).

3. **Sample complexity bound avoiding transition concentrability**: Theorem 4.1 provides an end-to-end bound whose unlabeled sample complexity does not depend on $\mathcal{C}_P$ (transition concentrability), which can grow exponentially (as noted in Proposition 2 of Zhan et al. 2024a). This is a genuine advantage over FREEHAND-transition and Sim-OPRL, and the sub-optimality decomposition proof sketch is insightful.

4. **Robustness to preference dataset size and hyperparameter variation**: Figure 2 shows APPO maintains strong performance with as few as 100 preference labels, and Figure 1 demonstrates stable learning across a wide range of $\lambda$ values. The method introduces only one algorithmic hyperparameter ($\lambda$), compared to IQL-based methods requiring at least two (expectile, temperature).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

### Major

1. **Theory-practice gap in the transition model**: Theorem 4.1 guarantees are proved for **Algorithm 2**, which uses a learned transition model $\hat{P}$ (line 120: $\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{traj}}}(f;\hat{P},\hat{r})$). However, the practical implementation evaluated in experiments (Section 5, described as Algorithm 4) replaces $\hat{P}$ with a TD bootstrap approximation: $P^\star(Q_\phi \circ \pi_\theta)(s_l, a_l) \approx (Q_\phi \circ \pi_\theta)(s_{l+1})$, explicitly to "avoid the need for a transition model." The paper acknowledges this is an approximation but does not analyze its effect on the theoretical guarantees. While this substitution (replacing an expectation with a single sample) is standard in deep RL and the core adversarial structure is preserved, the central claim of being "the first offline PbRL algorithm with both statistical bound and practical implementation" is weakened by the fact that the practical implementation differs from the object of the proof in a non-trivial way. The paper would be significantly stronger by either (a) adapting the theoretical analysis to cover the bootstrap approximation, or (b) including experiments using the model-based version (Algorithm 2 with a learned $\hat{P}$) to demonstrate the link.

2. **Narrow empirical scope with non-replicated baselines**: The experiments are limited to a single dataset suite (Meta-world medium-replay from Choi et al., 2024). While this is a valid benchmark, the generality of the empirical findings is unclear. Baseline results for MR, PT, DPPO, and IPL are cited from Choi et al. (2024) rather than re-run under controlled conditions — the paper acknowledges this (Table 1 caption: "results... are taken from Choi et al. (2024)"). Without re-running baselines, differences in implementation details, hyperparameter tuning, and random seeds could confound the comparison. Additionally, no standard deviations or confidence intervals are visible in the reported results, making statistical significance impossible to assess.

3. **Oracle-outperforming results noted but not analyzed**: APPO surpasses the policy trained with ground-truth rewards (Oracle) on dial-turn and button-press-topdown-wall tasks. This is an unusual result — if a method using learned rewards from limited preferences consistently beats training on true rewards, it demands analysis. Possible explanations (Oracle baseline under-tuned, APPO benefiting from implicit regularization, preference labeling threshold effects) are not discussed. This weakens confidence in the evaluation.

### Minor

4. **Computational efficiency claimed but not quantitatively demonstrated**: The paper repeatedly claims APPO is "computationally efficient" and contrasts with the "intractable" optimizations of prior work. However, the experiments report no wall-clock time, iteration counts, parameter counts, or training-time comparisons against any baseline. The argument is purely architectural (APPO uses standard non-convex optimizations). While the conceptual argument is reasonable, the claims overreach without quantitative support.

5. **Strong realizability assumption**: Assumption 3 requires that *every* $Q_{h,r}^\pi$ lies in the function class $\mathcal{F}$. For neural network parameterizations in practice, this is violated. The paper acknowledges this can be extended via covering numbers but the gap between the assumption and practice is significant.

6. **Sample complexity bound comparison is selectively favorable**: The paper claims the bound "matches" prior work for labeled data but notes prior work is "tighter in $R, H, \epsilon$" — the APPO bound scales quartically in $H$ ($H^5$ in the unlabeled sample complexity). This difference in horizon dependence is non-trivial and should be discussed more candidly.

## Novel Insights

The harsh critic's review and the strength finder's analysis converge on a consistent picture: the paper's core theoretical contribution — replacing explicit confidence sets with a two-player adversarial game using trajectory-pair ℓ₁ regularization — is genuinely novel and well-executed. The key unresolved tension is that the practical algorithm tested deviates from the theoretically analyzed algorithm in a standard but unanalyzed way (TD bootstrap vs. model-based expectation). This is not a fatal flaw (many RL papers have similar theory-practice gaps), but it means the paper's strongest claim — "first offline PbRL algorithm with both statistical bound and practical implementation" — requires qualification. An interesting observation from the reviews is that the Stackelberg game formulation naturally yields a cheaper algorithmic primitive (standard non-convex optimization) than confidence-set approaches, but the paper's experiments do not yet demonstrate whether this theoretical tractability translates into a practical advantage over well-tuned heuristic methods like MR. The most striking empirical finding — APPO beating the ground-truth reward oracle — is the one the paper analyzes least, and could be the most productive direction for future work.
