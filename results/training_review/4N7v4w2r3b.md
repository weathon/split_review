## Summary

This paper introduces ProxyBench, a benchmark for evaluating the robustness of neural-network proxy reward models against optimization pressure in the text domain. It provides three optimization methods (best-of-n, reinforcement learning, and white-box adversarial attacks), gold reward models (an ensemble trained on three human preference datasets), and metrics (Final Gold Reward and Root Mean Squared divergence). Through experiments, the paper uncovers that proxy gaming can occur on subsets of examples even when aggregate rewards look good, reveals scaling trends (larger proxies are harder to game; larger policies and more optimization exposure increase gaming), and tests existing robustness methods (adversarial training, SAM), finding they are insufficient for the general proxy gaming problem.

## Strengths

- **ProxyBench benchmark contribution.** The paper provides a concrete, reproducible evaluation framework (Section 4) with gold reward models, three distinct optimization methods, and two metrics. This fills a gap left by prior work (Gao et al., 2022) that did not release data or models. The benchmark's modular design enables standardized future research on proxy robustness.

- **Discovery that aggregate reward curves conceal subset-level gaming.** Section 5.1 demonstrates that even when mean gold reward improves, over 45% of gamed examples overlap across independently trained proxy models, with a hypothesis test rejecting random selection (p ≈ 0). Figures 2 and 3 illustrate this distributional insight — a novel and practically important finding for practitioners monitoring RLHF training.

- **Scaling laws for proxy vulnerability and defense.** Across all three optimizers, larger proxy models (DeBERTa-v3 Small → Base → Large) yield monotonically better gold reward and lower RMS (Table 1, Figure 5). Conversely, larger policy models and more optimization exposure increase gaming (Figure 6). These empirical trends provide actionable guidance for practitioners.

- **Negative results on existing robustness methods.** Adversarial training (PGD, PEZ) reduces white-box gaming but harms gold reward in BoN and RL settings; SAM provides no benefit (Section 5.5). This negative result is valuable because it challenges the assumption that standard adversarial defenses transfer to proxy gaming and clarifies that new methods are needed.

- **Qualitative characterization of different gaming patterns.** Section 5.3 explains how BoN gaming is "accidental," RL gaming involves learned exploits that spread across samples, and white-box attacks produce nonsensical text. Section 5.4 identifies phase-shift phenomena during RL training not captured by prior functional forms (Gao et al., 2022).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **The proxy gaming problem is redefined relative to a learned gold model, not human preferences.** The paper trains a neural network ensemble on human preference datasets and defines this as the "true" (gold) reward. While the paper transparently acknowledges this choice (Section 4.1: "This choice disregards how well the model captures the underlying objective of human raters"), the framing throughout — "true reward function," "faithfully represent the true objective," "Gold Reward" — can create a misleading impression that the benchmark tests alignment with human values rather than alignment between two learned models. All conclusions about gaming, scaling, and robustness are relative to a particular trained model that could itself be flawed or gameable. This does not invalidate the benchmark (many benchmarks use learned oracles), but it substantially narrows the scope of what the paper's results establish: they show how well smaller proxies approximate a larger ensemble on a specific distribution, not how well proxies approximate human judgment under optimization pressure.

- **The RMS metric is not validated against direct measures of proxy gaming.** The paper uses RMS as a primary metric throughout but never demonstrates that RMS values correlate with the paper's own instance-level gaming classification (negative correlation in either curve segment, Section 5.1). The metric depends on ad-hoc choices (normalization, smoothing window of 10% of evaluation points, derivative estimation via adjacent-step differences) that are not ablated or justified. While Table 2 compares RMS against alternative metrics (MAE, MaxAE, Pearson correlation), this shows internal consistency across divergence measures, not that RMS specifically captures the phenomenon of interest (proxy reward increasing while gold reward decreases). Without this validation, it is unclear what improvements in RMS actually mean for the proxy gaming problem.

### Minor

- **Tension between the abstract's "solvable" framing and the paper's actual results.** The abstract claims "proxy robustness is a solvable problem that can be incrementally improved," but the paper's own evidence shows: adversarial training helps only in the white-box setting and harms BoN/RL gold reward, SAM does nothing, and the paper concludes "existing solutions may not work as expected, and new methods may be required to fully solve the problem" (Section 1) and "new methods may be required" (Section 5.5). The only positive evidence for incremental improvement is the scaling result (larger proxies are harder to game) and the white-box adversarial training result. The abstract is more confident than the experiments warrant, though this is tempered by the paper's overall framing as a call for future work.

- **Potential confound in the scaling analysis.** Section 5.2 shows that larger proxy models are harder to game, but no controls are provided for training configuration across model sizes (e.g., whether smaller proxies had commensurate training data, steps, or regularization). The claimed scaling trend may confound model capacity with training resources.

- **No gold-model-as-policy baseline.** The paper does not establish an upper bound by training a policy directly against the gold model (with KL penalty). Without this, it is unclear how much of the observed degradation is caused by proxy gaming versus fundamental limitations of the optimization procedure itself.

- **Limited adversarial training experiments.** Only one set of adversarial hyperparameters is tested, and only PGD/PEZ attacks are used during training. Cross-attack transfer (e.g., train with PGD, test with PEZ) is not evaluated, and the paper does not analyze why adversarial training fails on BoN/RL.

### Trivial

- **Possible ambiguity about gold reward reporting for the white-box setting.** The paper states "We omit the gold reward in the white-box setting" (Section 4.4) but the caption of Table 1 describes trends "across all forms of optimization" for gold reward. The text is ambiguous about whether gold reward values are reported for WB in the table, creating momentary confusion.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Run a controlled scaling experiment that isolates model capacity from training resources (data size, steps, regularization) to confirm the scaling trend is driven by capacity.
- Test cross-attack transfer: evaluate models adversarially trained with PGD against PEZ attacks and vice versa, and also evaluate on BoN/RL to test whether robustness generalizes.
- Ablate the RMS metric's smoothing window and normalization choices, and show that RMS correlates with the instance-level gaming classification used in Section 5.1.
- Add a gold-model-as-policy baseline (RL with gold feedback) to calibrate how much room for improvement exists.

## Suggestions

1. **Tone down the abstract.** Replace "proxy robustness is a solvable problem that can be incrementally improved" with language that better reflects the paper's actual evidence, e.g., "our results provide empirical foundations for future work on proxy robustness, including scaling trends and the limitations of existing methods."
2. **Validate the RMS metric.** Add a simple experiment showing that examples classified as "gamed" (via the negative-correlation criterion) have significantly higher RMS values than non-gamed examples. Ablate the smoothing window choice.
3. **Add a gold-model-as-policy baseline.** Train a policy directly against the gold model with KL penalty and report the resulting reward and RMS. This will calibrate how much of the observed degradation is due to proxy gaming versus fundamental optimization difficulty.
4. **Clarify the scope of the gold model.** In the abstract and introduction, add a sentence making explicit that the benchmark measures proxy robustness relative to a learned ensemble of human preference models, not directly against human judgment.
5. **Run controlled scaling experiments.** If resources permit, train proxy models of different sizes with controlled data volume and steps to de-confound size from training resources.

The paper does not suffer from fatal flaws.
