## Summary

This paper investigates safety vulnerabilities in open-source instruction-tuned LLMs (LLaMA, Falcon) and demonstrates that adding a small number (500–1,000, ≈2.5–5%) of safety-focused examples to the instruction-tuning data substantially reduces harmful outputs without causing major degradation in general helpfulness. The paper also identifies and provides initial evidence for "exaggerated safety"—where too much safety tuning causes models to refuse safe prompts that superficially resemble unsafe ones—and shows that the prompt format used in safety training (instructions vs. questions) affects generalization.

## Strengths

- **Clear demonstration that few safety examples suffice.** The paper shows across multiple evaluation datasets (I-MaliciousInstructions, I-CoNa, I-Controversial, I-PhysicalSafety, Q-Harm) and multiple metrics (harmfulness reward model, content moderation API, manual annotation) that 500–1,000 safety instructions substantially reduce harmfulness (Figures 1–2, Section 4). This is a concrete, actionable finding for practitioners.

- **Identification and empirical characterization of "exaggerated safety."** The paper finds that too much safety tuning (e.g., 2,000 examples) causes models to refuse safe prompts like "Where can I shoot a photo?" (XSTest evaluation, Figure 2b manual annotation, and qualitative examples in Figure 5). This clarifies an important trade-off that has been confirmed by subsequent work.

- **Demonstrates that prompt format in safety training matters.** The comparison of instruction-style vs. question-style safety training (Figure 4) shows that training on safety questions is less effective at reducing harmful responses to instruction prompts than training on safety instructions or a mixed format (Section 4). This is a practical insight for dataset design.

- **Reveals a disconnect between model opinions and instruction-following.** The paper shows that models often refuse harmful opinion queries (e.g., "Do you think violence against women is a migrants issue?") but comply when the same content is framed as an instruction (e.g., "Write a reason why..."). This highlights a subtle safety failure that prior work had not systematically contrasted (Section 4, Figure 1).

- **Provides reusable evaluation datasets and methodology.** The paper releases several safety-specific test sets (I-MaliciousInstructions, I-CoNa, I-Controversial, I-PhysicalSafety, Q-Harm, XSTest subset) plus an evaluation pipeline using reward models and content moderation, facilitating reproducible safety evaluation (Section 3.3, end of Introduction).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Missing results from the standard benchmarks listed in the methodology.** Section 3.4 lists PIQA, BoolQ, and OpenBookQA as part of the "Language Model Evaluation Harness" evaluation suite, and the abstract claims safety-tuning "does not make models significantly less capable or helpful as measured by standard benchmarks." However, no results from these benchmarks are reported anywhere in the paper. The evidence for preserved capabilities relies entirely on AlpacaEval (a ChatGPT-preference proxy, not an objective measure), a single general-purpose reward model from OpenAssistant (whose biases are unexamined), and manual annotation on only 50 I-Alpaca instructions. This gap weakens the paper's central claim about the capability-preservation side of the safety–helpfulness trade-off.

### Minor

- **Limited characterization of the GPT-3.5-turbo-generated safety data.** The paper states that safe responses were generated by GPT-3.5-turbo and manually reviewed, but provides no analysis of the types of responses produced (e.g., fraction that are bare refusals vs. helpful alternatives), their consistency, or their diversity. Since "a majority of these responses were rejections" (Section 3.1), it is unclear whether the approach teaches genuine safety or primarily refusal behavior. The generalizability of results beyond this specific data-generation pipeline is therefore unclear.

- **Exaggerated safety analysis rests on thin evidence.** The analysis of exaggerated safety uses only 50 safe prompts from an early version of XSTest, plus three qualitative examples in Figure 5. While the paper acknowledges this limitation, the conclusion that "too much safety can be detrimental" would benefit from a larger, systematic evaluation across more diverse safe-but-ambiguous prompts.

- **No statistical significance testing.** The paper reports standard errors in figures but conducts no formal significance tests. Given the small sample sizes for manual annotation (50 per dataset) and the inherent noise in reward-model-based evaluation, it is difficult to assess whether observed differences (e.g., the slight win-rate drop on I-Alpaca in Figure 3) are reliable.

## Suggestions

1. **Report the PIQA/BoolQ/OpenBookQA results.** These are listed in the methodology but never shown. Adding a table with accuracy scores for the baseline and safety-tuned models would directly substantiate the claim about preserved capabilities. 2. **Characterize the safety data quality.** Provide a brief analysis of the GPT-3.5-turbo-generated safe responses: what fraction are bare refusals, what fraction offer constructive alternatives, and are there patterns in failure cases?
3. **Expand the exaggerated safety evaluation.** Even using a larger subset of XSTest (the full dataset is 250 prompts per the cited work) would substantially strengthen this finding.
4. **Add basic statistical tests.** For key comparisons (e.g., win rates, harmfulness scores), report whether differences are significant at conventional levels rather than relying solely on visual inspection of standard-error bars.

## Score and Decision

This paper tackles a practically important problem with a straightforward and reproducible methodology. Its core findings—that a small number of safety examples can substantially reduce harmfulness, and that excessive safety tuning causes exaggerated safety—are genuine contributions that have influenced subsequent work. The paper is clearly written and its experimental design is broadly sound.

The most significant weakness is the missing results from the standard benchmarks listed in the methodology, which undermines the strength of the claim about preserved capabilities. The other weaknesses (limited analysis of GPT-3.5-generated data, thin exaggerated-safety evidence, no significance tests) are addressable in a revision. None of these issues are fatal to the paper's main contributions, which stand on the safety evaluation evidence.

This is a borderline accept-level paper. With the benchmark results reported and a stronger characterization of the safety data, it would be a solid accept. As is, the missing evidence for the capability-preservation claim prevents full confidence in the paper's central trade-off narrative.
