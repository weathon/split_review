## Summary

This paper proposes a confidence decision boundary visualization method for active learning that uses Voronoi tessellation on 2D t-SNE projections of deep features. The key technical innovation is a "ridge confidence" metric that assigns confidence values to segments of the predicted decision boundary (Voronoi ridges between cells with different predicted labels), enabling the identification of which boundary regions are closer to the true decision boundary. Using this framework, the authors create three visualization types (Confidence Decision Boundary, Cumulative Sampling, Error Detection) and apply them to analyze the behavior of eight active learning query strategies on MNIST and CIFAR-10.

## Strengths

- **Ridge confidence metric with quantitative validation**: The paper defines a novel metric (C_pred = 1 - Σ_k P(ŷ_i=k)P(ŷ_j=k)) that scores each Voronoi ridge segment of the decision boundary by how distinct the predictions are on either side. Importantly, Figure 4 provides quantitative validation: ridge accuracy (fraction of predicted ridges matching ground-truth class boundaries) improves monotonically as the confidence interval threshold increases, and this trend holds across progressive AL rounds. This gives the method some internal grounding.

- **Multi-round visualization framework goes beyond single-snapshot prior work**: The paper designs three complementary visualization types — Confidence Decision Boundary, Cumulative Sampling over 5 Rounds, and Error Detection — that track model and strategy behavior across iterations. This addresses a genuine limitation of prior work (Mac Namee 2010, Liao 2016, Migut 2015) that focused on single-stage or static snapshots.

- **Visual analysis reveals nuanced strategy behavior**: The error detection visualizations distinguish between uncertainty from insufficient training samples (resolvable via concentrated sampling) versus uncertainty from inherently noisy data (harder to resolve), as shown in Figure 6. The cumulative sampling visualizations also reveal KCenterGreedy's class imbalance issues (Figure 8e), corroborating prior findings with direct visual evidence.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **t-SNE fidelity validation mentioned but not reported**: The paper states (Section 3.3) that "we conducted two quantitative evaluations: Correlation Test and Local Structure Preservation on the t-SNE method used in this study" to assess spatial distortion from dimensionality reduction. However, no results, thresholds, or even qualitative discussion of these evaluations are presented anywhere in the paper. Since the entire visualization pipeline projects high-dimensional deep features into 2D via t-SNE and then builds Voronoi diagrams in that space, the faithfulness of this projection is foundational. The paper's internal validation (Figure 4) shows that higher-confidence ridges align with ground-truth boundaries *within the 2D projection*, but whether the 2D neighborhoods preserve the structure needed for Voronoi cells to reflect meaningful high-dimensional relationships is never demonstrated. This is the single most important missing piece.

2. **Ridge confidence metric has a counterintuitive behavior when both sides are uniformly uncertain**: The metric C_pred = 1 - Σ_k P(ŷ_i=k)P(ŷ_j=k) can produce high values (e.g., 0.9 for 10-class problems) when both points on either side of a ridge have near-uniform predictions (high uncertainty). This means a ridge can be scored as "high confidence" precisely where the model is most uncertain — the opposite of the intended interpretation. The empirical validation in Figure 4 partially mitigates this concern by showing that in practice, higher C_pred ridges do align better with ground truth, but the paper offers no theoretical explanation for why the counterexample does not arise or why the metric still works despite it. A synthetic experiment on low-dimensional data where ground truth is known would help resolve this.

### Minor

3. **Qualitative claims lack quantitative support**: The paper repeatedly asserts findings such as "entropy sampling frequently engages in high-risk boundary-crossing sampling" (Section 4.1), "BALD dropout distributes sampling more broadly" (Section 4.1), and "KCenterGreedy suffers from significant class imbalance during sampling" (Section 4.3). These are supported only by visual inspection of a few figures. No counts, percentages, divergence metrics, or statistical comparisons are provided. For a visualization paper some qualitative analysis is expected, but the conclusions are presented as definitive findings about strategy behavior rather than as observations from the proposed visualization method. Adding basic quantitative summaries (e.g., percentage of sampled points in high- vs. low-confidence regions, class-distribution entropy of selected samples) would substantially strengthen the paper.

4. **Fixed visualization model vs. AL model feature alignment not discussed**: The paper trains a separate "visualization model" on the full pool to obtain a fixed 2D t-SNE projection, then uses this projection for visualizations of all AL strategies across all rounds (Section 3.3). This assumes that the fixed visualization space remains meaningful for AL models that have seen only 1.2% of the data. The paper shows one figure (Figure 8e) using the AL model's own features but does not discuss the inconsistency or validate whether the fixed projection preserves relevant structure for poorly trained early-round models. Given that AL models early in training may have very different feature spaces, this assumption deserves explicit discussion or validation.

## Novel Insights
The reviews reveal that the paper's strongest point — the quantitative ridge accuracy validation in Figure 4 — is also where the most interesting tension lies. The harsh critic identifies a genuine theoretical flaw in the ridge confidence metric (high C_pred can arise from uniform predictions on both sides of a ridge), yet Figure 4 empirically shows that the metric works in practice on real data. This suggests either that the degenerate case (uniform predictions on both sides of a predicted ridge) rarely occurs on actual decision boundaries, or that when it does occur those ridges genuinely lie near class boundaries. Neither the paper nor the critics explore this tension, which represents a potentially valuable direction for future analysis. The paper would benefit from a controlled experiment that isolates when and why the metric works despite its theoretical issue.
