## Summary

This paper proposes **ToM-agent**, a paradigm for equipping LLM-based generative agents with theory of mind by tracking beliefs, desires, and intentions (BDIs) with associated confidence levels during open-domain conversation. The key methodological novelty is a **counterfactual reflection** mechanism that predicts the counterpart's next utterance, compares it against the real utterance using similarity scoring, and uses this gap to update inferred BDIs. Experiments are conducted on empathetic and persuasion dialogue datasets with GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, evaluating first-order and second-order ToM inference accuracy and downstream task performance.

## Strengths

- **First work to apply ToM with disentangled belief and confidence to open-domain generative agents.** Prior ToM work focuses on binary (true/false) belief states in constrained or task-specific settings (psychological narratives, goal-oriented games). Extending this to open-domain dialogue with confidence as a separate dimension is a sensible and underexplored direction (Section 1, contribution list).

- **Counterfactual reflection is a theoretically grounded design.** Using prediction error between the predicted and actual utterance to indirectly reflect on unobservable mental states is a motivated approach, connecting to psychological literature on foresight and counterfactual thinking (Cuzzolin et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2023). The method addresses a real challenge: BDIs are inherently unobservable, so direct reflection on inference quality is infeasible.

- **Reported quantitative improvements over baselines across multiple conditions.** The paper consistently reports that the full ToM-agent (ToM+CR) outperforms the vanilla and reflection-only ablation on both first-order and second-order ToM metrics (Tables 1–2) and on downstream AT and SR@t metrics across both datasets and both LLMs (Table 3). This consistency across conditions is a positive signal.

- **Addresses an important and timely problem.** Equipping LLM-based agents with theory of mind reasoning for natural, unconstrained conversation is relevant to the broader goal of building socially capable AI. The paper's framing of this problem is clear and motivated.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None. The paper makes a reasonable conceptual contribution even if the evaluation has significant limitations.

### Major

1. **Near-vacuous similarity threshold for BDI classification (0.25/5).** The binary classification of whether inferred BDIs match "true" BDIs uses a threshold of >0.25 on a [0,5] scale — just 5% of the rating range. This is so low that almost any pair of BDIs would be classified as "similar," making precision, recall, and F1 scores in Tables 1 and 2 largely uninformative. Even random or poor BDI inferences would likely exceed this threshold. The paper does not justify this threshold or report what scores were actually observed (e.g., the distribution of annotator similarity scores), so the reader cannot determine whether the metrics discriminate meaningfully between methods.

2. **Downstream task success criterion is self-referential.** The success metric for downstream tasks (AT and SR@t) is defined by whether "agent A believes that agent B understands its BDI" — this judgment is generated by the same LLM-based agent A that is being evaluated (line 116). There is no external validation: no human evaluation of response quality (e.g., human-rated empathy), no behavioral outcome (e.g., actual donation behavior in persuasion), and no independent measure of task completion. The metric measures the LLM's self-assessment of being understood, not actual task success. This circularity means the results cannot support the claim that ToM "benefits downstream tasks" in any practically meaningful sense.

3. **Ground-truth BDIs are LLM-generated without human validation.** The "true" BDIs used as evaluation targets are generated by zero-shot LLM prompting from dialogue episodes (line 66). Human annotators only compare inferred BDIs against these LLM-generated "true" BDIs; they do **not** validate whether the "true" BDIs are themselves correct or natural given the dialogue context. The evaluation thus primarily measures consistency between two LLM outputs under different prompting schemes, not genuine theory of mind accuracy. Without human-annotated BDI gold standards, the results cannot be interpreted as evidence of ToM capability.

### Minor

4. **No calibration analysis for confidence values.** The paper presents LLM-generated confidence scores (Figures 3–4) as evidence of "dynamic and evolving" mental states, but provides no analysis showing that these confidence values correlate with actual BDI inference accuracy or task success. Without calibration, these curves could be prompt artifacts rather than meaningful indicators of epistemic certainty.

5. **No ablation of the Reverse BDIs Argumentation component.** The paper describes a technique to overcome commonsense bias by generating BDIs with opposite meanings (line 68), but never isolates its contribution. The reported experiments compare only Vanilla vs. Reflection vs. CR-based ToM; the Reverse BDIs step is used in all conditions (or none — it's unclear), so its individual effect is unknown.

6. **Confound between method and compute budget.** The CR-based ToM method uses additional LLM calls (for prediction, similarity scoring, virtual response generation, and comparison) compared to the Vanilla baseline. There is no control condition that isolates whether improved performance is due to the structured ToM reasoning or simply to having more processing / richer prompts.

7. **Confidence updating procedure is underspecified.** The counterfactual reflection description (Section 3.3) states that the LLM generates an "update plan" — but the decision logic between branches (e.g., "if S_v > S then update else update") occurs within a single LLM call, making it unclear what procedural constraints (if any) are imposed on the update mechanism versus being left entirely to prompt-based LLM reasoning.

8. **Use of deprecated text-similarity-davinci-001 model.** The similarity scoring relies on a model that is no longer supported, raising reproducibility concerns.

### Trivial
- Hyperparameters (max turns t=10, top-k=3) are set without justification or sensitivity analysis.
- The ethical discussion (Section 5.3) is brief; while the paper acknowledges concerns, it does not propose concrete safeguards or mitigation strategies.

## Nice-to-Haves
- **Human evaluation of downstream task quality** (e.g., human judges rating empathy or measuring actual persuasion outcomes) would transform the self-referential success metric into a meaningful evaluation.
- **Human-annotated BDI ground truth** on a held-out set of dialogue episodes would allow genuine validation of ToM inference accuracy.
- **Confidence calibration analysis** comparing LLM confidence scores against actual inference accuracy would validate whether the confidence tracking has meaning beyond being a prompt artifact.
- **Compute-matched control condition** where the baseline uses additional LLM calls for task-irrelevant reasoning would disentangle the effect of structure from budget.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, the most interesting observation is the tension between the paper's conceptual framing and its evaluation reality. The paper argues convincingly that ToM in open-domain conversation requires going beyond binary true/false beliefs, and the counterfactual reflection approach is conceptually elegant. Yet the evaluation chain is entirely self-contained within the LLM: the "ground truth" BDIs are LLM-generated, the success criterion is an LLM's self-assessment, and the confidence values are an LLM's self-reported numbers. This creates a situation where the paper's methodological contribution is substantially stronger than the evidence it provides. The real value of this work may lie more in the paradigm it proposes — which could serve as a foundation for future work with proper human-validated evaluations — than in the specific empirical results reported.

## Suggestions

1. **Report the full distribution of annotator similarity scores** (not just the binary classification from the 0.25 threshold) to show that the methods actually produce meaningfully different similarity distributions. Justify the threshold empirically or abandon the binary framing and report raw similarity scores.

2. **Add a proper human evaluation** for the downstream task: have human judges rate the quality of the ToM-agent's responses (e.g., empathy, persuasiveness) without knowing which condition produced them.

3. **Validate the "true" BDIs** by having human annotators independently generate BDIs from the same dialogue episodes, then measure how well the LLM-initialized BDIs match human judgments. This would anchor the evaluation in human understanding rather than LLM consistency.

4. **Add a compute-matched control** where the baseline receives additional LLM calls for a task-irrelevant reasoning step (e.g., summarizing the conversation) to isolate the effect of the counterfactual reflection structure from the increased computational budget.

5. **Report confidence calibration** by comparing LLM confidence values against a confusion matrix of correct vs. incorrect BDI inferences (using human-validated ground truth).

6. **Add concrete dialogue transcripts** showing the inferred BDIs and confidence trajectories turn-by-turn to give readers an intuitive understanding of what the method does in practice.
