## Summary

This paper identifies the "Feature Overlapping Phenomenon" — the observation that in SNNs, feature maps at consecutive time steps are highly similar due to weight sharing and static input encoding. The authors propose Temporal Differential Decoupling (TDD) to separate static (overlapping) and dynamic (changing) features by transforming computation into the differential domain, and a Gradient Sensitivity Criterion (GSC) to select which dynamic features matter most. Combined, these form the Differential Domain Low-Sparsity Approximation (DDLA) algorithm. The paper reports substantial spike count reductions (up to 80.9% per timestep, 57.8% total) but provides zero classification accuracy numbers to substantiate the claim that accuracy is maintained.

## Strengths

- **Concept of operating in the differential domain:** The idea of decoupling static and dynamic features across time steps and operating on changes rather than absolute activations is conceptually interesting and offers a fresh perspective on SNN computational redundancy that differs from dominant time-step reduction approaches. The joint temporal-spatial unfolding (Equations 5-7) provides a formal basis for this decomposition.

- **Theoretical error bound (Theorem 2):** The paper provides an error bound that relates approximation quality to the filtering ratio \(k\) and the number of time steps \(T\), showing formally that increasing time steps reduces algorithmic error. While the bound is loose and relies on strong independence assumptions, it represents a genuine attempt to characterize accuracy-efficiency trade-offs analytically.

- **Empirical demonstration of spike reduction:** The spike count reductions (57.8% total, 80.9% per-timestep at T=6 on CIFAR-100 with ResNet-19) are quantitatively substantial. If verified to come with acceptable accuracy, these reductions would represent a meaningful advance.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **No accuracy numbers reported anywhere in the paper.** The paper's central claim is that DDLA "significantly reduces computational resource consumption while maintaining computational accuracy" (abstract) and "ensur[es] accuracy comparable to baseline models" (contributions). No classification accuracy, error rate, or any other accuracy metric is reported for any model, dataset, or configuration. The abstract says these are "claims," not "results." Table 2 is described as "Comparison of spike number and simulation length T" — it contains only spike counts. Without accuracy-efficiency Pareto curves or even a single accuracy number, the reported spike reductions are uninterpretable: they could simply reflect a collapsed network that fires nearly nothing and achieves chance-level performance. This omission invalidates the paper's core empirical claim and makes it impossible to assess the method's practical value. No amount of supplementary strengths can compensate for the absence of the primary evaluation metric.

### Major

- **No concrete operationalization of computational savings.** The paper states that static features are "reused" and that after GSC filtering, only sensitive dynamic features are computed in the differential domain, resulting in sparser \(\Delta_l^t\). However, it never specifies an implementable procedure that actually reduces the number of synaptic operations or neuron evaluations during inference. Computing \(\Delta_l^t = O_l^t - O_l^{t-1}\) (or via XOR) still requires running the forward pass to obtain \(O_l^t\). On standard hardware, one cannot selectively compute a sparse subset of a dense matrix multiplication without specialized sparse computation support. The paper does not describe how filtered sensitivity maps translate to actual FLOP, energy, or latency reductions. Spike count alone is an insufficient proxy for computational cost.

### Minor

- **Mathematical inconsistency in the definition of feature maps.** Definition 1 defines the feature difference \(\Delta_l^t = O_l^t \oplus O_l^{t-1}\) using XOR, which is only defined for binary values (consistent with spike outputs). However, the GSC derivation and Taylor expansion (Section 5.1, Equations 9-10) treat \(O_{l,i}^t\) as continuous-valued features for gradient computation. The paper never clarifies whether the method applies to binary spikes, membrane potentials, or post-activation continuous features, creating ambiguity in the mathematical framework.

- **Overclaimed novelty.** The paper repeatedly claims to be "the first to discover the Feature Overlapping Phenomenon." The observation that consecutive time-step feature maps in SNNs are similar due to weight sharing and static input encoding is a straightforward consequence of SNN recurrence with static input. While the formalization via TDD is useful, the claim of novelty is inflated. The paper also claims testing on "event-based datasets" (Contributions) but experiments only use CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 (static image datasets); no DVS or event-based dataset appears in Section 7.

- **Gradient Sensitivity Criterion is not novel.** The criterion \(\zeta(O_{l,i}^t) = |\delta\mathcal{L}/\delta O_{l,i}^t \cdot O_{l,i}^t|\) (Definition 2) is the standard first-order Taylor pruning criterion (equivalent to SNIP and related gradient-magnitude methods). The paper does not distinguish its usage from prior work or justify why it is well-suited to the differential domain beyond general intuition.

- **Key experimental details missing.** The paper does not describe the baseline model accuracy (making it impossible to compute relative accuracy loss), does not specify the threshold \(\epsilon\) used in Definition 1, and does not report the filtering ratio \(k\) values used in experiments. The serial processing requirement (line 240) is acknowledged but its impact on wall-clock time and latency is not discussed.

### Trivial

- The term "feature shadowing" is introduced in the Introduction but replaced inconsistently with "Feature Overlapping" thereafter.
- Some typographical issues: "frst-of-its-kind" (line 18), "donates" instead of "denotes" (line 115).
- Figure references in text refer to Table 1's "orange/red/blue sections" but the table is a rendered image that is not readable in the text.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Extending evaluation to event-based datasets (e.g., DVS-Gesture, N-Caltech101) would substantiate the claim of generality to "event-based datasets."
- Reporting wall-clock time or estimated energy consumption on neuromorphic hardware would strengthen the efficiency claims beyond spike counts.
