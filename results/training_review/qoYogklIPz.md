## Summary

This paper proposes ELM (Embedding Language Model), a framework that uses a trainable adapter layer to map domain embedding vectors (e.g., movie embeddings from MovieLens) into the token-embedding space of a pretrained LLM, enabling the LLM to generate natural-language descriptions of entities represented by those embeddings. The method is evaluated on 24 movie-interpretation tasks and a user-profile generation task using MovieLens 25M data, with three evaluation approaches: semantic consistency (SC), behavioral consistency (BC), and human ratings from 100 raters per task. Experiments also cover interpolation between entities and CAV-based extrapolation.

## Strengths

1. **Novel architecture for embedding-LLM interfacing.** The ELM framework introduces a clean and technically sound mechanism (adapter layer $E_A$ mapping $\mathcal{W} \to \mathcal{Z}$) for injecting arbitrary domain embeddings into an LLM's token sequence. The two-stage training procedure (freeze-all-then-fine-tune) is well-motivated to address convergence challenges with continuous prompts. (Section 2)

2. **Multi-faceted evaluation design.** The paper uses three complementary evaluation approaches: a re-embedding consistency metric (SC) that works even for hypothetical entities, a behavioral prediction metric (BC) that connects generated text to actual user preference data ($R_u$ from MovieLens), and human evaluation with 100 raters per task. Having multiple independent evaluation signals is a strength, especially since each has different failure modes. (Section 3.2, Table 1)

3. **Demonstrated capability on hypothetical embedding points.** ELM generates meaningful descriptions for interpolated movie/user embeddings and CAV-direction extrapolations — scenarios where text-only LLMs have no grounding. The consistency metrics remain strong across interpolation coefficients, and the qualitative example (Figure 1) shows plausible semantic blending. (Section 4, Figures 4–5)

4. **Multi-task training across 25 diverse tasks.** Training on 24 movie tasks (summaries, reviews, comparisons, characterizations, etc.) plus a user-profile task demonstrates that ELM can learn a general mapping from embedding space to language, not just a single task-specific transformation.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Semantic Consistency (SC) has inherent circularity that is not acknowledged as a limitation.** SC uses the same dual-encoder DLM for both embedding movies and re-embedding ELM's output text (lines 120–125). This means SC primarily measures whether ELM's outputs invert that specific DLM — a form of autoencoding fidelity — rather than measuring whether the outputs correspond to ground-truth semantic content about the entity. High SC can be achieved by learning a mapping that produces text the DLM maps back to the input region, without necessarily "interpreting" the embedding. While the paper compensates with human evaluation and BC, the SC metric alone is stressed heavily (Figures 3–4, Table 1) and the circularity is not discussed as a limitation.

2. **No ablation isolating the embedding's contribution.** The paper never tests whether replacing the domain embedding with a simpler representation (e.g., a movie title tokenized as text, a learned embedding of the movie ID, or a random vector) would yield similar results. The LLM receives both the embedding vector and task-specific textual prompt tokens; without an ablation that removes or randomizes the embedding, we cannot conclude that the domain embedding contributes meaningful information beyond what the task-specific textual cues or positional biases in the training data provide. This is a significant methodological gap for a paper whose core claim is that ELM "interprets" embeddings.

### Minor

1. **The text-only LLM baseline is weak and not particularly informative.** For the interpolation comparison (Figure 2), text-only LLMs (PaLM 2-L, GPT-4) receive a one-shot example and are prompted to generate output — but are given no movie title, description, or embedding (line 205). Unsurprisingly, they perform poorly. A much more informative baseline would convert the interpolated embedding to a nearest-neighbor movie title and prompt the LLM with that title (e.g., "describe a movie that combines elements of Forrest Gump and [nearest movie]"). The current comparison only confirms that text-only LLMs cannot access embedding space — a fact that does not require experimental verification — rather than testing whether ELM outperforms reasonable interpretation proxies.

2. **Training data is entirely PaLM 2-L generated, creating an upper bound on what ELM can learn.** All 24 movie-task training targets are generated by prompting PaLM 2-L with movie titles (lines 106–107). ELM's outputs are fundamentally bounded by PaLM 2-L's knowledge, writing style, and biases. The paper does not discuss how this constrains the claims: ELM can only extract from an embedding what the teacher LLM could produce from a movie title. The model cannot surface information the teacher did not know or chose not to include, which weakens the claim that ELM extracts "implicit" information latent in the embedding.

3. **No confidence intervals or measures of variance.** The paper reports SC and BC scores as point estimates (Table 1, Figures 3–4) without standard deviations, confidence intervals, or significance tests, despite using 1000 test examples. Variability across movies/users could be non-trivial.

4. **Human evaluation procedure is underspecified.** The paper states that 100 raters assessed outputs for "consistency with a movie's plot" (line 116/186) but does not describe whether raters were shown ground-truth movie information, how they judged consistency without knowing all movie plots, what instructions were provided, or how inter-rater agreement was measured. The near-ceiling scores (1.0 for "neutral review" consistency and comprehensiveness) suggest either an ambiguous rating scale or a lenient evaluation setup.

5. **Behavioral Consistency uses a text-similarity proxy rather than the actual behavioral model.** BC for user profiles uses the DLM (a text-similarity model) as the ranker ρ (line 174 footnote), comparing its rankings against ground-truth $R_u$. While this is a reasonable approach, the paper does not validate that the DLM's text-similarity rankings correlate well with actual rating preferences — an implicit assumption of the metric. Using the actual CF model (matrix factorization) as the ranker would provide a more direct behavioral validation.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Reporting SC scores with the DLM as a function of interpolation coefficient while also showing results with a different, independently trained re-embedding model would strengthen the SC metric's validity.
- A factual task with objectively verifiable ground truth (e.g., "What year was this movie released?") would provide a cleaner test of information extraction from embeddings.
- Testing on a different embedding space (e.g., product embeddings) without retraining the adapter would demonstrate generalization beyond MovieLens.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The reviews surface a tension that is worth noting: the paper's technical contribution (adapter-based injection of domain embeddings into an LLM) is arguably stronger than what its evaluation framework can support. The architecture and training procedure are sensible and likely useful, but the claims about "interpretation" of embedding spaces outpace the evidence — especially given the absence of ablation studies isolating what the embedding contributes versus what the LLM hallucinates from correlated cues. This mismatch between a solid engineering contribution and overclaimed interpretability framing is the central tension the paper should address.

## Suggestions

1. **Add an ablation study** replacing the domain embedding with (a) a movie title tokenized as text, (b) a randomized embedding of the same dimension, and (c) a learned lookup-table embedding (movie ID → vector). If (a) yields comparable performance, the embedding is not being interpreted — the model is learning to rely on textual/prompt cues. If (b) yields chance performance, the embedding is providing real information. This is the single most impactful addition.

2. **Acknowledge the SC circularity explicitly** as a limitation, and consider reporting a "cross-encoder SC" where a different embedding model is used for re-embedding to demonstrate the metric's robustness.

3. **Strengthen the text-only LLM baseline** by converting interpolated embeddings to their nearest-neighbor movie titles, then prompting the LLM with those titles and an interpolation instruction (e.g., "describe a movie that combines elements of X and Y"). This would test whether ELM's advantage comes from accessing the embedding directly or from some other aspect of the pipeline.

4. **Replace or supplement the DLM ranker in BC** with the actual behavioral model (matrix factorization) to validate that the DLM-based text similarity correlates with genuine rating preferences.

5. **Report variance** (standard deviations or confidence intervals) for all quantitative metrics, and describe the human evaluation protocol in more detail (instructions provided to raters, whether ground-truth was visible, inter-rater agreement statistics).

## Score and Decision

The paper presents a technically solid framework for interfacing LLMs with domain embeddings, with a well-motivated architecture and a multi-task training approach. The core technical contribution — learning an adapter to map domain embeddings into LLM token space for generative interpretation — is genuine and potentially useful. However, the evaluation has significant gaps that prevent the paper from fully establishing its central claims about "interpretation": the SC metric has unacknowledged circularity, the text-only baseline is uninformative, the training data is entirely teacher-generated, and critically, the paper lacks any ablation study demonstrating that the embedding contributes meaningful information beyond what a simpler representation would provide. These issues are addressable but are not fixable within a rebuttal — they would require additional experiments. The paper is a solid contribution to embedding-LLM interfacing but overclaims on interpretability.
