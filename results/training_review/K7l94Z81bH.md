## Summary

This paper proposes RLD3, a multi-agent reinforcement learning algorithm for designated driver dispatch that addresses three forms of sparsity (dataset, feedback, and cross-effect sparsity) through three coupled techniques: group-sharing to balance heterogeneity with scalable learning, a neighborhood potential reward to provide dense per-step feedback, and window-lasting policy ensembles to capture long-term cross-effects among agents. The method is evaluated on a simulator built from real-world Hangzhou designated driving data.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated problem framing.** The paper clearly identifies and articulates three distinct sparsity challenges specific to designated driving (slow movement, hub-and-spoke order structure, sparse matches, lasting cross-effects) that distinguish it from standard ride-hailing. This domain-specific characterization is a genuine contribution.

- **Clean algorithmic synthesis.** The three core ideas — group-sharing (Section 3.2), neighborhood potential reward (Section 3.3), and window-lasting interaction (Section 3.4) — each target a specific sparsity type, and the ablation study (Table 2, referenced in lines 229–243) shows that removing any of them degrades completed-order performance, providing empirical support for each design choice.

- **Real-world simulator.** Building the simulator from actual Hangzhou driver and order traces (3,000+ drivers, ~13,000 orders/day, line 191) grounds the evaluation in practical relevance, a step beyond purely synthetic benchmarks.

- **Systematic ablation exists.** Despite gaps discussed below, the paper does isolate its components (group number, window interaction, state encoder, reward design) and reports their individual contributions, providing a foundation for understanding what matters in the method.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None. The paper's core claims are not fatally invalidated, though several require substantiation.

### Major

- **"Adherence to moving constraints" is claimed but never defined or measured.** The abstract, introduction, and conclusion all claim that RLD3 achieves better "adherence to moving constraints" (lines 4, 21, 252). However, the paper never defines what "moving constraints" are, what it means to adhere to them, or how this is operationalized as a metric. The only constraint-related mechanism is the move cost `-c_j` in the reward (Section 3.2). The paper reports moving distance qualitatively ("smaller moving distance compared to methods that had similar completed order performance," line 211) but this is a single proxy, not a definition of constraint adherence. The tables are embedded as images and their column contents cannot be verified from the text extraction, but even if moving distance values are present there, the broader claim about "constraint adherence" remains underspecified. This is the paper's most serious gap: the headline promise is unmeasurable from the information provided.

- **Group assignment mechanism is underspecified, compromising reproducibility.** The paper states that drivers "can be classified into several types based on their cost conditions" (line 94) but never explains how groups are actually assigned in the experiments. The cost values `c_j` per group are introduced in the move cost formula (lines 98–102) but their numerical values are never stated. Whether groups are defined by driver features (speed, mileage limits, etc.) or assigned arbitrarily is unclear. The paper even lists "making the grouping process trainable" as future work (line 254), suggesting the current grouping is fixed and heuristic, yet the heuristic itself is never described. This makes the experimental results difficult to interpret or reproduce.

- **Baseline DRL algorithms use identical hyperparameters with no tuning reported.** All DRL baselines (MADDPG, MAMFRL, RND, independent DDPG) use the same architecture and hyperparameters: two hidden layers of 64, batch size 512, learning rate 0.01 (line 198). No search or tuning for these baselines is reported. Since MADDPG is known to be sensitive to architecture choices (e.g., separate critic networks per agent vs. shared, network size), the gap between RLD3 and the baselines could be partially attributable to undertuned baselines rather than algorithmic superiority. This weakens the comparative claim.

- **Experimental scope is narrow for the scale of claims.** Training uses only 50 drivers and 500 orders (line 172) from a dataset containing 3,000+ drivers and ~13,000 orders (line 191). Testing is on 10 episodes with 4 seeds (lines 207, 172). No experiments examine scalability to larger driver/order counts, sensitivity to the sampling procedure, or out-of-distribution shifts. The IID generalization test (Section 4.3) uses the same initial driver state and the same underlying distribution as training, making near-identical performance expected rather than surprising. The paper's claims about real-world effectiveness and "robustness" (line 220) are not supported by this evidence alone.

- **Driver-order matching rule is never described.** The state transition description (line 74) mentions "matches between drivers and orders," but how matching occurs is never specified. Is it nearest-distance? First-come-first-served? An optimization? This is a critical detail because the immediate match reward hinges on when and how matches happen, and different matching rules could systematically advantage or disadvantage different dispatch policies.

### Minor

- **The window size `W` (set to 60 steps / 30 minutes) is not ablated.** The ablation tests removing the window-lasting mechanism entirely but does not vary the window size (e.g., 10, 30, 60, 120 steps). The method's sensitivity to this parameter is unknown.

- **The constants in the potential reward (power −0.5, additive 0.1) are not justified beyond a qualitative convexity argument** (line 122). The paper notes the function is chosen to be convex but does not analyze whether the specific values matter or how they were selected. Given that removing the potential reward causes a significant drop in performance (line 243), understanding whether this particular functional form is critical would strengthen the paper.

- **Only 4 random seeds are used** (line 172). For a stochastic MARL setting with random driver initialization and Poisson order cancelations, more seeds would give greater confidence in the reported differences.

## Suggestions

1. **Define and measure "moving constraints" explicitly.** What constraints exist (e.g., maximum per-driver mileage, speed limits, working hours)? Report both moving distance and a constraint violation rate (or similar) in the main evaluation. Without this, the paper's headline claim is unverifiable.

3. **Tune the DRL baselines** on a validation set and report the best-found hyperparameters, or at minimum justify why the chosen shared hyperparameters are appropriate for each baseline algorithm.
