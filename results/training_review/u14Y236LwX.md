## Summary
OpenWaves introduces a large-scale dataset of 16 million frequency-domain wave simulations for ultrasound computed tomography (USCT), featuring anatomically realistic breast phantoms (VICTRE-based, four density categories) and real USCT system parameters (220mm ring, 256 transducers, 300‑650 kHz). It benchmarks five forward neural operators (UNet, FNO, BFNO, AFNO, MgNO) and three inverse methods (DeepONet, InversionNet, NIO), plus a gradient-based optimization using learned forward surrogates. The dataset itself is the primary contribution — it fills a genuine gap between oversimplified PDE benchmarks and real biomedical imaging complexity.

## Strengths
- **Anatomically realistic phantoms replace oversimplified media.** Unlike OpenFWI (layered structures) and WaveBench (Gaussian random fields, MNIST), OpenWaves uses the FDA VICTRE tool to generate 8,000 breast phantoms across four density categories (HET, FIB, FAT, EXD) with realistic tissue morphology (Section 3.2.2, Figure 1b).
- **Large-scale simulations at clinically relevant USCT parameters.** The dataset provides 16M simulations at 8 frequencies (300‑650 kHz) on a 220 mm ring with 256 transducers, producing ROIs of 50–100 wavenumbers — substantially larger than prior wave-equation benchmarks (<40 wavenumbers) (Table 1, Section 3.2.1).
- **Unified evaluation framework with multiple baselines.** The paper provides a common PyTorch environment benchmarking five forward neural operators and three inverse models under consistent settings, lowering the barrier for future work (Section 2.2, Tables 2–4).
- **Systematic analysis of scaling and generalization.** Experiments varying dataset size (4,000–8,000 phantoms), source locations (8–64), and frequency expose different scaling behaviors across architectures, and OOD experiments show forward operators generalize better across breast types than inverse models (Figures 4a–c, Table 5, Section 4.3).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **Source mismatch between forward training and inverse optimization (Section 4.2, Section 4.1).** Forward models (FNOs) are trained on wavefields from **64 source locations** (Section 4.1), yet the gradient-based optimization baseline (Eq. 4) applies them to measurements involving **all 256 transducer positions** as sources. The paper does not acknowledge or discuss this discrepancy in Section 4.2. While Section 4.3 provides evidence that forward models generalize to unseen source locations (Figure 4c), the paper never explicitly links this analysis to the optimization setup, nor does it quantify the generalization error for the 192 held-out source positions within the optimization context. This leaves the central claim that "the neural operator-based optimization approach revealed significantly higher resolution than all direct inversion methods" (line 199) partially unsupported. The concern is not fatal (generalization evidence exists), but it must be addressed — either by retraining forward models on all 256 sources, restricting the optimization to the 64 training sources, or providing explicit quantification of the generalization error.

### Minor
- **Train/test split protocol not specified (Section 3.2, Section 4.1).** The paper never states whether the split is performed at the *phantom level* (ensuring no leakage) or *measurement level* (which would allow the same phantom to appear in both training and test sets). Given that each phantom generates 2,048 simulations, a measurement-level split would artificially inflate performance. The scaling experiment (Section 4.3.2) discusses "number of training phantoms," which strongly implies phantom-level splitting for that experiment, but this is not explicitly confirmed for the main benchmark results. This is a basic reproducibility detail that must be clarified.
- **Hyperparameter details and variance are missing.** The paper reports single point estimates for all metrics without confidence intervals or standard deviations. No hyperparameter search is described (learning rate, batch size, number of Fourier modes, optimizer settings, epochs). The Limitations section (line 247) acknowledges this ("without extensively exploring the influence of hyperparameters"), but the absence of any tuning protocol or variance reporting means the performance rankings (e.g., MgNO > FNO > UNet) cannot be assessed for statistical significance.
- **Only 3 of 8 frequencies and 64 of 256 sources are used in benchmarks (Section 4.1).** The choice of a 3-frequency subset (300, 400, 500 kHz) and 64-source subset is not justified. While computational constraints may explain it, the paper does not argue why this subset is representative, limiting the completeness of the benchmark.
- **Optimization baseline underspecified (Section 4.2).** The gradient-based optimization is described in a single sentence with no details on optimizer, learning rate, number of iterations, convergence criteria, or initialization strategy. This makes the results difficult to reproduce.
- **OOD evaluation limited to NIO for inverse (Table 5).** Table 5 shows OOD generalization for NIO only, not for DeepONet, InversionNet, or the optimization baseline. The paper mentions forward OOD results qualitatively but does not present them with the same rigor, making the claim that "forward models generalize better than inverse models" (Section 4.3.3) less well-supported than it should be.
- **CBS solver accuracy not discussed.** The paper does not report convergence tolerance or accuracy of the CBS solver used to generate ground-truth wavefields. If the solver is not fully converged, the "ground truth" contains numerical noise that sets an error floor for all learned models.

## Suggestions
1. **Explicitly address the source mismatch in Section 4.2.** Either retrain forward models on all 256 source locations, restrict the optimization to the same 64 sources used in forward training, or provide per-source-position error metrics quantifying how much the 192 unseen sources degrade the optimization objective. Without this, the optimization-vs-direct-inversion comparison is not cleanly interpretable.
2. **Specify the train/test split protocol.** State clearly whether the split is by phantom or by measurement, and report the number of phantoms in each split. Add this to Section 3.2 or as a footnote to the experimental setup.
3. **Add variance reporting.** Run each baseline with at least 3 random seeds and report mean ± std for all metrics. If this is computationally prohibitive, state the limitation explicitly with justification.
4. **Provide a hyperparameter table** listing learning rate, batch size, optimizer, scheduler, number of epochs, Fourier modes, number of layers, and other architectural choices for each baseline. 5. **Include the optimization baseline details** (optimizer, learning rate, iterations, convergence criteria) in Section 4.2.
6. **Extend OOD analysis** to at least one additional inverse baseline (e.g., InversionNet) to support the claim that inverse models memorize while forward models learn physics.

These issues are addressable in a revision.
