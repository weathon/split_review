## Summary

VEditBench introduces a standardized, open-source benchmark for text-guided video editing (TGVE) comprising 420 real-world videos (300 short 2-4s, 120 long 10-40s), six editing tasks (object insertion, removal, swap, scene replacement, motion change, style translation), and a nine-dimensional evaluation framework spanning semantic fidelity and visual quality. The paper evaluates ten state-of-the-art TGVE models, providing comparative analysis across tasks and metrics.

## Strengths

- **Substantially larger and more diverse dataset than prior TGVE benchmarks**: VEditBench contains 420 videos versus 76 in LOVEU-TGVE-2023 and 100 in BalanceCC (Section 2.2). The inclusion of 120 long videos (10-40 seconds) directly addresses a gap as prior benchmarks focus nearly exclusively on short clips. This is the paper's clearest contribution.

- **Expanded task coverage with six distinct editing tasks**: Prior benchmarks cover 3-4 edit types; VEditBench defines object addition, removal, swap, scene replacement, motion change, and style translation (Section 3.2). The radar charts (Figure 5) show that model performance varies significantly across tasks, confirming that broader task coverage enables more informative comparisons.

- **Multi-dimensional evaluation framework spanning nine metrics**: The framework measures text alignment (CLIP, ViCLIP), video alignment (SSIM, motion similarity via CoTracker+Hungarian), spatial quality (Q-Align aesthetics), temporal quality (motion smoothness, Content-Debiased FVD), and spatio-temporal quality (Q-Align video quality) (Section 4). This reveals trade-offs invisible in single-metric evaluations — e.g., RAVE scores high on alignment but low on visual quality (Table 2, Figure 5).

- **Rigorous data curation pipeline**: Videos are sourced from YouTube and Videvo, filtered via GPT-4o for keyword diversity, then manually checked for quality (blurry, shaking, ghosting). Captions and edit prompts are generated by GPT-4o and manually reviewed/revised to correct hallucinations and missing objects (Sections 3.1-3.2). This dual human-AI verification is a methodological strength.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **SSIM is applied uniformly across tasks where structural change is the intended edit (object removal, scene replacement, object swap)**: The paper states SSIM measures "preserving the integrity of the original content" and "helps identify any significant alterations that may compromise the original message" (Section 4.1, lines 106-107). However, for object removal ("remove the cat") or scene replacement, a perfect edit *intentionally and necessarily* alters scene structure. A flawless edit would receive a *low* SSIM score because the removed object's pixels are gone. The paper provides no per-task breakdown of SSIM scores or caveat that this metric is only meaningful for a subset of tasks (style translation, object addition). Since results are reported as aggregates (Table 2, 3), the scores for structure-changing tasks are systematically depressed, contaminating cross-model comparisons. This is not a fatal flaw — it can be addressed by reporting per-task metrics and excluding or reweighting SSIM for tasks where structural change is intended — but it is a significant methodological gap in the current version.

- **Motion similarity metric treats higher trajectory preservation as uniformly desirable, directly contradicting the motion change task**: The motion similarity score computes trajectory alignment between source and edited video and states "A higher score reflects greater similarity" (Section 4.1, line 120). For the *motion change* task (e.g., "tilt the camera downwards"), the model is supposed to *change* the motion. Maximizing trajectory similarity penalizes correct edits. For object removal and object swap, whether motion should be preserved is also ambiguous. The metric is applied identically across all tasks (aggregated in Tables 2, 3 and radar charts in Figure 5), making it impossible to tell whether a high score reflects genuine motion preservation or a failure to alter motion where required. This undermines the quantitative comparisons along this dimension.

### Minor

- **No human validation study correlating automatic metrics with human judgments of editing quality**: The paper proposes a nine-metric evaluation framework for TGVE but provides zero evidence that these metrics correlate with human perception of editing quality. While the individual metrics (CLIP, Q-Align, FVD) are established in adjacent contexts (text-to-image alignment, image quality assessment, video generation), their combined application to TGVE introduces unique confounds — a frame with high visual quality may still be a bad edit if it introduces semantic incoherence, and a low CLIP score may result from prompt ambiguity rather than poor editing. This does not invalidate the benchmark (many benchmark papers lack human studies), but it limits the strength of the paper's claim that VEditBench provides "standardized" evaluation that the community should adopt.

- **No direct comparison with existing benchmarks (LOVEU-TGVE-2023, BalanceCC) on common subsets to demonstrate added value**: The paper argues that existing benchmarks are inadequate but does not run the same models on prior benchmarks to show whether VEditBench yields different rankings or reveals failure modes that previous benchmarks missed (Section 2.2). Such a comparison would substantially strengthen the case that VEditBench changes how the field evaluates models.

- **Experimental settings lack detailed reproducibility specifications**: The "Settings" paragraph (Section 5) describes which models are evaluated on which subsets but provides no hyperparameters, inference steps, seeds, or configuration details. This limits reproducibility and makes it difficult for other researchers to replicate or build upon the reported results.

- **Naming inconsistency**: The task is called "object insertion" in the abstract and introduction (line 4, line 24) but "Object Addition" in Section 3.2 (line 83). These refer to the same task but use inconsistent terminology.

### Trivial

- The insights in Section 6 ("No single model dominates," "performance varies across tasks," "trade-off between fidelity and quality") are observations that any benchmark would enable; they are not surprising findings unique to VEditBench. This does not detract from the benchmark's utility — the benchmark still enables these observations — but the paper's framing slightly overstates the novelty of these conclusions.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A correlation analysis comparing VEditBench results on a common subset against LOVEU-TGVE-2023 or BalanceCC would demonstrate the benchmark's added value.
- Per-task reporting of all metrics (especially SSIM and motion similarity) with discussion of which metrics are meaningful for which tasks would resolve the major weaknesses identified above.
- A small-scale human evaluation study (even 50-100 edits rated by 3-5 annotators) would strengthen confidence in the metric suite.
- Failure case analysis showing where metrics give counterintuitive scores would help users interpret results.

## Novel Insights

The most notable observation from the reviews is that the evaluation framework, while ambitious in scope, applies metrics designed to measure *preservation* (SSIM for structure, motion similarity for trajectories) to tasks where the edit's purpose is to *change* those exact properties. This creates a systematic confound in the aggregated results that is not acknowledged in the paper. The reviews surface this as a design tension that the authors did not address — specifically, that a benchmark aiming to be "holistic" must condition its metrics on task type, not apply them uniformly. This insight goes beyond the paper's own discussion and points to a broader design principle for TGVE evaluation.

## Suggestions

1. **Report all metrics per-task** rather than (or in addition to) aggregates. This would immediately resolve the SSIM and motion similarity concerns by letting readers see which metrics are meaningful for which tasks.
2. **Exclude or reinterpret SSIM for object removal, object swap, and scene replacement tasks**, where structural change is intended. Alternatively, compute SSIM only on the unedited regions of the frame (e.g., background for object removal).
3. **Invert or reweight the motion similarity metric for the motion change task** so that lower trajectory alignment with the source is treated as correct when the prompt explicitly changes motion.
4. **Add a small human evaluation study** correlating automatic metrics with human ratings on a subset (50-100 edits). This would substantially strengthen the benchmark's credibility.
5. **Add a reproducibility appendix** with hyperparameters, inference steps, seeds, and model configurations for all ten evaluated models.
6. **Fix the naming inconsistency** ("object insertion" vs. "object addition").

## Score and Decision

The paper's dataset contribution is solid — 420 videos spanning six categories with two duration buckets is a meaningful expansion over existing benchmarks. However, the evaluation framework, which is a core part of the claimed contribution, has two significant flaws (SSIM applied to structure-changing tasks, motion similarity applied without task conditioning). These affect the interpretability of the aggregated results and weaken the paper's central claim of providing a "standardized" benchmark. The issues are fixable with per-task reporting and appropriate caveats, but in its current form the paper does not adequately address them. I view this as a borderline paper with a useful dataset contribution held back by a flawed evaluation design.
