## Summary

The paper introduces DeepFakeVox-HQ, a large-scale deepfake audio dataset (1.3M samples, 270K high-quality deepfakes from 14 sources), and proposes F-SAT (Frequency-Selective Adversarial Training) that targets high-frequency components during adversarial training to improve robustness. It also adapts RandAugment for audio augmentation. The empirical results show strong performance across multiple benchmarks.

## Strengths

- **Large, diverse, and up-to-date dataset:** DeepFakeVox-HQ is substantially larger (1.3M samples, 40 vocoders, both TTS and VC) and more diverse than existing public datasets like ASVspoof2019 or WaveFake (Table 1). The inclusion of 270K high-quality deepfakes from modern TTS/VC models and social-media samples is a genuine resource for the community.

- **Empirically effective frequency-selective adversarial training:** F-SAT delivers substantial robustness gains on frequency-domain attacks (fake audio accuracy: 17.9% → 92.8% in Table 3) and time-domain attacks (6.5% → 87.0%). The ablation in Table 4 systematically explores different frequency ranges, showing that 4–8 kHz targeting is most effective.

- **RandAugment for audio improves corruption robustness:** The adaptation of RandAugment independently lifts corrupted-data fake accuracy from 55.6% to 85.6% (Table 3). Combined with F-SAT, the system achieves a 15.3 pp average improvement across 24 corruption types over baselines.

- **Comprehensive evaluation across multiple benchmarks:** Results on DeepFakeVox-HQ test set, ASVspoof2019, and WaveFake (Table 2) demonstrate generalization beyond in-distribution data. Figure 2 (detailed per-model breakdown) shows significant gains on out-of-distribution sources.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The paper's central motivation is contradicted by its own high-pass filter experiment (Figure 2).**  
   The paper repeatedly claims that detection models "depend on high-frequency features" (abstract, lines 36–37, conclusion). However, Figure 2 applies a high-pass filter (removing low frequencies), and accuracy drops sharply when only frequencies above 4 kHz (real) or 6 kHz (fake) are retained. This means the model **needs** frequencies **below** 4–6 kHz — i.e., low-to-mid frequencies — to function well. The experiment therefore shows the opposite of what the paper argues. While other evidence in the paper (Figure 7b showing that high-frequency attacks degrade performance most) independently supports the importance of high-frequency vulnerability, the paper's core narrative claim about model feature reliance is factually wrong. This is a conceptual error in interpreting the paper's own data that runs through the abstract, introduction, and conclusion.

2. **Overstated novelty claims.**  
   The conclusion states "These results pioneer robust training for detecting fake audio for the first time" (line 388). This is inaccurate — the paper's own related work section cites adversarial training for ASR and speaker verification (line 61), and prior work on adversarial defenses for anti-spoofing exists. The claim should be removed or substantially qualified.

3. **Unclear attribution of component contributions in headline numbers.**  
   The abstract claims "robust training further improves accuracy by 7.7% on clean samples," but Table 3 shows that RawNet3+RandAug achieves 97.0% fake accuracy while adding F-SAT only brings it to 98.4% — the RandAugment data augmentation accounts for nearly all of the clean-data gain. Similarly, the 29.3% figure on "corrupted and attacked samples" lumps together corruption robustness (driven by RandAug) and attack robustness (driven by F-SAT) without distinguishing their contributions. This conflates the effects of two different components.

### Minor

1. **Missing attack parameters for evaluation results.**  
   The paper describes adversarial attacks at a high level (time-domain, frequency-domain) but does not specify the attack algorithm (e.g., PGD, FGSM), number of iterations, step size, perturbation budget (ε), or norm constraint used to produce the results in Table 3, Table 4, and Figure 8. The hyperparameter exploration (Figure 9) uses ε=0.01 on a 10% subset, but whether this matches the main evaluation is unclear. This hinders reproducibility and makes it impossible to gauge attack strength.

2. **Real vs. fake audio robustness tradeoff not sufficiently analyzed.**  
   Table 4 shows that narrowing the frequency range for F-SAT (e.g., 4–8 kHz) improves fake audio robustness but degrades real audio robustness on time-domain attacks (e.g., real attacked time accuracy: 97.8% at 0–8 kHz → 90.2% at 4–8 kHz). The paper notes this asymmetry (line 335) but does not discuss its practical implications or justify why prioritizing fake audio robustness over real audio robustness is acceptable in deployment scenarios.

3. **RandAugment implementation details are underspecified.**  
   The paper states that N transformations are applied with uniform probability and a probability p controls application (line 204), but does not specify what transformations are in the set, their magnitude ranges, the value of N, or the value of p. This prevents reproduction.

4. **The 33% dataset improvement claim is not directly shown in any table.**  
   The abstract claims "our training dataset boosts baseline model performance (without robust training) by 33%." This is mentioned in the text (line 41) and supported qualitatively by Figure 4, but no table directly compares a baseline trained on a prior dataset vs. on DeepFakeVox-HQ with controlled architecture. The exact calculation behind 33% is unclear.

## Suggestions

1. **Reinterpret the high-pass filter experiment honestly.** The Figure 2 result actually shows that models rely on frequencies *below* 4–6 kHz, and that fake audio features extend slightly higher (6 kHz) than real audio features (4 kHz). Coupled with the attack analysis (Figure 7b showing high-frequency attacks are most damaging), the paper could motivate F-SAT as: "models use features across a broad frequency range, but the high-frequency components they do incorporate are disproportionately vulnerable to attack." This framing is internally consistent and still justifies frequency-selective defense.

2. **Disentangle the contributions in the abstract.** Clearly separate what RandAugment contributes (corruption robustness, ~12 pp of the clean-data gain) from what F-SAT contributes (attack robustness, ~21–75 pp gains on attacked data depending on the metric). The 29.3% figure should be decomposed.

3. **Specify evaluation attack parameters explicitly.** State the attack algorithm, number of steps, step size, norm type, and ε value used for each evaluation table (Tables 3, 4 and Figure 8).

4. **Tone down the novelty claims.** Remove "pioneer...for the first time" and instead characterize F-SAT as a novel application of frequency-selective adversarial training to the deepfake audio detection problem.
