## Summary

This paper reframes adversarial graph structure attacks as an out-of-distribution (OOD) detection problem at the edge level. It proposes GOOD-AT, which trains an ensemble of MLP detectors on edges generated by PGD attacks (labeled as OOD) versus original clean edges (labeled as in-distribution), then uses the ensemble to prune detected OOD edges at inference before feeding the graph to a fixed GCN classifier. For poisoning attacks, the paper adopts an existing self-training strategy. Evaluated on the adversarial unit test of Mujkanovic et al. (2022) on Cora and Citeseer, GOOD-AT achieves RAUC values close to the theoretical upper bound for evasion attacks and substantially outperforms seven prior defenses.

## Strengths

- **Novel conceptual framing.** Modeling adversarial edges as OOD samples and learning a detector directly (rather than relying on hand-crafted properties like homophily or spectral smoothness) is a fresh and principled perspective. The paper correctly identifies that prior property-based defenses are vulnerable precisely because they encode an incomplete OOD model with a fixed inductive bias (lines 10, 25, 70).

- **Strong empirical results on the standard unit test.** GOOD-AT achieves RAUC close to the upper bound (0.61 on Cora) for evasion attacks and 3–5× higher RAUC than competitive defenses for poisoning attacks (Section 7.2, Figure 3). The method also shows strong cross-defense transferability: it achieves the best RAUC against six of the seven adaptive attacks in the transferability study (Figure 4).

- **Clever use of OOD exposure via attack generation.** The paper notes that graph adversarial attacks can *generate* abundant OOD training samples by repeatedly attacking the clean graph, turning a typical challenge for OOD detection (lack of OOD data) into an advantage (lines 98–99). This is a practical and well-motivated insight.

- **Systematic ablation of the ensemble size.** Table 1 provides a clear, evidence-based guideline showing performance saturates around 15 detectors, which is useful for practitioners.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The two adaptive attacks described against GOOD-AT are never quantitatively evaluated.** Section 6 describes two strategies for adaptive evasion attacks against GOOD-AT (generating perturbations the detectors cannot identify, and incorporating the detector's output as a regularization term) and claims "both strategies will lead to a notable decrease in attack effectiveness." However, the experiments section presents no accuracy numbers, RAUC values, or any quantitative results for these attacks. The paper's central claim — that GOOD-AT withstands adaptive attacks — is then supported only by the unit test from Mujkanovic et al. (2022), whose seven attacks were designed to circumvent *other* defense categories (Jaccard-based, spectral, structure-learning, etc.), not a learned OOD detector. While performing well on the unit test is meaningful and not "invalid," it does not substitute for evaluating against attacks designed to break GOOD-AT's specific mechanism. This gap is the single most significant weakness: the paper claims to have developed adaptive attacks and identified a trade-off, but does not show the data. Given that the abstract and introduction advertise "extensive experiments over 25,000 perturbed graphs" and "maintain good robustness against both adaptive and non-adaptive attacks," the omission is consequential.

- **The trade-off hypothesis (Hypothesis 1) is stated but not empirically supported with quantitative data.** Section 6 claims that adaptive attacks against OOD-based defenses inherently reduce attack effectiveness (e.g., becoming ineffective on vanilla GCN), and this is presented as a key insight. The paper acknowledges in Section 8 that it does not rigorously prove Hypothesis 1, but it also does not provide *any* experimental measurements of the claimed trade-off — no attack success rates, no accuracy degradation on vanilla GCN, no RAUC comparison between adaptive and non-adaptive variants. The trade-off therefore remains speculative.

### Minor

- **The OOD detector is trained exclusively on PGD-generated edges** (lines 82, 90), yet the evaluation includes Metattack and seven adaptive attacks. While the empirical results (Figure 3) suggest the detector generalizes to these other attack types, the paper does not analyze this explicitly — e.g., by training on multiple attack types and testing on held-out ones, or by reporting per-attack detection performance. A reviewer cannot assess whether GOOD-AT's robustness on non-PGD attacks is due to successful OOD detection or because those attacks happen to be weaker for other reasons.

- **Only two datasets (Cora, Citeseer)** are used for evaluation (line 141). Both are small, homophilic citation networks. Generalizability to larger graphs (e.g., PubMed, OGB-Arxiv, ogbn-products) or heterophilic graphs is untested. This limits the scope of the robustness claims.

- **The fixed GCN classifier is never adapted to the filtered graph.** The GCN is trained once on the clean graph and frozen (line 82). At inference, the OOD detector modifies the graph, potentially substantially. The paper does not compare against a variant that retrains or fine-tunes the classifier on the filtered graph, nor does it analyze whether performance degradation could arise from this mismatch.

- **No false positive rate or precision of the OOD detector is reported.** The ensemble uses a strict "any detector flags it" rule (line 103–106), which could remove many clean edges. Without false positive analysis, the reader cannot assess whether the defense is overly aggressive or whether clean accuracy is being sacrificed for robustness.

- **The theoretical argument against adversarial training for graphs** (Section 4, "Motivation: Failure of Traditional Adversarial training") is presented in a few sentences without formal development or supporting experiments. The claimed incorrect mapping is plausible but neither rigorously argued nor empirically validated.

## Suggestions

1. **Implement and quantitatively evaluate the two adaptive attacks described in Section 6.** Report accuracy vs. perturbation rate curves, RAUC, and the effectiveness of the same attacks against a vanilla GCN to substantiate the claimed trade-off. This is the single most impactful addition.
2. **Add a per-attack breakdown** of GOOD-AT's performance (e.g., a table showing RAUC against PGD, Metattack, and each of the seven adaptive attacks separately), to show the detector's generalization beyond its PGD-only training distribution.
3. **Report the detector's false positive rate** (fraction of clean edges incorrectly removed) on clean graphs to help assess the cost of the conservative ensemble rule.
4. **Add at least one larger dataset** (e.g., PubMed or a subset of OGB-Arxiv) and ideally one heterophilic graph to test the method's generality.

## Score and Decision

The paper introduces a genuinely novel perspective (OOD detection for graph adversarial edges) and achieves strong results on the standard unit test benchmark. However, the evaluation has a significant gap: the adaptive attacks that the paper itself proposes against GOOD-AT are described but never quantitatively evaluated, leaving the central claim of adaptive robustness incompletely supported. The trade-off hypothesis is presented as a key insight but lacks empirical backing. These issues are addressable but consequential as stated. The conceptual contribution is real; the evidential support for adaptive robustness is not yet commensurate with the paper's claims.
