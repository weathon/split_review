## Summary

The paper proposes ProFusion, a regularization-free framework for customized text-to-image generation. It consists of PromptNet (an encoder that maps an input face image into a word embedding $S^*$) and Fusion Sampling (a two-stage inference procedure that combines information from $S^*$ and arbitrary text $C$ without requiring regularization in the training loss). The key idea is to remove the $L_2$ regularization on $S^*$ used by prior work (which suppresses fine-grained details) and instead handle over-fitting during sampling through a fusion stage (iteratively injecting both conditions into the latent) followed by a refinement stage. Experiments on FFHQ show quantitative improvements over Textual Inversion, DreamBooth, and E4T on most CLIP and face-recognition similarity metrics, with only 30 seconds of fine-tuning per test image.

## Strengths

- **Conceptually interesting regularization-free design.** The paper identifies a real trade-off (regularization preserves text alignment but suppresses fine-grained details, Fig. 3) and proposes a genuine alternative: remove regularization from the training loss and handle over-fitting at inference through a modified sampling procedure. This is a principled departure from the predominant regularization-based paradigm.

- **Strong quantitative results across multiple metrics.** ProFusion achieves the highest image–prompt similarity on all 9 CLIP models (Table 1) and the highest identity similarity on 7 out of 8 face recognition models (Table 2), despite scoring lower on Facenet512 (0.597 vs. E4T's 0.621). The pattern is consistent and broad.

- **Very efficient fine-tuning.** The paper states that PromptNet + attention layers fine-tune for 50 steps in ~30 seconds on a single GPU. While the PromptNet pre-training (80k iterations on 8 A100s) is a separate cost, the per-subject fine-tuning cost is genuinely low and clearly reported.

- **Human evaluation confirms user preference.** An AMT pairwise preference study shows ProFusion preferred over all four baselines, corroborating the quantitative metrics with perceptual judgments.

- **Ablation of the two sampling stages.** Removing either the fusion stage or the refinement stage degrades results (Fig. 12/ablation), confirming that both components contribute meaningfully.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Uncontrolled baseline comparison for quantitative results.** The paper states that *qualitative* results are "directly taken from" E4T (line 247). For quantitative evaluations (Tables 1–2), the paper says "We also evaluate our methods and baseline methods quantitatively" but never specifies how baseline images were obtained — whether they were generated by re-running the baselines under matched conditions (same backbone SD2, same fine-tuning steps, same prompts) or taken from prior publications. Without this clarity, the comparison is ambiguous. The gains are often small (e.g., +0.016 on CLIP ViT-B/32, +0.005 on ArcFace) and on Facenet512 ProFusion underperforms E4T (0.597 vs. 0.621), making controlled re-evaluation essential to establish that the improvements are real.

2. **Critical hyperparameters undisclosed and unablated.** The fusion stage introduces $\gamma$ (a scaling factor on $S^*$ in Algorithm 1, required with $0\leq\gamma\leq1$) and the independent-fusion formula (Eq. 5) introduces separate guidance scales $\omega_1,\omega_2$. The paper specifies neither the values used for these parameters nor any sensitivity analysis. Since $\gamma$ directly controls how much $S^*$ influences the fusion stage, its absence is a significant reproducibility gap — a reader cannot replicate the method without guessing this value.

3. **Evaluation limited to a single domain (faces).** All experiments use FFHQ and face-recognition similarity metrics. The paper does not demonstrate generalization to objects, scenes, or artistic styles. While this is partially within the scope of the E4T line of work, the method's own claims (e.g., "novel concept") are not domain-restricted, and the absence of any non-face evaluation limits the strength of the contribution.

4. **No variance or statistical significance reported.** All quantitative results in Tables 1–2 are reported as point estimates with no standard deviations, confidence intervals, or hypothesis tests. The human evaluation (Fig. 9) is shown as preference-rate bar charts without error bars or sample sizes. This makes it impossible to assess whether the observed differences are meaningful or within the noise of the evaluation.

### Minor

5. **Algorithm 1 contains a forward–backward sampling step whose practical role is unclear.** Lines 125–127 sample $\tilde{x}_{t-1}\sim q(\tilde{x}_{t-1}|\tilde{x}_t,\tilde{x}_0)$ and then immediately sample $\tilde{x}_t\sim q(\tilde{x}_t|\tilde{x}_{t-1},\tilde{x}_0)$, effectively returning to the same timestep. Remark 2 shows that for $\sigma_t=\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}$ this simplifies to a direct update on $\tilde{x}_t$ that bypasses the intermediate step. The paper never states which $\sigma_t$ value is used in practice, so it is unclear whether the implementation follows the full loop or the simplified equation. This ambiguity is non-fatal (Remark 2 clarifies the special case) but hampers exact reproducibility.

6. **Inference-time overhead unquantified.** The paper acknowledges that Fusion Sampling increases inference time (Discussion, line 318–319) but provides no concrete numbers (seconds per image, relative slowdown vs. standard classifier-free sampling). The practical cost of the proposed method is therefore unknown.

7. **"Regularization-free" framing is slightly overstated.** The paper removes regularization from the training loss but replaces it with inference-time knobs ($\gamma$, $\omega_1$, $\omega_2$) that control the same trade-off between detail preservation and text adherence. The method does not eliminate the trade-off; it moves it from training to inference. This does not invalidate the contribution but warrants more precise framing.

## Suggestions

1. **Re-run or clearly document how baseline quantitative results were obtained.** State explicitly whether Textual Inversion, DreamBooth, and E4T were re-run under matched conditions (same SD2 backbone, same 50 steps, same prompts) or whether numbers were taken from prior papers. If re-run, report mean and standard deviation over 3–5 seeds.

2. **Specify $\gamma$ and the $\omega_1,\omega_2$ values used in experiments, and add a sensitivity analysis** showing generations across a grid of reasonable values. At minimum, state the fixed values used.

3. **Add statistical confidence** (standard deviations or confidence intervals) to Tables 1–2 and error bars to the human evaluation figure. Report the number of workers and comparisons in the human study.

4. **Clarify the algorithm/implementation alignment.** State whether $\sigma_t$ is set to $\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}$ (the Remark 2 special case) or another value, and whether the implementation uses the full forward–backward loop or the direct update.

5. **Qualify the domain scope** in the abstract or conclusion (e.g., "on human face images") or add one non-face experiment (e.g., DreamBooth's object dataset) to demonstrate generality.

## Score and Decision

The paper proposes a genuinely interesting idea — moving the regularization burden from training to inference — and backs it with a broad set of metrics on which it performs well. However, the experimental evaluation has several significant gaps: uncontrolled baseline comparison, undisclosed critical hyperparameters ($\gamma$, $\omega_1$, $\omega_2$), no variance reporting, and evaluation only on faces. These are addressable but non-trivial. The core contribution is solid enough that the paper warrants a borderline decision; with proper baseline verification, hyperparameter disclosure, and broader evaluation it could be strong.
