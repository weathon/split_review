## Summary

This paper proposes ACC-Debate, a framework that jointly trains a two-agent team (an actor providing answers and a critic providing feedback) for collaborative multi-agent debate. The authors introduce a guided-debate data generation scheme that steers conversations toward/away from correct answers to create high-quality preference data, then uses DPO and an iterative best-response optimization to train both agents. Experiments across three base model families (2B–8B) and five QA benchmarks show substantial improvements over existing debate methods (SoM, Persona, SFT, DebateTune, DebateGPT).

## Strengths

- **Novel joint training framework for debate teams.** The paper is the first to propose training both agents jointly for collaborative debate via an alternating optimization, moving beyond the standard paradigm of treating collaboration as an emergent zero-shot behavior. The Stackelberg-game framing (Section 4.1) provides a principled foundation.

- **Guided-debate trajectory generation is a practical contribution.** The off-policy scheme (Algorithm 1) efficiently creates positive/negative preference pairs by steering debates toward and away from the correct answer, solving the cold-start problem where base models perform too poorly to generate useful trajectories. The threshold-based filtering ensures training signal quality.

- **Strong and consistent empirical results.** ACC-Debate and ACC-Debate+ outperform all baselines across nearly all 15 model×dataset combinations (Table 1). Gains are often substantial — e.g., Mistral BBH from 0.48 (DebateGPT) to 0.601 (ACC-Debate+), Llama-3 BoolQ from 0.815 to 0.894. Results include 95% confidence intervals and show consistency across model families.

- **Ablation isolating actor and critic contributions.** Table 3 demonstrates that a trained actor paired with an untrained critic already outperforms SFT and DebateGPT in most cases, while pairing both trained agents yields further gains. This partially disentangles the contributions of the two components.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

1. **Guided-debate uses oracle knowledge of the correct answer during training, and the paper lacks a control experiment to assess the impact.** Algorithm 1 and Section 4.3 show that guided trajectories are generated by placing the correct (or wrong) answer directly in the prompt to steer the conversation. The model is then trained via DPO to prefer the "guided-toward-correct" responses over natural or "guided-away" responses. At inference, the answer is absent. The concern is that the trained model may learn superficial patterns specific to seeing the answer in the prompt (e.g., confidence markers, stylistic cues) rather than generalizable collaborative skills. The paper does not include a control (e.g., training on trajectories guided by *random* answers, or comparing against models trained solely on natural trajectories with reward-based selection) to rule this out. Without such a control, it is difficult to attribute the observed gains entirely to the training framework rather than to the guided data generation itself. This concern is partially mitigated because (a) the baselines (SFT, DebateGPT) also train using the correct answer, so the comparison is not entirely asymmetric, and (b) the paper acknowledges this limitation (Section 6). Nonetheless, a control experiment is needed to strengthen the claim.

2. **Baseline comparisons do not fully isolate the benefit of joint alternating training.** The comparison set mixes inference-only methods (SoM, Persona) with training methods (SFT, DebateTune, DebateGPT). However, the paper does not include ablations that control for the key variables changed simultaneously: (a) two independently SFTed models (actor and critic) trained on the same guided-debate data *without* alternating optimization, or (b) a single model fine-tuned on both actor and critic roles. Without these, the improvement cannot be definitively attributed to the *joint alternating* training rather than to (i) the quality of the guided-debate data or (ii) the benefit of having two specialized models. The Actor/Critic ablation (Table 3) partially addresses this by showing each trained component contributes, but it does not control for data quality.

3. **The evaluation does not fully disentangle improved standalone accuracy from improved collaborative refinement.** Figure 3 shows that ACC-Debate already has higher accuracy at round 0 (before any debate) compared to baselines. While the paper reports "improvement from debate" (Figure 2) as a percentage relative to round-0 accuracy, and ACC-Debate+ shows the highest relative improvement, this aggregate metric does not cleanly separate the contribution of better initial answers from collaborative gains. The "Actor only" ablation (Table 3) shows that a trained actor with an untrained critic often outperforms full debate baselines, suggesting the actor training itself is effective. An experiment comparing ACC-Debate against an ensemble of two independently trained models (or a single equally strong model) would help determine whether the debate process yields additional improvement beyond stronger individual models.

### Minor

- **The threshold ε is never specified or ablated.** The data generation uses ε to filter weak examples (Algorithm 1, Eq. 4), but its value is not reported, and no sensitivity analysis is provided. This affects reproducibility and the ability to assess how filtering intensity impacts results.

- **ACC-Debate+ performance degradation is noted but not analyzed.** ACC-Debate+ (two training rounds) sometimes underperforms ACC-Debate (one round) by 1–2 points (e.g., Llama-3 BBH: 0.593 vs. 0.574; Gemma-2 many tasks). The paper mentions this but provides no analysis of why performance degrades or whether it is due to optimization instability, overfitting, or distribution shift.

- **The "untrained" partner condition is underspecified.** In Table 3, the Actor and Critic columns pair a trained agent with an "untrained" partner. The paper does not explicitly state whether "untrained" means the original base model, a frozen copy, or something else. While it seems clear from context, precision would aid reproducibility.

- **Per-round accuracy is shown only for BoolQ (Figure 3).** Similar plots for other datasets would help assess whether ACC-Debate's advantage pattern is consistent across tasks or whether it benefits certain benchmarks more than others.

- **Qualitative analysis of critic behavior is anecdotal.** The paper provides one example (Figure 4) and a general claim that trained critics are "less agreeable." Quantification (e.g., average length of critic responses, counts of agreement/disagreement markers, automated metrics) would substantially strengthen this claim.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Comparing against stronger untrained debate protocols (e.g., Evince, Reconcile) that are cited in the related work would further substantiate the claim that training adds value over sophisticated prompting-based methods.
- A small-scale pilot on tasks without easy answer verification (e.g., summarization, open-ended generation) would test the generalizability of the guided-debate approach.
- Reporting the fraction of trajectories retained per dataset/model for the chosen ε would improve reproducibility.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a control experiment for oracle guidance:** Train a version of the method where guided trajectories are generated using *random* answers instead of the correct/wrong answer. If this variant performs significantly worse, the concern about information leakage is validated. If it performs similarly, the concern is mitigated.
2. **Add ablations to isolate the joint training benefit:** Compare ACC-Debate against (a) two independently SFTed models on guided-debate data (without alternating optimization) and (b) a single model fine-tuned on both actor and critic roles. This would clarify whether the alternating optimization is the source of the gains.
3. **Report and ablate ε:** Specify the threshold value used and show its impact on data retention rates and downstream accuracy.
4. **Provide per-round accuracy plots for all five datasets** (in the supplement if not in the main paper) and quantify critic disagreement behaviors across a held-out set.

## Score and Decision

The paper proposes a genuinely novel framework for training collaborative debate teams, supported by a clever data generation scheme and strong empirical results across diverse settings. The main weaknesses concern the need for additional controlled experiments to isolate the sources of improvement, rather than fundamental flaws in the method or results. These are addressable in a revision.
