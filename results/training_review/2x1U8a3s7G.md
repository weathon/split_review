## Summary

This paper introduces prompt diffusion, a method that trains a diffusion model in prompt space to generate sample-specific prompts from random noise conditioned on image features. The approach first overfits prompts per training sample using labels (obtaining "oracle" targets), then trains a diffusion transformer to reconstruct these targets from noisy versions conditioned on image features. At test time, it iteratively denoises random prompts conditioned on test image features. Evaluated on 5 prompt-learning baselines (textual, visual, and multi-modal) across 15 datasets in 3 generalization settings, the method yields consistent improvements of 1–3% in harmonic mean.

## Strengths

- **Novel application domain for diffusion models.** The paper is the first to apply diffusion models within the prompt space for prompt learning. Rather than generating images or text, it generates customized prompts. The ablation (Table 4) confirms that diffusion substantially outperforms alternatives (GAN, VAE, normalizing flows, MLP, Transformer) for this task, establishing that the specific choice of generative model matters.

- **Consistent plug-and-play gains across modalities.** Prompt diffusion is evaluated on 5 diverse baselines covering textual prompts (CoCoOp), visual prompts (VPT), and multi-modal prompts (MaPLe, PromptSRC, CoPrompt). Tables 1–3 show consistent harmonic mean improvements (up to ~2.9% for CoPrompt new-class accuracy) across all three settings — base-to-new, cross-dataset, and domain generalization — validating the modular design.

- **Robust generalization under distribution shifts.** The gains are not limited to base classes. New-class accuracy improves consistently across all 5 baselines (e.g., CoCoOp new: 71.69 → 74.97, VPT new: 70.03 → 72.63), and cross-dataset / domain generalization also show clear improvements. This distinguishes the method from simple memorization of training labels.

- **Efficient inference with ODE solver.** The AMED-Solver enables strong performance with only 5 function evaluations at test time, adding only 0.045 seconds per sample over CoCoOp (Figure 1). This makes the method practical despite the added generative model.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None. The reviewer-raised concern about label contamination in the per-sample overfitting step does not constitute a fatal flaw. The paper's empirical results — **improved new-class accuracy** across all baselines (e.g., CoCoOp new: 71.69 → 74.97, CoPrompt new: 77.10 → 79.97 from Tables 1) — directly contradict the concern that the method merely encodes training labels. If the oracle targets simply encoded label information, new-class accuracy would be expected to drop, not rise by 2–3%. The ablation (Table 4) further shows that per-sample overfitting with deterministic models yields marginal gains, and the substantial improvement comes from the stochastic diffusion process, which the paper does not fully explain but which is empirically validated.

### Major
None that threaten the core claims.

### Minor

- **Unexplained mechanism of stochastic superiority.** The ablation (Table 4) shows that replacing the diffusion model with a deterministic transformer yields essentially no improvement (H 75.88 vs. CoCoOp's 75.83), while the full diffusion model yields 78.02 — a 2.14% jump. The paper offers no theoretical or mechanistic explanation for why stochastic sampling helps. This leaves an open question about whether the benefit comes from the generative modeling of prompt distributions, the specific noise schedule, or other artifacts of the diffusion process. While the empirical result stands, the lack of analysis weakens the scientific contribution.

- **Missing training cost analysis.** The paper reports only inference overhead (0.045s). The per-sample overfitting step requires "iterative gradient descent" on every training sample independently — for a 16-shot × 500-class dataset, this is 8,000 individual gradient runs. Training the 12-layer GPT-2-style diffusion transformer also adds overhead. Without reporting training time, parameter counts, or FLOPs for the added module, the claimed "good trade-off" (abstract) between performance and efficiency is only partially substantiated.

- **No statistical significance.** Results are averaged over 3 seeds, but no confidence intervals, standard deviations, or statistical tests are reported. Given the modest 1–3% improvements, it is difficult to assess whether gains are robust or within noise range.

- **Per-sample overfitting hyperparameters under-specified.** The paper mentions "iterative gradient descent on Eq. (2)" and shows iterations up to 5 (Fig. 5), but the learning rate, optimizer, and exact stopping criterion for the per-sample overfitting step are not reported in the main paper.

- **Scope of "any-modality" claim.** The title and abstract claim "any-modality prompt learning" and "modality-agnostic." The experiments are exclusively on CLIP-based image classification (textual, visual, and multi-modal prompts, all for vision-language tasks). While the paper does test three types of prompts (text, visual, multi-modal), it does not demonstrate applicability to non-CLIP modalities such as pure audio, video, or text-only tasks. The claim is partially supported within the CLIP ecosystem but oversold in scope.

### Trivial

- The t-SNE visualization of generated prompts (Fig. 6, stable diffusion figure) showing class-separable clusters is unsurprising — discriminative prompts are expected to separate by class — and does not directly demonstrate "unique domain details" as claimed.

## Nice-to-Haves
