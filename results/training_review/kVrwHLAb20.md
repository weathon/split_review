## Summary

This paper formalizes the problem of RAG Dataset Inference (RAG-DI), where a data owner detects unauthorized inclusion of their dataset in a RAG system. It introduces FRAD, a synthetic benchmark dataset with controlled fact redundancy, and several baselines adapted from membership inference attacks. The main contribution is Ward, a proactive method that watermarks documents using LLM watermarks and detects unauthorized usage via a joint statistical test across RAG responses. Experiments show Ward achieves perfect accuracy across diverse settings while baselines fail under fact redundancy.

## Strengths

- **Rigorous formalization of a timely, underexplored problem.** The paper provides a clean problem definition for RAG-DI with explicit desiderata (monotonicity, statistical guarantees, robustness) that future work can build on. This addresses a genuine gap: no prior work studied dataset-level inference for RAG corpora.

- **Ward provides statistical guarantees that baselines fundamentally cannot match.** By combining LLM watermarks with a joint p-value test, Ward offers rigorous Type I error control, with p-values orders of magnitude below the decision boundary (e.g., max in-case p-value of \(2.15\times10^{-25}\) in the hard setting with Claude 3 Haiku, Table 1). All baselines lack any such guarantees.

- **Consistent 100% accuracy across challenging settings where baselines fail.** In the main experiment (Figure 2), Ward achieves perfect accuracy across all three LLMs (Claude 3 Haiku, GPT-3.5, Llama3.1-70b), both system prompts (naive and defended), and both easy/hard settings. Under fact redundancy — which causes all baselines to produce false positives/negatives — Ward remains unaffected.

- **Thorough robustness evaluation.** The paper tests Ward against a defended system prompt designed to prevent repetition of context, and against MemFree decoding (n-gram blocking). Ward maintains 100% accuracy in both cases. The imperfect-retrieval experiment using real embeddings (93.6% retrieval success) confirms the method works beyond the idealized setup.

- **FRAD dataset is thoughtfully designed.** While synthetic, FRAD isolates the key hardness factor (fact redundancy) that makes RAG-DI difficult, using fictional RepliQA articles (provably not in LLM training data) with multiple LLM authors per group. This enables a clear demonstration of why fact redundancy breaks existing methods.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Entire evaluation is on a synthetic, LLM-generated corpus with no real-world validation.** The FRAD dataset consists entirely of articles generated by four LLMs (GPT-4, Sonnet, Llama3.1-70b, Qwen). While the authors justify this choice (ensuring data is unseen by LLMs, controlling fact redundancy), no experiment is conducted on any human-written, non-synthetic corpus (e.g., a subset of news articles or Wikipedia). Real RAG corpora contain diverse, noisy, human-written content, and retrieval-generation dynamics may differ. The paper's concluding claim that Ward "can be directly applied to protect the rights of data owners in current RAG systems" (Sec. 6) is premature without at least one realistic non-synthetic sanity check. This is the most significant limitation to practical relevance.

### Minor

- **Scope framing overreaches relative to the method's requirements.** The problem is introduced as "detect unauthorized inclusion of their dataset within a RAG corpus" (abstract, Sec. 1), which suggests a general detection problem. However, Ward requires proactive watermarking of all documents *before* publication (Sec. 4, line 181: "We assume the data owner has protected each \(d_i\) by embedding an LLM watermark"). The paper does not discuss practical cases where owners cannot or have not watermarked their data (e.g., legacy content, third-party scraped data). This mismatch between the broad problem framing and the proactive prerequisite of the proposed solution should be more explicitly acknowledged as a limitation.

- **Baseline comparison lacks explicit disclosure about whether baselines used watermarked or unwatermarked documents.** The paper does not state whether the baselines (SIB, IBM, FACTS) were evaluated on the original (unwatermarked) versions of the owner's documents or on the watermarked versions that Ward uses. If baselines used unwatermarked documents, the comparison is confounded by an asymmetry Ward exploits by design. The paper should either (a) test baselines on watermarked documents as a control, or (b) explicitly state that baselines were given the same watermarked documents and explain why they could not exploit the watermark signal. This is a transparency issue that should be addressed.

- **No mechanistic analysis of how/why the watermark signal survives RAG generation.** The paper provides strong empirical evidence (p-values many orders of magnitude below α) but does not analyze the mechanism: what fraction of response tokens originate from the watermarked context? How does the RAG system's generation distribute watermarked tokens? Understanding *why* the signal survives would clarify when the method might fail (e.g., if the RAG system heavily paraphrases or uses only a small fragment of the retrieved document).

- **Limited defense evaluation.** Only one specific decoding defense (MemFree, n-gram blocking) is tested. A RAG provider could also apply random perturbations to responses, re-order or filter retrieved contexts, or use adversarial monitoring to detect watermarking patterns in submitted documents. These are not explored.

### Trivial

- FRAD currently uses only RepliQA split 0 (the only one available), limiting diversity. The authors note plans to expand (line 131).

- The "inconspicuousness" claim (end of Sec. 4) is supported only by a text-quality comparison (Table 3), not by testing against an adversary who actively monitors query semantics or document patterns.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Testing baselines on watermarked documents to isolate whether Ward's advantage comes from the watermark signal or the method's design.
- A case study showing a concrete example of a watermarked document, a generated query, the RAG response, and the green/red token trace to make the mechanism more transparent.
- Modeling an adaptive adversary who detects the watermark pattern in the owner's public documents and removes/rewrites them before incorporation.

## Novel Insights

The key insight that emerges from the reviews — beyond the paper's own contributions — is that the paper cleanly demonstrates a fundamental limitation of *passive* dataset inference methods (semantic similarity, explicit prompting, fact-based questioning) in the presence of fact redundancy, and shows that a *proactive* watermark-based approach bypasses this limitation entirely. This dichotomy (passive vs. proactive for RAG-DI) is a useful organizing principle for future work. However, the reviews also surface a tension: the paper's empirical case is built on a fully synthetic corpus, and the jump from "works on synthetic FRAD" to "can be directly applied to protect data owners in current RAG systems" remains unbridged. The most impactful follow-up direction suggested (though not by the reviewers themselves) would be to test on a corpus like news articles from a specific time window that post-dates LLM training data, combining the proactive watermarking approach with a realistic, human-authored RAG corpus.

## Suggestions

1. **Add at least one experiment on a non-synthetic corpus.** Even a limited experiment using a set of human-written news articles (e.g., from a specific date range post-dating LLM training) would substantially strengthen the claim of practical applicability. The FRAD benchmark is valuable for controlled comparisons, but the method needs at least one real-data sanity check.
2. **Explicitly disclose whether baselines used watermarked or unwatermarked documents,** and ideally run baselines on both versions as a control experiment. This would clarify whether the observed gap is due to the watermark signal or the method design.
3. **Add a dedicated limitations paragraph** acknowledging (a) the need for proactive watermarking as a prerequisite, (b) the synthetic-only evaluation, and (c) that Ward's guarantee is contingent on the watermark surviving the specific RAG pipeline.
4. **Provide token-level statistics** (e.g., green-token ratio in RAG responses for in-case vs. out-case, fraction of response tokens traceable to watermarked context) to shed light on *why* the watermark survives RAG generation.
5. **Tone down the final claim** (line 536: "a practical tool that can be directly applied") to better match the demonstrated scope, i.e., "a promising approach that warrants validation on real-world RAG corpora."
