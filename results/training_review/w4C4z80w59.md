## Summary

This paper proposes GIE (Growth Inhibitors for Erasure), a method for suppressing inappropriate concepts (NSFW content, artistic styles, objects) in text-to-image diffusion models by manipulating cross-attention at inference time. Instead of fine-tuning the diffusion model, GIE computes separate attention maps for the target concept, re-weights them via a learned "suppression scale," and injects the result into the prompt's attention map group. An MLP adapter is trained to predict the suppression scale from early-step cross-attention statistics using GPT-4o-provided labels. Experiments on SD v1.4 show strong NSFW erasure against eight baselines and competitive FID/CLIP scores, with additional qualitative results for style and object erasure.

## Strengths

- **Novel image-space attention manipulation for concept erasure.** GIE operates on cross-attention maps rather than modifying text embeddings or model parameters, enabling it to handle implicit unsafe prompts (e.g., "Japan body" generating nudity) where the unsafe concept never appears as a token in the prompt. This is a genuinely different technical approach from fine-tuning methods (ESD, CA, SalUn) that dominate the literature.

- **Strong NSFW erasure results with baseline comparisons.** On the I2P dataset with 4,703 prompts, GIE achieves substantially higher NSFW removal rates than all eight baselines (Figure 4). The comparison includes SD v1.4, SD v2.1, negative prompts, SLD, Safe-CLIP, ESD-u, ESD-x, and SPM, providing a thorough benchmark.

- **Good preservation of image quality and semantic alignment.** On COCO-30k neutral prompts, GIE achieves the best FID and CLIP scores among all methods (Table 1), indicating that the erasure operation does not compromise generation quality on unrelated content.

- **Practical deployment advantages.** The method requires no fine-tuning of the diffusion model itself, adapter training completes in seconds with "a few dozen" images, and it can be applied as a plug-in during inference across different pre-trained diffusion models.

- **Multi-concept erasure demonstration.** The paper shows simultaneous erasure of multiple NSFW concepts (e.g., "nude" and "horror") and mixed types (NSFW + style), illustrating practical usability.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Insufficient explanation of the suppression mechanism.** The paper never clearly explains *why* injecting the target concept's attention maps (even re-weighted) suppresses rather than reinforces the concept. The procedure is mathematically specified (Eqs. 7–8): the growth inhibitor I = W ⊙ M* is computed from the target concept's attention and inserted into the prompt's attention group, with corresponding text embeddings also inserted into k', v'. But the authors do not articulate how this operation achieves erasure. The metaphor "growth inhibitor" is evocative but not grounded in an intuitive account of the attention dynamics. This is a significant clarity gap that undermines reader trust in the method. While the empirical results suggest the approach works, the paper's central conceptual contribution remains opaque.

- **Object erasure evaluation lacks baseline comparisons.** Table 2 reports GIE's object removal rates for "car," "airplane," and "cat," but does not compare against any baseline method (ESD, SLD, etc.) on the same task. The paper's abstract and introduction claim "superior performance in erasing concepts related to... common objects," yet no comparative data supports this claim for object erasure. This is a significant gap in the evidence.

- **Style erasure is purely qualitative.** The style erasure results (Figure 6) are shown as example images with no quantitative metric (e.g., style classifier accuracy, FID on style-specific datasets). Given that style erasure is one of the paper's three claimed domains of application, the lack of any numerical evaluation makes these results unconvincing.

- **The claim of adapter generalization to unseen concepts is not empirically validated.** The paper states (Section 4.2) that the adapter "can also be extended to previously unseen concepts of the same type" and gives the example of training on "cat" generalizing to "dog" and "airplane." However, Table 2 does not distinguish which concepts were seen vs. unseen during adapter training — all results are aggregated. A clear ablation (train on concept A, test on concept B) is needed to support this claim.

### Minor

- **The "no fine-tuning" phrasing is imprecise.** The paper repeatedly describes GIE as operating "without fine-tuning" (abstract, introduction, conclusion). While it is true that GIE does not fine-tune the diffusion model (unlike ESD or CA), it does train an MLP adapter on data labeled by GPT-4o. This is lightweight training, but it is training nonetheless. The phrasing should be clarified to "without fine-tuning the diffusion model" to avoid misleading readers.

- **Only three objects tested for object erasure.** Three objects (car, airplane, cat) is a narrow basis for claiming general-purpose object erasure. The paper would benefit from testing a broader set (e.g., 10+ objects) to support its generality claims.

- **GPT-4o as the sole label source for adapter training is not validated.** The adapter is trained on labels from GPT-4o selecting the best suppression scale. No human evaluation or validation against human judgments is provided. While GPT-4o can be useful for relative comparisons, the lack of any verification of its reliability for this specific task is a concern.

### Trivial

- **Line 156:** The NRR formula is rendered incorrectly in the text as "norigninal−nerase" — a formatting artifact.
- The paper does not report the specific thresholds or full training hyperparameters for the adapter.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation comparing GIE with a fixed suppression scale vs. the learned adaptive scale would demonstrate the adapter's necessity.

## Suggestions

1. **Clarify the injection mechanism.** Add a paragraph explaining the intuition: injecting the re-weighted target-concept attention into the prompt's attention group (along with the corresponding text embedding into k', v') modifies the cross-attention output so that the target concept is explicitly processed with controlled/limited weight. Connect this conceptually to how negative prompts work by steering attention away from unwanted content.

2. **Add baseline comparisons for object erasure.** Run ESD-x, SLD, negative prompts, and at least one other method on the same object erasure setup and report ORRs in a comparative table.

3. **Provide quantitative style erasure evaluation.** Use a style classifier (e.g., from ArtGAN or a VGG-based style predictor) to measure the fraction of images retaining the target style after erasure.

4. **Validate the adapter's generalization claim.** Present a clear experiment: train the adapter on one concept (e.g., "cat"), test on held-out concepts ("dog," "airplane"), and report ORRs separately for seen vs. unseen concepts.

5. **Include absolute NSFW rates.** Report the raw NudeNet detection counts per method alongside the relative NRR so readers can see the actual base rates.

## Score and Decision

This paper introduces a genuinely novel approach to concept erasure via attention map manipulation, achieves strong NSFW erasure with good quality preservation, and offers practical deployment advantages. However, the core mechanism is insufficiently explained, the object erasure evaluation lacks baseline comparisons, style erasure lacks quantitative evidence, and the claimed adapter generalization is unvalidated. The paper has real contributions but its current evaluation supports the claims only partially.
