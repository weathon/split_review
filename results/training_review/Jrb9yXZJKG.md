## Summary

The paper introduces CAD-Editor, the first model for text-based CAD editing. It formulates the task as sequence-to-sequence using LLMs (LLaMA-3-8B), generates a large synthetic training dataset by combining design variation models (SkexGen, Hnc-CAD) with MLLM-based multi-level captioning, and further fine-tunes on a small selective dataset constructed via D-CLIP or human feedback. The paper also builds the first benchmark for this task, reporting metrics across validity, alignment, and generation quality.

## Strengths

- **Novel task formulation and first model for text-based CAD editing**: The paper is the first to formulate text-based CAD editing as a seq2seq problem and demonstrates that LLMs can be adapted for this task. The CAD sequence representation (variable-length, textual tokens for categorical/numerical values) is a practical design choice that makes CAD structures processable by LLMs. This opens a new direction for the CAD community.

- **Effective multi-level captioning strategy for synthetic data generation**: The three-stage captioning pipeline (describe images → identify differences → compress instructions) is well-motivated and quantitatively shown to improve over basic captioning. Table 2 (10k subset) shows D-CLIP improving from 0.02 (basic captioning) to 0.07 (multi-level captioning) and Valid Ratio from 84.2% to 88.3%, directly supporting the claim that this reduces noise in training data.

- **Two-stage fine-tuning with selective data shows consistent improvement**: The selective dataset construction via automatic selection (DCF) or human feedback (HF) improves D-CLIP scores over the non-selective baseline (from 0.18 to 0.21 for DCF, 0.20 for HF on the mini model in Table 2). Both selection methods improve performance, suggesting the improvement is genuine and not solely a metric-optimization artifact.

- **Comprehensive evaluation with human raters vs. GPT-4o baselines**: The human evaluation (Table 1) shows CAD-Editor achieving 31.1% success rate vs. 12.9% for GPT-4o basic and 24.3% for GPT-4o 3-shot, providing direct evidence of practical improvement over strong foundation-model baselines.

- **First benchmark for text-based CAD editing**: By establishing the evaluation protocol (valid ratio, D-CLIP, point cloud metrics, human eval) on the DeepCAD dataset, the paper creates a standard for future work in this under-explored area.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **D-CLIP circularity between selection and evaluation**: D-CLIP is used both as the automatic selection criterion for constructing the selective dataset (Section 3.3, DCF) and as a primary alignment metric in evaluation (Table 1, Section 4.2). When the model is fine-tuned on data selected to maximize D-CLIP and then evaluated on D-CLIP, the reported improvements are not cleanly interpretable as evidence of genuine alignment improvement — they may partly reflect metric overfitting. This concern is somewhat mitigated by: (a) the HF alternative does not use D-CLIP for selection yet also improves D-CLIP, and (b) human evaluation provides an independent signal. However, the paper does not discuss this circularity or provide an analysis of how much of the D-CLIP gain is genuine vs. an artifact of selection. The correlation between D-CLIP and human judgment is also not examined.

- **Unexplained human evaluation inconsistency between tables**: Table 1 reports a Human Eval score of 31.1% for CAD-Editor (full model, 92k synthetic data + selective data). The ablation table (Table 2) reports Human Eval scores of 38.4% for the model without selective data, 58.0% with DCF, and 55.2% with HF. Critically, Table 2 uses CAD-Editor-mini (trained on a 10k subset), so these are different models — yet the paper never explicitly states this contrast or explains why a model trained on 1/9th the data achieves substantially higher human eval scores. If the human evaluation methodology, sampling procedure, or test subset differs between tables, this must be clarified. As presented, the numbers are contradictory on their face and undermine confidence in the evaluations.

- **Test set instructions generated by GPT-4o (distribution overlap with training)**: The test set instructions are generated using the same GPT-4o-based pipeline (Section 3.2) as the training data, with human verification. This means the evaluation measures performance on a distribution closely matching the training distribution, but does **not** measure generalization to human-written instructions — the real-world use case. The paper should include evaluation on human-authored instructions to demonstrate practical usefulness. While the human evaluation (using human raters judging outputs) provides some evidence of generalization, the test set's instructions are GPT-4o-derived, which limits the scope of the quantitative claims.

- **Missing baseline: full model trained without selective data in the main results**: The main comparison table (Table 1) should include CAD-Editor trained on the full synthetic dataset **without** the selective stage. Currently, the only comparison of with/without selective data is in Table 2 using the mini model (10k subset). Without this baseline in the main results, it is impossible to quantify the marginal contribution of the selective stage for the full model, which is one of the paper's core claims.

### Minor

- **Negative D-CLIP scores for GPT-4o unexamined**: GPT-4o achieves D-CLIP scores of -0.65 (basic) and -0.01 (3-shot). While these can occur when image changes are orthogonal or opposite to textual directions, the paper does not discuss what negative D-CLIP means for the metric's validity in this domain, nor does it analyze whether D-CLIP correlates with human perception of alignment for CAD edits.

- **Filtering thresholds not justified**: The cosine similarity thresholds (0.95 for SkexGen pairs, 0.9 for Hnc-CAD pairs) used for data filtering appear arbitrary and are not motivated or ablated. A sensitivity analysis would strengthen the paper.

- **Reliance on GPT-4o for both captioning and test set construction**: The synthetic data pipeline and test set both depend on GPT-4o, a closed-source model. The paper acknowledges this as a limitation in the conclusion, but the impact on reproducibility and the ability of the field to build on this work is nontrivial.

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The reviews surface a tension that the paper itself does not address: the D-CLIP circularity and human eval inconsistency both stem from an evaluation framework that was designed before the authors recognized the risk of distributional shortcutting. The paper would benefit from treating these as a coherent methodological issue — the selective dataset construction and the test set construction both rely on the same few sources of supervision (GPT-4o captions and D-CLIP), creating an echo chamber effect. The most impactful revision would be a single held-out evaluation on human-written instructions assessed by human raters, which would simultaneously address the circularity concern, the GPT-4o test set concern, and provide a clean signal of real-world usefulness.

## Suggestions

2. **Add the full-model baseline without selective data to Table 1**: This is the cleanest way to demonstrate the contribution of the selective stage.
3. **Evaluate on human-written instructions**: Even a small set (100–200 instructions) written by CAD users would substantially strengthen claims of practical generalization.
4. **Analyze D-CLIP correlation with human judgment**: Provide a per-sample scatter plot or compute rank correlation to justify using D-CLIP as a proxy metric.

## Score and Decision

**Overall assessment**: This paper tackles a genuinely new and important problem — text-based CAD editing — and proposes a sensible pipeline to address it. The synthetic data generation pipeline with multi-level captioning is well-designed, the ablation experiments are informative, and the human evaluation shows meaningful improvement over GPT-4o baselines. However, the evaluation has several significant issues: the D-CLIP circularity is unaddressed, the human evaluation scores across tables are inconsistent in a way that is not explained, and the test set shares its instruction distribution with the training data. These issues do not invalidate the contribution but they substantially weaken the quantitative evidence for the paper's core claims, and the paper cannot be accepted in its current form. With major revisions — particularly clarifying the human evaluation, breaking the D-CLIP circularity (or at minimum discussing it transparently), and adding the missing baseline — the paper could be strong.
