## Summary

This paper studies attention entropy collapse in Transformers, comparing softmax-based self-attention against Lipschitz kernel attention (ReLU, ELU+1) on an in-context linear regression task. The authors demonstrate that softmax attention suffers from entropy collapse and gradient explosion, while Lipschitz kernel attention remains stable. They attribute this to softmax's non-Lipschitz nature causing sensitivity to logit variance, and argue that entropy collapse inflates the Frobenius norm of the attention matrix ‖P‖_F, which in turn drives gradient explosion.

## Strengths

1. **Clean controlled experiment demonstrates softmax's sensitivity to variance (Figure 3)**: By fixing the query and sampling keys from N(0, σ²I) with increasing variance, the paper cleanly isolates how softmax produces highly peaked distributions (low entropy) as logit variance increases, while kernel attention maintains stable entropy. This is the paper's most convincing empirical contribution — it directly illustrates the sensitivity gap between the two function classes under controlled conditions.

2. **Interesting negative result for QK-LayerNorm**: The paper shows that applying QK-LayerNorm (a standard mitigation for logit magnitude growth) still fails to prevent entropy collapse in softmax attention in this setup (Figure 1), while kernel attention requires no such normalization. This suggests the issue is structural to the softmax function itself, not merely a matter of unbounded logits.

3. **Empirical correlation between ‖P‖_F and gradient norm (Figure 5)**: The paper quantifies that softmax attention's ‖P‖_F rises toward √N (the one-hot bound) during training, and shows this correlates with increasing gradient norms, whereas kernel attention maintains ‖P‖_F ≈ 1 with stable gradients. This correlation is suggestive and worth further investigation.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The causal chain from entropy collapse to gradient explosion via ‖P‖_F is asserted but not established.** The paper uses an upper bound (Eq. 9, from Dasoulas et al., 2021) on the derivative of the attention *output with respect to the input* X (i.e., dA/dX). However, the experiments measure the **parameter gradient norm** (dL/dθ). The paper never bridges this gap — a bound on dA/dX does not directly bound dL/dθ, even in a model composed only of self-attention layers, because dL/dθ also depends on dL/dA (the derivative of the loss with respect to the attention output). The paper shows correlation (Figure 5, Right) but does not isolate whether gradient growth is driven *through* ‖P‖_F or through other channels (e.g., the second term in Eq. 9 involving ‖DZ_X‖). Without a causal intervention (e.g., ablating the ‖P‖_F contribution while holding other factors fixed) or a direct link to parameter gradients, the paper's central explanatory claim remains unsupported.

2. **The comparison between softmax and kernelized attention confounds Lipschitzness with other structural differences.** The paper attributes stability entirely to the "Lipschitzness" of the kernel function, but:
   - **Softmax attention** uses P_{i,j} = exp(Q_i·K_j) / Σ exp(Q_i·K_j) with the standard attention formulation A_i = Σ_j P_{i,j} V_j.
   - **Kernelized attention** uses the linear attention formulation A'_i = (φ(Q_i) Σ_j φ(K_j)^T V_j) / (φ(Q_i) Σ_j φ(K_j)^T), which has a different normalization mechanism (denominator involves sums over keys in feature space) and different gradient propagation structure (the associative property changes how gradients flow compared to explicit row-wise softmax normalization).
   
   A controlled ablation would compare softmax against a row-wise normalized Lipschitz function applied to the *same inner product*: P_{i,j} = φ(Q_i·K_j) / Σ_j φ(Q_i·K_j) for a Lipschitz φ. Without this, the observed stability cannot be uniquely attributed to Lipschitzness rather than to differences in normalization, functional form (dot-product-of-nonlinearities vs. elementwise-nonlinearity-of-dot-product), or computational structure. The controlled variance experiment (Figure 3) partially addresses this by computing probabilities row-wise for both methods, but the similarity functions still differ (exp(Z) vs. φ(Q)φ(K)^T).

3. **The experimental setup is too narrow to support broad claims about "Transformer training instability."** The paper uses a single toy task (in-context linear regression) with a minimal architecture (self-attention layers only, no MLPs, and it is unclear whether residual connections or position encodings are used). Real Transformer training instability in LLMs involves interactions between attention, normalization, residuals, and feedforward layers. While the paper justifies toy models (citing Wortsman et al., 2023), it then draws general conclusions such as "softmax-based self-attentions exhibit instability due to decreasing attention entropy" without validating on any more realistic setup. Furthermore:
   - Figures show single runs with no error bars, statistical summaries, or multiple seeds, so variability is unknown.
   - The softmax baseline diverges (loss → ∞), making it impossible to compare task performance (e.g., prediction error) against the stable kernel runs.

### Minor

1. **No comparison against existing methods that directly target the identified root cause.** The paper identifies variance growth in attention logits as the cause of entropy collapse (Section 3.4). However, it does not compare against NormSoftmax (which normalizes softmax inputs to unit variance) or σReparam (spectral normalization of Q/K weights) — both of which directly address the variance/norm growth mechanism the paper identifies. Including these baselines would substantially strengthen the paper's claims about the unique benefits of Lipschitz kernels.

2. **The analysis of why softmax logit variance grows during training is absent.** The paper observes (Figure 4, Left) that softmax attention's logit variance grows abruptly while kernel attention's variance stabilizes. It does not investigate *why* — whether this is driven by Q/K weight norms growing, by alignment properties of the keys, or by feedback from the loss. A controlled experiment (e.g., spectrally normalizing Q/K weights) could isolate the cause.

3. **No quantitative statistics for attention matrices across layers/heads.** Figure 2 shows attention matrices from the first layer for illustration but provides no quantitative assessment of how many heads/layers exhibit collapse, whether collapse is gradual across layers, or the variability across runs.

### Trivial

- The term "gradient norm" is used throughout (Figures 1, 5) without specifying whether it refers to the full parameter gradient norm or the gradient norm per layer. This should be clarified.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A comparison against ReLUFormer (Shen et al., 2023), which replaces the exponential in softmax with ReLU while keeping the same row-wise normalization and standard (non-linear) attention structure, would help disentangle whether the benefit comes from Lipschitzness specifically or from removing the exponential.
- Validation on a language modeling task (e.g., WikiText-2) with full Transformer blocks (including MLPs, residual connections, and layer normalization) would significantly strengthen the generality of the claims.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The primary insight — that softmax's exponential nature makes it disproportionately sensitive to logit variance compared to Lipschitz kernels — is demonstrated clearly in Figure 3. However, the paper's attempt to extend this into a mechanistic explanation of gradient explosion via ‖P‖_F growth is not convincingly established. The key novelty of the paper is the observation itself, not the explanation.

## Suggestions

1. **Reframe the contribution.** The paper's strongest finding is the empirical observation that Lipschitz kernel attention maintains stable entropy under increasing logit variance, while softmax does not. The paper would be stronger if it positioned this as the main contribution and treated the ‖P‖_F → gradient explosion mechanism as a plausible hypothesis requiring further validation, rather than a demonstrated result.

2. **Add a controlled ablation of Lipschitzness.** Compare softmax against P_{i,j} = φ(Q_i·K_j) / Σ_j φ(Q_i·K_j) for a Lipschitz φ (e.g., ReLU, clipped exponential) in the *same* row-wise normalization framework. This would isolate the effect of replacing the exponential with a Lipschitz function while keeping the attention structure identical.

3. **Include NormSoftmax and σReparam baselines.** Since the paper identifies variance growth as the root cause, comparing against methods that directly control variance would contextualize the kernel attention results and strengthen the paper's claims.

4. **Run multiple seeds and report statistics.** Single-run figures are not sufficient to establish reliability. Report mean/variance of entropy, gradient norm, and loss over at least 5 runs.

5. **Validate on at least one more realistic setup.** A small-scale language modeling task (e.g., Transformer with MLP layers on WikiText-2) would go a long way toward showing the phenomenon generalizes beyond the toy setting.

## Score and Decision

This paper makes an interesting empirical observation: softmax attention is highly sensitive to logit variance, leading to entropy collapse, while Lipschitz kernel attention avoids this. The controlled variance experiment (Figure 3) and the negative result for QK-LayerNorm are genuine contributions. However, the paper's central claim — that entropy collapse causes gradient explosion via ‖P‖_F growth — is not rigorously established; the bound used bounds dA/dX, not dL/dθ, and the evidence is correlational. Additionally, the comparison between attention mechanisms confounds Lipschitzness with other structural differences, and the experimental setup is too narrow to support the paper's general claims about "Transformer training instability." The paper would benefit from a more modest framing and additional experiments.

**Borderline.** The paper has a real contribution (the variance sensitivity observation) but its main explanatory claim is not adequately supported, and the experimental validation is limited. With major revisions (fairer comparisons, statistical rigor, more realistic validation) this could become a solid empirical study.
