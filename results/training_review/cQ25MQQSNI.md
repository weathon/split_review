## Summary

This paper introduces CertainlyUncertain, a 178K-sample VQA benchmark based on a five-category taxonomy of multimodal uncertainty (epistemic: knowledge, complexity, extraneous; aleatoric: temporal, ambiguity). The dataset is constructed via two pipelines: (1) inpainting salient objects to turn answerable questions into unanswerable ones, and (2) prompting GPT-4 with image captions to generate answerable/unanswerable contrastive pairs. The paper also proposes a confidence-weighted accuracy metric and shows that fine-tuning on CertainlyUncertain improves VLMs on refusal and some hallucination benchmarks without degrading standard VQA performance.

## Strengths

- **Systematic taxonomy of multimodal uncertainty with five fine-grained subcategories.** The epistemic/aleatoric distinction with concrete subcategories (knowledge, complexity, extraneous, temporal, ambiguity) is more structured than prior refusal datasets which treat unanswerability as monolithic (Table 2 shows no existing benchmark covers all five types). This provides a useful conceptual framework for the community.

- **Large-scale contrastive dataset (178K pairs) via two complementary construction pipelines.** Both caption-based generation (GPT-4 prompting on DOCCI captions) and image-perturbation (saliency-driven inpainting with SAM/LaMa) produce answerable/unanswerable pairs that are visually or semantically similar, avoiding the random mismatched pairs in UNK-VQA and TDIUC (Table 2). The contrastive-pair design is sensible for training models to distinguish certain from uncertain cases.

- **Fine-tuning on CertainlyUncertain generalizes to refusal and hallucination benchmarks without degrading standard VQA.** Substantial gains are demonstrated on UNK-VQA (LLaVA-7B: 29.6→68.5), TDIUC, POPE F1, and MMHal hallucination ratio, while VQAv2 accuracy remains within 1 point of baseline and VizWiz improves (Table 6). This shows the data carries useful signal beyond its own distribution.

- **Honest reporting of the "Generative AI Paradox" and negative results.** The paper identifies that GPT-4V fails to answer its own generated uncertain questions (Figure 3) and honestly reports degradation on AMBER for Qwen-VL-Chat, indicating awareness of limitations.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None. The benchmark's core claims (that the taxonomy is useful, that the data improves refusal behavior) are supported by the empirical results, and no methodological error invalidates the main findings.

### Major

- **The confidence-weighted accuracy metric is insufficiently justified and plays no role in the main results.** The metric uses a *separate self-verification prompt* ("is your answer correct?") to obtain P(pred), following Whitehead et al. (2022), rather than the model's own output probability from the original prediction. The formula's sign asymmetry and scaling by LAVE_idk (which itself can be 0.5) are presented without principled motivation. The correlation evidence (Figure 4) shows only a scatter plot from one evaluation split (extraneous) across model variants, without correlation coefficients, confidence intervals, or comparisons to standard alternatives (Brier score, NLL). Critically, the metric is not used in any subsequent experiment — all tables report LAVE_idk or F1_idk. As presented, the metric is a speculative proposal rather than a validated contribution. This weakens a claimed contribution but does not affect the paper's main empirical results.

- **The majority of the dataset lacks human validation, leaving label quality uncertain.** Only the extraneous test split (4.8K of 6K samples, validated by one author) and the first 5K DOCCI test samples are human-validated. The remaining ~168K samples — including all caption-based training data for knowledge, complexity, temporal, and ambiguity categories — are automatically generated by GPT-4 with no human quality check. While the paper candidly acknowledges the "Generative AI Paradox" (the generator cannot answer its own uncertain questions), this same concern applies to label quality: the data may contain incorrectly labelled or unnatural phrasings. The strong transfer results on refusal benchmarks suggest the data has genuine signal, but the actual error rate and distributional biases in the training data remain unquantified.

### Minor

- **Insufficient detail on the inpainting artifact control experiment.** The paper states that masking/inpainting random (non-salient) objects did not cause performance to "fluctuate significantly," but provides no numbers, no indication of which model was tested, no effect size, and no statistical test. This makes it difficult for readers to assess whether the extraneous split measures uncertainty awareness or artifact detection. A brief quantitative report would resolve this.

- **Disconnect between the Section 2.1 definition of "extraneous" and its operationalization.** The Section 2.1 definition describes extraneous awareness as "identify[ing] and disregard[ing] elements within an image that are not relevant to the question at hand" — i.e., recognizing irrelevance of *present* elements. However, the data construction removes the salient object via inpainting, testing the model's ability to detect *missing* elements. (The introduction's phrasing at line 12 — "parts of the necessary context or details are missing" — aligns better with the operationalization.) This inconsistency in the paper's own definitions is confusing.

- **Limited per-category analysis despite the taxonomy being a central contribution.** Figure 5 is described as a "breakdown ... on finegrained categories," but the paper provides no textual discussion or table of per-category results. The main experimental analyses (Tables 5, 6) report only aggregate scores. This makes it difficult to validate whether different uncertainty types respond differently to training or whether some categories drive the observed improvements.

- **Limited baseline comparisons for uncertainty-aware training.** The only inference-time baseline is a naive selective prediction approach (thresholding prediction probability). There is no comparison to temperature scaling, confidence-thresholded SFT, or other calibration methods that could provide a stronger lower bound.

### Trivial

- The correlation plot for the confidence-weighted metric (Figure 4) would benefit from reporting Pearson/Spearman coefficients and regression lines rather than just raw scatter points.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation comparing caption-sourced vs. image-sourced data contributions would clarify whether the expensive image-perturbation pipeline is necessary or whether caption-based generation alone suffices.
- Sampling ~200 instances per uncertainty category for human agreement with the IDK label would establish a ceiling and quantify label noise.
- Qualitative side-by-side examples of original vs. inpainted images with realism ratings would help readers judge the extent of potential artifacts.
- Analysis of AMBER failure modes (e.g., does fine-tuning cause over-refusal on attribute questions?) would clarify the observed degradation.

## Suggestions

1. **Report the inpainting artifact control with quantitative detail** — even a brief table showing accuracy on the random-perturbation set for one model, with a simple effect-size statement, would substantially strengthen confidence in the extraneous split.
2. **Either validate the confidence-weighted metric rigorously** (with correlation coefficients, confidence intervals, comparison to Brier score/NLL, and a study of how the self-verification proxy relates to true prediction probability) or demote it from a claimed contribution to a brief discussion of future work.
3. **Provide a dedicated limitations section** acknowledging the scope of human validation, the definitional inconsistency for extraneous, and the absence of per-category analyses. The paper currently addresses limitations only in passing.
4. **Add a per-category results table** (LAVE_idk and F1_idk for each of the five uncertainty types) for at least one model variant, to validate the taxonomy's utility.
5. **Conduct a small human agreement study** (200 samples per category) to establish the benchmark's ceiling and label error rate, even if just reported as a supplement.
