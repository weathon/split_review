## Summary

This paper presents the first systematic benchmark evaluating adversarial defenses for Image Quality Assessment (IQA) metrics. It compares 25 defense methods (adversarial purification, adversarial training, and certified robustness) against 14 attack algorithms on 7 NR-IQA metrics in both non-adaptive and adaptive settings, using metrics tailored to the IQA domain (score disparity, rank correlation, and perceptual quality). The benchmark includes a released dataset of over 215,000 adversarial images and an online leaderboard.

## Strengths

- **First comprehensive benchmark for IQA defenses**: Prior work studied IQA metric robustness without defenses (e.g., MSU Metrics Robustness Benchmark). This paper fills that gap by systematically evaluating 25 defense methods under a unified framework, covering purification, adversarial training, and certified approaches — making it the most extensive study in this niche (Sections 1, 3.1, Table 1).

- **Adaptive vs. non-adaptive analysis is insightful and actionable**: By integrating differentiable defenses into the attack pipeline, the paper reveals that simple defenses (e.g., Flip: SROCC drops from 0.710 to 0.356 under adaptive attacks; Table 3) collapse against aware adversaries, while stochastic methods (Rotate, Crop, DiffJPEG) retain more robustness. This distinction is essential for practitioners and correctly handled.

- **Open infrastructure supports reproducibility and community progress**: The pipeline is automated via GitLab CI/CD, and the dataset of >215K adversarial images is released, enabling direct replication and extension. The commitment to an online leaderboard accepting submissions is a valuable community resource (Section 3.5).

- **Broad attack and defense coverage with careful evaluation metrics**: 14 attacks (white-box, black-box, restricted, unrestricted) at three strength levels, evaluated using D_score, SROCC_adv, and Q_score — a multi-faceted assessment appropriate for IQA where both score fidelity and perceptual quality matter (Section 3.4, Table 1).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **Certified defenses evaluated on only 10 images (Section 3.4, Table 4).** The paper explicitly states "we used a smaller set of 10 images for certified defenses." With such a tiny sample, the reported SROCC_adv values (e.g., 0.76 for DDRS/DensePure) have wide confidence intervals that likely underestimate true variability, and results could be heavily sensitive to idiosyncratic image characteristics. Computing cost is acknowledged, but the conclusions drawn from Table 4 (e.g., "defenses involving diffusion denoising show better results") are not statistically grounded. This evaluation should be labeled as exploratory or expanded substantially.

- **Defense parameters selected by optimizing SROCC_adv on the test set (Table 1 caption, Figure 1 caption).** The paper selects "defense parameters' values with the highest SROCC_adv" for each method. This is test-set performance selection: a practitioner deploying a defense would not know which parameter setting is optimal for unseen attacks. While showing each defense at its best configuration is informative, the headline results (e.g., Flip SROCC_adv=0.710, JPEG SROCC_adv=0.718) are optimistic relative to what realistic deployment would achieve. The paper should either use held-out validation or report results across all parameter variants.

### Minor

- **Conceptual gap between the formal definition of adversarial purification (Eq. 2) and the tested methods.** Eq. 2 defines purification as an optimization over x'' that explicitly requires the original clean image x and the metric f_ω: P(x') = argmin |f_ω(x'')-f_ω(x)| + λρ(x'',x). However, none of the tested methods (JPEG, blur, spatial transforms, denoising networks, etc.) use knowledge of x or f_ω — they are preprocessing transforms applied without solving this optimization. This creates a misleading theoretical framing. The paper should clarify that Eq. 2 describes an idealized objective and that the tested methods are heuristic approximations not derived from it. The experimental results are unaffected, but the methodology section needs correction.

- **Adversarial training tested on only 2 of 7 IQA metrics** (LINEARITY and KONCEPT, Section 3.4). The computational justification is understandable, but this limits the scope of conclusions about adversarial training. At least one additional metric (e.g., MANIQA or SPAQ) would help assess whether findings are metric-specific. This limitation should be stated more prominently.

- **Large confidence intervals throughout Table 1** (e.g., "W/o Defense" D_score = 60.25 ± 56.59, many purification D_score intervals span 30–50 percentage points). The paper attributes this to diversity across metrics and attacks, which is correct, but it also means many pairwise defense comparisons are not statistically significant. The absence of statistical significance testing (e.g., paired bootstrap) makes it unclear which differences are meaningful.

### Trivial

- The Q_score metric (SSIM + PSNR/40) is an ad-hoc linear combination with no principled justification. The paper partially acknowledges this for spatial transformations, but a standard perceptual metric (LPIPS, DISTS) or at least justification of the equal-weighting scheme would be cleaner.

- In Table 1, adaptive attack results are missing ("---") for several defenses (JPEG, Color Quantization) without a clear visual cue distinguishing "not applicable" from "not computed." A footnote in the table would improve readability.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A cross-dataset evaluation (e.g., LIVE or TID2013) would strengthen claims about generalizability beyond KONIQ-10K.
- Qualitative examples showing purified attacked images alongside originals would help illustrate practical artifacts introduced by defenses.
- A trade-off plot (SROCC_adv vs. SROCC_clear or Q_score) per defense would help practitioners choose based on their tolerance for quality degradation.

## Removed Points

- *Criticism about Eq. 1 attack direction (increase vs. decrease scores) being insufficiently justified*: The paper clearly states "We choose to increase the model's values during the attack process since it reflects the motivation to conduct attacks in real-life applications" (Section 3.1). This is a reasonable and clearly scoped choice. Both directions being plausible doesn't invalidate the paper's consistent choice.

- *Claim that the certified defense 10-segment choice is not ablated*: The paper states "According to our experiments, ten segments showed optimal correlations with subjective scores" (Section 3.2). While the ablation isn't shown in the main text, the paper claims it was performed. This is a presentation choice, not a missing experiment.

- *Request for larger dataset when 1000 images with diverse clustering is already substantial*: The paper uses a principled K-means clustering selection strategy on SI, CF, and MOS to ensure diversity. 1000 images for the main purification benchmark is adequate for a benchmark of this scope.

## Novel Insights

The reviewer critiques converge on a key observation that the paper itself does not fully articulate: the gap between the idealized mathematical framing of adversarial defense (Eq. 2, which requires access to the original image and metric) and the practical heuristic methods tested (preprocessing transforms that are fully blind to both) reflects an unresolved tension in how "adversarial purification" is operationalized for IQA. The paper would benefit from explicitly positioning its tested methods as heuristic preprocessing defenses rather than invoking optimization formalisms that do not govern the experiments. This would prevent readers from over-interpreting the theoretical framing while preserving the paper's empirical value.

## Suggestions

1. **Clarify the definition of adversarial purification (Eq. 2)** — explicitly note that it is an idealized formulation, and that the tested methods are heuristic approximations not derived from solving Eq. 2. This resolves the conceptual inconsistency.
2. **Add a held-out validation split for defense parameter selection** — or at a minimum report results for all five parameter sets alongside the selected ones, so readers can assess sensitivity.
3. **Expand the certified-defense evaluation substantially** (at least 100–200 images) before drawing conclusions from Table 4, or clearly label the results as preliminary/exploratory.
4. **Add statistical significance tests** (e.g., paired bootstrap or Wilcoxon signed-rank) to the main comparison table so readers can assess which defense differences are reliable given the large confidence intervals.
5. **Include at least one more IQA metric in the adversarial training experiments** to broaden the basis for those conclusions.

## Score and Decision

This paper introduces a needed benchmark for a relatively underexplored problem — adversarial defense of IQA metrics. The scope is impressive (25 defenses, 14 attacks, 7 metrics) and the adaptive vs. non-adaptive distinction is correctly handled. The main weaknesses are real but addressable: test-set parameter selection inflates results (mitigable with held-out validation), the certified-defense evaluation on 10 images is too small to support its conclusions, and Eq. 2 creates a misleading theoretical framing. None of these are fatal — they do not invalidate the paper's core contribution of providing the first systematic empirical comparison. With revisions addressing the evaluation protocol issues, this would be a valuable resource for the community.
