## Summary

This paper conducts a large-scale empirical study of whether soft prompts (learned word embeddings) that encode visual concepts are transferable across three multimodal models (Stable Diffusion for generation, OWL-v2 for detection, CLIP/DFN for classification). Across 4,800 trained embeddings covering 40 concepts and 4 datasets, the authors show that embeddings are generally not linearly transferable between models. They discover a "fracturing" phenomenon: performant solutions for any concept exist within small neighborhoods of arbitrary anchor words, and these perturbative solutions primarily affect the final layers of text encoders. The work contributes a benchmark and methodology for studying prompt interoperability.

## Strengths

1. **Large-scale, multi-task, multi-model study**: The paper trains 4,800+ embeddings across three models (generation, detection, classification), four datasets (ImageNet, DreamBooth, COCO, PASCAL VOC), and 40 diverse visual concepts. This scale makes the negative result robust and goes well beyond prior work on prompt transfer, which typically studies a single task.

2. **Discovery of "fracturing" in embedding space**: Section 5 shows that for any tested anchor word, a performant embedding for an arbitrary concept exists within its local neighborhood, and in-domain performance saturates at constraint level δ=0.5 (where the nearest neighbor is still the anchor word). This is a non-trivial finding that reframes soft-prompt learning as finding perturbations rather than learning compositional representations. The paper provides striking qualitative examples (Figure 4) showing near-identical generations from perturbations near different anchors.

3. **Layer-wise analysis of where perturbations act**: The truncated-encoder experiment (Figure 6) and TSNE visualizations of layer-wise activations provide mechanistic evidence that non-transferable solutions primarily affect the final layers of the text encoder. Early layers still encode the anchor word, explaining why transferred embeddings "snap back" to known words. This is a novel diagnostic that could inform future work on transferable prompts.

4. **Generalization of textual inversion to detection/classification**: The paper adapts soft-prompt training to open-set detection (OWL-v2) and classification (DFN CLIP) using contrastive losses, expanding the scope of this methodology beyond text-to-image generation where it is typically applied.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The non-transferability claim is only tested under a linear map, with insufficient justification.** The paper restricts transfer functions to linear least-squares (Eq. 2), acknowledged as a "simplification" (line 59), but provides no evidence that a linear map is adequate to capture the relationship between these embedding spaces. The number of shared words (>40,000) makes the least-squares problem well-posed but does not imply linearity. If the correct relationship is nonlinear (e.g., because models use different tokenizers, text encoder architectures, or learned factorizations of semantic space), then linear transfer would fail even when embeddings are "transferable in principle." The paper's central claim — "embeddings for visual concepts are not transferable" — should therefore be understood as "not linearly transferable under the learned linear map." The Discussion section (Sec. 6) does not acknowledge this limitation, which is a significant omission for a paper making strong negative claims.

2. **The claim that "performant solutions are everywhere" extrapolates beyond the evidence.** The fracturing experiments (Section 5.1) use 10 anchor words per concept (across 40 concepts). While 10 anchors × 40 concepts × 3 models × 4 thresholds = 4,800 experimental conditions, the claim that "near the representation for *any* word in embedding space, there is a perturbation" (line 176) generalizes from 10 out of 40,000+ words. The paper hedges with "suggesting it may be a general phenomenon," but the title "Soft Prompts Are Fractured" and the strong "any word" language imply broader coverage than tested. The paper also does not compare constrained solutions to a random untrained embedding to quantify how much the optimization meaningfully improves over the anchor itself. This would help establish that "solutions are everywhere" rather than "the anchor was already somewhat effective."

### Minor

1. **No zero-shot or standard-prompt baseline is reported for the transfer experiments.** The paper reports transfer performance as a percentage of in-domain training performance (e.g., "classification→generation achieves 84%"), which is sensible as one baseline. However, a reader cannot tell whether 84% means the transferred embedding is nearly as good as in-domain training, or whether the standard prompt (e.g., "a photo of an orange cat") already achieves close to in-domain performance, making the transferred result less impressive. Adding a simple row to Figure 2 showing the target model's performance with just the class name would make the transfer results interpretable without changing any conclusions.

2. **The linear transfer analysis could be strengthened by reporting reconstruction error.** The paper fits linear maps on >40,000 shared words but never reports the reconstruction error (MSE or cosine similarity) on held-out shared words. If the residual is large, the linear assumption is suspect. Reporting this would either justify or undermine the core experimental design, and it costs almost nothing to compute.

3. **The paper does not test whether shared text encoder architecture improves transfer.** All three models use some form of CLIP text encoder, yet the paper tests transfer across all of them. A cleaner ablation would be: if two models share the exact same CLIP text encoder, does transfer improve dramatically? If yes, the non-transferability is partly a consequence of different text encoders. If no, the result points to something deeper about how different training objectives shape the embedding space. This is scoped as "obvious next steps" by the reviewer, and including it would significantly strengthen the paper.

4. **The causal role of final layers is not fully established.** The truncated-encoder experiment (Figure 6) shows correlation: when early layers are used alone, generations reflect the anchor word. But the paper does not test whether explicitly regularizing early-layer activations to stay fixed (or perturbing them directly) changes transferability. The claim that perturbations "target the final layers" is supported by observational evidence, not causal intervention.

### Trivial
- The generically bound evaluation metric in Table 1 for generation (𝔼[𝟙(I has the concept)]) is underspecified — the actual implementation (CLIP-based classifier) is described later in §4, but the equation itself could be clearer.
- The example of an "identical image" for the duck concept (Figure 4) is anecdotal; no statistical test quantifies how often this occurs across the full set of concepts.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Nonlinear transfer (e.g., a 1-hidden-layer MLP) trained on shared vocabulary. If it also fails, the non-transferability claim is much stronger. If it succeeds, the paper's conclusion is reversed — this is the single highest-impact follow-up experiment.
- A random-embedding control for the fracturing experiments to quantify improvement over the anchor word initialization vs. chance.
- Nearest-neighbor analysis: after linear transfer, which word in the target model's vocabulary is the transferred embedding closest to? Does this explain transfer failures (embedding "snaps to" an unrelated word)?
- Ablation on the normalization used in Equation 4: results for different δ definitions or multiple random seeds per δ.

## Removed Points
These points are flagged to be removed; treat them with caution.

- **"The paper never tests the exact scenario of prompts trained for black Labradors being reused."** The paper tests cross-task transfer (generation→detection, etc.), which is a broader and more demanding test of the reuse scenario. This criticism demands a specific instantiation of a general class the paper already covers.
- **"Hyperparameters shared across models may not be optimal for each."** This is a standard trade-off in any multi-model study. The paper uses 1,000 steps and reports final performance; the criticism could apply to any multi-model comparison and does not identify a specific failure.
- **"Figure 2 reports percentages without variance in the text (though intervals are shown in the figure)."** Confidence intervals are shown in the figure; the text summarizes the qualitative trend. This is a presentation choice, not a weakness.
- **"Using OWL-v2 to generate bounding box labels could introduce bias."** The paper explicitly states these were "manually verified as correct" (line 108), which directly addresses this concern. The criticism ignores the paper's own addressal.
- **"The paper overstates the conclusion in the abstract — should say 'not linearly transferable.'"** The abstract is a high-level summary; the body explicitly restricts to linear maps and describes the transfer function. This level of precision in an abstract is standard and expected.

## Novel Insights
The most interesting observation to emerge from these reviews is that the fracturing phenomenon (Section 5) is the paper's strongest and most original contribution, yet the linear transfer limitation (Section 3) has attracted the most critical scrutiny. These are actually separable: the fracturing results do not depend on the linear transfer function at all — they study in-domain constrained optimization. This means the paper's "second finding" stands independently and is not weakened by any concerns about the transfer methodology. A reviewer who accepts the fracturing finding but rejects the transfer methodology would still view the paper as making a significant discovery about the structure of soft-prompt embedding spaces. Conversely, the transfer results are best interpreted as a demonstration that the fracturing phenomenon *causes* linear non-transferability, rather than as an independent claim about impossibility of transfer.

## Suggestions
1. Add a brief justification or test of the linear assumption: report the reconstruction error (cosine similarity or MSE) of the linear map on held-out shared words. If error is small, the assumption is validated. If large, add a caveat to the Discussion.
2. Add a single column to Figure 2 showing each target model's performance with the standard class-name prompt (no optimization). This takes negligible effort and immunizes the paper against the zero-shot-baseline criticism.
3. Test at least one shared-text-encoder condition (e.g., two models that use the same CLIP checkpoint) to isolate whether non-transferability stems from different text encoders vs. different training objectives.
4. Soften the language in the abstract and Section 5 from "any word" to "every tested word" or "all tested anchors," and note the sampling limitation explicitly in the Discussion.

## Score and Decision

The paper makes a genuine empirical contribution by documenting the fracturing phenomenon at scale, and the layer-wise analysis provides a plausible mechanistic explanation. However, the central transferability claim is weakened by the unverified linear assumption and the lack of basic baselines (zero-shot, reconstruction error). These are addressable — they do not invalidate the fracturing discovery — but they do mean the strongest conclusions about non-transferability are not as airtight as the paper's language suggests. The paper would benefit from one additional round of experimental validation and more cautious framing.
