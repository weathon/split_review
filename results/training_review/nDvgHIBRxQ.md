## Summary

**MathCheck** introduces a checklist-based evaluation framework for mathematical reasoning in LLMs, organized along two axes: task generalization (problem solving, answerable judging, outcome judging, process judging) and reasoning robustness (original, problem understanding, irrelevant disturbance, scenario understanding). The authors propose an automatic pipeline using GPT-4-Turbo to generate checklist data from seed problems, producing MathCheck-GSM (3,096 samples from GSM8k) and MathCheck-GEO (1,440 geometry samples). They evaluate 43 models across both benchmarks and present correlation analyses suggesting MathCheck correlates more strongly with proxies of genuine mathematical ability (private GSM1k data, compression efficiency on math papers) than vanilla GSM8k.

## Strengths

- **Principled 2D evaluation design**: The 4×4 checklist (4 tasks × 4 robustness variants) is a conceptually clean and well-motivated framework. It goes beyond single-task problem solving and captures dimensions that existing benchmarks largely ignore — task generalization and robustness jointly. The paper explicitly operationalizes "understanding" as robust performance across this matrix (lines 34, 77), which is a coherent and testable definition.

- **External validation via two proxies**: The correlation analysis (Section 4.4) with private GSM1k data and compression efficiency (BPC-loss on ArXiv math papers) provides meaningful evidence that MathCheck's signal aligns with independent measures of mathematical ability, and that it does so better than vanilla GSM8k (p = -0.915 vs. -0.822 for BPC-loss). This is a form of validation most math benchmarks do not attempt.

- **Reveals overfitting in math-specialized models**: The behavior analysis (Figure 4, Section 5) empirically demonstrates that DeepSeek-Math-7B-Instruct/RL and MetaMath improve substantially on solving tasks after training but show limited or declining performance on judging tasks. This is an actionable insight that single-task benchmarks cannot surface. It supports the paper's core thesis that narrow training data produces narrow reasoning.

- **Broad model coverage**: Evaluating 26 LLMs and 17 MLLMs (Table 1 and 2) provides a useful snapshot of the current landscape, including API-based, open-source, generalist, and math-specialized models at various scales.

- **Automatic generation pipeline with manual validation**: The pipeline (Section 2.3, Figure 2) is clearly described and achieves an 84% pass rate after human verification by trained graduate students, making the approach scalable and extendable to other datasets and domains.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Generator bias from using a single proprietary model for dataset creation.**  
   The entire MathCheck dataset is generated by GPT-4-Turbo (line 102): all robustness variants, answerability decisions, outcome labels, and process-judging mistake steps. The same model family (GPT-4o, GPT-4-Turbo, GPT-4o-mini) then achieves top performance on the resulting benchmark. While the 84% manual validation pass rate (line 124) ensures individual samples are correct, it does not address the deeper concern: that GPT-4-Turbo may systematically favor patterns its own family handles well, creating an inherent advantage unrelated to mathematical ability. This concern is partially mitigated by the external correlation evidence (GSM1k and BPC-loss are independent of the generator), but the paper never directly analyzes whether GPT-4-family models benefit from being evaluated on data their own model generated. A direct test (e.g., re-generating a subset with an open-source model and checking whether GPT-4o's advantage shrinks) would substantially strengthen the benchmark's credibility.

### Minor

1. **Limited comparison for the "more linear" claim.**  
   Section 4.4 compares MathCheck-GSM only against vanilla GSM8k. The paper does not compare against existing robustness-oriented math benchmarks (e.g., GSM-Symbolic, GSM-IC, MathAttack), which also aim to address overfitting and surface true ability. Without such comparisons, the claim that MathCheck "represents mathematical intelligence more linearly" is only supported relative to the simplest possible baseline, not against the state of the art in robust evaluation.

2. **Missing details on rejected samples and replacement strategy.**  
   The 84% pass rate (line 124) implies 16% of automatically generated samples were rejected during manual validation. The paper does not describe what happens to these rejected samples — whether they are re-generated, replaced, or simply dropped — nor whether any checklist groups end up with missing cells. This makes it difficult to assess the completeness and consistency of the dataset.

3. **Underspecified evaluation metrics.**  
   The paper uses "Acc" for Process Judging and Problem Solving (line 223) without clarifying whether Accuracy requires exact match of the error step number for Process Judging or whether partial credit is given. For Outcome Judging (F1), the class distribution of correct vs. incorrect solutions is not reported, nor is the rationale for choosing F1 over Accuracy. These details are important for reproducibility.

4. **Seed sample size and coverage.**  
   MathCheck-GSM uses 129 seed problems from GSM8k (<10% of the test set), sampled "evenly according to difficulty" based on reasoning steps (line 120). The number of reasoning steps is a coarse proxy for difficulty; the paper does not demonstrate that the 129 problems capture the full range of problem types and mathematical concepts in GSM8k. Variance estimates for the 129-group checklist scores are absent.

5. **Correlation analysis lacks uncertainty quantification.**  
   Pearson correlation coefficients (p = -0.915, -0.822) are reported without confidence intervals, bootstrap estimates, or significance tests. The number of models contributing to each correlation is not stated, making it impossible to assess the reliability of the reported improvements.

6. **Behavior analysis conclusion is slightly overstated.**  
   The claim that "training solely on massive solving data is not the right direction to improve mathematical reasoning ability" (line 275) is drawn from observing DeepSeek-Math and MetaMath. The analysis does not control for data quality, training data mixture, or model size. The conclusion is suggestive but would be strengthened by controlling for these factors or by including additional math-specialized models.

### Trivial

1. The scatter plots (Figures 1 and 2) are difficult to interpret because not all model labels are shown; the paper describes only a few representative points. The number of models in each correlation plot is not stated explicitly.
2. Section 6 (Extension to other tasks) presents commonsense reasoning and code generation as illustrative suggestions with no experimental results. This is fine for an extension section, but its framing could be clearer to avoid appearing incomplete.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Comparing MathCheck-GSM against existing robustness benchmarks (GSM-Symbolic, GSM-IC) in the correlation analysis to strengthen the "more linear" claim.
- Computing per-checklist-group standard errors or bootstrap confidence intervals for model rankings, enabling readers to assess which differences are statistically significant.
- Performing a subset-level validation: re-generating a small set of checklist data using an open-source model (e.g., Llama-3-70B) to directly test whether GPT-4-family models benefit from generator bias.
