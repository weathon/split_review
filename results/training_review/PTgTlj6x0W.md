## Summary

TREANT proposes a tree-based automated red-teaming framework for text-to-image models that uses two strategies—semantic decomposition and sensitive element drowning—to craft adversarial prompts that bypass safety filters. The novel Prompt Parse Tree (PPT) representation encodes objects, attributes, and relations hierarchically, and the framework iteratively refines prompts using LLMs in a black-box setting. The paper reports strong results on DALL·E 3 and Stable Diffusion XL, with an ablation study confirming that both strategies contribute to the overall performance.

## Strengths

- **Novel tree-based prompt representation (PPT).** The Prompt Parse Tree is a principled hierarchical structure that captures object relations and attributes beyond what standard dependency parses offer. The ablation study (Figure 6) shows a clear positive correlation between node count (i.e., decomposition granularity) and success rate, rising from ~40% at 1 node to ~88% at 12 nodes, directly validating the design choice.

- **Ablation confirms synergy of both strategies.** The integrated TREANT outperforms TREANT-SD (semantic decomposition only) and TREANT-SED (drowning only) across all categories in Table 1. This demonstrates that the two-stage design is not redundant; each component contributes independently and they compound when combined.

- **Strong results on the more challenging models.** On DALL·E 3 (the most restrictive commercial model) and Stable Diffusion XL, TREANT achieves 63% and 92% success rates respectively on NSFW-200, substantially outperforming all baselines (next best: SneakyPrompt at 33% on DALL·E 3). Per-category results on NSFW-1k reach 99% in several categories (Political, Public and personal health).

- **Query efficiency in the few-shot regime.** Figure 5 shows TREANT approaching ~88% success within 5 queries, whereas the best baseline (SneakyPrompt) reaches ~75%. This demonstrates practical value for budget-constrained red-teaming scenarios.

- **Fully automated black-box pipeline.** The method requires no manual prompt engineering or model-internal access, using LLMs for PPT construction, prompt refinement, and semantic preservation in a closed loop. This addresses a genuine scalability bottleneck in prior work.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Misleading headline success-rate claim.** The abstract and conclusion claim an "88.5% success rate on a range of platforms, including DALL·E 3 and three versions of Stable Diffusion." However, per-model results from the paper's own Table 2 are: DALL·E 3: 63%, SD XL: 92%, SD v1.4: 22.5%, SD v2.1: 30.5%. The 88.5% figure—which appears to derive from the NSFW-1k dataset on DALL·E 3 alone—does not reflect performance across all platforms as claimed. The average across the four models in Table 2 is approximately 52%. The conclusion conflates a single-dataset result with cross-platform performance, which is a significant misrepresentation of the experimental evidence.

- **Restrictive 6-query budget limits evaluation scope.** The paper imposes a 6-query cap on all methods (Section 4). While applied uniformly, this budget is far below the typical operating regime of RL-based methods like SneakyPrompt (which often use 100+ queries). The paper's central efficiency claim—that TREANT surpasses baselines in few queries—is well-supported, but the comparison says nothing about relative performance under realistic budgets where SneakyPrompt might catch up or surpass TREANT. Moreover, calling this a "fair" comparison (line 78) conflates equal treatment with ecologically valid treatment. The paper should report success-rate-vs-query curves up to a realistic budget (e.g., 500 queries) for all methods.

### Minor

- **"First fully automated" novelty claim is inaccurate.** The paper states it is "to our best knowledge, the first fully automated red teaming framework" (line 16) for black-box T2I testing, but then cites SneakyPrompt (Yang et al., 2023) as an "automated adversarial testing method" (line 49) and uses it as a baseline. SneakyPrompt is also a fully automated black-box approach. The novelty lies in TREANT's specific methodology (tree-based transformation, two-stage strategy), not in being the first automated framework overall.

- **No variance or significance reporting despite multiple runs.** The paper states "we repeat each experiment ten times" (line 78) but reports only point estimates in Tables 1–2 and Figure 5. Without standard deviations, confidence intervals, or significance tests, the reader cannot assess whether TREANT's advantage over baselines is statistically reliable.

- **Dataset generation lacks validation details.** The NSFW-1k dataset is described as generated by ChatGPT with "100 target prompts for 11 different scenarios" (line 80), but no prompt templates, quality checks, or validation of category-to-policy mapping are provided. NSFW-200 is cited from prior work but not described. Reproducibility of the dataset is currently limited.

- **Attention-locality mechanism is asserted without evidence.** The paper explains semantic decomposition's effectiveness by claiming safety filters' attention mechanisms have a "locality" property that makes grouped sensitive phrases more detectable (Section 3.3). This is a plausible hypothesis, but the paper provides no evidence that commercial safety filters (e.g., OpenAI's) actually operate this way. The design rationale would benefit from direct empirical validation or at least a citation establishing the filter architecture.

- **PPT complexity ablation confounds structure with length.** The experiment varying node count from 1 to 12 (Figure 6) does not decouple tree complexity from prompt length (more nodes = longer prompts). It is unclear whether the improved success rate comes from the hierarchical decomposition itself or simply from generating longer, more detailed prompts.

- **Weak performance on SD v1.4 (22.5%) and v2.1 (30.5%) is hand-waved.** The paper attributes this to "weaker semantic understanding" of these models (line 97), but this explanation is speculative and untested. The result substantially narrows the scope of the claimed generality.

- **Mitigation discussion is superficial.** Section 5 suggests using GPT-4o without any experimental evaluation of whether the proposed defenses actually reduce TREANT's success rate. The paper describes mitigations that the framework itself is designed to test, but provides no evidence either way.

## Novel Insights

The reviews surface an interesting tension in how the paper positions its results. The strongest evidence for TREANT's effectiveness comes from the NSFW-1k per-category evaluation on DALL·E 3 (Table 1), where per-category success rates are generally high and the 88.5% headline is arguably defensible. However, the paper then extends this claim to a "range of platforms" in the conclusion, creating a mismatch. This suggests the authors may have organized the paper around the best-performing setting while downplaying the weaker ones. A more honest framing — "strong on capable models (DALL·E 3, SD XL), weaker on earlier SD versions" — would better serve the contribution. Separately, the 6-query limit raises an underexplored question: is TREANT's advantage genuinely about algorithmic sophistication, or does it reflect a methodological specialization for extreme low-query settings that is orthogonal to what prior methods were designed for? The paper does not disentangle these.

## Suggestions

1. **Fix the headline claim.** Clearly state that the 88.5% applies to the NSFW-1k dataset on DALL·E 3, and report the full per-model breakdown of the "overall" success rate in the abstract and conclusion. Alternatively, report a properly weighted cross-model average.

2. **Add statistical reporting.** Include standard deviations or confidence intervals for all main results (Tables 1–2, Figure 5). Perform a significance test (e.g., McNemar's) between TREANT and each baseline.

3. **Validate with larger query budgets.** Run all methods with budgets up to at least 100 queries to rule out the concern that TREANT's advantage is an artifact of the 6-query limit.

4. **Tone down the "first" claim.** Replace "the first fully automated framework" with a more precise claim such as "the first tree-structured automated framework" or simply remove the primacy claim.

5. **Provide the full method in the main text.** Ensure Sections 3.4 and 3.5 are present and complete in the submission (they likely are in the original — this is a formatting advisory).

6. **Decouple prompt length from tree complexity** in the PPT ablation by controlling for token count across node-count conditions.

7. **Analyze failure cases.** Why does the "Sexual" category achieve only 67% on DALL·E 3 while "Political" achieves 99%? Qualitative analysis of failures would strengthen the paper.

## Score and Decision

This paper presents a genuinely novel idea (tree-based prompt transformation for T2I red-teaming) with an interesting two-stage strategy and ablation support. However, the misleading presentation of the headline success rate—conflating a single-dataset result with cross-platform performance—significantly undermines the paper's central claim. Combined with the restrictive evaluation protocol, unsubstantiated novelty claim, and absence of statistical reporting, the paper in its current form cannot be accepted. The core methodology is promising, and the identified weaknesses are addressable, but the paper needs substantial revision before it meets the threshold for publication.
