## Summary

The paper proposes KIRL, a reinforcement learning approach for the integrated toolpath smoothing and feedrate planning problem in CNC machining. The key idea is to formulate the problem as an MDP where an RL agent (PPO or SAC) predicts boundary kinematic states (position, velocity, acceleration) for each segment, while within-segment trajectories are generated in closed form via quintic polynomials that minimize jerk given those boundary conditions. Segment duration is optimized by Brent's method. Experiments on four 2D toolpaths compare KIRL against two decoupled corner-rounding baselines (ICR and CCR).

## Strengths

- **Novel hybrid RL–analytic decomposition of a difficult nonconvex problem**: The paper decomposes the integrated trajectory optimization into RL-predicted boundary conditions and analytic quintic polynomials for within-segment trajectories (Theorem 1, Section 3.1–3.2). This is a creative architecture that reduces the search space while maintaining a provably optimal segment trajectory given the boundary states. This decomposition is the paper's primary intellectual contribution.

- **Clear identification of a real practical limitation**: The paper correctly identifies that decoupled smoothing-then-feedrate-planning approaches produce suboptimal results because the smoothed path ignores kinematic constraints. The motivation for the integrated formulation (Section 2) is well-articulated and grounded in the CNC machining literature.

- **Empirical indication of smoother trajectories**: Over four toolpaths, both KIRL variants achieve lower maximum curvature, RMS acceleration, and RMS jerk compared to the ICR and CCR baselines (Table 1). For example, on the Dolphin toolpath, KIRL-SAC reduces RMS jerk to 0.007 vs. ICR's 0.015 and CCR's 0.017. The pattern across multiple toolpaths provides some evidence that the integrated approach produces kinematically smoother trajectories.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Missing comparison against integrated optimization baselines**: The paper cites Yang et al. (2015), Lin et al. (2019), and Wu et al. (2023) as prior work on *integrated* toolpath smoothing and feedrate planning, but compares KIRL only against decoupled methods (ICR and CCR). Since the paper's contribution is an RL-based approach to the integrated problem, it must establish whether RL offers any advantage over existing classical integrated solvers. A gain over decoupled methods is expected — it confirms advantages of integration already demonstrated by those cited works — and does not validate the RL-specific contribution. The paper acknowledges the lack of publicly available baselines, but this practical difficulty does not eliminate the evidential gap.

2. **No ablation isolating the RL component's benefit**: The core claim is that RL-based prediction of boundary kinematic states is beneficial. However, the paper provides no comparison against (a) handcrafted heuristics (e.g., zero velocity/acceleration at junctions, placing q_i proportional to chord tolerance), (b) random search over the action space, or (c) a simple grid or evolutionary optimizer. Without such ablations, it is impossible to determine whether the observed improvements come from the RL agent or from the quintic polynomial formulation and Brent's-method duration search that are applied *after* the policy's decision. The results may be driven by the structure of the integrated optimization rather than by learned behavior.

3. **Lack of statistical significance reporting**: RL training is inherently stochastic; policy outcomes vary across random seeds. The paper reports all metrics as single numbers per toolpath (Table 1) with no standard deviations, confidence intervals, or statement about the number of independent runs. For any RL-based evaluation, this is a critical omission — the observed differences between KIRL variants and baselines cannot be assessed for reliability. Multi-seed results (≥5 seeds, mean ± std) are necessary.

### Minor

4. **Mismatch between the formal optimization objective and the RL reward function**: The paper states the optimization objective (Eq. 5) as minimizing the *integral of squared jerk* ∫‖j̈(t)‖² dt, which is a standard smoothness measure. However, the RL reward function (Section 3.3) uses `r_i^jerk = ∫[j(t) − j_max]_+ dt` — a penalty for *exceeding the jerk limit*, not a minimization of squared jerk. The RL agent therefore has no direct incentive to reduce jerk below the limit. While the constraint penalty and chord error terms indirectly encourage smoothness, the stated objective and the training signal are misaligned. This weakens the coherence between the problem formulation and the solution.

5. **Key implementation details are underspecified**: (a) The endpoints `p_i^L` and `p_i^R` of the angle-bisector segment in the action space are mentioned but never defined — the paper does not specify how they are computed from the chord error tolerance δ_max, which is critical for reproducibility. (b) The training procedure (network architecture, hyperparameters λ₁, λ₂, λ₃, C_penalty, episode count, how Brent's method is integrated with RL training) is entirely omitted from Algorithm 1, which shows only the inference loop. (c) The paper describes Brent's method for optimizing segment duration but does not discuss the non-convex nature of the objective or the risk of local optima.

6. **Chord error is not reported as a metric across methods**: KIRL explicitly constrains chord error in its optimization, while the baselines (ICR, CCR) may produce geometrically different paths with different maximum chord errors. The paper evaluates smoothness metrics (curvature, turning angle, RMS jerk) without reporting or controlling for chord error across all methods. This makes cross-method smoothness comparisons potentially unfair — lower curvature may simply reflect larger geometric deviation from the original path.
