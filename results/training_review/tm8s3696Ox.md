## Summary

This paper proposes Co-Boosting, a one-shot federated learning (OFL) framework that iteratively improves both synthetic data quality and ensemble quality through a mutually enhancing adversarial loop. High-quality hard samples are generated from the current ensemble, then used to reweight client contributions for a better ensemble, which in turn enables generation of even harder samples, leading to a superior distilled server model. Experiments across five datasets with varying heterogeneity levels, client counts, and heterogeneous architectures show Co-Boosting outperforming existing data-free one-shot FL methods in most settings, with particular practical appeal since it requires no client-side training changes, no additional transmissions, and supports heterogeneous client models.

## Strengths

1. **Novel mutual-boosting framework for OFL.** The paper formalizes a previously overlooked synergy in one-shot FL: synthetic data quality and ensemble quality can be improved simultaneously rather than sequentially. The min-max formulation (Eq. 14) and the full algorithm (Alg. 1) cleanly tie together hard-sample generation, on-the-fly diversity perturbation, and gradient-based ensemble reweighting. The ablation (Table 7, lines 314-327) demonstrates that the full combination (GHS+DHS+EE) outperforms any subset, confirming that the components work synergistically (65.40% vs. 58.46% baseline on SVHN; 47.20% vs. 39.72% on CIFAR-10).

2. **Consistent and often large gains over strong baselines across diverse settings.** In Table 1, Co-Boosting outperforms all baselines (FedAvg, FedDF, F-ADI, F-DAFL, DENSE) on 13 of 15 dataset×heterogeneity combinations, with particularly large margins under high heterogeneity (e.g., CIFAR-10 α=0.05: 47.20% vs. 38.37% for the best baseline, a ~23% relative gain). These gains extend to heterogeneous client architectures (Table 3), varied client counts (Table 6, up to 50 clients), extreme label shift (Table 5, C_cls=2: 36.37% vs. 24.53% for best baseline), and unbalanced local data amounts (Table 4/Fig. 2).

3. **Practical appeal for model-market scenarios.** Co-Boosting requires no modifications to client local training, no extra data or model transmissions, and supports heterogeneous client architectures (Table 3 validates this with CNN, ResNet, MobileNet, ShuffleNet clients). This directly addresses real-world constraints where only pre-trained models (with diverse architectures) are available and privacy prevents additional communication.

4. **Well-designed experimental evaluation.** The paper covers general results (Table 1), heterogeneous architectures (Table 3), unbalanced data (Table 4), extreme label shift (Table 5), varying client counts (Table 6), and component ablations (Table 7). Each core claim has supporting evidence.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The claimed benefit of *iterative* co-boosting (the paper's central contribution over multiple epochs) is not directly validated.** The ablation (Table 7) shows that each component (GHS, DHS, EE) individually helps and that combining all three performs best. However, this does not isolate the effect of running multiple co-boosting *epochs* (the outer loop in Algorithm 1, lines 141-157). A single-pass baseline — generate synthetic samples once with the averaged ensemble, reweight the ensemble once, distill the server — is not compared. Without this, a reader cannot tell whether the iterative loop actually adds value beyond applying the three components in a one-shot fashion. Given that the paper's title and contributions emphasize "co-boosting" as a *progressive mutual enhancement*, this gap undermines the central advertised claim. **This is the most critical unresolved issue.**

2. **Factually inaccurate claim about Table 1 results.** The paper states (line 213): "From the table, we can conclude that Co-Boosting consistently outperforms all other baselines in all settings." This is false. In Table 1, FedDF achieves **97.66%** vs. Co-Boosting's **97.25%** on MNIST (α=0.3), and **83.14%** vs. **83.11%** on FMNIST (α=0.3). While FedDF uses a real validation set (which gives it an unfair advantage, as the paper notes), the quoted claim is still factually incorrect. The paper should accurately characterize its results and discuss these two cases.

### Minor

3. **The roles of the two generator loss terms are conflated and not separately ablated.** The generator loss (Eq. 9) combines the hard-sample-enhanced loss ℒ_H and the adversarial loss ℒ_A. ℒ_H weights cross-entropy by sample difficulty *d*, and minimizing it makes the generator produce samples that the ensemble classifies correctly (i.e., *easier* for the ensemble). The adversarial loss ℒ_A is what actually makes samples hard (for the server). The paper's presentation (lines 86-102) conflates these two mechanisms under the umbrella of "hard sample generation." The ablation (Table 7) only ablates "GHS" (which combines both losses) vs. "DHS" (on-the-fly diversity), never separating ℒ_H from ℒ_A. A clean ablation would disambiguate whether ℒ_H is beneficial, neutral, or counterproductive on its own.

4. **Connection between the diversity perturbation (Eq. 12) and sample hardness is insufficiently justified.** The paper replaces iterative adversarial perturbation with a one-step gradient maximizing u^⊤A_w(x_s) for a random vector u, claiming this "diversifies" and makes samples "harder and more diverse." The link from maximizing alignment with a random direction to *hardness* (as measured by d(x,A_w)) is non-obvious and not explained. The ablation shows DHS helps empirically, which is fine, but the theoretical motivation is unclear.

5. **Multi-round FL comparison is mentioned but results are absent from the main text.** The "More facets" paragraph (line 329) states the paper compares with multi-round FL and includes sensitivity analyses, but no summary or key findings are provided in the body. While the appendix (stripped by the parser) likely contains these results, the main text would benefit from at least a brief summary to support the positioning claim.

### Trivial

- MNIST/FMNIST α=0.3 are the only two Table-1 settings where Co-Boosting does not lead; this deserves acknowledgment in the text alongside the paper's otherwise strong results.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A convergence/stability analysis showing how ensemble accuracy and server accuracy evolve over co-boosting epochs for a representative setting would strengthen the paper.
- An experiment where the synthetic dataset is periodically refreshed rather than accumulated (to check whether older, lower-quality samples from early epochs hurt later performance).
- A discussion of whether the co-boosting loop could potentially widen the gap between synthetic-data-based weights and true-optimal weights if early epochs produce poor data.
- A study of the effect of server initialization on early-epoch generation quality.

## Novel Insights

The most striking observation emerging from this review is that the paper's experimental design — while extensive — systematically conflates two distinct claims: (a) that each proposed *component* (GHM weighting, adversarial loss, on-the-fly perturbation, ensemble reweighting) helps individually, and (b) that the *iterative co-boosting loop* over multiple epochs provides additional benefit beyond a single-pass application of these components. The ablation table cleanly supports (a), but it does not address (b) at all. This is a non-trivial gap because the framework's novelty label ("co-boosting") implies a dynamic, progressive enhancement over time, yet a skeptical reader could argue that the gains come entirely from the individual components applied once. The authors should isolate this by comparing a T=1 (single epoch, all three components) baseline against their T>1 results.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a single-epoch baseline** to the ablation study: run one pass of (generate data → reweight ensemble → distill server) with all three components (GHS+DHS+EE), and compare to the multi-epoch result. If the gap is small, reframe the contribution around the component design rather than the iterative loop. If the gap is large, show convergence curves to demonstrate the benefit of iteration.

2. **Correct the overclaim on line 213.** Acknowledge the two FedDF-leading cases (MNIST α=0.3, FMNIST α=0.3), note that FedDF uses real validation data (an unfair advantage), and temper the claim to "consistently outperforms all data-free baselines in all settings" or "outperforms in 13/15 settings."

3. **Add an ablation separating ℒ_H from ℒ_A** in the generator loss. Run (ℒ_H only), (ℒ_A only), and (ℒ_H+ℒ_A) to disambiguate their individual contributions. If ℒ_H alone is neutral or harmful, the paper should explain why it is retained.

4. **Add a brief summary of the multi-round FL comparison** to the main text (e.g., "Co-Boosting achieves X% of the performance of FedAvg over Y rounds while using only one round"), even if the full results are in the appendix.

The paper tackles an important problem, proposes a well-motivated framework, and shows strong empirical results. However, the two major weaknesses — the missing validation of the iterative co-boosting loop (the paper's core novelty) and the factually inaccurate claim in the main results — prevent acceptance in the current form. The paper's strengths (novel framework, practical appeal, extensive experiments, strong results in most settings) are genuine, but the central advertised contribution remains unvalidated.
