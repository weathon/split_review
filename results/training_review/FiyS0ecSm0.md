## Summary

This paper introduces LIPS, a neuro-symbolic framework for proving Olympiad-level algebraic inequalities in Lean. The key insight is a principled division of labor: scaling tactics (applying known lemmas) are handled by symbolic enumeration with CAD-based counterexample pruning, while rewriting tactics (equivalent transformations) are generated by LLMs to navigate an infinite space. A two-stage goal selection pipeline (symbolic filtering via homogeneity/decoupling scores, then LLM-based neural ranking) guides proof search. On 161 problems across three benchmarks, LIPS achieves state-of-the-art results, proving 56/61 Olympiad problems (vs. 41 for the previous best approach) and producing human-readable, Lean-verified proofs.

## Strengths

- **State-of-the-art empirical performance**: LIPS proves 56 out of 61 Olympiad-level inequalities within 90 minutes, a substantial improvement over the previous best approach (41 problems). On the full 161-problem benchmark, LIPS outperforms CAD and MMA by 14–24 percentage points. These gains are demonstrated across multiple datasets and backed by Lean verification.

- **Well-motivated neuro-symbolic division of labor**: The paper makes a principled argument that scaling tactics (finite, enumerable, requiring correctness checks) map naturally to symbolic methods, while rewriting tactics (infinite space, requiring mathematical intuition) map naturally to LLM generation. This design choice is concretely illustrated with a running example (162 possible AM-GM pattern matches → only 6 correct after symbolic pruning).

- **Efficient proof search**: LIPS averages 15.75 search loops per successful proof, only 2.17× the oracle's 7.25 loops (Figure 3). The scaling tactic pruning achieves 7.92% higher pruning ratio than AIPS. The paper demonstrates that the system is not tightly coupled to a single LLM (Mathstral 7B, LLaMA-3 8B, DeepSeek-chat V2.5 all achieve comparable performance) and that success rates improve monotonically with larger tactic libraries.

- **Human-readable, formally verified proofs**: Unlike CAD/MMA which are black-box decision procedures, LIPS produces step-by-step proofs in Lean that are both human-readable and formally verified, a qualitative advantage over symbolic baselines.

A core design claim about the utility of these domain-specific metrics is therefore untested, and the contribution of this component is not empirically validated.

Table 1 (an image in the submission) likely contains this information, but the text should explicitly identify the baseline and contextualize the result — especially because 41 happens to equal the size of the ChenNEQ dataset, suggesting the prior method may solve all ChenNEQ problems but zero MO-INT problems. This ambiguity makes the central claim harder to evaluate on its own.

- **Per-dataset breakdown of results is not discussed in the text.** The paper reports overall success rates (e.g., CAD 59.0%, MMA 57.1%) and the aggregate 56/61 Olympiad result, but does not break down performance by dataset in the prose. Table 1 (an image) presumably contains per-dataset numbers, but the reader should not have to rely on a figure to understand the basic experimental results.

- **Analysis of LLM-generated rewrite quality is absent.** The paper describes using LLMs to generate rewriting tactics (Section 3.2), noting that rewriting tactics are "equivalent transformations." However, it does not analyze what fraction of LLM-suggested rewrites are actually productive versus leading to dead ends, nor does it explicitly discuss how the system handles an LLM-proposed rewrite that is mathematically unsound (beyond the implicit fact that Lean checks the final proof). Understanding this failure mode would help assess the system's robustness and guide future improvements.

- **Choice of top-10 filtering size is stated without supporting data.** The paper fixes the filtered goal set size to 10 because it is "the largest size that ensures GPT-4's efficacy" (Section 5.1), but presents no experiment or analysis supporting this threshold. While Figure 4(b) later shows the impact of varying this size on ChenNEQ, the relationship between the stated justification and the empirical finding is not made explicit.

Given the small dataset size (41 problems), a difference of a few problems is meaningful, and exact numbers are needed.

Nevertheless, wall-clock time and runtime breakdowns (e.g., time spent in CAD vs. LLM calls vs. tactic application) would help practitioners assess practical trade-offs.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, a noteworthy observation emerging from the review is that the *scope of failure cases* matters as much as the success rate. The fact that LIPS fails on 5/61 Olympiad problems — while no method solves all 61 — raises the question of whether these failures cluster around a specific mathematical pattern (e.g., problems requiring a particular rewriting creativity that the LLM cannot supply, or problems where the symbolic pruning is too aggressive). Characterizing this "hard core" of problems would be a valuable diagnostic for the community, potentially revealing whether the neuro-symbolic boundary needs to be redrawn for particularly resistant cases.

## Score and Decision

The paper makes a genuine contribution: it identifies a principled division of labor between LLMs and symbolic methods for inequality proving, implements a working system that achieves clear SOTA results on challenging benchmarks, and produces human-readable Lean-verified proofs that symbolic solvers cannot match. The main empirical gap is the lack of an ablation for the symbolic filtering component, which prevents full validation of one design claim. However, this does not undermine the overall contribution — even without symbolic filtering, the core insight (LLM for rewriting, symbolic for scaling) would still be validated by the overall system performance. With the suggested ablations and clarifications, this would be a strong paper.
