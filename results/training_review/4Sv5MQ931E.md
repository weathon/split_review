## Summary

MSR-ViR proposes a modular VideoQA framework that decomposes questions into structured execution policies (JSON-format module calls), uses these policies to invoke off-the-shelf temporal (UniVTG) and spatial (YOLO-World) grounding modules, and feeds the resulting grounded frames to a multimodal LLM for answer generation. An Alternate Self-reflection Training strategy alternately fine-tunes the multimodal LLM via SFT and the question parser LLM via DPO (using the LLM's loss as a reward signal). Experiments on NExT-QA, STAR-sub, and NExT-GQA show competitive or state-of-the-art results.

## Strengths

- **Modular, interpretable-by-design architecture**: The question parser generates explicit JSON-format execution policies that decompose complex VideoQA questions into a sequence of sub-tasks (e.g., DetectAct → LocateObj). This provides structural interpretability — a traceable, human-readable reasoning path from question to answer — that is absent in end-to-end black-box VideoQA systems (Section 3.1, Figure 3). The policies are directly inspectable and show which modules were called and in what order.

- **Alternate self-reflection training with DPO demonstrably improves performance**: The proposed training strategy (Section 3.4) uses the multimodal LLM's loss as a reward to refine the question parser's policies via DPO. The ablation study (Table 3) shows that removing self-reflection reduces average NExT-QA accuracy by 1.5 points and degrades temporal grounding (mIoU, Acc@GQA), confirming its empirical value even though the reward assumption is not independently validated.

- **Strong empirical results across multiple benchmarks**: MSR-ViR achieves top results on NExT-QA overall accuracy and STAR-sub, outperforming prior grounding-based methods (LSTP, SeViLa, GCG) particularly on temporal and interaction questions (Table 1). On NExT-GQA, it achieves the best mIoP and Acc@GQA among comparably-sized models (Table 2), demonstrating simultaneous improvements in QA accuracy and temporal grounding precision.

- **Principled ablation study**: Table 3 systematically ablates self-reflection training, the spatial localizer, global video representation, and instruction prompts. Each removal causes measurable degradation, providing evidence that all components contribute to the overall performance.

- **Practical reuse of off-the-shelf models**: MoST-Grounding dynamically assembles existing models (UniVTG for temporal grounding, YOLO-World for object detection) according to learned policies, avoiding the need to train specialized grounding modules from scratch (Section 3.2).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The interpretability claim is asserted but not evaluated.** The paper prominently motivates interpretability as its central challenge and claims that execution policies "provide a clear reasoning path from the question to the answer, providing interpretability for our framework" (Section 1). However, the evaluation (Section 4) measures only VideoQA accuracy, temporal grounding metrics (mIoP, mIoU), and grounded QA accuracy (Acc@GQA). No experiment assesses whether the generated policies are actually understandable to humans, whether they faithfully reflect the model's decision process, or whether they provide practical transparency benefits. The only qualitative evidence is a single example in Figure 4. While the modular design provides *structural* interpretability (the policies are by construction inspectable), the paper would need some form of interpretability evaluation (even a simple user rating or faithfulness check) to fully substantiate claims made in the abstract and introduction that the method "provides interpretable reasoning paths" and that existing models "lack interpretability as they can neither present a reasoning path nor indicate where the answer is derived from."

- **The comparison against direct baselines is confounded by auxiliary inputs.** In Table 1, MSR-ViR is compared against Qwen-VL and Llava-Next trained with uniformly sampled frames. However, MSR-ViR additionally provides the multimodal LLM with (a) a global video representation (averaged pooled frames) and (b) a guiding prompt explaining the visual input components (Section 3.3). The ablation study (Table 3) confirms that removing either auxiliary input reduces accuracy, showing these components contribute to the gain. The direct baselines do not receive these inputs, so the reported improvement cannot be attributed solely to the MoST-Grounding module — it may partially or entirely result from the extra inputs. A controlled baseline that gives the same LLM the global representation and guiding prompt *without* MoST-Grounding is needed to isolate the grounding module's contribution. Without this, the headline improvement is not properly attributed.

- **The STAR evaluation excludes two of four question types without justification that this is fundamental (as opposed to convenient).** The paper creates STAR-sub by excluding Prediction and Feasibility questions, stating they "lack temporal and spatial grounding in videos, making them unsuitable for our framework" (Section 4.1). While the paper acknowledges this, it raises a question about scope: if the framework cannot handle 17.5% of the STAR benchmark, this limitation should be discussed more explicitly. Can the framework be extended? Is there a fundamental constraint, or is it merely that no modules were implemented for these types? The answer to this affects how general the contribution is.

### Minor

- **The DPO training loop's core assumption is stated but not validated.** The approach assumes that lower multimodal LLM loss implies more reasonable policies (Section 3.4). This is plausible but unverified: a policy that produces lower loss could exploit spurious correlations (e.g., selecting frames that are easy regardless of relevance). The paper does not check whether DPO-preferred policies actually yield higher grounding IoU or whether the loss-based reward correlates with policy quality in a meaningful way. The ablation shows the training *works*, but why it works is less clear.

- **Key details of the DPO preference pair generation are missing.** The paper states: "We prompt the question parser LLM to view the same question from multiple perspectives, generating different modular policies" (Section 3.4). It does not specify how many candidates are generated per question, how the "perspectives" are varied, the temperature settings used for diverse generation, or the policy for handling ties. This makes the procedure difficult to reproduce.

- **The global video representation is under-specified.** The paper says "we sample several frames from the video at regular intervals" (Section 3.3) but does not state the number of frames sampled for this global representation. The implementations paragraph (Section 4.1) specifies frame counts for the direct baselines and grounded inputs but not for the global representation, leaving a reproducibility gap.

- **Only two of seven module types are described in the main text.** DetectAct (temporal, using UniVTG) and LocateObj (spatial, using YOLO-World) are described in Section 3.2. The remaining five modules are relegated to Table 4 at the end of the paper without explanation in the main body. This makes it hard for the reader to assess the full capability of the module set without cross-referencing.

### Trivial

- No statistical significance or variance is reported for any experiment. While single-run evaluation is the norm for large-scale VideoQA benchmarks, noting this would help transparency.
- The recursive call mechanism for composing modules is mentioned (Section 3.1) but not elaborated; the exact JSON schema of the policy format is not defined.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A controlled baseline that gives the same backbone LLM the global representation and guiding prompt *without* MoST-Grounding output, using uniformly sampled frames instead. This would cleanly isolate the grounding module's contribution.
- A small human evaluation (e.g., 20–50 examples where raters indicate whether the policy helps them understand how the answer was derived) to substantiate the interpretability claim beyond structural arguments.
- A random-policy baseline where the question parser is replaced by a random or fixed policy generator, to quantify the value of learned policies independent of the grounding modules themselves.
- Sensitivity analysis on the number of policy candidates generated per question for DPO training, and on the alternating training period (200 steps).

## Suggestions

1. **Isolate the grounding contribution in the comparison.** Add an ablation (or a new row in Table 1) where the same backbone LLM receives both the global representation and guiding prompt but uses uniformly sampled frames instead of MoST-Grounding outputs. This directly measures what the grounding module adds beyond the auxiliary inputs.

2. **Add a lightweight interpretability evaluation.** A small-scale human study where annotators rate whether policies help them understand the answer's derivation, or a faithfulness metric comparing policy-module choices to human rationales (if available), would substantially strengthen the core claim without requiring a massive user study.

3. **Document DPO details.** Specify the number of policy candidates generated per question, the diversity-promoting prompt, temperature settings, and tie-breaking rules for preference pair construction. This is essential for reproducibility.

4. **Specify the global representation frame count.** State explicitly how many frames are sampled at regular intervals to produce the global video representation (Section 3.3).

5. **Discuss the STAR-sub limitation more prominently.** Address whether the excluded question types (Prediction, Feasibility) could be handled by extending the module set, or whether they require fundamentally different capabilities.

## Score and Decision

The paper presents a well-designed modular framework with a clever self-reflection training strategy and achieves strong empirical results across multiple benchmarks. The core weaknesses are: (1) the interpretability claim is structurally motivated but not empirically evaluated, (2) the comparison against direct baselines is confounded by auxiliary inputs that baselines do not receive, and (3) several experimental details are under-specified. These are significant but addressable — they do not invalidate the framework's contributions or its competitive results. The modular design and self-reflection training represent genuine steps toward more transparent VideoQA systems.
