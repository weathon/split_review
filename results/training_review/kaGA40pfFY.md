## Summary

The paper proposes "Rationality of Thought" (RoT), a zero-shot prompting method inspired by Kahneman's System 2 (deliberate, rational) thinking. RoT structures the model's reasoning into six steps (Identification, Decomposition, Execution, Reflection, Computation, Assessment). The authors construct a 464-question cognitive bias dataset covering 29 bias types, show that GPT‑4's accuracy on this dataset improves from ~78–82% (inferred) to 97.4% with RoT, and report modest gains over zero-shot CoT on standard reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, SVAMP, AQUA‑RAT, ARC). The paper is motivated by an interesting cross-disciplinary question—whether cognitive-science-inspired prompting can improve LLM reasoning.

## Strengths

- **Novel, psychologically grounded method.** RoT explicitly operationalizes the System 2 (deliberate/rational) process from dual-process theory into a structured zero-shot prompt, distinguishing it from ad‑hoc prompt engineering. The six-step framework (Section 2, Figure 1) is clearly conceptualized.

- **Ablation study provides internal validation.** The stepwise ablation (Section 3.4) on GPT‑4 shows that removing any of the six steps degrades performance, with step 3 (Execution/Calculation) causing the largest drop ( −9%). This supports the claim that each component contributes positively and that the steps are not equally redundant.

- **Zero-shot practical advantage.** RoT achieves its gains without task-specific few-shot examples. Since the same prompt structure is applied across all tasks, this offers a practical advantage in generality over few-shot CoT, which requires hand-crafted exemplars per dataset.

- **Honest reporting of boundary conditions.** The paper transparently shows that RoT can *degrade* performance on weaker models (LLaMA2‑13B drops by 12.1% with RoT vs. Direct; GPT‑3.5‑turbo gains only 1.5%), and acknowledges that the method is only effective for sufficiently capable models (Section 3.3). This candid reporting is a strength.

- **Cognitive bias dataset fills a gap.** The dataset covering 29 bias types with 464 professionally sourced and filtered questions (Section 3.1) is a useful resource for the community, despite the contamination concern discussed below.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The RoT prompt template is not provided.** The paper describes the six steps conceptually but never shows the actual prompt text (the exact wording given to the model). Without this, the method cannot be independently reproduced, compared with alternative zero-shot strategies, or analyzed for confounds (e.g., prompt length, phrasing artifacts). This is a fundamental reproducibility gap. (Section 2, Section 3.4)

- **No control for prompt length/detail confound.** The baselines are "Direct answer" (minimal instruction) and "Let's think step by step" (2 words of instruction). RoT is a long, multi-sentence, structured instruction. The improvement could stem from generic prompt detail rather than the specific "rational thinking" framing. The ablation (removing steps) partially addresses this by showing differential effects, but a proper control—a zero-shot prompt of comparable length and complexity without the cognitive-bias framing—is absent. Without this, the core attribution to "rationality of thought" is not uniquely supported. (Section 3.2, Section 3.4)

- **Selective framing in the abstract.** The abstract reports improvements over *few-shot* CoT (GSM8K +0.4, AQUA‑RAT +4.8, ARC‑c +0.7) while burying the zero-shot CoT comparison. The paper's own Table 3 shows RoT's advantages over zero-shot CoT are different (e.g., the text reports a 4.1 point improvement over CoT‑zero‑shot on ARC‑c, and a 6 point improvement on AQUA‑RAT). The abstract's framing is technically correct but selectively highlights the weakest comparison, making the gains appear smaller and less relevant than they are under the appropriate zero-shot baseline. (Abstract, Section 4.3)

- **Self-Consistency mentioned as a baseline but never evaluated.** The introduction claims RoT "outperformed other reasoning enhancement techniques like Chain-of-Thought and Self-Consistency," yet Self-Consistency is never tested in any experiment. This claim is unsubstantiated and should be removed or supported. (Section 1, line 19)

### Minor

- **Cognitive bias dataset contamination risk.** The test set for the cognitive bias task was partially generated by GPT‑4 (348 of 464 questions were GPT‑4‑generalized from 116 seed questions). While the authors performed manual screening and all methods (Direct, CoT, RoT) were evaluated on the same dataset (so relative comparisons remain fair), the absolute accuracy numbers and the 18.7% improvement headline may be inflated. A cleaner test set using only externally sourced questions would strengthen confidence. (Section 3.1)

- **Absolute baseline on cognitive bias task not clearly reported.** The paper reports "GPT‑4_RoT (97.4)" and an "18.7% improvement" but never explicitly states the direct-answer accuracy of GPT‑4 on this dataset. The reader has to infer the baseline (~78–82%). This is a minor presentation gap. (Section 3.3)

- **Dataset construction procedure underspecified.** The five filtering criteria for biases (Section 3.1) are listed but their operational application is not described in a reproducible way (e.g., how was "replicable in LLMs" judged? How were candidate questions screened for correctness?). This limits the dataset's reusability.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A control experiment comparing RoT against a zero-shot prompt of matched length and structural complexity (e.g., a generic multi-step instruction without the "rational thinking" framing) would substantially strengthen the attribution.
- Qualitative case studies showing specific bias questions where RoT changes the answer, and analyzing whether the model genuinely follows the rational steps vs. benefiting from extended generation.
- Confidence intervals or statistical significance tests for the reported improvements, especially for small gains on reasoning benchmarks.
- Re-evaluation on a cognitive bias test set free of GPT‑4 contamination (e.g., using only the 116 seed questions or sourcing from third parties).

## Removed Points

*These points are flagged to be removed; treat them with caution.*

- **"The 18.7% improvement is relative to an unreported baseline"** (partial removal): The baseline is inferable (~78–82% from 97.4−18.7 or 97.4/1.187). The paper should state it explicitly, but the criticism that it's "impossible to assess whether the claimed gain is large or small" is overstated — a gain from ~80% to ~97% on a challenging test is clearly large.
- **"The claim that RoT 'does not require additional manual annotations' is misleading because the cognitive bias dataset required manual curation"**: The paper's claim is about RoT *as a prompting method* (which is indeed zero-shot and requires no few-shot annotation for each new task), not about dataset creation. This is a misreading.
- **"No confidence intervals or statistical tests"**: This is common practice for large-scale LLM benchmarking with single-run evaluation. While desirable, it is not a weakness unique to this paper.
- **"The mathematical formulation (Equation 2) is trivial"**: The equation formalizes the optimization objective. It is not intended to be a structural innovation but a framing device. Criticizing it for not providing structural insight is scope creep.
- **"The System 1 vs. System 2 analogy is used as post-hoc explanation, not as a design principle"**: The paper explicitly states that RoT is *designed* to elicit System 2-like reasoning in Section 2. This claim contradicts the paper's stated design rationale.
- **General claims about "the paper overstates the analogy" or "treats cognitive bias test performance as a direct measure of rational thinking ability without validating this for LLMs"**: These are opinion-based and not specific enough to constitute actionable weaknesses.
- **"Missing related works"**: Cannot be confirmed without external sources.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The reviews do not surface a genuinely novel observation about the method or results that the paper itself missed.

## Suggestions

1. **Release the exact RoT prompt template** in the paper or a public repository. This is the single most important change for reproducibility.
2. **Restructure the abstract and comparisons** to use zero-shot CoT as the primary baseline, while noting few-shot CoT results as a secondary reference. This would present the contribution more fairly.
3. **Add a length-matched control prompt** (e.g., a generic "First, identify key information. Second, decompose the problem. Third, analyze..." without cognitive bias framing) to support the attribution to rationality rather than prompt verbosity.
4. **Clearly report the GPT‑4 direct-answer baseline accuracy** on the cognitive bias dataset as an explicit number.
5. **Either remove the Self-Consistency claim** from the introduction or add an experiment comparing against it.
6. **Provide more detail on the dataset screening process** (inter-annotator agreement, how the five filtering criteria were applied) to make the resource reproducible.

## Score and Decision

The paper proposes a genuinely interesting method with a strong cognitive-science motivation and provides evidence on multiple benchmarks. However, the evaluation is weakened by (a) the missing prompt template (reproducibility), (b) the lack of a length/detail control for the attribution mechanism, and (c) the selective framing in the abstract. None of these are fatal — the method is well-conceptualized, the ablation is informative, and the results on standard benchmarks are concrete — but they collectively reduce confidence in the headline claims. With the prompt disclosure, a length control experiment, and honest reframing, the paper could become a solid contribution. In its current form, it is borderline.
