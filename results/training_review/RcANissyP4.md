## Summary

The paper proposes SelfEval, a method for evaluating text-to-image diffusion models by using the generative model itself—rather than external discriminative models like CLIP or LLMs—as a discriminative evaluator. SelfEval estimates the likelihood of real image-text pairs under the diffusion model and measures classification accuracy across six fine-grained reasoning tasks (attribute binding, color, count, shape, spatial, text corruption). The method shows alignment with human evaluations across pixel and latent diffusion models with different text encoders, and achieves non-trivial Winoground image scores where prior ELBO-based approaches gave 0%.

## Strengths

- **Eliminates reliance on external models and their biases**: SelfEval uses the generative model itself, avoiding documented issues with CLIP score sensitivity to the exact CLIP backbone (Figure 2 shows ranking flips), CLIP's poor performance on compositional reasoning (Table 6: CLIP near random on spatial/text corruption), and LLM hallucination in VPEval/LLMScore (Figure 7). This is a principled and well-motivated design choice.

- **Demonstrates meaningful alignment with human evaluations**: In Tables 1–2, SelfEval agrees with human judgment on 9/10 splits for PDM and 9/10 for LDM. More importantly, Figure 6 shows SelfEval is the *only* metric with positive Spearman correlation on **both** PDM and LDM simultaneously (all other metrics show negative correlation on at least one model type). This cross-model consistency is a genuinely novel empirical finding.

- **Enables non-trivial Winoground image scores**: Table 3 shows SelfEval achieves 7.25–14.00% image score on Winoground while concurrent ELBO-based work (li2023diffusion) yields 0%. The paper correctly identifies that ELBO estimates are not comparable across different images, which explains the failure.

- **Provides interpretable fine-grained evaluation across six reasoning tasks**: The benchmark construction from TIFA, ARO, and CLEVR allows per-task diagnosis of model weaknesses (e.g., CLIP-based models perform worse than random on counting; both model types struggle with text corruption). This granularity is useful for guiding model improvements.

- **Thorough critique of existing metrics**: The paper convincingly demonstrates three concrete problems with existing metrics—CLIP model sensitivity, CLIP's own task limitations, and LLM hallucination—each supported by empirical evidence (Figures 1, 7; Tables 5, 6).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Evaluation uses real images while claiming to evaluate generation faithfulness**: SelfEval computes accuracy on ground-truth (real) image-text pairs (explicitly stated in lines 34, 54, 264), whereas human evaluations and all other metrics operate on *generated* images. The paper argues (lines 192–193) that "text faithfulness of a generative model inherently also measures its vision-language reasoning abilities," but this link is an assumption, not a validated fact. The correlation shown in Tables 1–2 and Figure 6 is between *real-image* discriminative accuracy and *generated-image* human judgment—a meaningful but indirect proxy. A direct experiment (e.g., computing SelfEval accuracy on images generated by the same model) would substantially strengthen the paper's central claim. As it stands, the metric is better characterized as evaluating the text *understanding* capabilities of diffusion models rather than the faithfulness of their *generations*.

2. **Limited model diversity and partially private training data**: Only four models are evaluated. Of these, three are trained internally on a private dataset ("DATASET"). Only one model (LDM-CLIP) uses publicly available weights. This limits both the reproducibility of the results and the generalizability of the claims, especially the strong "first automated metric to show high agreement" framing.

### Minor

1. **Overclaim in the "first" framing**: The abstract states SelfEval is "the first automated metric to show a high degree of agreement" with human evaluations. However, in Table 1 (PDM), CLIPScore agrees with human rankings on 9/10 splits—already showing substantial agreement. The paper's real novelty is that SelfEval maintains positive correlation on *both* PDM and LDM simultaneously (Figure 6), which CLIPScore does not. The framing should emphasize cross-model consistency rather than "first" agreement.

2. **Unvalidated likelihood bound**: The Jensen-based lower bound (Eq. 5) is used without any analysis of its tightness. The bound is mathematically valid (the ≥ direction is correct—the critic who claimed otherwise is mistaken), and the missing 1/N factor cancels for ranking decisions, but the approximation quality could vary across models or captions and is never characterized. A small ablation comparing bound-based rankings to a more accurate (but expensive) Monte Carlo estimate would address this.

3. **No statistical significance for correlation measures**: The Spearman correlations in Figure 6 are reported without confidence intervals or p-values. Given that each ρ is computed over only 5 tasks × 2 models = 10 data points, this limits the reliability of the claim that SelfEval is "the only metric that positively correlates with both types of models."

### Trivial

1. The derivation of Eq. 5 applies Jensen's inequality to claim \( \log(\sum_n a_n) \geq \sum_n \log(a_n) \), applying Jensen to the sum directly without explicitly inserting the \(1/N\) factor. While the inequality itself is mathematically valid (the correct bound \( \log(N) + \frac{1}{N}\sum_n \log(a_n) \) is provably ≥ the paper's RHS), the derivation skips steps and could confuse readers.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Include at least one additional open-source model (e.g., SDXL) to broaden validation beyond the three internally-trained models.
- Report confidence intervals for the Spearman correlations in Figure 6 and include p-values or bootstrap estimates.

## Suggestions

1. **Reframe the contribution more precisely**: Replace "evaluating text faithfulness of generative models" with "evaluating text understanding capabilities of diffusion models as a proxy for generation faithfulness." This would make the metric's real-image evaluation transparent and honest while preserving the paper's genuine contribution.

2. **Add a direct validation experiment**: Compute SelfEval accuracy on images generated by each evaluated model (using the same benchmark captions as prompts). Plot SelfEval(real) vs SelfEval(generated) vs human ratings. This would either validate the proxy assumption or reveal its limits, either of which is valuable.

3. **Add confidence intervals to Figure 6**: Bootstrap the Spearman ρ values or report exact p-values so readers can assess the reliability of the correlation differences across metrics.

## Score and Decision

SelfEval tackles an important problem with a conceptually elegant approach. Its core insight—using the generative model itself as a discriminative evaluator to avoid external model biases—is both novel and well-motivated. The empirical evidence shows genuine alignment with human evaluations, particularly the unique cross-model consistency (positive correlation on both PDM and LDM). However, the gap between evaluating on real images versus evaluating generation faithfulness remains the central unresolved limitation. The paper is transparent about this gap but does not fully bridge it. Combined with limited model diversity and some overclaiming, these issues prevent the paper from being a clean accept. With a more precise reframing and one additional validation experiment, this would be a strong paper.
