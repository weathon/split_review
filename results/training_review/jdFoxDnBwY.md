## Summary

The paper proposes Part321, a framework for recognizing 3D object parts from a single 2D image using only one annoted 3D mesh per category. It builds a category-level neural mesh via two types of correspondence: mesh-to-mesh (to share features across differing geometries) and mesh-to-image (to align 3D vertex features with 2D image features via contrastive learning on synthetic images). At inference, part pose, scale, and shape are jointly optimized through a differentiable render-and-compare procedure with geometric consistency constraints. Experiments on 2D part segmentation (VehiclePart3D, PartImageNet, UDA-Part) and 3D detection (on synthetic 3D DST data) are reported.

## Strengths

- **Novel formulation for 3D part recognition from a 2D image with minimal annotation.** The idea of building a deformable, category-level neural mesh using two types of correspondence (mesh-to-mesh, mesh-to-image) is well-motivated and addresses a genuine gap: existing work either requires dense 2D part labels or operates directly on 3D input. The 3D DST results (Table 4) provide quantitative evidence that the pipeline can recover 3D part configurations from images.

- **Mesh-to-mesh correspondence overcomes geometric variance without additional part labels.** By learning unsupervised geometry descriptors (PointNet++ on point clouds, following Sun et al. 2021) and computing dense vertex correspondences across 30 unlabeled meshes, the method builds a shared category-level feature bank. The deformation network (Section 3.2, Figure 7) demonstrates that this allows a single annotated mesh to be reshaped into diverse geometries while preserving part labels — a clear solution to the intra-category shape variation challenge.

- **Mesh-to-image correspondence via contrastive learning avoids 2D pixel-level part annotation.** The use of part-agnostic synthetic images (generated by DST) to train a feature extractor that aligns 3D vertex features with 2D pixels (Section 3.3) is a clever design. It means the method never needs human-labeled 2D segmentations, which is the primary efficiency claim.

- **Strong 2D segmentation results against established baselines.** On VehiclePart3D (Table 1), Part321 achieves 37.9 mIoU, outperforming DeepLabV3+ with pseudo-labeling (30.6) and SegFormer (25.9). Similar gains are shown on PartImageNet (Table 2) and UDA-Part (Table 3). These results hold even though the method solves the harder 3D-to-2D projection problem.

- **Introduction of VehiclePart3D dataset.** The paper collects and releases a dataset with 279 part-annotated real images, 47 CAD models, and synthetic training images across 5 vehicle categories — a useful resource for future work on one-shot 3D part recognition.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The 2D segmentation comparison is confounded by asymmetric access to unlabeled 3D data.** Part321 uses 30 unlabeled 3D meshes per category to learn mesh-to-mesh correspondence and train the deformation network. The 2D baselines (SegFormer, DeepLabV3+) are trained only on segmentation maps rendered from the single annotated mesh (Section 4.1: "segmentation maps generated from the same annotated CAD model"). The paper states "the baselines always have the same amount of *labeled* training images" — but the word "labeled" is doing critical work. Part321 benefits from 30 unlabeled 3D shapes that the baselines never access. While the "DeepLab w/ Pseudo" baseline partially mitigates this by leveraging unlabeled images, it does not get the 3D meshes. This means the reported advantage in Tables 1-3 may reflect richer geometric priors from more diverse shapes rather than the part-reasoning mechanism specifically. A controlled experiment where baselines also receive pseudo-labels propagated across the 30 meshes (via the same mesh-to-mesh correspondence or a simpler baseline alignment) is needed to isolate the true contribution.

- **3D part detection accuracy is evaluated only on synthetic data (3D DST).** The core claim of the paper is "recognizing 3D object parts from a 2D image." Yet Table 4, which measures 3D pose accuracy, Chamfer distance, and 3D IoU, uses only synthetic images where ground-truth CAD models are perfectly aligned. For real images (VehiclePart3D, PartImageNet, UDA-Part), the evaluation is limited to 2D projection metrics (mIoU). While qualitative 3D results on real images are shown (Figures 4, 6), there is no quantitative evidence that the inferred 3D configurations are accurate on real data — the very setting the paper motivates. This is a meaningful gap between the claimed capability and the empirical validation.

### Minor

- **No baselines (not even simplified ones) for the 3D task.** The paper acknowledges that "3D part detection from images using only one annotation lacks comparable baselines" (Section 4), and it positions itself as "pioneering" rather than SOTA for 3D. This honesty is appreciated, but a simple baseline — e.g., projecting 3D parts from a category-level mean shape or adapting a 3D pose estimation method — would significantly strengthen the claim that the 3D results are meaningful. As it stands, Table 4 reports numbers without any reference point for comparison.

- **The ablation study (Table 5) does not ablate the mesh-to-mesh or mesh-to-image correspondence components.** The ablation tests part scaling, geometry consistency loss, and part deformation — all at inference time. However, the mesh-to-mesh correspondence (feature bank construction and deformation network training) and the mesh-to-image correspondence (contrastive feature learning) are the paper's two main methodological claims. Their individual contributions are not isolated. An ablation that removes the mesh-to-mesh correspondence (e.g., using a fixed template without deformation) or the mesh-to-image correspondence (e.g., using a different feature alignment method) would be informative.

- **"One-shot" framing overstates the method's practical data requirements.** While it is true that only one mesh needs part *annotation*, the method requires 30 unlabeled 3D meshes per category plus an external diffusion pipeline (DST) for synthetic image generation. The introduction says "annotation process is much faster compared with 2D approaches," which is accurate for annotation effort specifically. However, readers could reasonably infer "one-shot" to mean minimal total data requirements. The paper would benefit from explicitly discussing the practical overhead of acquiring 30 meshes per category.

- **Some implementation details are unclear.** The "background term" in the contrastive loss (Section 3.3) is mentioned but never defined. The negative set construction in Equation 3 is implicitly handled by the spatial neighborhood condition but could be stated more explicitly. These are clarity issues rather than methodological flaws.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, the reviews surface an important meta-point about benchmarking "pioneering" tasks: the paper dances between two framings — "pioneering" for 3D (no baselines needed) and "SOTA" for 2D (baselines exist) — but the two are coupled. The 2D SOTA claim is used to indirectly validate the 3D capability ("if it works in 2D projection, the 3D parts must be accurate"), yet the experimental asymmetry (30 unlabeled meshes for Part321 vs. 1 for baselines) muddies this inference. A cleaner evaluation strategy would explicitly separate the validation of the 3D geometry (via synthetic benchmarks or real 3D ground truth) from the 2D segmentation comparison (with carefully controlled data access).

## Suggestions

1. **Run a controlled 2D comparison where baselines also receive pseudo-labels propagated across the 30 unlabeled meshes** (using the same mesh-to-mesh correspondence or a simple nearest-neighbor shape alignment). This isolates whether Part321's advantage comes from the part-reasoning mechanism or simply from having more diverse shape priors.

2. **Add at least one simplified 3D baseline to Table 4** — e.g., a per-part 3D bounding box regressor trained on the same synthetic data, or projecting a category-level mean shape with an off-the-shelf pose estimator. Without any reference point, the 3D numbers are hard to interpret.

3. **Validate 3D part accuracy on real images** — either by annotating 3D part pose for a subset of VehiclePart3D (e.g., via manual alignment of CAD models to images) or by using a proxy metric such as 3D reconstruction consistency. This directly addresses the gap between the paper's core claim and its current evidence.

4. **Ablate the mesh-to-mesh correspondence component** (e.g., by fixing the template shape without deformation during inference) and the mesh-to-image contrastive learning (e.g., by using a different feature alignment method). The current ablation (Table 5) only tests inference-time components, not the training-time contributions that are the paper's primary methodological novelty.

5. **Clarify the "one-shot" framing** by explicitly distinguishing between annotation effort (1 mesh) and total data requirements (30 unlabeled meshes + DST pipeline). A data sensitivity analysis showing performance vs. number of unlabeled meshes would substantially strengthen the paper.
