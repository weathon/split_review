## Summary

This paper introduces *audio doppelgängers* — positive pairs for contrastive learning generated by perturbing the parameters of sound synthesizers. Rather than applying post-hoc augmentations to real recordings, the method randomly synthesizes sounds and then slightly perturbs their synthesis parameters to create paired examples that share a similar generative origin but differ in acoustic detail. The approach is lightweight (1–2 hours training, single hyperparameter δ, on-the-fly generation with no data storage), and the paper provides a thorough analysis of the synthetic data distribution across spectral features, causal uncertainty, and FAD scores. Models trained purely on synthetic audio are evaluated on 8 downstream tasks and compared against internal baselines trained on VGGSound under matched conditions.

## Strengths

- **Novel and clean formulation.** The idea of using synthesizer parameter perturbation as a data-generating mechanism for contrastive pairs is genuinely original. Unlike standard augmentations that operate on already-recorded waveforms, this approach intervenes directly in the causal generative process of sound, yielding positive pairs with more naturalistic variation in timbre, pitch, and temporal structure. This is a conceptually important distinction.

- **Lightweight and practical pipeline.** Training takes 1–2 hours (vs. 6–8+ hours for on-disk real data pipelines), requires no data download or storage, and has only a single interpretable hyperparameter δ. The paper backs these claims with explicit timing comparisons and a clear algorithmic description (Algorithm 1), making the method accessible.

- **Thorough multi-task evaluation and ablations.** The paper trains and reports results for over 20 model variants across 8 diverse benchmarks (ESC-50, UrbanSound8K, NSynth, CREMA-D, FSD50k, etc.) using linear probing. The ablation of δ across all tasks (Figure 3) is extensive, showing a clear sweet-spot at δ=0.25 and establishing that δ monotonically controls positive-pair dissimilarity in CLAP embedding space (Figure 2).

- **Informative distributional analysis.** The analysis of spectral features, causal uncertainty, and FAD scores (Section 4.2) provides genuine insight into how synthetic audio differs from real data. The finding that synthetic Voice data achieves lower FAD than VGGSound on 5/6 downstream tasks (Table 2) is interesting and non-obvious. The demonstration that augmentations become redundant when δ-based perturbations are used (Table 1, Figure 3) is a clean ablation result.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The claim "competitive with real data" is misleading without qualification.** The paper's internal real-data baseline (VGGSound SSL with augmentations, 48.85% on ESC-50) is convincingly beaten by the synthetic method (58.90%). However, the HEAR/ARCH SSL row in the same table — which aggregates established self-supervised audio methods trained on real data — achieves 80.50% on ESC-50, and the difference is similar across most tasks (e.g., 24.12% vs. 50.88% on FSD50k, 48.43% vs. 75.21% on CREMA-D). The abstract and introduction state the method is "competitive with real data on standard audio classification benchmarks" without caveating that this refers only to the controlled internal comparison. A reader would reasonably expect "competitiveness" to be assessed against the broader literature the paper itself cites. The paper should either substantially qualify this claim or add a direct comparison to an SSL method (e.g., BYOL-A or SimCLR-audio) trained on real data under the same architecture and probe protocol.

- **Missing direct baseline against a standard SSL audio method under matched conditions.** The paper cites BYOL-A~\cite{niizumi2021byol}, COLA~\cite{saeed2021contrastive}, and CLAR~\cite{al2021clar} in the related work, and the HEAR/ARCH SSL row provides a reference point. However, HEAR/ARCH results use different architectures, training setups, and probe types (MLP vs. linear), making the comparison inexact. A direct comparison — training a ResNet18 with BYOL-A or SimCLR on VGGSound or AudioSet under the same linear-probe evaluation protocol — would substantially strengthen the paper and clarify whether the synthetic approach is competitive in any meaningful sense or merely less weak than a poorly-configured real-data baseline.

- **No error bars or measures of variability.** Results are reported as point estimates without standard deviations, confidence intervals, or significance tests. Given the modest size of some performance margins (e.g., Best Synthetic 24.12 vs. VGGSound SSL Jitter 24.02 on FSD50k; 48.43 vs. 47.86 on CREMA-D), the stability of these comparisons is unclear. This is a standard expectation for empirical papers.

### Minor

- **The synthetic data uses only 1-second audio clips**, which is restrictive for tasks requiring longer temporal context (e.g., LibriCount for speaker counting). The paper acknowledges this can be extended but does not demonstrate it. This choice also disadvantages the real-data baseline: cropping VGGSound's 10-second clips to 1-second segments likely discards much of the structure that makes real audio useful.

- **The causal uncertainty analysis is speculative.** The paper measures that synthetic sounds have higher "causal uncertainty" (via AST classifier probabilities) and speculates this "might be subtly helpful for representation learning," but no experiment causally tests this hypothesis. A controlled experiment that filters or manipulates causal uncertainty would strengthen this claim.

- **Hybrid training (mixing synthetic and real data) is excluded.** The paper acknowledges this as a limitation and future work, but it is the most obvious and likely fruitful direction. A proof-of-concept showing that synthetic pretraining can improve upon or complement real-data pretraining would significantly increase the practical value of the contribution.

### Trivial

- No spectrogram examples or audio samples of the synthesized sounds are shown in the paper body (though the paper references a figure). Qualitative demonstration would help readers assess whether the generated audio is semantically plausible.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Reporting results when synthetic data is used as a regularizer or pretraining initialization before fine-tuning on real data (even a small-scale experiment).
- A diversity metric (e.g., number of distinct clusters in embedding space) to quantify how many distinct "concepts" the synthesizer covers with random parameter sampling.

## Novel Insights

The reviews collectively surface an important tension that the paper does not fully resolve: the synthetic data beats the paper's own real-data baseline convincingly, yet its absolute performance is far below established SSL methods. This suggests the *internal* comparison is fair but the *external* gap is large — which points to an unresolved question: is the synthetic data genuinely useful, or is the internal real-data baseline simply undertrained? The FAD analysis showing synthetic data is closer than VGGSound to 5/6 target distributions is the paper's best evidence that the synthetic approach has unique value, but without a direct comparison to a well-tuned SSL baseline (e.g., BYOL-A on VGGSound with the same ResNet18 backbone), the true contribution remains ambiguous. The paper would benefit from acknowledging this ambiguity explicitly rather than claiming competitiveness.

## Suggestions

1. **Tone down the "competitive with real data" claim** or explicitly qualify it (e.g., "competitive with real data under comparable training conditions using the same architecture and data budget").
2. **Add at least one direct SSL baseline** — train BYOL-A or SimCLR on VGGSound (or AudioSet) using the same ResNet18 encoder and linear-probe evaluation. This is the single experiment that would most clarify the paper's contribution.
3. **Report standard deviations** for the main table results, especially where margins are narrow.
4. **Add a small hybrid experiment** — e.g., fine-tune a synthetic-pretrained model on a small amount of real data — to demonstrate practical utility.

## Score and Decision

The paper presents a genuinely novel idea with a clean formulation and thorough analysis. The method is lightweight and the distributional analysis is informative. However, the central claim of being "competitive with real data" is misleading given the large gap to established SSL audio methods, and the absence of a direct, matched baseline against a standard SSL method undermines the ability to assess the true contribution. The paper is a solid exploratory work that opens a new direction, but in its current form the evidence does not support the strength of the claims made.
