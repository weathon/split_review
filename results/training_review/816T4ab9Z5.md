## Summary

This paper studies how augmentation affects Graph Contrastive Learning (GCL). It argues that perfect alignment (making positive pairs identical) is not beneficial and can even be harmful to downstream task performance, while stronger augmentation helps primarily through inter-class separation rather than intra-class gathering. The authors derive a theoretical bound connecting augmentation distance to downstream generalization, analyze it through information theory and graph spectrum theory, and propose two augmentation methods (information-based and spectrum-based) that they apply to existing GCL algorithms (GRACE, GCA, AD-GCL) across six datasets.

## Strengths

- **Identifies a genuine tension in GCL**: The paper correctly observes that stronger augmentation weakens alignment but often improves downstream performance — a real empirical phenomenon that existing theory (alignment + uniformity) does not fully explain. This is a worthwhile research question.

- **Multi-perspective theoretical framing**: The paper attempts to analyze the augmentation-generalization relationship from information theory (Corollary 1 — connecting to mutual information) and graph spectrum theory (Corollary 2, Theorem 3 — connecting to eigenvalues and over-smoothing). This multi-lens approach is ambitious and, if executed correctly, could provide richer insight than single-framework analyses.

- **The spectrum augmentation idea has intuitive appeal**: The observation that smoothing the graph spectrum can relieve over-smoothing in GCL (Figure 3 showing improved accuracy at deeper layers) is a practically interesting direction that could be developed further, even if the current instantiation is preliminary.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **Theorem 2's lower bound does not support the paper's core argument, and the interpretation is mathematically unsound.**  
  Theorem 2 gives a lower bound on the mean CE loss:  
  \(\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{CE}} \geq \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}} - 3\delta_{aug}^2 - 2\delta_{aug} - \log\frac{M}{K} - \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{var}(f(v^+)|y) - \sqrt{\mathrm{var}(f(v^0)|y)} - e\,\mathrm{var}(\mu_y) - O(M^{-\frac{1}{2}}).\)  
  As \(\delta_{aug}\) increases, the right-hand side becomes *more negative*. The paper then claims (line 118) that "when the lower bound becomes smaller, the best solution is better so the model potentially performs better." This is incorrect. The mean CE loss is nonnegative (it is a negative log-probability). A lower bound that is \(\leq 0\) is automatically satisfied and carries **no information** about the actual achievable loss. Providing a heuristic example (line 118): "if there exists two models with \(\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{CE}} \geq 0.7\) and \(\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{CE}} \geq 0.3\) respectively, the latter one would be preferred" — this reasoning is backwards. A lower bound of 0.7 is *tighter* and more informative than a lower bound of -3 (which becomes vacuous). A more negative lower bound does not tighten the feasible region or imply better potential performance; it means the bound has become looser. **The entire theoretical justification for why stronger augmentation improves generalization rests on this misinterpreted bound.** Without a valid theoretical link, the paper's headline claim that "perfect alignment may be poisonous" loses its primary support.

### Major

- **The core mechanistic claim (GCL works by inter-class separation, not intra-class gathering) rests on thin evidence.**  
  Figure 1 plots positive center distance (PCD), negative center distance (NCD), and accuracy against dropout rate on an unspecified dataset, with no error bars, no variance estimates, and no replication across datasets or augmentation types. The paper uses this single plot to support the general conclusion that "contrastive learning mainly contributes to downstream tasks by separating nodes of different classes... rather than gathering nodes of the same class" (line 94). The observed correlation does not establish the causal mechanism — both accuracy and NCD could increase simply because the classifier adapts to increased variance, for example. A single unlabeled curve on one dataset is insufficient for a claim of this generality.

- **The proposed methods show only partial empirical support and the spectrum method is underspecified.**  
  - **Significance is mixed**: Of the six method-over-baseline comparisons in Table 1, only three have \(p < 0.05\) (GRACE+S \(p=0.003\), AD-GCL+I \(p=0.003\), AD-GCL+S \(p=0.006\)). GRACE+I (\(p=0.155\)), GCA+I (\(p=0.089\)), and GCA+S (\(p=0.147\)) are not significant. The paper's conclusion that the methods are "effective" overall is overstated relative to the evidence.  
  - **No correction for multiple comparisons**: Six p-values are reported with no adjustment (e.g., Bonferroni, FDR), which inflates the risk of false positives.  
  - **Spectrum method lacks an implementable mechanism**: The paper describes adjusting eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\) based on changes in parameters \(\theta_i\) (line 234-248) but provides **no concrete procedure** for how to modify the adjacency matrix's eigenvalues during training in a differentiable or even feasible manner. This is not a reproducible algorithm as described.  
  - **Baselines GRACE+SpCo and GCS appear in Table 1 without citations or introduction** in the main text, making the comparison confusing.

- **Figure 3 (over-smoothing) is insufficient to support the claim.**  
  The accuracy-vs-layers plot is shown for an unspecified dataset and only one method-pair (GRACE+S vs GRACE). A single curve cannot establish that spectrum augmentation "relieves over-smoothing" in general.

### Minor

- **The information method's hyperparameters are unablated.** The choices of top \(\xi\) retained (50% probability), bottom \(2\xi\) deleted (50% probability), and the factor of 2 are presented without any sensitivity analysis or justification. The paper acknowledges this (line 167: "There are many details that could be optimized") but the lack of ablation makes it unclear whether reported improvements are robust or accidental.

- **The spectrum method's update rule is a pure heuristic** (lines 236-247) with no convergence guarantees or theoretical grounding. While not necessarily a fatal issue for an empirical paper, the method's ad-hoc nature weakens the "principled" framing.

- **Theorem 1's bounds are loose and do not establish causation.** The inequalities (lines 82-84) show that both positive and negative center distances are bounded above by quantities that increase with \(\delta_{aug}\), but the paper then concludes that "stronger augmentation separates both inter-class and intra-class nodes... the performance gain may be brought by inter-class separating more than intra-class gathering" (line 90). The bounds do not support a comparative claim about *which effect dominates*; they only show both distances *can* increase.

## Suggestions

1. **Re-examine the interpretation of Theorem 2.** The bound as written is a lower bound; a more negative value makes it less informative, not more favorable. If the intended direction was an upper bound (showing that stronger augmentation gives a tighter upper bound on the CE loss), the theorem would need to be re-derived. Alternatively, if the bound is meant to show that the gap \(\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{CE}} - \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}}\) is reduced by larger \(\delta_{aug}\), this should be stated directly and the bound's sign should be checked.

2. **Strengthen the empirical support for the core mechanism claim** by: (a) specifying the dataset and providing error bars for Figure 1; (b) replicating the analysis across multiple datasets and augmentation types; (c) ideally including a controlled experiment (e.g., synthetic data with known class structure) where inter-class separation and intra-class gathering can be independently manipulated.

3. **Provide a concrete, reproducible specification of the spectrum augmentation method** — in particular, how changes to \(\lambda_i\) are realized as changes to the adjacency matrix during training, and how the modified adjacency is used in subsequent forward passes.

4. **Add multiple comparison correction** (e.g., Bonferroni or FDR) to the p-value reporting in Table 1, or use a global test.

5. **Add citations for GRACE+SpCo and GCS baselines** in the main text, or clarify what they are.
