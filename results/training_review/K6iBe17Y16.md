## Summary
This paper proposes a statistically principled approach to learning forward search heuristics: instead of minimizing MSE (which implicitly models the heuristic as a Gaussian), the authors model the learned heuristic as a Truncated Gaussian where admissible heuristics provide the lower bound. The loss function follows naturally from the negative log-likelihood of this distribution. Experiments in four classical planning domains show that models trained with the Truncated Gaussian NLL loss generally yield more accurate heuristics and better planning performance than those trained with the standard Gaussian NLL (MSE) loss.

## Strengths

- **Principled theoretical justification via Maximum Entropy**: The paper grounds its loss function choice in the maximum entropy principle (Section 2.3, Section 3), showing that when a variable has known lower/upper bounds, finite mean, and finite variance, the correct maximum-entropy distribution is the Truncated Gaussian — not the Gaussian. This provides a clear "why" behind the loss function, which most prior heuristic learning work lacks.

- **Novel application of Truncated Gaussian with formally derived bounds**: While Truncated Gaussians are standard in statistics, the paper is the first to show that *formally derived* admissible bounds from planning heuristics (e.g., lmcut) can be used as the lower bound of a Truncated Gaussian for learning. This is a conceptually clean and actionable contribution.

- **Systematic exploration of design axes**: The paper jointly varies (i) distribution choice (Gaussian vs. Truncated Gaussian), (ii) sigma prediction (learned vs. fixed), and (iii) residual learning (none vs. FF), presenting all 8 combinations in Table 1. This enables readers to assess the interaction between these factors.

- **Clear advantage in the proposed configuration**: In the learn/ff configuration (the paper's recommended setup), Truncated Gaussian achieves lower MSE than Gaussian (+clip) in 3 of 4 domains in Table 1 and outperforms all baselines in planning coverage and node evaluations across all four domains in Table 2.

- **Informative clipping ablation**: The "+clip" variants (Table 1) isolate the effect of naive post-hoc clipping from the full Truncated Gaussian approach, showing that clipping alone is often insufficient (e.g., gripper learn/none: TN MSE=5.65 vs. N+clip MSE=13.37).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Missing admissible-heuristic planning baseline**. The paper claims the learned heuristic can achieve "super-symbolic improvement over admissible heuristics" (line 414) and frames the contribution around surpassing poly-time admissible heuristics. However, Table 2 (planning evaluation) includes only the FF heuristic as a symbolic baseline; it does not include lmcut (the very admissible heuristic used as the lower bound) as a planning baseline. Without this comparison, the reader cannot assess whether the learned heuristic actually solves more instances or uses fewer evaluations than simply running GBFS with lmcut. This gap weakens the "super-symbolic benefit" narrative. (Note: the paper's primary contribution — TN loss vs. Gaussian/MSE — is still supported by the existing comparisons, but the broader claim about surpassing admissible heuristics is incompletely verified.)

### Minor

- **Convergence speed claim is insufficiently supported**. The paper states that TN "converges faster during training" (abstract, line 21, line 625), but the evidence consists of a single training curve (Figure 2) from one unspecified domain. The caption does not name the domain, and no quantification (e.g., steps to reach a given loss threshold) is provided across domains. This is a notable gap for a claimed advantage that appears in both the abstract and conclusion. The claim may well be true, but the paper does not offer enough evidence.

- **TN advantage is marginal in several well-configured comparisons**. In many configurations where the Gaussian model is reasonably specified (i.e., with residual learning and/or learned sigma), the advantage of TN over N(+clip) is small or even negative. For example: gripper learn/ff (TN=3.70 vs. N=3.65 — TN is slightly worse), ferry fixed/ff (TN=3.85 vs. N=2.98 — TN worse), visitall fixed/ff (TN=6.62 vs. N=6.49 — TN worse). The largest gains occur in configurations where the Gaussian model fails catastrophically (e.g., ferry learn/none, N=141 vs. TN=8.63). This pattern is consistent with the paper's own explanation (TN requires learned sigma), but it means the advantage is clearest when the baseline is most poorly specified.

- **Potential selection bias in data generation**. The paper retries instance generation up to 20 times if the planner fails to solve within resource limits (line 465). This may systematically select for easier instances within a given parameter setting, potentially making the learned heuristic's generalization look better than it would on a representative sample of instances. The paper does not discuss this bias.

- **Subsampling to 400 training instances is not justified**. The paper subsamples 400 instances from a pool of 456–1536 (line 511) without explaining why this specific number was chosen or analyzing the sensitivity of results to this choice.

## Novel Insights

The reviews collectively identify a tension between the paper's ambitious framing and its somewhat narrow evaluation. The strength of the paper is its clean theoretical derivation: the Maximum Entropy principle forces the Truncated Gaussian form when bounds are known, which elegantly explains why MSE is suboptimal for this setting. However, the experimental evaluation, while supporting the core claim (TN > Gaussian), does not chase the paper's own most provocative claim — that the learned heuristic should surpass the admissible heuristic used as a bound. This gap between theoretical ambition and empirical completeness is the paper's main weakness, but it is addressable (adding lmcut planning results). The insight about TN requiring learned sigma (Section 4, Observations) is a non-obvious and valuable finding that distillation from the experiments revealed more clearly than the paper's own presentation.

The paper makes a genuine theoretical contribution (Maximum Entropy justification for Truncated Gaussian loss) and provides generally supportive experimental evidence across multiple domains, configurations, and evaluation metrics. None of these issues is fatal to the core claim (TN loss > Gaussian/MSE loss for heuristic learning). The paper is solid work with a clear contribution.
