# Review

## Summary
This paper introduces a new dynamic reasoning evaluation benchmark, DRE-Bench, designed to assess the fluid intelligence of large language models (LLMs). DRE-Bench comprises 36 abstract reasoning tasks organized across four cognitive levels, with each task featuring multiple dynamic variants to test the same underlying latent rule. The authors evaluate a range of state-of-the-art LLMs, including both general LLMs and reasoning LLMs. The experimental results indicate that while most LLMs achieve competent and robust performance in low-level cognition, they struggle with high-level cognition and exhibit limited generalization as task complexity increases.

## Strengths
1. The paper is well-written and easy to follow.
2. The paper introduces a novel dynamic evaluation benchmark, DRE-Bench, designed to assess the fluid intelligence of LLMs through abstract reasoning tasks. This benchmark offers a new approach to evaluating LLMs' reasoning capabilities.

## Weaknesses
1. The paper does not provide a comprehensive comparison with existing benchmarks. While it mentions some limitations of current benchmarks, it lacks a detailed comparison of DRE-Bench's performance with other established benchmarks. This makes it difficult to assess the relative advantages of DRE-Bench and to understand its contribution to the field.
2. The paper focuses on evaluating LLMs' performance on DRE-Bench but does not provide a detailed analysis of why models fail at certain tasks. A deeper exploration of the models' error patterns and failure modes would offer more insights into their limitations and potential areas for improvement.
3. The paper does not discuss the potential biases or limitations of DRE-Bench itself. For example, the benchmark may inadvertently favor certain types of reasoning over others, or it may lack diversity in the tasks or scenarios it covers. Addressing these potential issues would strengthen the validity of the benchmark and the conclusions drawn from it.

## Questions
1. Could the authors provide a more detailed comparison between DRE-Bench and existing benchmarks? Specifically, how does DRE-Bench address the limitations of current benchmarks, and what specific advantages does it offer in terms of task design, scalability, and interpretability?
2. Could the authors provide a more detailed analysis of the failure modes of the LLMs on DRE-Bench? What specific patterns or errors do the models exhibit when performing poorly on certain tasks, and how do these errors differ across different cognitive levels?
3. Could the authors discuss the potential biases or limitations of DRE-Bench itself? For example, does the benchmark inadvertently favor certain types of reasoning over others? Is the benchmark diverse enough to cover a wide range of reasoning skills and scenarios?
