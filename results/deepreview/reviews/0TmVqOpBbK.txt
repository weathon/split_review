# Review

## Summary
This paper investigates the relationship between model architecture and the inference cost and accuracy of LLMs. Specifically, it studies the impact of hidden size, MLP-to-attention ratio, and Grouped-Query Attention (GQA) on inference cost and accuracy. The authors introduce a conditional scaling law that augments the Chinchilla framework with architectural information. This framework enables the identification of architectures that balance inference efficiency and accuracy. The paper presents extensive experimental results, training over 200 models ranging from 80M to 3B parameters and 8B to 100B training tokens. The findings demonstrate that the conditional scaling law can predict optimal architectural choices, and the resulting models outperform existing open-source baselines. Under the same training budget, the optimized architectures achieve up to 2.1% higher accuracy and 42% greater inference throughput compared to LLaMA-3.2.

## Strengths
1. The paper presents a novel approach by introducing a conditional scaling law that incorporates architectural information, extending the Chinchilla framework to balance inference efficiency and accuracy.
2. The authors conduct extensive experiments, training over 200 models, which provides a robust validation of their proposed framework and the resulting findings.
3. The paper demonstrates practical significance by showing that the optimized architectures outperform existing baselines, achieving higher accuracy and inference throughput, which is crucial for the deployment of large language models.

## Weaknesses
1. The paper focuses primarily on models up to 3B parameters, which is relatively small compared to state-of-the-art LLMs. This limitation may impact the generalizability of the findings to larger models.
2. The analysis is restricted to dense models and does not explore the applicability of the proposed methods to Mixture of Experts (MoE) architectures, which are becoming increasingly popular.
3. The study is limited to pre-training and does not investigate how the proposed methods would perform under post-training scenarios, which is important for the full life cycle of LLMs.
4. The paper does not thoroughly explore the robustness of the fitting-data strategy, which is crucial for the predictive performance of the scaling laws at larger scales.

## Questions
1. How do the authors plan to address the limitations of their study regarding model size, architectural choices, and training phases? Are there plans to extend the research to larger models, including MoE architectures, and to investigate post-training scenarios?
2. Can the authors provide more insights into the robustness of the fitting-data strategy and its impact on the predictive performance of the scaling laws? Are there alternative strategies or refinements to the current approach that could improve this aspect?
3. How generalizable are the findings of this study to other types of language models or tasks beyond the ones considered in this paper? Are there specific characteristics of the models or tasks that might affect the applicability of the proposed methods?
