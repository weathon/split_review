# Review

## Summary
This paper introduces a novel framework for merging large language models (LLMs) by operating in a learned latent space rather than traditional weight space. The authors propose LS-Merge, which addresses key limitations in existing model merging techniques, such as the need for architecturally identical models and the difficulty in handling heterogeneous architectures. The framework leverages a transformer-based variational autoencoder (VAE) trained in a two-stage curriculum to encode model weights into a smooth latent space. This enables merging operations across different architectures by aligning models within the latent space through linear interpolation and decoding back to weights.

## Strengths
1. The introduction of a latent space approach for model merging is novel and addresses significant limitations in existing methods, particularly the need for architecturally identical models. This innovation opens new possibilities for reusing pre-trained models across diverse architectures.
2. The use of a two-stage curriculum for training the VAE, along with optimal transport (OT) alignment for heterogeneous merging, demonstrates a high level of technical sophistication. These methods are well-suited to the complexities of LLM weight distributions and cross-architecture merging.
3. The empirical results are robust, covering multiple architectures and downstream tasks. The consistent outperformance of LS-Merge compared to weight-space averaging and other baselines is compelling evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed method.

## Weaknesses
1. The paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational costs associated with training the VAE and performing latent-space operations. This information is crucial for assessing the practical applicability of the method, especially for large-scale models.
2. While the paper demonstrates the effectiveness of LS-Merge on several downstream tasks, a more comprehensive evaluation across a wider range of tasks and datasets would strengthen the claims about the method's generalization capabilities.

## Questions
1. How does the computational cost of LS-Merge compare to traditional weight-space merging methods, particularly for large-scale models?
2. Can you provide more insights into the choice of the two-stage curriculum for training the VAE? How critical is this choice for the method's performance?
3. Have you explored the potential of LS-Merge for merging models beyond LLMs, such as vision-language models? What challenges do you anticipate in such extensions?
