# Review

## Summary
The paper presents a rigorous mathematical proof demonstrating that Transformer-based language models are injective (one-to-one mapping) from discrete input sequences to continuous hidden representations. This finding challenges the prevailing view that Transformer components, such as non-linear activations and normalization layers, are inherently many-to-one due to their non-linear nature and normalization mechanisms. The authors also introduce SIFT (Sequential Inverse Prompt via Iterative updates), an algorithm that efficiently reconstructs the exact input sequence from hidden activations, with provable linear-time guarantees.

## Strengths
1. The paper provides a rigorous mathematical proof that transformer-based language models are injective, meaning different input sequences map to distinct hidden states. This is a significant finding that challenges previous assumptions about the nature of Transformers.

2. The introduction of the SIFT algorithm, which can reconstruct the exact input sequence from hidden activations with provable linear-time guarantees, is a practical contribution with important implications for interpretability and transparency in language models.

3. The paper's results have direct implications for privacy and data protection, as they suggest that hidden states in Transformer models are effectively equivalent to the original user input, which may have significant regulatory and compliance implications.

## Weaknesses
1. The proof assumes standard initialization schemes (e.g., Gaussian, uniform, Xavier/Glorot) and training methods (gradient descent), which may not fully encompass the range of possible parameter settings or alternative training approaches. 

2. The proof relies on the assumption that model components are real-analytic, which may not hold for all types of Transformers or alternative architectures.

## Questions
1. The proof relies on the assumption that model components are real-analytic. Are there any known cases or potential scenarios where this assumption might not hold? How might the results be affected if this assumption is violated?

2. The proof focuses on the last-token state. How do the results extend to other states within the sequence? Is there any loss of information as one moves away from the last token?
