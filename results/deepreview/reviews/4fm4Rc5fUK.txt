# Review

## Summary
The paper introduces the Autoformalizer with Tool Feedback (ATF), a novel approach to autoformalization that incorporates syntactic and semantic consistency information as tools into the formalization process. This method aims to address the limitations of existing formalization models by actively refining generated statements based on feedback from Lean 4 compilers (syntax) and a multi-LLMs-as-judge approach (semantics). The training process consists of a cold-start phase, an expert iteration phase, and Direct Preference Optimization, resulting in a model that outperforms baselines on benchmarks like FormalMath-Lite, ProverBench, and CombiBench. The authors also open-source Numina-ATF, a dataset of 750K synthetic formal statements to aid future research in autoformalization and Automated Theorem Proving (ATP).

## Strengths
1. The paper introduces a novel approach by incorporating compiler feedback and a multi-LLMs-as-judge method into the autoformalization process, actively guiding the model to refine formal statements based on tool feedback. This tool-integrated approach is a creative combination of existing ideas (e.g., tool-integrated reasoning, multi-LLMs-as-judge) applied to the specific challenge of autoformalization, demonstrating originality in methodology.

2. The paper is well-structured, with clear explanations of each component in the ATF framework, from the initial cold-start phase to the final Direct Preference Optimization stage. The use of figures, such as the pipeline overview and examples of tool feedback integration, aids in understanding the methodology. 

3. The significance of this work is underscored by its practical impact on autoformalization and ATP research. The open-sourced Numina-ATF dataset is a valuable contribution, providing a large dataset of synthetic formal statements that can support further development in these areas. The performance improvements over existing models on standard benchmarks indicate the potential of this approach to advance autoformalization in practical applications.

## Weaknesses
1. While the paper demonstrates strong performance on the selected benchmarks, it does not sufficiently explore how well the model generalizes to a broader range of mathematical domains or diverse problem types beyond competition-level math problems. For example, the paper could be strengthened by including more varied datasets or by conducting experiments on different types of mathematical problems, such as those found in undergraduate textbooks or in real-world applications. 

2. The paper would benefit from a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed approach. For instance, it is not fully addressed how the model handles ambiguous or poorly phrased informal statements, or how it deals with cases where the desired formalization is not unique. 

3. The paper lacks a thorough comparison with a broader set of baseline models. While it compares against several recent formalization models, it does not include comparisons with older or less sophisticated models that may still be widely used, providing a more complete context for evaluating the performance improvements. For example, a comparison with a simpler rule-based autoformalization system could highlight the advantages of the proposed approach more clearly.

## Questions
1. How does the model perform on a broader range of mathematical domains, such as undergraduate-level textbook problems or real-world mathematical applications, which may differ significantly from the competition-level problems in the Numina-ATF dataset?

2. Could you elaborate on how the model handles ambiguous or poorly phrased informal statements, and whether there are specific strategies within the framework to resolve such cases?

3. Given that the performance of the model relies heavily on feedback from Lean 4 compilers and multi-LLMs-as-judge consistency checks, how does the model handle cases where the feedback is inconclusive or conflicting?

4. Could you provide more details on the specific types of errors that the model is most prone to making, particularly in terms of syntactic and semantic consistency?

5. How does the model compare in performance to simpler, rule-based autoformalization systems, or to older formalization models that may still be widely used?
