## Summary
The paper proposes IncentRL, an RL framework that augments external rewards with a KL divergence penalty between predicted outcome distributions p(o|s,a) and preferred outcome distributions q(o|s). The core idea is that this KL term provides dense shaping signals in sparse-reward settings. The paper further claims a Bayesian adaptation scheme for the incentive weight β as its central novelty, aiming to eliminate manual trade-off tuning. Experiments on a toy MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid Doorkey show improvements over standard RL (β=0).

## Strengths
- **Clean, interpretable incentive-shaping formulation**: The core equation r_total = r_ext − β·KL(p(o|s,a) ‖ q(o|s)) (Section 3.2) is simple, well-motivated, and algorithm-agnostic. The mechanism cleanly separates the shaping signal from the base RL algorithm, making it straightforward to apply atop any existing method.
- **Clear differentiation from related work**: Section 2 effectively distinguishes IncentRL from KL-regularized policy methods (TRPO/PPO constrain policy changes, not outcome distributions), the Free Energy Principle (FEP adapts preferences; IncentRL fixes them and shapes behavior), and intrinsic motivation methods (which lack explicit preferred-outcome representations). This positioning helps readers understand what is genuinely new.
- **Meaningful gains on MiniGrid Doorkey with minimal regularization**: Figure 2 shows that β=0.01 yields a meaningful improvement on a standard sparse-reward benchmark: final success rate rises from 90.5% to 98%, and mean episode length drops from 75 to 29 steps. That a mild KL penalty produces measurable improvement in both success rate and trajectory efficiency is a practically relevant result.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **The Bayesian adaptation method — the paper's self-identified primary contribution — is never specified**: The paper places Bayesian β adaptation front and center: it appears in the title, is listed first among contributions (lines 21–25), is highlighted in the abstract and conclusion as the central novelty, and occupies dedicated figures (Figures 3–4) and an appendix table (Table 3). Yet nowhere in the paper is the actual Bayesian scheme described. There is no prior distribution over β, no likelihood function, no posterior update rule, and no algorithm describing how β is sampled or adapted during training. The paper uses Bayesian terminology ("posterior mean," "posterior concentration," "Bayesian random variable") and presents evidence of "posterior narrowing" across "rounds," but a reader cannot determine what these rounds consist of, what data informs the posterior update, or whether the scheme is Bayesian in any meaningful sense. The claim that "Bayesian adaptation eliminates the need for manual trade-off tuning" (lines 247, 289) is unsubstantiated because the method itself is undefined. This is a structural gap in the paper's most prominently advertised contribution.
- **The KL divergence is mathematically undefined in the experiments as described**: In Experiment 1 (line 166), q(o|s₀) = {s₁:1.0, s₀:0.0} assigns zero probability to s₀, yet p(o|s₀,a₁) = {s₁:0.3, s₀:0.7} assigns 0.7 to s₀. The KL divergence KL(p‖q) = Σ p(o) log(p(o)/q(o)) contains the term 0.7·log(0.7/0), which is infinite. The same problem arises in MountainCar (line 199), where q assigns all probability to the goal — any non-goal transition produces an infinite KL penalty. The paper never acknowledges this support mismatch. Either the implementation used a different divergence, added smoothing, or diverges from the described method in some unreported way. This makes the experimental method as described inconsistent with the reported finite results.
- **p(o|s,a) is never concretely specified for the continuous-state experiments**: For MountainCar (continuous position and velocity) and MiniGrid Doorkey, the paper provides no concrete specification of how the predicted outcome distribution p(o|s,a) is represented, parameterized, or learned. Section 4.3 discusses latent representations and Gaussian models in an aspirational tone, but the experiments never state whether such mechanisms were actually implemented or how. The algorithm description in Section 5.1 (line 151) says "Estimate p(o|s,a)" as step 1 with no further mechanism. Without this specification, the experiments are not reproducible, and it is unclear whether the claimed mechanism — aligning predictions with preferences — is actually what drives the reported improvements.
- **No comparison to any intrinsic motivation or exploration baseline**: The paper positions itself as addressing limitations of intrinsic motivation methods (abstract, line 9; Section 2.1) and claims to improve exploration in sparse-reward settings. Yet the only baseline in all experiments is standard RL (β=0). There is no comparison to curiosity-driven exploration (ICM), count-based exploration, RND, empowerment, or any other intrinsic motivation method. The claimed improvement over standard RL could be trivially achieved by existing methods, and the paper provides no evidence to the contrary.

### Minor
- **Toy MDP uses ground-truth transition probabilities as p(o|s,a)**: In Experiment 1, the predicted outcome distribution is set to the true environment dynamics (p(o|s₀,a₁) = {s₁:0.3, s₀:0.7}), giving the agent oracle knowledge of transitions and conflating "predicted outcomes" with "true dynamics." This weakens the claim that the method works via learned predictive models.
- **Theoretical propositions are straightforward and provide limited insight**: Proposition 1 (small β preserves optimal policy) follows from continuity of the objective. Proposition 2 (β→∞ yields pure preference alignment) is immediate from inspection. Neither result characterizes when or how fast the method helps, connects to the Bayesian adaptation scheme, or reveals any non-obvious property of the framework.
- **Only 3 seeds for DQN on MountainCar**: DQN is known to have high variance, and the reported standard deviations on β=0.3 (±909) and β=1.0 (±691) are enormous relative to their means (1655 and 875), raising questions about the reliability of any signal at those β values.
- **MiniGrid tested at only β=0.0 and β=0.01**: Only two β values are tested on MiniGrid, making it impossible to assess robustness to β choice on this task. Combined with the two-order-of-magnitude discrepancy between optimal β on MountainCar (0.1) and MiniGrid (0.01), this underscores the very tuning problem the Bayesian method claims to solve.
- **Key supporting claim presented as "not shown"**: Line 228 states "In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of β achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value." A key piece of evidence for the Bayesian method is asserted without any data — this claim should either be supported or removed.

### Trivial
- The outcome space "o" is never formally defined — is it a next state, a state feature, a latent embedding? This leaves the framework notationally underspecified, though the conceptual idea remains understandable.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Defining the Bayesian adaptation scheme explicitly (prior, likelihood, update rule, and what data feeds the update) would transform the paper's headline contribution from a black box into an evaluable method.
- Resolving the KL support mismatch (e.g., using Jensen-Shannon divergence, Wasserstein, or additive smoothing) and reporting what was actually implemented.
- Adding intrinsic motivation baselines (ICM, RND, or count-based) to contextualize the claimed gains against existing exploration methods.
- Running the MiniGrid experiment with more β values to connect to the MountainCar sweep and the Bayesian adaptation results.
- Specifying neural architectures and training hyperparameters for DQN experiments (currently only mentioned as "the same neural architecture and training hyperparameters" without concrete values).

## Suggestions
- The paper's core KL-based incentive shaping idea is coherent and distinguishable from prior work. The authors should consider either (a) actually describing the Bayesian adaptation scheme in full detail (prior, likelihood, update rule, data sources) or (b) repositioning the Bayesian adaptation as a secondary or future-work contribution and focusing the paper on the KL-shaping framework, which stands on its own. A paper that thoroughly validates the KL-shaping mechanism with proper intrinsic motivation baselines, clear specification of p(o|s,a), and correct divergence handling would be considerably stronger than the current submission.
- Specify p(o|s,a) concretely for each experiment: what is the outcome space, how is the distribution parameterized, and how is it learned or supplied. This is essential for reproducibility and for verifying that the shaping mechanism actually works as claimed.
- Address the KL support mismatch: either use a divergence that handles zero-probability outcomes (Jensen-Shannon, Wasserstein), or define q with full support (e.g., using additive smoothing), and report what was actually implemented.
