## Summary

The paper introduces the Open Proof Corpus (OPC), a dataset of 5,062 human-evaluated mathematical proofs across 1,010 problems from prestigious competitions (IMO, USAMO, Putnam, etc.), generated by six state-of-the-art LLMs. Using this dataset, the paper resolves three open questions: (1) natural-language proof generation dramatically outperforms formal proof generation (82.7% vs. <19% on PutnamBench), (2) final-answer accuracy substantially overestimates proof correctness for some models (e.g., o3 drops 30pp), and (3) ranking-based best-of-n selection significantly improves proof quality over standard methods. The paper also fine-tunes an 8B open model to 88.1% judge accuracy, matching Gemini-2.5-Pro.

## Strengths

- **Large-scale human-validated proof dataset**: The OPC is the first dataset of this scale (5,062 proofs, 1,010 problems) containing human evaluations of LLM-generated proofs from IMO-level competitions, with 10% double-graded and 90.4% inter-judge agreement (§4, Table 1). This is a significant resource for the community, substantially larger than prior efforts (e.g., Petrov et al. 2025 on 6 problems).

- **Quantified informal-formal proof gap**: On the PutnamBench subset, Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves 82.7% accuracy while Goedel-Prover-V2 achieves <19% (§5.3, Figure 4). The paper explicitly measures this gap, going beyond prior qualitative speculation.

- **First tied evaluation of final-answer accuracy vs. proof correctness on an established benchmark**: Using the MathArena subset, the paper shows o3 drops from 87.6% final-answer accuracy to 59.5% proof correctness (~30pp gap), while Gemini-2.5-Pro drops only from 84.9% to 77.6% (~7pp) (§5.4, Figure 5). This reveals model-specific differences previously unknown.

- **Ranking-based best-of-n outperforms standard selection**: The Rank (Swiss) method improves accuracy from 22.7% (pass@1) to 40.0% on 134 problems, while discrete and continuous methods plateau at 32-33% (§5.5, Figure 6b). The ranking approach continues to improve beyond n=5, unlike the other methods (Figure 6a).

- **Fine-tuned 8B open model matches frontier models on proof judging**: OPC-R1-8B achieves 88.1% judgment accuracy (maj@5), tying Gemini-2.5-Pro and approaching GPT-5 at 90.8% (Table 2). The 17% improvement over the base R1-Qwen3-8B (70.7%→88.1%) directly demonstrates the dataset's value for training.

- **Rigorous annotation methodology**: Judge selection from former IMO participants (§3.1), pilot phase with ~300 proofs and active calibration (§3.3), double-grading of 10%, LLM issue summaries checked for bias (no significant change in agreement, §3.2), and allowance for abstention/uncertainty. These quality controls provide confidence in the human labels.

- **Contamination analysis**: Providing ground-truth solutions alongside proofs to be judged changes model accuracy by at most +4.7%, with most models at ≤ ±2.8% (Table 4). This supports robustness of the judging results against data contamination (§5.6).

- **Self-evaluation limitation identified and measured**: Table 3 shows that all evaluated models except Qwen3-235B-A22B perform worst when judging their own proofs (e.g., Gemini judges Gemini at 79.4% vs. 85.9-87.1% for other models). This is a concrete, quantitative finding about a critical limitation.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

None. The paper's central contribution — the OPC dataset — is solid and validated. The experimental findings, while containing caveats discussed below, do not invalidate the core contribution.

### Minor

- **Human baseline for proof judging is not measured on the exact test set** (§5.2). The 90.4% human baseline is the inter-annotator agreement on all double-graded proofs in the OPC, while the LLM test set is 293 proofs from a problem-level split of the generic subset. The paper asserts "since the test samples are uniformly drawn from the OPC, this does not significantly affect the comparison" — a reasonable statistical argument, but it is stated without formal evidence (e.g., a demonstration that the difficulty distribution of the test set matches the full dataset). Because the 90.8% GPT-5 accuracy is close to the 90.4% baseline, small shifts in baseline difficulty could change whether GPT-5 is "on-par" or meaningfully below human performance. The authors acknowledge the issue; strengthening this comparison would substantiate the headline claim.

- **Active-sampling bias in problem selection** (§3.1). Problems were selected to maintain roughly 50% model accuracy, with active monitoring and adjustment. This means the OPC over-represents problems at the models' capability boundary and is not a random sample of competition problems. The 43% overall correctness rate and per-model statistics thus reflect a deliberately balanced set, not unconditional performance on competition problems. The paper is transparent about the methodology but does not explicitly caveat this when discussing aggregate statistics in §4.

- **The "uniformly drawn" justification for the human baseline** (discussed above) would benefit from explicit demonstration. The test set is a "problem-level split" — confirming that the split preserves the difficulty distribution would turn a reasonable assertion into a robust justification.

### Trivial

- A small bug in the Rank (Swiss) method caused incorrect selections for 18 questions (footnote 1, §5.5). These are excluded from analysis but the issue should be documented in code release.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Error type categorization**: The qualitative observations in §E are a start, but a systematic breakdown of error types per model would deepen the dataset's value.
- **Correlation of proof length with correctness**: Would help distinguish "lucky hits" from genuine capability.
- **Concrete failure examples** for each model (beyond the functional equation example in Fig. 1a) would help readers understand the kinds of mistakes LLMs make.
