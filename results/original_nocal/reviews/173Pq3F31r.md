## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models expressive piano performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT) by disentangling note-level score content from a global performance style embedding. It introduces a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings directly from score content, and formulates EPR as a sequence-to-sequence task that does not require fine-grained note-level alignment. Evaluated on the ASAP and ATEPP datasets, the framework achieves competitive performance on both tasks, demonstrates content-style disentanglement through identification experiments and visualizations, and produces stylistically appropriate renderings via the PSR module.

## Strengths

- **Strong evidence of disentangled content-style representations.** Table 4 shows that a classifier using the style embedding achieves 77.46% composer identification accuracy (vs. 29.99% using the content embedding) and 42.07% performer accuracy (vs. 9.94% using content). Figure 3 further visualizes clear clustering of style embeddings by composer and performer. This provides compelling evidence that the disentanglement design successfully separates score content from performance style.

- **Competitive EPR without note-level alignment.** Table 3 reports that Ours (PSR) achieves the highest alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%) among all methods, outperforming alignment-dependent baselines DExter and VirtuosoNet. Table 2 further shows Ours (Target) achieves the lowest MAE for both duration (0.37) and velocity (10.33). This validates that the Seq2Seq formulation — which relaxes the strict alignment requirement of prior EPR systems — can still produce accurate expressive performances.

- **Score-conditioned style generation via the PSR module.** Figure 4 shows that style embeddings generated by the PSR module from score content alone form era-wise clusters (Baroque, Classical, Romantic, Modern) that closely mirror real-performance style clusters, with similar centroid locations. Figure 2 confirms that Ours (PSR) achieves subjective ratings competitive with or exceeding those of baselines. This module is a novel approach to automating style recommendation without requiring manual or label-based control.

- **State-of-the-art or competitive APT performance across multiple metrics.** Table 1 shows that the joint model achieves the best overall MUSTER average error (12.48) and the best ScoreSimilarity pitch spelling error (6.24) among all baselines, while also attaining the best or second-best on E<sub>extra</sub> (7.33), E<sub>onset</sub> (16.26), E<sub>offset</sub> (27.30), and E<sub>dur</sub> (51.75). The claim of "performance comparable to the state-of-the-art" is well-supported by the data.

- **Well-structured style latent space enabling transfer and interpolation.** The subjective style transfer evaluation (Figure 5) shows the Target condition achieves the highest style similarity in two of three samples, and the Mean (interpolated) condition yields consistently strong quality, indicating the style space supports smooth manipulation.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The central claim of mutual supervision benefit from joint modeling is confounded with increased training data.** The comparison between Ours (Target) and EPR-Only differs on two axes: Ours (Target) uses both the full joint architecture AND additional unpaired data (75,913 MuseScore scores + YouTube transcriptions), while EPR-Only is trained solely on the 967 ASAP performances. The paper writes that "joint modeling, together with training on additional unpaired data, leads to better EPR performance" — explicitly bundling the two factors. The improvement observed in Table 2 (e.g., KL duration 5.51 vs. 6.43, MAE duration 0.37 vs. 0.42) cannot be attributed to joint modeling alone; it may equally stem from the substantially larger training set. Since "leveraging the duality between the two tasks for mutual supervision" is presented as the first contribution, this gap needs to be addressed (e.g., by ablating the joint model on the same data as EPR-Only). **This is the paper's most significant weakness.**

- **No ablation demonstrating whether APT benefits from joint training.** The paper shows that the joint model achieves strong APT results, but does not compare against a separately trained APT model on the same data. Without this comparison, the "mutual supervision" claim for APT is unsubstantiated — we cannot tell whether the shared content representation helps, hurts, or is neutral for transcription quality.

### Minor

- **The PSR module's evaluation is qualitative only.** Figure 4 provides a compelling visual comparison of era-wise clustering between generated and real styles, but no quantitative metric (e.g., centroid distance, cluster purity, or distribution similarity) is reported. A baseline comparison against simple alternatives (e.g., using the mean training style, or composer-specific mean style) would strengthen the evaluation.

- **No ablation of the KL regularization term (Eq. 5).** The paper claims the KL penalty promotes smoothness and supports disentanglement, but its effect is not isolated. An ablation removing the KL loss and measuring whether identification accuracy or style space structure degrades would validate the design choice.

- **The unpaired performance dataset's transcription quality is not reported.** The YouTube piano cover dataset is created using an audio-to-MIDI transcription model, selected via a "pilot study." However, no accuracy metrics (note precision/recall, onset/offset accuracy) are provided. Since the unpaired data contributes to training, the noise level in this dataset is a relevant factor for understanding the results.

## Suggestions

1. **Deconfound the joint-modeling experiment:** Train both the full joint model and the EPR-Only variant on identical data (ASAP only, no unpaired data). This directly tests whether joint modeling alone provides measurable benefit for EPR. If the gap persists, the mutual supervision claim stands; if it narrows substantially, the unpaired data was the primary driver.

2. **Add an APT-only ablation:** Train the APT branch in isolation (score encoder + score decoder, without the performance encoder/style encoder) on ASAP and compare to the joint model's APT results. This would quantify any benefit APT derives from the shared framework.

3. **Quantify PSR evaluation:** Add a quantitative measure (e.g., centroid distance, coverage score, or divergence between real and generated style distributions) to support the qualitative era-wise clustering observation. Compare against a simple baseline (e.g., using the mean style embedding).
