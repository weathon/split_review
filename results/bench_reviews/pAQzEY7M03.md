## Summary

FakeShield proposes an explainable image forgery detection and localization framework that combines a domain-tag-guided multimodal LLM for authenticity judgment/textual rationale generation with a SAM-based localization module guided by the generated text. The paper also constructs MMTD-Set by augmenting image-mask forgery datasets with GPT-4o-generated descriptions and reports strong detection, explanation-similarity, and localization results across Photoshop-style, DeepFake, and AIGC-editing settings. The direction is timely and potentially valuable, but the current evidence does not fully support the paper’s strongest claims about explainability, cross-domain generalization, and broad superiority over prior IFDL methods.

## Strengths

- **Concrete multi-output framework for detection, explanation, and localization.** The paper does more than add captions to a detector: Figure 3 and Sections 3.3–3.4 define a two-stage architecture in which DTE-FDM generates a detection result and judgment basis, and MFLM uses this text with a SAM-based decoder to produce a tamper mask.

- **Strong reported detection performance across several domains.** In Table 1, FakeShield achieves the best ACC/F1 among the compared methods on all listed Photoshop datasets and on DeepFake/AIGC-Editing, e.g., 0.95/0.95 on CASIA1+, 0.97/0.98 on DSO, 0.93/0.93 on DeepFake, and 0.98/0.99 on AIGC-Editing. These numbers suggest that the approach is promising, although the generalization interpretation is weakened by split-detail issues discussed below.

- **Domain-tag conditioning is supported by a useful ablation.** Section 4.6/Table 5 shows that removing the Domain Tag Generator substantially reduces performance, especially on AIGC-Editing from 0.93/0.93 to 0.72/0.78 ACC/F1 and DeepFake from 0.98/0.99 to 0.89/0.90. This is concrete evidence that the domain-conditioning mechanism matters within the paper’s training/evaluation setup.

- **Localization results improve over many conventional IFDL baselines.** Table 4 shows FakeShield has the highest IoU/F1 on most listed localization benchmarks, including CASIA1+ at 0.54/0.60, IMD2020 at 0.50/0.57, Columbia at 0.67/0.75, NIST at 0.32/0.37, and DSO at 0.48/0.52. The qualitative examples in Figure 5 also support that the method can produce cleaner masks than several compared baselines in selected cases.

- **The MMTD-Set construction targets a genuinely missing supervision format.** Section 3.1 explicitly creates image-mask-description triplets, using different prompts for Photoshop, DeepFake, and AIGC-editing artifacts. This is a useful resource direction for multimodal forensic models, even though the annotation/evaluation protocol needs stronger validation.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None. The paper is a real and potentially useful contribution, but several major evidential gaps prevent the strongest claims from being established.

### Major

- **The explanation evaluation does not establish explanation correctness, faithfulness, or human usefulness.** The central claim is that FakeShield is “explainable,” but the paper evaluates explanations primarily by cosine semantic similarity to GPT-4o-generated descriptions. Section 3.1 states that for tampered images the authors provide GPT-4o with both “the edited image” and “its corresponding forgery mask,” enabling GPT-4o to describe the tampered location. Section 4.3 then says the “tampered area descriptions generated by GPT-4o” are used as ground truth and evaluated with CSS. This measures imitation of synthetic descriptions, not whether the model’s explanations identify real forensic evidence, are faithful to the detector’s decision, or help humans assess authenticity. The comparison with general-purpose M-LLMs in Table 3 is also limited because FakeShield is fine-tuned on the target annotation style while the baselines are not.

- **Dataset construction and split details are too sparse to support strong cross-domain generalization claims.** Section 4.1 says training uses CASIAv2/Fantastic Reality, FFHQ/FaceApp, and self-constructed AIGC-editing data, while testing includes several Photoshop benchmarks plus DeepFake “FFHQ, FaceApp” and self-generated AIGC-Editing data. The paper does not provide enough detail on train/test sizes, source-image separation, subject separation, manipulation-method separation, generator separation, or whether the same construction pipeline appears in both train and test. This is especially important because Table 2 reports near-perfect DeepFake performance by FakeShield while dedicated DeepFake baselines are near chance, which is hard to interpret without ruling out dataset/source artifacts or split leakage.

- **The baseline evidence is not sufficient for the broad “superior to previous IFDL methods” claim.** The paper compares against several conventional IFDL baselines, but the main localization/detection tables do not include some highly relevant contemporary methods discussed in the paper’s own related work, such as IML-ViT and DiffForensics. More importantly, FakeShield uses a large M-LLM plus SAM-style segmentation, but the paper does not compare against architecture-matched alternatives such as simpler SAM-prompt variants or reasoning-segmentation systems adapted to the same forgery data. For the explanation task, Table 3 compares a task-fine-tuned model to generic pretrained M-LLMs using a metric aligned with FakeShield’s training target, so it cannot by itself show superior forensic reasoning.

- **The localization evaluation is not clearly end-to-end.** The proposed pipeline in Figure 3 uses the generated detection/explanation output to drive MFLM, but Table 4 does not clearly state whether localization uses predicted explanations, GPT-4o/generated ground-truth descriptions, or another teacher-forced prompt during evaluation. This matters because deployed performance depends on how localization behaves when detection or explanation is wrong. The localization results also omit authentic-image behavior: a practical IFDL system should avoid hallucinating tamper masks on real images, but Table 4 evaluates only tampered-region localization.

### Minor

- **The Domain Tag Generator may learn dataset/domain shortcuts rather than forensic structure.** Section 3.3 classifies images into Photoshop, DeepFake, or AIGC-based tampering and inserts a prompt such as “This is a suspected {data domain}-tampered picture.” The paper does not explain how authentic images are tagged, nor does it test wrong tags, random tags, unseen manipulation domains, or leave-domain-out settings. Since domain tags are central to the reported gains, this leaves open whether DTG learns manipulation-relevant cues or dataset/source provenance.

- **Some reported numbers appear inconsistent or at least insufficiently explained.** Table 1 reports FakeShield DeepFake ACC/F1 as 0.93/0.93, while Table 2 reports 0.9835/0.9915 and Table 5 reports 0.98/0.99. Similarly, Table 1 reports AIGC-Editing as 0.98/0.99, while Table 5 reports 0.93/0.93. These may correspond to different subsets or protocols, but the paper does not clearly explain the differences.

- **Claims of precise localization should be tempered.** Table 4 is favorable overall, but performance remains low on some benchmarks, e.g., Korus at 0.17 IoU/0.20 F1 and NIST at 0.32 IoU/0.37 F1. FakeShield also ties MVSS-Net on AIGC-Editing and is below PSCC-Net on Korus F1. Thus the paper should state “best on most datasets” rather than implying consistently precise localization across all settings.

- **The robustness study is limited.** Table 5 evaluates only FakeShield on CASIA1+ under JPEG compression and Gaussian noise and reports explanation/localization but not detection. This supports limited stability under simple degradations, but not a broader practical robustness claim or a comparative robustness advantage over baselines.

- **The MFLM training/evaluation description is somewhat unclear.** Section 3.4 says MFLM consumes the detailed explanation output, but the training objective in Eq. 5 refers to a short ground-truth prompt such as “It is `<seg>`.” The paper should clarify whether detailed natural-language forensic explanations are used during MFLM training and evaluation, and how the `<SEG>` token is appended or generated.

### Trivial

None that should affect evaluation. Formatting and parser artifacts are ignored.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Add AUC/AP or threshold-sensitivity results for detection, since the paper currently reports ACC/F1 with a default 0.5 threshold.
- Include more qualitative examples showing the image, predicted explanation, predicted mask, and ground-truth mask together, especially for failure cases and authentic images.
- Report a confusion matrix for DTG and its behavior on authentic images, wrong tags, random tags, and unseen manipulation types.
- Expand robustness evaluation to include baselines and additional realistic distortions, but this is secondary to the larger explanation and split-protocol issues.

- **Generic strength: “the task formulation is meaningful for practical forensic tools.”** This is directionally fair, but it is not an evidential strength by itself. The review instead credits the concrete multi-output framework.

- **Formatting, grammar, spelling, capitalization, or parser-artifact criticisms.** The extracted text contains obvious formatting artifacts, but these are not evaluation-relevant.
- **Requests for exhaustive implementation logs or trivial hyperparameter details.** The paper already reports LoRA ranks, training epochs, learning rates, batch sizes, and hardware in Section 4.1; more detail could help reproducibility but is not a core weakness.
- **Missing appendix/proof-style concerns.** Not applicable and not considered.
- **Overly strong version of “the method cannot generalize beyond three domains.”** The paper does evaluate three manipulation families, so the fair criticism is not that it has no generalization evidence, but that the split/protocol details and closed-set domain-tag design do not justify the strongest real-world generalization claims.

## Novel Insights

A key issue is that FakeShield’s strongest empirical tables and its weakest conceptual evidence are linked: the model is trained and evaluated in a pipeline where GPT-4o descriptions, generated with mask access, define the explanation target. This makes the reported CSS gains plausible and useful as annotation-style imitation, but not sufficient as evidence of faithful forensic reasoning. Similarly, the domain-tag mechanism may genuinely improve performance on the benchmark, yet without stronger split controls and tag diagnostics, the same mechanism could amplify dataset/domain shortcuts rather than robust manipulation evidence.

## Suggestions

- Add an explanation evaluation that is independent of GPT-4o label imitation: human/expert judgments of correctness, specificity, and usefulness; region-text consistency checks; and faithfulness tests where suspected regions are occluded or altered.
- Clearly document MMTD-Set statistics and split construction: image counts per domain, authentic/tampered balance, train/val/test sizes, source datasets, manipulation methods, generator/editing tools, subject/source-image separation, and overlap prevention.
- Evaluate strict out-of-distribution protocols, such as leave-dataset-out, leave-manipulation-method-out, leave-generator-out, or unseen-domain testing.
- Clarify whether Table 4 localization uses predicted explanations or teacher-forced descriptions, and add a fully end-to-end evaluation on mixed authentic and tampered images, including false-positive mask rates for authentic images.
- Add architecture-matched baselines: SAM with short prompts, SAM with GPT-4o descriptions, LISA/GLaMM-style reasoning segmentation adapted to the same data, and relevant contemporary IFDL methods already discussed in the paper.
- Diagnose DTG with wrong/random tags, authentic-image tags, confusion matrices, and unseen-domain experiments.
- Temper claims about “precise localization,” “explainability,” and “strong generalization” to match what the current experiments actually demonstrate.
