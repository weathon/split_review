## Summary

This paper proposes the Fast and Slow Effect (FSE) framework, a fully autonomous method for evaluating whether LLM/VLM-generated concept annotations are sufficiently discriminative to characterize their target classes. The framework simulates a progression from "fast mode" (visual-only classification without textual reasoning) to "slow mode" (classification using only progressively gathered textual concepts), measured via the Class Representation Index (CRI). The key finding is a consistent and substantial gap on fine-grained datasets: slow mode drops ≥25% behind fast mode on average across six models, while on general datasets slow mode actually outperforms fast mode. The paper also shows that fusing visual and concept inputs (simulating the utility-as-proxy approach) masks this insufficiency, demonstrating that high downstream accuracy does not guarantee annotation quality.

## Strengths

- **Empirical refutation of the utility-as-proxy assumption (Table 4):** The paper provides direct evidence that high fused-mode accuracy (~84–97%) can coexist with low slow-mode accuracy (~42–69%) on the same datasets. This cleanly demonstrates that strong downstream performance does not imply annotation sufficiency, a well-motivated and practically important finding.

- **Cross-dataset contrast as a partial control:** The finding that slow mode *outperforms* fast mode on general datasets (CIFAR-100, Caltech-101) while severely underperforming on fine-grained datasets is a genuine insight. This contrast rules out the trivial explanation that the models are simply bad at text-based reasoning — if they were, they would fail everywhere. The domain-specific nature of the gap supports the paper's claim that fine-grained concepts are genuinely insufficient.

- **Comprehensive multi-model, multi-dataset evaluation:** The paper evaluates 6 LLMs/VLMs across 5 datasets (CUB, Cars, Flowers, CIFAR-100, Caltech-101) with three runs each and negligible variance, lending credibility to the observed trends. The inclusion of both post-hoc and visual-grounded annotation scenarios increases the coverage.

- **Systematic distractor selection via contradiction test (Section 5.3, Table 1):** The preliminary experiment comparing random vs. semantically related distractors, and the finding that semantically related distractors increase contradiction rates from ~14–20% to ~34–45%, provides a principled methodological basis for constructing the candidate sets used in CRI evaluation.

- **Clear motivating example (Figure 1):** The dialogue showing the model correctly identifying a Red-faced Cormorant from pixels but then misidentifying it from its own concepts provides an intuitive and compelling illustration of the problem.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Self-evaluation design limits interpretability of CRI as a measure of annotation sufficiency:** The CRI uses the *same* model to generate concepts and then predict the class from those concepts (Eq. 2). This conflates two confounded factors: (a) whether the concepts are semantically sufficient, and (b) whether the model can effectively reason over textual descriptions. A model that generates adequate concepts but has poor text-only reasoning would produce low CRI scores that misleadingly suggest annotation insufficiency. The paper acknowledges the self-assessment framing (citing Kiciman et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2023; Panickssery et al., 2024) and the cross-dataset contrast provides a partial control, but this does not fully disentangle concept quality from reasoning ability. An independent evaluation — using a held-out model to predict from the generated concepts, or establishing a human-concept baseline — is needed to validate the core claim that "current annotation methods fail to provide sufficient semantic coverage." *This is the most significant limitation and should be addressed for the paper's central claims to be fully substantiated.*

- **No human-annotation or cross-model calibration baseline:** All CRI results are relative (slow vs. fast mode from the same model). Without a human-annotated concept baseline or an evaluation where concepts generated by model A are evaluated by model B, there is no independent reference point to calibrate what CRI scores are achievable. The paper's conclusion that current CRI scores are "insufficient" cannot be properly grounded without such a comparison. Even a small-scale human annotation study on one dataset would substantially strengthen the claims.

### Minor

- **CRI is classification accuracy in a specific setup, not a fundamentally novel metric:** The CRI (Eq. 2) is the accuracy of the model when restricted to concept-only inputs with a semantically similar candidate set. While the *setup* (progressive concept gathering, distractor selection) provides useful context, the paper's claim that CRI is a "novel evaluation metric" (contributions list) overstates the novelty. The metric itself does not directly measure concept expressiveness or semantic coverage — it measures whether the concepts suffice for the model's own correct classification, which is a useful but limited proxy.

- **No ablation of the five-stage prompting design:** The paper uses five refinement stages (Background, Superclass, Salient Features, Detailed Features, Auxiliary Features) building on prior hierarchical extraction work, but does not ablate whether all five stages are necessary or whether the ordering matters. A simpler two- or three-stage baseline would clarify whether the slow mode's underperformance is due to prompt design artifacts rather than a fundamental limitation of concept-based reasoning.

- **The utility-as-proxy critique, while well-demonstrated, echoes established leakage findings:** The finding that fused-mode accuracy can mask poor annotations is a clean empirical demonstration, but the theoretical point — that downstream accuracy does not guarantee meaningful concept usage — is already established in the concept leakage literature (Havasi et al., 2022, cited by the paper). The paper's contribution here is empirical confirmation rather than new theoretical insight.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Cross-model evaluation:** Evaluate concepts generated by model A using model B's predictions (e.g., GPT-4o concepts evaluated by Llama reasoning). This would help isolate concept quality from reasoning quality.
- **Human annotation baseline:** Even a small-scale human concept annotation study on one dataset (e.g., 20 classes from CUB) would provide an upper-bound reference for CRI scores.
- **Error analysis of slow mode failures:** The paper aggregates CRI scores but does not analyze whether failures stem from missing concepts, overly generic concepts, or incorrect concepts. A qualitative analysis of failure cases (e.g., what types of concepts lead to correct vs. incorrect predictions) would strengthen the paper's diagnostic value.
- **Ablation on the number of prompting stages:** Testing a simpler 2-stage or 3-stage scheme would show whether the 5-stage design is necessary or potentially detrimental.

## Novel Insights

The reviewer analysis surfaces a recurring tension in XAI evaluation: the more "autonomous" a framekwork claims to be, the harder it is to escape self-referential validation. The FSE paper makes a genuine empirical contribution by documenting a consistent and domain-specific fast/slow gap, but it illustrates a broader challenge in the field — evaluating annotation quality without human ground truth seems to inevitably require some form of independent verification (different model, human annotators, or a held-out reasoning module) to break the circularity. The cross-dataset comparison in this paper is an elegant partial solution, but the field would benefit from establishing standardized independent evaluation protocols.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a cross-model evaluation experiment:** Have concepts generated by GPT-4o evaluated by Llama or Qwen (and vice versa). If the CRI gap persists across evaluators, the claim of annotation insufficiency would be substantially strengthened.

2. **Provide a small human-concept baseline:** Even 50–100 classes from one fine-grained dataset annotated by a domain expert (e.g., from existing CUB attribute annotations) would calibrate what CRI values are achievable.

3. **Tone down claims about CRI novelty:** The CRI is accuracy in a designed setup. Reframe it as "a quantitative index operationalized through self-evaluation in a structured candidate set" rather than a fundamentally new metric.

4. **Ablate the number of prompting stages:** Compare the 5-stage chain against a 2-stage or 3-stage alternative to ensure the observed slow-mode gap is not an artifact of the specific prompt design.

**Rationale:** The paper identifies a genuine and important problem — current LLM-generated concept annotations may be insufficient for fine-grained classification — and presents an intuitive evaluation framework with extensive experimentation across 6 models and 5 datasets. The cross-dataset contrast (slow mode fails on fine-grained but works on general data) is a real insight and provides partial mitigation for the self-evaluation concern. However, the core evaluation design uses the same model for both concept generation and assessment, which fundamentally conflates annotation quality with reasoning quality. Without independent validation (different evaluator model, human baseline, or both), the paper's central claim — that "current annotation methods fail to provide sufficient semantic coverage" — cannot be reliably distinguished from the alternative explanation that models simply struggle with text-only fine-grained reasoning. This is a significant limitation for a paper whose primary contribution is an evaluation framework. The paper has real value as a diagnostic tool and deserves encouragement for revision, but in its current form the evidence does not fully support the strength of the claims.
