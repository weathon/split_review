## Summary

This paper identifies and systematically quantifies a "priming vulnerability" in masked diffusion language models (MDLMs): when affirmative tokens appear at intermediate denoising steps, they steer generation toward harmful outputs even in safety-aligned models. The authors design the anchoring attack to characterize this vulnerability, derive a monotonicity-based lower bound to justify a practical optimization-based attack (First-Step GCG) that requires no denoising intervention, and propose Recovery Alignment (RA) — an RLHF-style training method that teaches models to recover safe responses from adversarially contaminated intermediate states. Experiments across three MDLMs and two datasets show that RA reduces ASR from >60% to single digits under priming attacks while largely preserving general capabilities.

## Strengths

- **Systematic quantification of the priming vulnerability**: The anchoring attack in Section 4.1 provides a clean, controlled measurement — injecting a single affirmative token at the first denoising step raises ASR from 2% to 21% on aligned LLaDA Instruct (Figure 2). The ASR scales predictably with intervention step, confirming the vulnerability is real and measurable.
- **Practical attack derivation**: Theorem 4.1 provides theoretical motivation for First-Step GCG, which achieves up to 4× higher ASR and 20× lower runtime than MC-based GCG (Table 1). This demonstrates the vulnerability can be exploited without denoising intervention, addressing a more realistic threat model.
- **Effective and well-designed defense**: Recovery Alignment reduces ASR under anchoring attacks dramatically — e.g., from 68.7% to 3.0% at t_inter=4 on LLaDA Instruct (Table 2). The linear scheduling curriculum is a practical contribution validated by ablation (Figure 3b).
- **Broad experimental coverage**: Evaluation spans three MDLMs (LLaDA Instruct, LLaDA 1.5, MMaDA MixCoT), two datasets (JBB-Behaviors, AdvBench), three safety evaluators (GPT-4o, LLaMA Guard 3, keyword matching), and four priming attacks plus three conversational attacks (Tables 2–3, Appendix C). General capability is measured across 11 benchmarks (Table 4).
- **Well-executed ablations**: The ablation on intervention step scheduling (linear vs. uniform vs. constant, Figure 3b) and on max intervention step (Figure 3a) provide actionable insights. The RA w/o inter ablation convincingly shows that training on contaminated intermediate states is essential.
- **The paper is clearly written and well-structured**, with a logical flow from vulnerability characterization to attack derivation to defense design, and honest discussion of limitations.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

None. The core claims — that MDLMs exhibit a priming vulnerability, that it can be exploited without denoising intervention, and that RA substantially mitigates it — are all well supported.

### Minor

- **Theorem 4.1 validation is limited in scope**: The monotonicity assumption underpinning Theorem 4.1 is empirically checked only on anchor-contaminated states generated by the anchoring attack (Appendix C.2), not on the full forward-process distribution q(r_t | r_T) that appears in the ELBO derivation. While the empirical validation across three models is encouraging and the paper acknowledges edge cases (C.2.1: "this property is not guaranteed to hold uniformly for every possible response r"), the theorem's proof relies on the assumption holding across the distribution of forward states in the ELBO. The paper would benefit from recasting the result more cautiously as a well-motivated heuristic, or noting that the assumed monotonicity has been verified only for a restricted class of intermediate states. The empirical success of First-Step GCG partially mitigates this concern.

- **Residual vulnerability to conversational attacks**: RA leaves substantial residual ASR against strong black-box attacks — ReNeLLM still achieves 72.3% on LLaDA Instruct and Crescendo 45.0% (Table 3). The paper acknowledges this limitation (lines 602–605), but it tempers the practical significance of RA as a general safety solution. The defense is primarily effective against the priming vulnerability class; its generalization to conversational jailbreaks is partial.

- **Non-trivial HumanEval degradation on LLaDA Instruct**: HumanEval drops from 22.0 to 17.1 (~22% relative decrease, Table 4) on LLaDA Instruct after RA. While the overall average across 11 benchmarks is maintained (52.2 → 52.6), this specific regression deserves more analysis. The paper attributes this broadly to "potential forgetting effects or output style shifts" (line 777) but does not investigate whether it reflects over-refusal, generation style changes, or catastrophic forgetting.

### Trivial

- The paper could benefit from a clearer upfront acknowledgment that the priming vulnerability bears conceptual similarity to prefilling attacks in ARMs. While Section 2.2 and the introduction do note this connection, the framing could be more precise about what is genuinely MDLM-specific (the iterative/parallel mechanism) versus what generalizes from ARMs.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A qualitative example showing a recovery trajectory (intermediate states transitioning from contamination back to safety) would make the RA mechanism more concrete and compelling.
- A DPO-style instantiation of RA (acknowledged in Limitations, line 821) could reduce training cost. While not required for the current contribution, exploring this direction would address the practical barrier of ~16 hours of training on 4 GPUs.
- A simple refusal-rate analysis on harmless queries before and after RA would help clarify whether the HumanEval drop reflects over-refusal or a different mechanism.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, the review process surfaces an interesting meta-observation: the safety vulnerability landscape for diffusion language models is converging rapidly across concurrent work. The priming vulnerability characterized here, the mask-based attacks in DIJA, and the denoising-path dependence in DiffuGuard all point to the same root cause — MDLM training that conditions only on fully masked initial states fails to prepare models for the distribution of intermediate states encountered during generation. This suggests that safety alignment for diffusion models may require a fundamentally different approach than for autoregressive models, one that explicitly considers the entire denoising trajectory rather than just endpoint behavior.

## Suggestions

- Recast Theorem 4.1 more cautiously. Rather than presenting it as a theorem requiring a strong assumption validated only on a restricted class of states, frame the monotonicity as an empirically observed property that motivates First-Step GCG. The attack's success stands on its own empirical merits.
- Add a brief analysis of the HumanEval drop: is it over-refusal, style shift, or forgetting? A simple check of whether the model still attempts to generate code (vs. refusing) on HumanEval prompts would clarify.
- Consider including a qualitative recovery trajectory example in the camera-ready version to make RA more intuitively graspable.
