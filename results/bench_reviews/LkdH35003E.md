## Summary

The paper argues that the ML community should adopt **centaur evaluations** — benchmarks where humans and AI jointly solve tasks — rather than the current practice of evaluating AI systems in isolation. It advances three supporting arguments: (1) centaur evaluations can be harder and more realistic, resisting saturation; (2) they enable direct evaluation of human-centered desiderata like interpretability and helpfulness; and (3) most importantly, they can re-center ML development toward human augmentation rather than human replacement. The third argument is formalized through a "centaur production function" Y = f(K, L) whose partial derivative ∂f/∂L measures the marginal value of human input, making current evaluations that measure only f(K, 0) structurally blind to augmentation.

## Strengths

- **Clear, important position with genuine contemporary relevance.** The central claim — that ML evaluation should systematically include humans as collaborators — is crisply stated and addresses an urgent gap as AI systems increasingly operate as assistants rather than autonomous agents. The paper stakes out a position that is neither trivially true nor uncontroversial.

- **Insightful identification of a structural methodological gap (Section 4.2).** The observation that current evaluations evaluate f(K, 0) — performance with zero human input — and are therefore blind to learning-to-defer, interpretability for deployers, and other human-centered desiderata is a precise, consequential critique. The EU AI Act example (Art. 13 requiring explainability "to enable deployers") is well-chosen and demonstrates that this isn't merely an academic concern.

- **Productive conceptual contributions.** The three-component definition of centaur evaluations (Human, Interface, Scoring), the "centaurized evaluations" concept for retrofitting existing benchmarks, and the five stages of automation framework (Figure 3) all provide useful scaffolding that enables concrete discussion rather than vague advocacy.

- **Honest engagement with counterarguments (Section 6).** The paper takes on the strongest objections — that augmentation doesn't exist, that centaur evaluations are noisy, and that they aren't worth their cost — rather than ignoring them.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **The production function formalization (Section 4.3) is undermined by the proxy problem, and the paper acknowledges this without resolving it.** The paper flags the critical assumption with "For a moment assume that the evaluation performance is a good proxy for the monetary value of an economically relevant task" and then never justifies it. The paper's own critique in Section 4.1 argues that current benchmarks are too narrow and saturate quickly — which directly undermines using benchmark performance Y as a proxy for economic output. The fallback ("Even beyond tasks for which we cannot assume that success is a good proxy of monetary value… ∂f/∂L(K,L) is a helpful measure") is reasonable as a measure of marginal benchmark points per human minute, but strips the formalization of its economic content — it no longer measures "augmentation" in the wage-and-power sense that motivates the entire section. This matters because the paper marks Section 4.3 as "for us most importantly." The core position doesn't collapse without this formalization (Sections 4.1 and 4.2 stand independently), but the section that carries the paper's strongest normative claims requires more than a conditional that is then retracted.

- **The response to the "augmentation doesn't exist" counterargument (Section 6.1) is the weakest part of the paper's argumentation.** The cited metastudy (Vaccaro et al., 2024) finding "mostly no human augmentation" across 2020–2023 studies is a serious empirical challenge. The rebuttal lists four cherry-picked positive examples (child protective services, call centers, entrepreneurship, chess) without addressing why these should be considered representative or why the broader finding should be discounted. The chess example is particularly problematic for the paper's thesis: the own account shows a trajectory from human → centaur → full automation, suggesting that centaur evaluations measure a transient state rather than redirecting technological change. If automation naturally progresses from augmentation to replacement regardless of evaluation methodology, this undermines the claimed causal link between centaur evaluations and redirecting ML development. The paper needs either a mechanism explaining why centaur evaluations would arrest this trajectory or an account of which tasks can sustainably remain in augmented states.

### Minor

- **The "centaur evaluations can be harder" claim (Section 4.1) rests on a diagram that asserts its conclusion rather than demonstrating it.** Figure 1 shows nested rectangles (centaur tasks ⊃ human tasks ⊃ scorable tasks), which assumes the containment rather than arguing for it. Adding a human to an AI system can make tasks easier (the human does the hard part) or create tasks that resist scoring, which could place some centaur tasks *below* scorable tasks in evaluative rigor. The reference to Moravec's paradox concerns what is difficult for AI vs. humans, not whether joint evaluation creates harder benchmarks.

- **The definition of centaur evaluations (Section 2.1) is broad to the point of allowing trivial involvement.** A "centaurized MMLU" where a human can be involved allows for scenarios where the human simply relays the AI's answer. The paper does not distinguish between substantive human contribution and decorative involvement, yet this distinction determines whether the proposal is substantive or trivially true.

- **Goodhart's law for centaur evaluations is not addressed.** If centaur evaluations become high-stakes, systems will optimize for centaur scores — potentially making humans dependent on AI rather than genuinely enhancing their capabilities. This could distort the very measure of augmentation the paper proposes.

- **"Not enough empirical evidence / experiments":** This is a position paper arguing from reasoning, historical analysis, and conceptual argument. Demanding empirical validation of the position is inappropriate. The paper is not making an empirical contribution—it makes a normative argument about what should be done.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most distinctive contribution is the observation that current evaluation practice mathematically institutionalizes a blind spot: by evaluating f(K, 0) — system performance with zero human input — the community systematically creates incentives for replacement rather than augmentation, because the marginal value of human time ∂f/∂L is structurally unmeasured. Even if the production function formalization has gaps, this observation reframes the augmentation-vs-replacement debate from a moral question to a measurement question, which is a genuinely productive reframing.

## Suggestions

- Either deepen the production function formalization by discussing when benchmark performance can plausibly proxy economic value, or demote it and lead with the stronger desiderata argument from Section 4.2, using the production function as an illustrative analogy rather than the structural foundation.
- Address the chess counterexample head-on by providing an account of *which tasks* can sustainably remain in augmented states and why evaluation methodology would affect the trajectory. The five stages framework is a good starting point but currently leaves the reader with the natural inference that all tasks progress to full automation.
- Sharpen the definition of centaur evaluations by specifying what constitutes *meaningful* human involvement (e.g., the human must have causal influence on the outcome beyond mere relay), to distinguish substantive proposals from trivial ones.
