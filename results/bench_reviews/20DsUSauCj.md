## Summary

This paper introduces "persona vectors" — linear directions in LLM activation space extracted automatically from natural-language trait descriptions — and demonstrates their use for monitoring, controlling, and predicting personality shifts in LLMs. The authors validate persona vectors through steering experiments, show that finetuning-induced persona shifts correlate strongly with activation shifts along these vectors (r=0.76–0.97), propose a novel preventative steering method that mitigates undesirable shifts during training while better preserving general capabilities, and demonstrate that projection onto persona vectors can predict post-finetuning trait expression before training begins.

## Strengths

- **Novel preventative steering method with compelling evidence.** Steering *toward* an undesired direction *during* training (rather than against it at inference) to proactively counteract drift is a genuinely novel and non-obvious contribution. The fact-acquisition case study (Figure 6) provides concrete evidence that this approach reduces hallucinations to baseline while preserving both new-fact accuracy and MMLU performance — unlike inference-time steering, which degrades both. This is the paper's strongest contribution.

- **Strong correlation evidence linking finetuning to linear activation changes.** The demonstration that finetuning-induced persona shifts correlate at r=0.76–0.97 with activation shifts along the corresponding persona vector (Figure 4), and that these correlations are higher than cross-trait baselines (r=0.34–0.86), provides clean evidence for the linear representation hypothesis in the practically important context of model safety. The breadth of datasets (trait-eliciting and EM-like) and models (Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B) strengthens the result.

- **Pre-finetuning data screening is a practically useful capability.** The projection-difference metric (Figure 7, r=0.88–0.95) predicts post-finetuning trait expression *before any training*, and sample-level separability (Figure 8) holds even for emergent-misalignment datasets not explicitly designed to elicit traits. This enables proactive identification of problematic data, a capability not demonstrated in prior activation-steering work.

- **Automated, language-driven pipeline.** The extraction procedure requires only a natural-language trait description and produces validated persona vectors without manual dataset construction, making the approach broadly applicable. The pipeline is well-described and builds sensibly on prior work (Wu et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2025).

- **Comprehensive experimental design.** The paper evaluates across two model families, three+ traits, multiple dataset types, and four distinct applications (steering, monitoring, training intervention, data screening). The honest discussion of limitations (e.g., modest within-group correlations in monitoring, cross-trait correlations) adds credibility.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Steering results (Figures 5, 6) lack uncertainty quantification.** The paper does not report error bars, confidence intervals, or variance across random seeds or independent training runs for these figures. The claim that preventative steering "better preserves general capabilities" than inference-time steering rests on point estimates — it is unclear whether the observed differences would replicate across multiple seeds. Given that each experiment involves a single training run per dataset, the reported MMLU and new-fact accuracy values could reflect noise rather than meaningful differences. This undermines the strength of the paper's most novel contribution.

- **Heavy reliance on LLM-based evaluation without human agreement numbers in the main text.** The paper's central metric (trait expression score) is produced by GPT-4.1-mini using a rubric generated by Claude 3.7 Sonnet. The paper states that the judge is validated against human evaluators and external benchmarks (Appendix D), but no numeric agreement (e.g., Cohen's κ, Pearson r, or AUC) appears in the main body. Readers cannot assess the reliability of the metric that underpins essentially every quantitative result. The appendix being stripped by the parser does not mitigate this — key numbers should be in the main text.

### Minor

- **The data-screening application is demonstrated as a predictor, not validated as an intervention.** The paper claims persona vectors "can be used to flag training data that will produce undesirable personality changes" — and the correlation (Figure 7) and separability (Figure 8) evidence supports flagging/prediction. However, the paper never shows the natural next step: finetuning on a dataset filtered by projection and measuring whether the persona shift is actually reduced. This leaves the practical utility of the screening method one step short of full validation. The paper is honest about what it demonstrates, but the headline application would be substantially stronger with this experiment.

- **The evaluation pipeline could suffer from coupling between vector extraction and evaluation.** The same LLM-judge family (GPT-4.1-mini with the pipeline-generated rubric) is used both to filter the contrastive responses during extraction and to evaluate the downstream trait expression. While the vectors themselves are derived from activation differences (not judge scores), and the paper validates against humans (Appendix D), the lack of main-text validation numbers means the full evaluation pipeline's independence from the extraction pipeline remains unclear.

- **Only tested on 7–8B parameter models.** The linear representation assumption and preventative steering method are only validated on two models of similar scale. Scaling to larger models (e.g., 70B+) would substantively strengthen claims about general applicability.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Full filtering intervention experiment:** Finetune on a dataset from which high-projection samples have been removed and measure whether post-finetuning trait expression is reduced, confirming the practical utility of data screening.
- **Distribution or box plots for Figures 5 and 6** to visualize variance across prompts/datasets at each steering coefficient.

## Novel Insights

The most insightful observation that emerges across reviews — beyond the paper's own contributions — is that the paper reveals a *practical asymmetry* between inference-time and training-time interventions that has implications beyond personality traits. The finding that steering *toward* an undesired direction during training (preventative) preserves capabilities better than steering *against* it at inference (remedial) suggests a general principle: interventions that work *with* the training dynamics (by counteracting the gradient's tendency to push along a direction) may be fundamentally less destructive than post-hoc corrections that fight against the model's learned representations. This insight could generalize to other safety domains, such as preventing capability degradation during continued training on sensitive domains.

## Suggestions

1. **Add error bars or confidence bands to Figures 5 and 6** from multiple seeds or bootstrapping, and state the number of independent runs in the caption. This is the single most important fix.
2. **Include human-judge agreement statistics** (Cohen's κ, Pearson r, or AUC) prominently in the main text, not only in the appendix.
3. **Add the filtering intervention experiment** (finetune on data filtered by projection difference, measure whether trait shift is reduced) as a new figure in Section 6. This would turn a correlational result into a validated tool.
4. **Report variance across prompts** for steering experiments — e.g., scatter points or box plots at each coefficient value rather than only mean lines.
