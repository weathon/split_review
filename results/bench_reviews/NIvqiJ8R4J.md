## Summary

This paper introduces PELICAN, a two-stage LLM-based tutoring framework that combines collaborative cognitive diagnosis (using a successor-first strategy with an expert-assistant-verifier pipeline) with adaptive tutoring (using fast/slow thinking for strategy selection via a Simulated Teaching Tree). The approach is evaluated on the Gaokao dataset in simulation and with a human study of 169 students.

## Strengths

- **Novel two-stage design with principled motivation.** The integration of cognitive diagnosis and adaptive tutoring into a single LLM-powered pipeline is genuinely novel. The successor-first diagnostic strategy that leverages knowledge dependencies and the expert-assistant-verifier pipeline for question quality control are well-motivated. The ablation study (Table 3) shows that removing the diagnosis stage drops coverage from 54.84% to 47.76% and frequency from 61.47% to 48.21%, providing direct evidence that the integration contributes to performance.

- **Slow-thinking simulation for strategy selection is inventive.** The Simulated Teaching Tree that evaluates candidate strategies by simulating future dialogue paths is a creative application of dual-system theory to tutoring. The ablation confirms its value: removing slow thinking reduces Suitability from 4.17 to 4.00 and Overall quality from 4.28 to 4.08 (Table 3).

- **Strategy distribution analysis confirms adaptive behavior.** Figure 4 shows that PELICAN adaptively uses different strategies at different cognitive levels (e.g., analogies used 22% for low-level vs. 15% for high-level students), providing behavioral evidence that the framework actually personalizes.

- **Real human evaluation strengthens ecological validity.** The study includes a human experiment with 169 students (1,335 tutoring reports), and the results (Table 6) show the same trends as the automated evaluation, with PELICAN achieving the highest scores on most metrics including R_coverage (70.04 vs. next best 63.91) and Overall (4.39 vs. 4.14).

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **The +18.7% and +22.4% improvement numbers in the abstract are not traceable to any specific table, metric, or baseline in the paper.** The abstract states "significant improvements in critical thinking stimulation (+18.7%) and task completion rates (+22.4%) compared to baseline models," but nowhere in the paper are these percentages derived. "Critical thinking stimulation" does not correspond to a defined metric; Inspiration (the closest proxy) shows a 74% relative increase over Free-Prompt and an 8% increase over Socratic in the human evaluation. "Task completion rate" does not match the 1.9% relative improvement in success rate (86.8% vs. 85.2%) from Table 6. The paper must clarify which baseline and which metric produce these specific numbers. As written, the abstract misrepresents the empirical results.

- **Massive unexplained discrepancy between PELICAN's R_coverage in Table 2 (72.36) and Table 3 (54.84).** In Table 2, PELICAN achieves R_coverage=72.36, well above all baselines. In Table 3 (ablation study, same metric name), the full PELICAN model achieves R_coverage=54.84 — a drop of 24%. Moreover, this 54.84 value is *lower* than Socratic's 64.47 and Free-Prompt's 59.81 from Table 2. If the ablation experiments were run under the same conditions, this would mean the full PELICAN performs worse than baselines in the ablation setting. If conditions differ (different data subsets, evaluation protocols, etc.), the paper must explain why. Without clarification, this discrepancy severely undermines confidence in the reported metrics.

- **Main experiments (Tables 1–5) use simulated students without explicit acknowledgment in the main text.** The paper references "Design details of the student role (Appendix G)" in the Implementation Details section and says "we initialize three different cognitive levels for the students" (Section 4.4), but never explicitly states that student responses in the main experiments are generated by a simulated student model. For a paper whose headline claim is personalization for real students, the central evidence coming from a simulated proxy should be clearly disclosed. The human evaluation (Section 4.6) partially addresses this, but the gap between simulation-based and human results (e.g., Inspiration margins) is notable.

### Minor

- **GPT-based evaluation may suffer from model-family confound.** The tutoring results in Tables 2, 3, and 4 rely on GPT-based evaluations for subjective dimensions (Suitability, Logic, Inspiration, Reliability). Since the teacher model is GPT-4o, using the same model family (or the same model) as evaluator could systematically favor outputs matching its own style. This is a known issue (self-preference bias). The paper should clarify whether the evaluator model differs from the teacher and report human correlation for subjective dimensions.

- **Qwen-max outperforms GPT-4o on R_coverage (64.41 vs. 54.84) in Table 4, but this is not discussed.** If the framework's coverage metric is supposed to measure attention to non-mastered knowledge, this result deserves analysis — it could indicate that weaker base models compensate differently, or that coverage is influenced by factors beyond the framework's design.

- **The human evaluation success rates are nearly indistinguishable.** PELICAN's 86.8% success rate is very close to Free-Prompt (85.2%) and Sepwise (86.5%). While other metrics show clearer advantages, the headline success metric shows only marginal improvement. Statistical significance (ANOVA referenced in Appendix K.1) should be reported in the main text.

### Trivial

- The hyperparameter M=1 (activating slow thinking after one round) is aggressive but not ablated or justified.

- Token cost (~230k tokens for slow thinking, ~40% of total) is discussed but not compared to baseline costs.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation that emerges from these reviews is the disconnect between the paper's evaluation architecture and its claims. The framework itself is innovative and the design decisions (successor-first diagnosis, simulated teaching tree) are well-grounded. However, the paper's evaluation has a "two-tier" problem: the simulation experiments show large margins but are conducted on a synthetic proxy, while the human study is more ecologically valid but shows much smaller improvements. The abstract cherry-picks the large simulation numbers without acknowledging this gap. Combined with the unexplained R_coverage discrepancy between the main and ablation tables, the paper's empirical foundation is significantly weaker than its methodological contribution. The real value may be in the framework's architecture as a testbed for tutoring strategy research rather than as a deployable system validated by the current evidence.

## Suggestions

1. **Clarify the abstract numbers.** Either remove the specific percentages (+18.7%, +22.4%) or explicitly state which baseline and which metric they derive from. If they come from specific comparisons (e.g., Inspiration over Free-Prompt in simulation), say so.

2. **Explain the Table 2 vs. Table 3 R_coverage discrepancy.** If the ablation study uses a different experimental setup (e.g., different data subset, fewer questions, different student model), clearly state this. If the metrics are computed differently, explain how. Without this, the metric appears unstable.

3. **Explicitly disclose the simulated student setting in the main text.** A single sentence in Section 4 stating "Student responses in the main experiments are generated by an LLM-based student model (see Appendix G)" is needed for transparency.

4. **Report confidence intervals and statistical tests for the human evaluation in the main text**, not only in the appendix.
