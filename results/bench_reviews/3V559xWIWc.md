## Summary

This paper identifies a training-inference misalignment problem in tree-based speculative decoding: existing draft model training methods (EAGLE, HASS) optimize for linear token sequences, while inference uses token trees. The authors propose two components: (1) **TALF**, a tree-aware loss function that aggregates cross-entropy over all nodes of a target-generated tree, training the draft model to match the target's distribution across branches; and (2) **SALF**, an early-stopping criterion for dynamic tree construction that stops drafting when the estimated probability gain falls below a threshold. Experiments on three model families and five benchmarks show consistent speedups over EAGLE-2 and HASS.

## Strengths

1. **Well-motivated problem identification with diagnostic evidence.** The paper identifies a genuine gap in existing SpD methods — that sequence-level training ignores the tree structure used at inference. Figure 2 provides clear diagnostic evidence that HASS underperforms on lower-ranked tokens when self-conditioned, which is precisely the regime where tree-based decoding operates. This analysis is the strongest part of the paper.

2. **Principled tree-aware training objective (TALF).** TALF is a clean, conceptually sound approach: it takes target-generated trees as training references and computes per-node cross-entropy losses, aggregating them via tree attention. Algorithm 1 is clear, and Figure 2(b) directly shows TALF achieving ~5% higher accuracy and 0.05 lower ECE on lower-ranked tokens compared to HASS. Table 2 shows that under the same tree construction method, TALF consistently improves τ over both EAGLE-2 and HASS by 7–13%.

3. **SALF provides a practical speedup-overhead tradeoff.** The SALF early-stopping criterion is simple, has a proven monotonicity guarantee (Theorem 1), and Table 2 demonstrates that despite reducing τ by ~6%, SALF improves end-to-end speedup by 14–19% over optimal tree search by cutting drafting overhead. Table 4's threshold sensitivity analysis provides useful practical guidance.

4. **Well-structured ablation study.** Table 2's 3×3 grid (three tree construction methods × three loss functions) cleanly separates the contributions of TALF and SALF. Table 3 shows that wider training trees (larger k) improve τ, and Table 4 systematically explores the SALF threshold. This is how ablation studies should be done.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

1. **The EAGLE-2 comparison in Table 1 is confounded by unequal training.** For Llama2-7B and Llama3-8B, the paper trains EAGLE for 10 epochs, while HASS and TALF receive 10+3=13 epochs of total training (10 epochs of EAGLE pre-training + 3 epochs of fine-tuning). This means the headline "15.6–39.4% over EAGLE-2" numbers in Table 1 partially reflect the additional training, not purely the methodological improvements. The HASS comparison (6.5–24.4%) is fair (same 13 epochs), and the Deepseek results in Table 2 use equal-time training (24 hours each) and show the same qualitative pattern. However, the paper should report corrected numbers where EAGLE-2 is also fine-tuned for the same additional 3 epochs, or train all methods from scratch for the same number of steps.

2. **Missing ablation: TALF with and without regression loss.** The paper states (Section 3.2): "Unlike EAGLE and HASS, TALF does not use a regression loss for feature alignment." This is a significant architectural departure from prior work, yet the paper provides no ablation showing what happens when regression loss is added to TALF or removed from HASS under the same tree-aware setup. The claim that "training solely on the token probability distributions... was sufficient" references experiments that are not shown. This is a confound: it is unclear whether TALF's gains come from tree awareness, from dropping the regression loss, or from both. The diagnostic evidence in Figure 2(b) and the k-ablation in Table 3 (where increasing tree width improves τ) suggest tree awareness contributes, but a controlled ablation is needed to quantify the contribution of each design choice.

### Minor

3. **"Without any generation quality degradation" claim lacks supporting evidence.** The conclusion claims SALF & TALF yield speedups "without any generation quality degradation." While rejection sampling in standard SpD preserves the target distribution exactly, tree-based SpD with tree attention involves architectural approximations. The paper measures only speedup, not output quality (e.g., perplexity, downstream task accuracy). This claim should be either supported with quality metrics or caveated.

4. **Training tree construction uses the target model, creating a potential inference-time mismatch.** As acknowledged in Section 3.2, the tree structures during training are generated by the target model (not the draft model) and fixed across epochs. During inference, the draft model constructs its own trees, which may differ in structure. The paper identifies training-inference misalignment as a core problem with prior methods, yet TALF introduces its own (mitigated but not eliminated) version of this mismatch. The impact of this discrepancy on downstream performance is not quantified. This is a pragmatic design choice, but the paper would benefit from analyzing how much tree structures at training vs. inference actually differ and whether this matters for final speedup.

5. **SALF's conceptual justification could be sharper.** Theorem 1 proves monotonic decrease of the probability sum S_i, which is a reasonable basis for early stopping. However, the connection between "sum of probabilities of the B frontier nodes" and "expected benefit of continuing drafting" is asserted rather than derived. The threshold th is an opaque hyperparameter — while Table 4 shows empirical tuning works, the paper defaults to th=0.6 despite th=0.5 giving higher mean speedup (2.62× vs 2.59×) on the Deepseek model, with the justification being "more consistent performance" across models without showing the cross-model data that supports this.

### Trivial

6. The pseudocode in Algorithm 2 has minor clarity issues: the comment on line 1 says G "contains up to N nodes" but the algorithm never explicitly prunes G, so a reader could be confused about whether G can exceed N during execution.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A fair comparison where EAGLE-2 is also fine-tuned for 3 additional epochs (or all methods trained from scratch for equal steps) for the Llama models would make Table 1's EAGLE-2 comparison interpretable.
- Reporting drafting overhead times (not just τ and total speedup) would help verify that SALF's speedup mechanism works as claimed — i.e., that the reduced τ is indeed compensated by lower drafting cost.
- Dynamic SALF threshold adaptation (noted as future work) would strengthen the practical contribution.

## Suggestions

1. **Run the fair training comparison.** Fine-tune EAGLE-2 for the same additional 3 epochs (or train all methods from scratch for equal steps) on the Llama models and report corrected speedup numbers. This is the single most important revision.

2. **Add the regression loss ablation.** Compare (a) TALF with regression loss, (b) TALF without regression loss, (c) HASS with regression loss, and (d) HASS without regression loss, all under the same tree construction method and training budget. This will disentangle the effect of tree awareness from the effect of dropping the regression loss.

3. **Provide generation quality metrics.** Report perplexity or downstream task accuracy (e.g., pass@1 for HumanEval, accuracy for GSM8K) for SALF & TALF generations vs. target model baselines to support or qualify the "no quality degradation" claim.

4. **Quantify the training/inference tree structure mismatch.** Measure how often and how severely the tree structures generated by the draft model during inference differ from those generated by the target model during training, and whether this affects speedup.
