## Summary

The paper introduces **Ambig-SWE**, an underspecified variant of SWE-Bench Verified, and uses it to systematically evaluate how LLM-based coding agents handle incomplete instructions through three sub-capacities: detecting underspecification (RQ2), asking targeted clarification questions (RQ3), and leveraging interaction to improve task completion (RQ1). The study evaluates six proprietary and open-weight models (Claude Sonnet 4/3.5, Haiku 3.5, Qwen 3 Coder, Deepseek-v2, Llama 3.1 70B) and finds that interaction can significantly improve resolve rates, but models default to non-interactive behavior and struggle to reliably detect missing information. The qualitative analysis of question-asking strategies is a genuine strength.

## Strengths

- **Systematic decomposition of underspecification resolution into three distinct capacities (detection, questioning, integration) with dedicated evaluations.** Prior work typically treats ambiguity/detection in isolation or uses end-to-end metrics. The paper breaks the problem into RQ1 (integrated resolution), RQ2 (detection), and RQ3 (question quality), enabling finer-grained diagnosis of where models succeed and fail. Evidence: Sections 3–5 each target one capacity.

- **Introduction of Ambig-SWE, a controlled underspecified variant of SWE-Bench Verified, enabling causal measurement of interaction impact.** The dataset pairs each fully-specified SWE-Bench Verified issue with a synthetic underspecified version generated by GPT-4o, allowing controlled studies with known ground truth. Evidence: Section 2.1 describes construction and distributional analysis.

- **Empirical demonstration that interaction recovers substantial performance lost to underspecification, with all models showing statistically significant gains.** Claude Sonnet 4 achieves 89% relative performance of its fully-specified setting (61.4% vs. 68.0%), and all models improve significantly from Hidden to Interaction settings (Wilcoxon Signed-Rank tests, Table 4). The recovery of the Hidden→Full performance gap reaches 76% for Claude Sonnet 4. Evidence: Figure 3, Section 3.2, Table 4.

- **Qualitative analysis of question-asking strategies revealing concrete, actionable insights for agent design.** The paper identifies an exploration-first strategy (Claude models explore codebase before asking) vs. ask-first strategy (Qwen, Deepseek), and distinguishes informational from navigational details. The finding that Qwen's performance actually *worsens* with navigational information due to rigid protocol-following is a specific, actionable insight. Evidence: Section 5.3, Table 1.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Detection experiment (RQ2) conflates "detecting missing information" with "deciding to interact," undermining construct validity.** The experiment presents fully-specified or underspecified issues and measures whether the model *chooses to interact* — recording this as accuracy, FPR, and FNR. This cannot distinguish between (a) the model not detecting missing information and (b) the model detecting it but strategically choosing to proceed (e.g., relying on internal knowledge). The paper itself acknowledges in Section 3.2 that Qwen 3 Coder "relies on its internal knowledge for key insights about missing information" and that its "correct assumptions potentially inflate its performance in the Hidden setting." This means Qwen's 100% FNR in RQ2 may reflect a rational strategy rather than detection failure. The prompt engineering analysis adds further confounds (instruction-following vs. detection). While the paper partially acknowledges limitations, the core metrics and framing ("underspecificity detection," Table 2 heading) imply a measurement of detection ability that the experimental design does not support. The conclusions about "models struggle to detect missing information" are weakened as a result. This is fixable by reframing RQ2 as measuring "tendency to ask" rather than "detection ability," and possibly supplementing with a forced-choice detection probe.

- **Unequal turn limits between models confound cross-model comparisons in RQ1.** Claude Sonnet 4 and Qwen 3 Coder receive up to 100 turns, while all other models are limited to 30 turns (Section 3.1). The paper acknowledges this difference but does not discuss how it could inflate the Interaction-setting performance of these models relative to others. Higher resolve rates for Claude Sonnet 4 and Qwen 3 Coder in the Interaction setting could partially reflect the luxury of more attempts and broader exploration rather than superior interaction ability. Within-model Hidden vs. Interaction comparisons are valid (turn limits are the same within each model), but any cross-model comparison of Interaction setting performance — or claims about which model "leverages interaction best" in absolute terms — is confounded. A sensitivity analysis (e.g., verifying that these models still benefit from interaction when restricted to 30 turns) is needed.

### Minor

- **The Ambig-SWE dataset's validity as a proxy for real-world underspecification is partially unverified.** The underspecified variants are generated by GPT-4o summarizing original issues. The paper's own distributional difference analysis (Section 2.1) shows these differ from natural underspecified issues — they lack concrete technical details, reproducibility information, and conversational fragments. While the paper argues some differences "may not directly impact agent performance," this is asserted without evidence. Behaviors observed on synthetic underspecification may not generalize to natural underspecification. This is a standard limitation of synthetic data but deserves emphasis.

- **The cosine distance metric for information gain (Section 5.2) is difficult to interpret.** The metric measures change in the embedding of the *task description* after interaction. A large change could mean the agent asked relevant questions, or that the user's responses were verbose and added tangential content. It does not directly measure whether the agent extracted the specific needed information. The paper acknowledges this indirectly ("models may prioritize details differently," Section 7) but the disconnect between cosine distance and resolve rates (e.g., Qwen gains the most information but converts it to proportionally less resolve improvement) suggests the metric captures something other than task-relevant information gain.

- **Claude Sonnet 4 is evaluated on only 100/500 instances in the Hidden setting** (footnote 4), justified by cost. While the paper claims findings remain statistically significant, the reduced sample for a key model in a key condition (the non-interactive underspecified baseline) means that some of the most impressive improvement numbers (Claude 4: 40% → 61.4%) are computed with partial data, adding uncertainty.

### Trivial

- The headline claim of "up to 74% improvement over non-interactive settings" is ambiguously sourced. The maximum relative improvement is 100% (Haiku), while 74% approximately matches Claude 4's gap recovery rate (76.4%). The phrasing is not technically wrong ("up to" is a bound) but could mislead readers about which quantity is being reported.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Equal-turn-limit subanalysis:** Rerun Interaction and Hidden experiments for Claude Sonnet 4 and Qwen 3 Coder with a 30-turn limit to isolate the effect of interaction from extra attempts.
- **Forced-choice detection probe:** Supplement RQ2 with a pure detection task (e.g., binary classification: "is information missing?") to separate detection ability from the decision to interact.
- **Human baseline or validation:** A small-scale human study validating that GPT-4o-generated underspecified issues elicit similar clarification behavior from real developers would strengthen external validity.
- **Trajectory length distribution:** A histogram showing how often Claude Sonnet 4 and Qwen 3 Coder exceed 30 turns would help readers gauge the magnitude of the turn-limit confound.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting finding is the interaction between model capability and interaction strategy: Claude Sonnet 4 achieves comparable information gain to Qwen 3 Coder with 50% fewer questions through an exploration-first strategy (exploring the codebase before asking), while Qwen asks more but integrates information more rigidly. This suggests that the bottleneck in underspecified code generation is not just whether models ask questions, but *how* they integrate the answers — a finding that resonates with the "lost in conversation" literature but extends it to the agentic tool-use setting. The counterintuitive result that Qwen's performance worsens with navigational information also challenges the assumption that more interaction is always better.

## Suggestions

1. **Reframe RQ2 as measuring interaction tendency, not detection ability.** Rename the research question and metrics to reflect what is actually measured: the propensity to ask for clarification under different prompt conditions. Supplement with a forced-choice detection task to separately measure detection capability.
2. **Provide a sensitivity analysis for the turn-limit confound.** At minimum, report how often Claude Sonnet 4 and Qwen 3 Coder exceed 30 turns in the Interaction setting and whether late turns drive their success.
3. **Present the "74%" and "89%" metrics with precise definitions** (e.g., "Claude Sonnet 4 recovers 76% of the performance gap between Hidden and Full settings") rather than aggregating across potentially different quantities.
4. **Consider validating a subset of Ambig-SWE with human annotators** to establish that the synthetic underspecification patterns produce similar agent behavior to naturally underspecified issues.
