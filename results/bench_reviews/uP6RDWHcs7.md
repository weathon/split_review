## Summary

The paper proposes Marginal Flow, a density estimation framework where a parametric distribution \(q(x|w)\) is marginalized by sampling latent parameters \(w\) from a learnable distribution \(q_\theta(w)\), implemented by pushing noise through an unconstrained neural network. The resulting density \(q_\theta(x) = \frac{1}{N_c}\sum_i q(x|w_{\theta,i})\) resembles a mixture model whose components are resampled at each evaluation. The method supports exact density evaluation (for the defined model), efficient sampling, unconstrained architectures, lower-dimensional manifold learning, and flexible choices of the parametric family \(q(x|w)\).

## Strengths

- **Orders-of-magnitude faster density evaluation and sampling than common baselines (Figure 3):** For models with comparable parameter counts (~100k), Marginal Flow is 10–100× faster than Normalizing Flow, Flow Matching, and Free-form Flow for both operations across dimensions \(d=10^2\) to \(10^5\). This is a genuine practical advantage for applications requiring repeated density evaluation.

- **Unconstrained architecture enables manifold learning and multi-modality (Figures 4, 5):** Because Marginal Flow does not require bijections or Jacobians, it correctly learns densities on lower-dimensional manifolds (Figure 4) and multi-modal targets (Figure 5) where Normalizing Flows and Flow Matching struggle. These are concrete demonstrations of a structural advantage.

- **Principled theoretical analysis of the nested Monte Carlo KL estimator (Appendix A.5–A.6):** The paper derives bias and variance expressions for the KL estimator (Lemmas A.2–A.3) and shows that the Monte Carlo penalty term depends on intrinsic dimension \(m\) rather than ambient dimension \(d\) (Theorem A.3). This analysis is technically sound under the stated assumptions and is empirically validated (Figure 17).

- **Flexible choice of parametric family for non-Euclidean data (Section 4.3):** The Wishart mixture experiment demonstrates that Marginal Flow can be adapted to distributions on positive-definite matrices simply by changing \(q(x|w)\), succeeding in a 100×100 setting where Normalizing Flows are computationally infeasible.

- **State-of-the-art or competitive results on SBI benchmarks (Figure 14):** Marginal Flow achieves best or near-best C2ST scores across several SBI tasks, particularly in low-data regimes (1,000 simulations).

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Overclaimed novelty and positioning:** The paper presents Marginal Flow as a fundamentally new framework, but it is essentially a learned mixture model where component parameters are generated by a neural network. The paper itself acknowledges that the model "resembles a mixture model with \(N_c\) components" (Section 2.1). The claimed distinction — that components are "resampled at each evaluation" — makes the model stochastic (the density at a fixed \(x\) changes between evaluations) but does not constitute a new class of models. The name "Marginal Flow" is misleading: there is no flow, transport, invertible transformation, or differential equation; the connection to normalizing flows is superficial. The paper would be better served by honestly positioning itself as a learned mixture model with theoretical analysis of its Monte Carlo estimator properties.

- **No ablation on the critical hyperparameter \(N_c\):** The number of Monte Carlo components \(N_c\) controls the essential trade-off between computational cost and approximation quality. The paper sets \(N_c\) to half the training set size for synthetic experiments and to 2048 or 128/256 for others, but never varies \(N_c\) to show how performance degrades as it decreases or how quality improves as it increases. Without this, the reader cannot assess whether the method's efficiency gains come from using small \(N_c\) that would be underpowered for complex distributions. This is the single most important missing experiment.

- **The "exact density" claim conflates model definition with practical behavior:** The paper repeatedly claims "exact density evaluation" (Table 1, abstract, conclusions). While technically true — \(q_\theta(x)\) is defined as the Monte Carlo average — this framing is misleading. For any finite \(N_c\), the model is a finite mixture, not a marginalization over a continuous latent variable. To achieve good density estimates on complex targets requires large \(N_c\), which increases evaluation cost to \(O(N_c\cdot d)\). The paper never discusses this trade-off. Moreover, the training objective itself uses a nested Monte Carlo estimator that Lemma A.2 confirms is biased for finite \(N_c\). The paper cannot present "exact density" as a clean advantage without a candid discussion of the \(N_c\)-dependent fidelity and cost.

### Minor

- **Runtime and convergence comparisons control for model size, not approximation quality (Figures 3, 7):** Figure 3 compares runtime for models with ~100k parameters, but this favors Marginal Flow because it does not need invertible layers or Jacobians. The more meaningful comparison would control for approximation quality (e.g., match test NLL across methods, then compare runtime). Similarly, Figure 7 uses wall-clock time on the x-axis, which conflates the advantage of cheaper iterations with better per-iteration optimization. While wall-clock speed matters in practice, the claim of "orders of magnitude quicker convergence" conflates two separate factors.

- **Simulation-based inference comparison uses published benchmark numbers rather than controlled re-implementations (Figure 14):** Results for Free-form Flows, Flow Matching, and Normalizing Flows are taken from Draxler et al. (2024) rather than re-implemented under identical conditions. This is common practice but weakens the evidence for superiority claims.

- **Image manifold experiments lack quantitative evaluation (Figures 10, 11):** The qualitative demonstrations on MNIST and JAFFE are visually interesting but no quantitative metrics (held-out log-likelihood, FID, reconstruction quality) are reported. The claim that the manifold "disentangles" digits and writing style or faces and emotions is subjective without quantitative backing.

- **Theoretical analysis relies on strong assumptions:** Theorem A.3's result that bias/variance depend only on intrinsic dimension assumes the latent posterior is centered at the prior mean and uses a linear-Gaussian toy model for validation (Figure 17). The paper does not show that the result holds for general nonlinear \(f_\theta\).

- **The "not a mixture model" argument (Figure 1, Figure 5 caption) is unconvincing:** The paper argues that because \(w_i\) are resampled at each evaluation, the model differs from a finite mixture. But this makes the model *stochastic* — evaluating the density at the same \(x\) gives different values each time. The practical consequences of this stochasticity for optimization and evaluation are not discussed.

### Trivial

- The citation of Liao & He (2021) to support the claim that "bijections struggle to learn new modalities" is a mismatch — that paper is about Jacobian determinants, not multimodality specifically. The general point about limited expressiveness of bijections is well-known, but the multimodality claim needs better support.

- Figure 3 caption says "Runtime for sampling (left) and exact density evaluation (right) of 100 points" — the paper should clarify whether these include GPU warmup and how variance across runs is handled.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Ablation on \(N_c\)** showing how test log-likelihood, sample quality, and training time vary with the number of components. This is the key missing experiment.
- **Controlled baseline comparison** where baselines are re-implemented under identical conditions (same data splits, same training protocol, same compute budget) rather than citing published numbers.
- **Quantitative metrics for the image manifold experiments** (e.g., held-out log-likelihood, coverage estimates).
- **A discussion of when Marginal Flow would fail** — e.g., high-dimensional targets where the Gaussian component \(q(x|w)\) would have negligible density in most of the ambient space unless \(N_c\) is impractically large.

Lemma A.2 analyzes bias of the *KL estimator* (the training objective's nested Monte Carlo approximation), not bias of the density \(q_\theta(x)\) itself. ## Novel Insights

The reviews surface a tension that the paper itself does not adequately address: the framework's claimed advantages stem from two somewhat contradictory properties. On one hand, "exact density evaluation" relies on the model being defined as a finite mixture (Eq. 2), which is exact but requires the user to accept that the model is simply a mixture with \(N_c\) components. On the other hand, the theoretical motivation and the name "Marginal Flow" appeal to the idea of marginalizing a continuous latent variable, which would require \(N_c\to\infty\) to be exact. The paper leans on the former for its engineering claims (speed, exactness) and the latter for its conceptual novelty, but never cleanly separates these two perspectives. The theoretical analysis in Appendix A.5 is actually a contribution toward understanding this gap (how large \(N_c\) needs to be to make the MC approximation faithful), but it is presented in service of validating the model rather than as the central intellectual contribution. A stronger paper would center this analysis and honestly frame the method as a learned mixture with a principled understanding of its Monte Carlo approximation error.

## Suggestions

1. **Honestly re-frame the contribution:** Position Marginal Flow as a learned mixture model with resampled components, not a fundamentally new framework. Drop the "flow" branding or provide a genuine connection to flows.
2. **Add an ablation study on \(N_c\)** showing how test log-likelihood, training time, and evaluation cost scale with the number of components across multiple datasets.
3. **Add a candid discussion of the \(N_c\) trade-off** in the main text, including the \(O(N_c\cdot d)\) evaluation cost and the fact that \(q_\theta(x)\) is stochastic for finite \(N_c\).
4. **Add quantitative metrics for the image manifold experiments** (e.g., held-out log-likelihood on latent-space test points).
5. **Re-run the runtime comparison** controlling for approximation quality (match held-out NLL across methods) rather than parameter count, or at least add this as a secondary comparison.
6. **Add error bars / multiple seeds** to the convergence plots (Figure 7) and report iteration counts alongside wall-clock time.
