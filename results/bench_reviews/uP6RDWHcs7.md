## Summary

Marginal Flow proposes a density estimation framework where the model density \(q_\theta(\mathbf{x})\) is defined as a finite average of simple parametric distributions \(q(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{w})\) with parameters \(\mathbf{w}\) resampled at each evaluation from an implicit learnable distribution \(q_\theta(\mathbf{w})\). Because the parameters are generated by an unconstrained neural network from base noise, the model bypasses the need for bijective mappings, Jacobian determinants, or ODE solvers, enabling efficient single-step sampling and density evaluation. The framework also naturally supports learning densities on lower-dimensional manifolds by using a base distribution with reduced dimensionality. Experiments span synthetic data, simulation-based inference, Wishart mixtures, and manifold learning in image latent spaces.

---

## Strengths

- **Novel and flexible framework**: The core idea—defining a density model as a finite average over resampled component parameters drawn from an implicit distribution—is genuinely original. Unlike standard mixture models where components are fixed, the resampling mechanism (Section 2.1, Eq. 2–3) creates a qualitatively different model class that circumvents the typical tradeoffs between exact likelihood, sampling speed, and architectural constraints. This flexibility is demonstrated by adapting the component distribution to Gaussians, Wisharts, and conditional variants across diverse experiments (Sections 4.1–4.4).

- **Convincing runtime advantage**: Figure 3 provides clear empirical evidence that Marginal Flow is orders of magnitude faster at density evaluation than competing models (NF, FM, FFF), while matching the sampling speed of Free-form Flows. This advantage is structural—density evaluation requires only a feedforward pass through \(f_\theta\) followed by closed-form component density evaluations, with no Jacobian or ODE computation—and it holds across data dimensions up to \(10^5\).

- **Native manifold support**: By choosing a base distribution in \(\mathbb{R}^m\) with \(m < d\), the model can learn densities supported on lower-dimensional manifolds without approximations (Section 2.1). Figure 4 shows Marginal Flow accurately recovering a 1D manifold embedded in 2D, while FM and NF cannot capture the manifold at all. This capability is further validated on Wishart mixtures (Figure 9) and image latent-space manifolds (Figures 10–11), where smooth, interpretable interpolations are learned.

- **Broad experimental validation**: The paper evaluates Marginal Flow on a wide range of tasks: log-likelihood training on synthetic data (Figures 6–7), reverse KL training against unnormalized targets (Figure 8), simulation-based inference on the SBI benchmark (Appendix Figure 14), learning on positive-definite matrices via Wishart components (Figure 9), and conditional manifold learning on MNIST and JAFFE face datasets (Figures 10–11). The model performs competitively or better than relevant baselines across these settings.

- **Training flexibility**: Because the model is efficient at both sampling and density evaluation, it can be trained with either forward KL (log-likelihood) or reverse KL divergence (Section 2.3, Appendix A.2). Figures 6 and 8 demonstrate successful training with both objectives, and Figure 7 shows faster convergence than competing models when trained via log-likelihood.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **The "exact density evaluation" claim oversells what the method provides**: The model density defined in Eq. 2 is a finite-sample Monte Carlo average \(q_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{N_c} \sum_i q(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{w}_i)\) with resampled \(\mathbf{w}_i\). Evaluating this expression is exact w.r.t. the model definition, but the model itself is a stochastic estimator: different draws of \(\{\mathbf{w}_i\}\) produce different density values at the same \(\mathbf{x}\), with variance scaling as \(O(1/N_c)\). This is qualitatively different from the "exact likelihood" provided by Normalizing Flows, where the density at a point is a deterministic, uniquely defined value. The paper repeatedly uses language like "exact density evaluation" (abstract, Table 1, Section 2.1, conclusions) without adequately distinguishing between exactness w.r.t. the defined formula and the stochastic/approximate nature of the model relative to the intended marginal integral of Eq. 1. This rhetorical gap weakens the claimed advantage over NFs and could mislead readers about what is actually being computed.

- **Training objectives rely on biased estimators without empirical validation of bias magnitude**: Both the forward KL objective (Eq. 5–6) and reverse KL objective (Eq. 9–11) use \(-\log(\frac{1}{N_c}\sum_i q(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{w}_i))\). Because \(\log \mathbb{E}[\cdot] \neq \mathbb{E}[\log \cdot]\), these are biased estimators of the corresponding KL divergences. The appendix (Sections A.5–A.6) provides a theoretical analysis showing the bias decays as \(O(1/N_c)\) and the variance inflation as \(O(1/(N N_c))\). However, the paper never connects this analysis to the experimental settings: for the values of \(N_c\) used in experiments (e.g., \(N_c = 50\)–\(2048\)), how large is the bias relative to the differences being measured? Without this, the reader cannot assess whether the reported log-likelihoods and KL divergences are meaningful, or whether the optimization is converging to a genuinely good solution versus being driven by estimator bias. This is a significant methodological gap.

### Minor

- **No systematic ablation of \(N_c\) on downstream performance**: The theoretical analysis shows that \(N_c\) controls the bias-variance tradeoff of the density estimator, yet there is no experiment studying how varying \(N_c\) affects training convergence, final density quality, or test log-likelihood. Such an ablation would substantially strengthen the empirical case and help practitioners choose \(N_c\) appropriately.

- **Comparisons with NFs on log-likelihood are not fully controlled**: In the forward KL experiments (Figures 6–7), the Marginal Flow log-likelihood uses a biased Monte Carlo estimator while the NF log-likelihood is exact. Even if the bias is small at the \(N_c\) values used, the paper should at minimum report the estimator variance to allow readers to judge whether the reported convergence advantage is genuine or an artifact of the estimator.

### Trivial

- The paper states in Section 2.2 that "only Marginal Flow and Normalizing Flow (NF) provide exact density by construction" — this ignores that Free-form Flows also provide an approximate but tractable likelihood, as the paper itself acknowledges elsewhere. The statement should be more precise.

## Removed Points

These points were flagged by reviewers but are not valid criticisms upon verification against the paper:

- **"The claim of exact density evaluation is false / the model does not provide exact likelihood"**: This overstates the issue. The model IS defined by Eq. 2 as a finite sum; evaluating that formula at a given \(\mathbf{x}\) and a given set of \(\{\mathbf{w}_i\}\) is exact arithmetic. The real concern is the stochastic/approximate nature of the model relative to its intended marginal, which is captured as a Major weakness above (the overselling point).

- **"All likelihood-based experiments are unreliable and comparisons are unfair"**: This is too sweeping. While the estimator is biased, the bias decreases with \(N_c\) and the paper uses reasonable \(N_c\) values (50–2048). The experiments demonstrate the method works in practice; the concern is about precision of the reported numbers and whether the bias is negligible, not that all results are categorically unreliable. This has been reframed as a Major weakness about missing empirical validation of bias magnitude.

The paper provides a theoretical analysis of the bias and its asymptotic behavior. The concern is about connecting theory to practice, not fundamental unsoundness.

While reasonable, this is scope creep—the paper's baseline comparisons are against the major density estimation paradigms (NF, FM, FFF, GAN, VAE) and are adequate for establishing the method's position.

## Suggestions

- **Rephrase "exact density evaluation"**: Replace with more precise language such as "closed-form density evaluation (up to Monte Carlo sampling of latent parameters)" or "tractable density evaluation without Jacobians or ODE solvers." Be explicit that the model is a finite-sample Monte Carlo estimator whose expectation converges to the true marginal as \(N_c \to \infty\), and that at finite \(N_c\) the evaluation is stochastic with controllable variance.

- **Add an \(N_c\) ablation experiment**: On one or two synthetic datasets, vary \(N_c\) across a range (e.g., 10, 50, 200, 1000) and report both the test log-likelihood (with standard errors from multiple resampling runs) and the visual quality of the learned density. This would address the major concern about bias and give practitioners guidance.

- **Report estimator variance at test time**: For the main experimental results, report the standard deviation of the log-likelihood estimator across multiple independent draws of \(\{\mathbf{w}_i\}\) at test time. This costs almost nothing computationally and would substantially improve trust in the numbers.

- **Clarify the training objective bias in the main text**: Briefly mention in Section 2.3 or 4.1 that the log-likelihood training objective uses a nested Monte Carlo estimator with known bias properties (referring to Appendix A.5–A.6), and that the bias is controlled by choosing \(N_c\) sufficiently large.
