## Summary

Marginal Flow proposes a density estimation framework where the model density is a finite mixture of parametric distributions \(q(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{w}_i)\), with the mixture parameters \(\mathbf{w}_i\) generated by passing samples from a base distribution through an unconstrained neural network \(f_\theta\). The parameters are resampled on every evaluation/sampling call, effectively creating an infinitely resampled mixture. This design enables both efficient density evaluation and efficient single-step sampling, supports learning on lower-dimensional manifolds, and imposes no bijectivity constraints on the architecture. The paper demonstrates speed advantages, manifold learning, multi-modal capability, and adaptability across synthetic data, SBI, Wishart distributions, and image latent spaces.

## Strengths

- **Unified framework addressing multiple pain points**: Table 1 provides a clear comparison showing Marginal Flow simultaneously achieves efficient exact likelihood evaluation, efficient single-step sampling, efficient training, free-form Jacobian, and lower-dimensional base distribution support — a combination no other method offers.

- **Simple and implementable design**: The model definition (Eq. 2 + Eq. 3) requires only a feed-forward network \(f_\theta\) and a chosen parametric family \(q(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{w})\). No bijectivity constraints, no ODE solvers, no adversarial training.

- **Convincing runtime advantage**: Figure 3 demonstrates orders-of-magnitude speedup over NF, FM, and FFF for both sampling and exact density evaluation, with comparable parameter counts and measured on identical hardware.

- **Manifold learning without approximation**: Figure 4 shows Marginal Flow recovering an unknown 1D manifold embedded in 2D, where NF and FM cannot handle the dimensionality mismatch and FFF learns an incorrect manifold. The paper provides theoretical backing in Appendix A.6 showing the MC estimator's bias/variance depend on intrinsic dimension \(m\), not ambient dimension \(d\).

- **Seamless adaptation to domain-specific distributions**: The Wishart mixture experiment (Section 4.3) demonstrates that swapping \(q(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{w})\) from Gaussian to Wishart allows learning on the cone of positive-definite matrices, including a 100×100 (\(d=5050\)) setting where NFs are computationally prohibitive.

- **Multi-modal capability and objective flexibility**: Figure 5 shows easy handling of multi-modal targets. Section 4.1 demonstrates training under both forward KL (log-likelihood) and reverse KL without architectural changes.

- **Rapid training convergence**: Figure 7 shows Marginal Flow reaches high test log-likelihood significantly faster than NF, FM, and FFF across synthetic datasets.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **"Exact density evaluation" framing needs qualification**. The model in Eq. 2 is a finite-sample Monte Carlo estimator of the marginal \(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}}[q(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{w})]\). Because \(\mathbf{w}_i\) are resampled on each evaluation, evaluating \(q_\theta(\mathbf{x})\) twice on the same \(\mathbf{x}\) yields two different numbers — the model density is stochastic by design. This differs fundamentally from the deterministic exact likelihood of Normalizing Flows. The paper does acknowledge the resampling mechanism in Section 2.1 ("the parameters \(\mathbf{w}_{\theta,i}\) are not fixed themselves but rather resampled from \(q_\theta(\mathbf{w})\) at each iteration"), and the appendix (A.5–A.6) provides a thorough bias/variance analysis. However, the main text repeatedly uses "exact density evaluation" without clarifying this stochasticity, and Table 1 directly equates Marginal Flow's "exact likelihood" with NF's. The method is still valid — the density computation is exact for the defined finite mixture — but the comparison with NF's deterministic likelihood is misleading without qualification. This is addressable through clearer framing in the main text.

### Minor

- **No sensitivity analysis for \(N_c\)**: Different \(N_c\) values are used across experiments (half the training points for synthetic data, 2048 for SBI, 256 for MNIST, 128 for JAFFE) without any rationale or ablation. The appendix provides theoretical scaling results, but a reader cannot judge how much empirical performance depends on this hyperparameter given the bias terms in the MC estimator (Lemma A.2, Eqs. 20–21).

- **Qualitative-only evaluation for image latent manifold experiments**: Section 4.4 shows visually appealing interpolations on MNIST and JAFFE, but provides no quantitative metric (e.g., reconstruction quality, FID, manifold smoothness). The claim of "disentanglement of faces and emotions" is not supported by any numerical measure.

- **Baseline tuning in convergence experiments could be more thorough**: While the runtime comparison (Figure 3) matches parameter counts and uses the fastest NF architecture (explicitly chosen for speed), the convergence experiments (Figure 7) use 5-layer invertible ResNets for NF. The paper states no hyperparameter tuning was performed on Marginal Flow, but it's unclear how much NF's performance gap could be narrowed with architecture or learning-rate tuning.

- **Novelty relative to mixture models could be better positioned**: The core mechanism — neural network generates mixture parameters, density is evaluated as a finite mixture — has conceptual overlap with amortized mixture density networks and latent-variable mixture models. The resampling aspect is the key differentiator, but this is not contrasted explicitly against related mixture-based density estimators in Section 3.

### Trivial

- The abstract's claim of "overcomes these limitations altogether" is somewhat overstated given the stochastic nature of the density and the \(N_c\)-dependent bias.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A study of empirical variance of \(\log q_\theta(\mathbf{x})\) across repeated evaluations (different \(\mathbf{w}_i\) draws) would help readers understand the practical reliability of the stochastic likelihood and how it behaves with \(N_c\) and dimensionality.

- Comparison with injective flows (Negri et al., 2025) or Brehmer & Cranmer (2020) on the manifold learning tasks, since these methods also handle lower-dimensional manifolds without bijectivity.

- Exploration of variance reduction techniques (e.g., importance weighting) to reduce the required \(N_c\), which would strengthen the method's scalability in high dimensions.

## Novel Insights

The paper makes an interesting connection that has not been widely appreciated: by resampling mixture parameters on every call rather than storing them, one effectively creates a model with the practical benefits of a mixture (flexibility, speed) without the fixed-capacity bottleneck of a traditional finite mixture model. The appendix's theoretical result that the MC bias and variance inflation depend only on intrinsic manifold dimension \(m\) rather than ambient dimension \(d\) (Theorem A.3) is a genuinely useful insight for understanding why the method works well on high-dimensional manifold data, though it deserves more prominence in the main text.

- Include an \(N_c\) ablation on at least one synthetic dataset to give readers practical guidance on this hyperparameter.
