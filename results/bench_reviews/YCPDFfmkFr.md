## Summary
The paper proposes QPLayer, a differentiable convex QP layer that extends the layer output to infeasible QPs by solving a hierarchical “closest feasible QP” problem with an optimal shift \(s^\star\). It defines an “extended conservative Jacobian” (ECJ) through a least-squares linearization of a KKT-like residual map \(G\), derives forward/backward computational formulas, and demonstrates the approach on Sudoku LP/QP layers, including a setting where learned constraints may be infeasible during training.

## Strengths
- The paper targets a concrete limitation of existing QP-layer architectures: enforcing primal feasibility by construction can restrict what is learned. The introduction gives the specific OptNet-style example \(u=Cz+\exp(h)\), which prevents independently fixing \(u\) while learning \(C\) (line 27), and the Sudoku “learn \(A\)” example makes this issue tangible.
- The closest-feasible formulation is operationally clear. Section 3.1 defines a hierarchical problem in which \(s^\star\) minimizes \(\frac12\|s\|_2^2\), and Lemma 1 connects solutions of this problem to roots of the residual map \(G(x,z,t;\theta)\) with \(s^\star=[t^\star]_+\) (lines 67–94).
- The feasible regular case is reasonably grounded: Lemma 3 states that when the QP is feasible, the active-constraint KKT matrix is nonsingular, and strict complementarity holds, the ECJ matches the standard Jacobian (lines 190–192). This helps situate the method relative to standard KKT/implicit-differentiation QP layers.
- The paper derives specialized backward formulas rather than relying solely on unrolling solver iterations. Section 3.4.1 gives a backward expression for \(\partial \mathcal L/\partial\theta\) using auxiliary variables \(b_1^\star,\dots,b_4^\star\) (lines 145–169), and Section 3.4.2 gives a simplified feasible-case active-set system (lines 194–207).
- The Sudoku experiments are suggestive that relaxing feasibility constraints can be useful. In Section 4.1.1, QPLayer learns LPs and reports 33 prediction errors over the last 5 epochs versus 162 for OptNet and 1531 for CvxpyLayer (line 248). In Section 4.1.2, penalizing \(s^\star\) is used to drive the learned \(Ax=1\) constraint toward feasibility (lines 255–258).
- The paper is unusually candid about its theoretical limitations. Section 3.4.3 explicitly states that the authors have not proved the chain rule for ECJs in the general case, have not confirmed that ECJs reduce to conservative Jacobians, and have not resolved the degenerate IFT issue (lines 214–217). This honesty is valuable, even though the gaps are central.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None. The paper is a real and coherent contribution, with a meaningful formulation and a plausible practical method. However, the central theoretical and empirical support is incomplete enough to weigh strongly against acceptance.

### Major
- **The central ECJ object is not established as a conservative Jacobian or as a valid derivative of a QP solution selection in the infeasible case.** Lemma 2 only proves that the residual map \(G\) is path differentiable with respect to its arguments and \(\theta\) under differentiability of \(H,g,C,u\) (lines 101–103). Definition 1 then defines ECJs as least-squares minimizers of a linearized residual equation (lines 105–109). This does not by itself prove that a selected solution map \((x^\star,z^\star,t^\star)(\theta)\) is single-valued, locally Lipschitz/path differentiable, or that the least-squares object is a conservative Jacobian of that solution map. The paper itself confirms this gap in Section 3.4.3: “we have not proved that we could apply the chain rule to ECJs in the general case” and “it should be confirmed that ECJ indeed reduces to CJ” (lines 214–217). This directly weakens the main theoretical claim of differentiating infeasible QP solutions.
- **The general backward-pass justification relies on assumptions that do not cover the broad infeasible-QP setting claimed.** The backward pass invokes Assumption 2, namely path differentiability of \(x^\star,z^\star,t^\star\) with respect to \(\theta\) (lines 145–152), which is precisely what the theory needs to establish. The paper then gives Assumption 3 as a sufficient condition for the linear system to have solutions: \(C(\theta)\) full column rank, \(s^\star>0\) for all constraints, and \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial z^\star=0\) (lines 167–169). This is a narrow regime: it excludes many mixed feasible/infeasible, partially active, and boundary cases likely to arise during training. Since the experiments use the general backward mechanism when penalizing \(s^\star\) (line 257), the mismatch between theory and use case is substantive.
- **Solution selection and nonuniqueness are not adequately handled, especially for LP layers.** The paper acknowledges early that QP solutions need not be unique (line 27), and the experiments emphasize learning LPs, i.e. \(H=0\)-type settings where nonunique optima are common. Yet Definition 1 starts from any \(v^\star=(x^\star,z^\star,t^\star)\) satisfying \(G(v^\star;\theta)=0\) and defines ECJs as solutions of a least-squares problem, which itself may have multiple minimizers (lines 105–109). The paper does not specify a deterministic solution-selection rule or minimum-norm/tie-breaking convention. For an optimization layer used inside training, different solver selections or least-squares minimizers can yield different gradients and learning dynamics.
- **The empirical evidence does not isolate the proposed infeasible-QP differentiation mechanism.** The Sudoku results are interesting, but the main comparisons conflate several differences: QPLayer is allowed to learn LPs, whereas OptNet is described as restricted to strictly convex QPs (Figure 4 caption, line 234); in the “learn \(A\)” experiment, OptNet is given a small quadratic term because it can only learn strictly convex QPs (line 267). Thus, the observed gains may reflect representational differences, solver differences, or formulation differences rather than the proposed ECJ/backward method itself. The CvxpyLayer comparison in Section 4.1.1 is also not described in enough controlled detail to establish that it was given the same LP/infeasibility-relaxation formulation and training setup.
- **Claims about speed, robustness, and accuracy are stronger than the controlled evidence in the main text.** The main reported timing comparison is QPLayer at \(0.45\pm0.07\) seconds per batch versus OptNet at \(8.99\pm0.91\) seconds per batch (line 269). This is suggestive, but it compares different solvers, implementations, and formulations; QPLayer uses a C++ ProxQP backend (line 224), while the baseline formulation differs. Without matched tolerances/accuracy targets and more controlled breakdowns, the result does not cleanly support a claim that the proposed differentiation method itself is faster.

### Minor
- **The method’s behavior at projection kink points is underanalyzed.** The paper fixes particular subgradient choices, e.g. \((\Pi_2)_i=1\) when \(t_i^\star=0\) (lines 137–143) and \((\Pi_1)_i=1\) at a zero condition in the feasible case (lines 184–188). These choices can materially affect gradients near feasibility boundaries, exactly where the method is intended to be useful. This is not necessarily wrong, but it should be justified or empirically stress-tested.
- **The main experimental narrative is narrow relative to the abstract’s breadth.** The abstract claims “superior predictive performance in some conventional learning tasks” and improved robustness/speed/accuracy (lines 5–7), while the main text’s substantive learning evidence centers on Sudoku. The paper does point to additional experiments elsewhere (lines 40 and 276), so this should not be treated as absence of all supporting material, but the main narrative would be more convincing if the central claims were supported by controlled ablations in the main body.
- **The Sudoku performance reporting is suggestive but not robustly quantified.** The paper reports final/last-epoch error counts and curves (line 248), but the main text does not show multiple seeds or uncertainty for prediction performance. This does not invalidate the experiment, but it makes the performance claim less robust.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Add finite-difference gradient checks on small QPs across feasible, infeasible, boundary, degenerate, and LP/nonunique cases. Given the theoretical gap, empirical gradient validation would be especially valuable.
- Include ablations in the “learn \(A\)” experiment separating: learning an LP versus a QP, adding the \(s^\star\) penalty, backpropagating through \(s^\star\), and relaxing feasibility during training.
- Compare against a convex-layer baseline given the same relaxed LP/QP formulation, same \(s^\star\)-style penalty, and matched solver accuracy/tolerance targets.
- Report active-set and \(s^\star\) statistics during training to show how often the layer operates in infeasible or kink regimes.
- Specify the deterministic convention used by the implementation when the primal solution or ECJ least-squares solution is nonunique.

## Removed Points
These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution.

- **Pure “important problem” strength.** The fact that infeasible optimization layers are an important problem is true but generic; it is not retained as a standalone strength without concrete evidence.
- **Strong claim that the differentiation rule is fully unified across feasible and infeasible regimes.** The paper defines a common least-squares ECJ object, but because Section 3.4.3 admits unresolved chain-rule and conservative-Jacobian questions, this was weakened to a computational/formulational strength rather than kept as a fully established theoretical strength.
- **Criticisms based on missing appendix material.** The extracted paper explicitly refers to additional simple QP examples, timing experiments, and robustness experiments (lines 40 and 276). ## Novel Insights
The most interesting aspect of the paper is not merely that it differentiates a QP layer, but that it reframes infeasibility as a learnable signal through the optimal shift \(s^\star\). This creates a bridge between optimization-layer feasibility and training objectives: rather than requiring a neural architecture to guarantee feasibility at every training step, the model can temporarily pass through infeasible QPs and be penalized toward feasible structure. That idea is promising, but the current paper has not yet fully separated three notions that are treated as nearly equivalent: differentiating a true solution map, differentiating a least-squares residual sensitivity, and using a practical surrogate gradient that empirically improves training.

## Suggestions
- Reframe the ECJ more carefully unless the missing theory is completed. A defensible version would present the general infeasible-case ECJ as a least-squares sensitivity/surrogate-gradient method, with proven agreement with standard Jacobians only in the feasible regular case.
- Provide a theorem or precise conditions under which a solution selection \((x^\star,z^\star,t^\star)(\theta)\) exists, is locally regular/path differentiable, and admits the proposed ECJ or a conservative-Jacobian interpretation.
- Define the implementation’s tie-breaking convention for nonunique primal solutions and nonunique least-squares ECJs, especially for LP layers.
- Add controlled experiments where QPLayer, OptNet-like formulations, and a generic convex-layer baseline receive as similar a problem formulation as possible.
- Report gradient checks and active-set/kink sensitivity studies; these would directly address the central uncertainty around whether the proposed gradients are meaningful in infeasible and degenerate regimes.
- Separate timing into forward solve, backward linear solve, and total training time under matched accuracy targets.
