## Summary

DebateGPT fine-tunes GPT-3.5 on 5,000 Alpaca-sourced instruction-response pairs, where responses are generated by an improved multi-agent debate procedure using four GPT-3.5 agents over three rounds. The improvements over Du et al.'s original debate framework consist of three components: a Summarization step (condenses prior-round responses to manage context length), Confidence Scoring (each agent self-assigns a 1–100 certainty score used to weight its influence), and Cleaning (a separate GPT-3.5 model removes debate artifacts using GPT-4 formatting examples). The paper evaluates the resulting model on five benchmarks and claims it achieves performance comparable to GPT-4 at a fraction of the cost.

---

## Strengths

- **Ablation on debate components is specific and informative**: Table 1 quantifies the individual contribution of each proposed enhancement over the original Du et al. baseline (Summarization: +3.6%, Confidence: +0.8%, all combined: +13.4%), providing a concrete breakdown of what drives improvement.

- **Cost analysis is specific and actionable**: Section 5.3 and Figure 6 give per-token pricing with actual totals — total fine-tuning cost of $20–$100, data generation cheaper than GPT-4 annotation — rather than vague qualitative claims about cost efficiency.

- **Breadth of evaluation**: Five distinct benchmark types (instruction following via AlpacaEval, arithmetic, commonsense reasoning, factual MMLU, ARC) provides broader coverage than typical single-task fine-tuning papers, and the model shows genuine zero-shot generalization outside the Alpaca distribution.

- **Diagnosis of Du et al.'s failure modes is concrete**: The paper identifies specific, practical problems (context window saturation, lack of quality weighting, extraneous debate artifacts) as motivations for the three proposed enhancements, grounding the design choices in observed failure cases.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
*(None that fully invalidate all results, but see Major below)*

### Major

- **The central comparative claim is untested — the key missing baseline is GPT-3.5 fine-tuned on direct GPT-4 responses to the same 5K questions.** Section 5.3 acknowledges this possibility ("we could have theoretically achieved these results by fine-tuning the model on Alpaca data with responses generated by GPT-4") but only provides a cost comparison, never a quality comparison. The paper's entire argument structure is that multi-agent debate *with cheaper GPT-3.5 agents* produces data *comparable in quality to GPT-4* at lower cost. Without running this baseline, neither side of the tradeoff (quality parity, cost advantage) can be established. If GPT-4–directly-annotated fine-tuning yields materially better results, the paper's cost savings come at a quality penalty it does not acknowledge.

- **The cleaning step injects GPT-4 style examples into every training sample, creating a circular confound with AlpacaEval.** Section 3.2 states: *"We also provide this model with four GPT-4 response examples in the prompt to help it generate text with a concise and coherent format."* AlpacaEval's evaluation methodology (Section 4.1) uses GPT-4 to judge outputs. A model whose training data was formatted to mimic GPT-4 style will be preferentially rated by a GPT-4 judge. Table 1 shows the cleaning step contributes +6.6 percentage points to the AlpacaEval win rate — the largest single contribution — yet this is precisely the component that stylistically aligns outputs to GPT-4. The paper cannot disentangle whether this gain reflects improved reasoning quality or stylistic alignment to the judge. Notably, the other benchmarks (Arithmetic, Winogrande, MMLU-from-OpenAI-report) are not GPT-4-judged and still show improvements, partially mitigating this concern, but AlpacaEval results should be treated with caution.

- **Heterogeneous baseline protocol mixes incompatible model versions.** Section 4.2 reports MMLU, ARC, and Winogrande for GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 "as reported in OpenAI (2023)," which evaluated earlier model checkpoints (the technical report GPT-3.5 is described as built on `text-davinci-002`), while the AlpacaEval and Arithmetic baselines use `gpt-3.5-turbo-0613`. DebateGPT is a fine-tuned `gpt-3.5-turbo-0613`. Claiming DebateGPT "improves by 5.11% over GPT-3.5" conflates two distinct model versions. Improvements on MMLU/ARC/Winogrande relative to the OpenAI-report GPT-3.5 may partially reflect differences between `text-davinci-002`-era and `gpt-3.5-turbo-0613` rather than the effect of fine-tuning.

### Minor

- **Confidence scoring mechanism is entirely unspecified.** The paper states that agents with lower confidence "have a smaller influence on the final answer" but provides no equation, pseudocode, or procedure for how confidence scores are aggregated. The ablation (+0.8%) is therefore uninterpretable and the contribution is not replicable as described. This is a significant gap for a claimed methodological contribution.

- **Super-additivity in ablation is unexplained.** Summarization alone yields +3.6%; Confidence alone yields +0.8%; together they yield +6.8% (+2.4% more than their sum). No explanation is offered for this synergy, which is statistically non-trivial.

- **Numerical inconsistency between Introduction and Table 1.** The contribution bullet on p.1 states "13.2% enhancement" while Table 1 caption and Section 5.1 both report 13.4%.

- **Figure 5 (data scaling) uses MMLU, while ablation uses AlpacaEval win rate.** Using different metrics across analyses makes it difficult to assess the relative importance of data quality vs. data quantity.

### Trivial

- The aggregate "5.11% improvement over GPT-3.5 across different datasets" mixes accuracy percentages on classification tasks with AlpacaEval win rates, inflating apparent effect magnitude.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation of the cleaning step *without* GPT-4 few-shot examples (e.g., using only a plain prompt or GPT-3.5-sourced examples) would isolate how much of the AlpacaEval gain is due to stylistic mimicry vs. artifact removal.
- A non-GPT-4 judge (reward model or human) on a subset of AlpacaEval examples would help validate robustness of the main instruction-following result.
- Formal specification (even pseudocode) for confidence-score aggregation is necessary for reproducibility.
- Error analysis by MMLU subdomain (the paper mentions law and historical events qualitatively in 4.3 but does not quantify this) would clarify where fine-tuning helps vs. hurts.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel observation in this paper — reusing multi-agent debate not as an inference-time procedure but as a *data generator* for supervised fine-tuning — is a conceptually clean pivot that prior work had not demonstrated. The framing that debate consensus produces higher-quality training signal than single-model generation is interesting, though the cleaning step's GPT-4 dependency somewhat muddies the mechanistic claim. A cleaner ablation (cleaning without GPT-4 examples) would reveal whether it is the debate process or the stylistic transfer that drives AlpacaEval gains, and would either solidify or reframe the contribution.

## Suggestions

1. **Run the missing baseline**: Fine-tune `gpt-3.5-turbo-0613` on direct GPT-4 responses to the same 5K Alpaca questions. Report quality alongside cost. This single experiment either validates or reframes the paper's central claim.

2. **Formally specify confidence aggregation**: Provide a formula or pseudocode for how confidence scores modulate final answer selection. This is required for reproducibility of the +0.8% contribution.

3. **Ablate cleaning without GPT-4 examples**: Use the same cleaning prompt but replace GPT-4 few-shot examples with GPT-3.5 examples or no examples. This directly measures how much AlpacaEval gains are attributable to stylistic transfer vs. artifact removal.

4. **Align baselines to the same model version**: Re-run GPT-3.5 (`gpt-3.5-turbo-0613`) on MMLU/ARC/Winogrande using your own evaluation code, so all comparisons use the same model version and protocol.

5. **Resolve and explain super-additivity**: Report combined-without-cleaning, combined-without-confidence, etc. to show whether the 6.8% combined gain is due to multiplicative synergy or an artifact.
