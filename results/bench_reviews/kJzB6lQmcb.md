## Summary

The paper argues that current 3D generative models produce outputs (typically meshes, point clouds, or NeRF-derived representations) that are fundamentally inadequate for real-world manufacturing, which requires parametric boundary representations (BREP/STEP) with tight tolerances, 2-manifold watertight geometry, and proper geometric decomposition. It demonstrates empirically that: (1) even under ideal conditions, NeRF→mesh conversion yields ~50% non-manifold outputs; (2) state-of-the-art CAD reconstruction models catastrophically fail on realistic datasets (81%+ non-manifold rates); and (3) providing ground-truth BREP segmentation to reconstruction pipelines substantially reduces (but does not eliminate) errors. The paper proposes that **classless geometric decomposition**—segmenting shapes at the granularity of BREP faces without relying on restrictive primitive-class labels—is the critical missing capability, and provides an augmented dataset and ParseNet baseline to support this direction.

## Strengths

- **Concrete, important, and underappreciated problem articulation.** The paper identifies the gap between 3D generation outputs and manufacturing requirements with specificity rarely seen in ML venues. The detailed enumeration of actual manufacturing constraints (±0.005 inch tolerances, 2-manifold watertightness, the mechanical role of fillets/chamfers) translates engineering requirements into terms the ML community can engage with. The observation that benchmark datasets systematically exclude manufacturing-relevant features (Section 1.4: DeepCAD restricted to sketch-extrude, MFCAD++ synthetic and simple, CAD-MLLM suppresses fillets/chamfers) identifies a genuine community blind spot.

- **Stark empirical demonstration of SOTA failure.** The results are compelling and sobering: Point2CAD achieves only 18.9% manifold outputs on F360 and 6.9% on Thang3D (Table 2); even "perfect-condition" NeRF-to-mesh conversion yields ~50% non-manifold outputs (Table 1). These numbers directly substantiate the paper's core contention that current approaches are far from manufacturing-grade.

- **The ground-truth segmentation experiment isolates a meaningful bottleneck.** Providing ground-truth BREP segmentation to existing reconstruction pipelines reduces non-manifold rates from 81% to 47% on F360 (Section 4), demonstrating that the featurization step accounts for a substantial portion of failure. This is quantitative evidence that classless decomposition is a necessary direction, even if not sufficient on its own.

- **Constructive contribution.** The paper doesn't just diagnose problems—it provides a relabeled F360 dataset with BREP-level annotations, a Thang3D evaluation set, and a ParseNet decomposition baseline (Table 4), giving the community concrete starting points for future work.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **The paper's strongest prescriptive claim (classless geometric decomposition as the key pathway forward) is weakened by its own evidence.** Providing ground-truth BREP segmentation—which is the ideal version of the paper's proposed direction—only reduces non-manifold rates from 81% to 47%. The paper acknowledges this ("these numbers are still below a practical threshold") and calls decomposition "the first step" in Section 7, but the framing elsewhere (abstract: "we have identified a common pattern... a restrictive featurization step"; Section 3: "the initial featurization step which is incapable of breaking down shapes into sufficiently elementary parts"; Section 4: "underscores the importance of generating much finer-grained decomposition") consistently positions decomposition as the central bottleneck. The paper would be significantly stronger if it explicitly framed decomposition as *necessary but not sufficient* and discussed what else must change in the pipeline—e.g., the fact that 47% non-manifold with perfect segmentation implies that downstream primitive-fitting and topological reconstruction also need fundamental improvement. This is partially acknowledged in Section 7 ("Once fine-grained decomposition is achieved, next steps would involve training any of the aforementioned downstream pipeline models") but treated as future work rather than integrated into the position. A position paper that claims to identify "the key missing ingredient" should be upfront that even the ideal version of that ingredient leaves half the problem unsolved.

- **Three distinct claims are interleaved without clear prioritization.** The paper simultaneously argues: (a) discrete representations (meshes, NeRFs, point clouds) are fundamentally inadequate for manufacturing; (b) classless geometric decomposition is the key bottleneck for pipeline reconstruction models; (c) manufacturing constraints must be incorporated into 3D generation. These have different implications and require different evidence. If claim (a) is the primary position, then improving decomposition (b) cannot resolve the fundamental representation problem—you need native parametric generation. If claim (b) is primary, then claim (a) is a supporting motivation rather than the core argument. The paper shifts between these without establishing which is central, making it harder for readers to engage productively. For example, Sections 2.1 (NeRF inadequacy) and 4 (decomposition bottleneck) support different claims; connecting them requires stating whether the position is "discrete representations are dead ends for manufacturing" or "within the pipeline paradigm, decomposition is the critical fix."

### Minor

- **The NeRF "perfect conditions" framing is slightly misleading in scope.** The experiment trains Instant-NGP on ground-truth CAD meshes and converts back to mesh form. This demonstrates that the mesh↔NeRF↔mesh round-trip introduces manufacturing-incompatible errors—a valid representation-level point. However, calling this "perfect conditions" (Section 2.1) implies it tests all NeRF-based generation under best-case assumptions, when it actually only tests representation format fidelity, not generative capability. The title ("You Can't Manufacture a NeRF") compounds this by suggesting a broader claim than the experiment supports. A clearer statement that this experiment specifically targets representation-level limitations of the discrete extraction step (marching cubes), rather than generative NeRF methods as a whole, would strengthen the argument's precision.

- **The alternative views section (Section 5) is underdeveloped relative to the claims it addresses.** The response to the possibility that neural/implicit representations might bypass parametric formats is addressed in just two sentences ("relying on post-hoc conversion from implicit to explicit geometry can introduce new sources of error and there is currently no automated pipeline for doing such conversions with arbitrary complexity"). This is a significant counterargument given that the field is actively developing differentiable rendering and neural surface reconstruction methods. The "more classes" counterargument receives somewhat fuller treatment but still lacks engagement with the possibility that expanding primitive vocabularies combined with learned parametric representations could achieve the decomposition granularity the paper calls for.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Error decomposition analysis.** The paper shows end-to-end failure rates but does not break down what fraction of errors come from segmentation vs. primitive-fitting vs. topological reconstruction. This would clarify which bottleneck to prioritize and would strengthen the paper's argument about decomposition being the main culprit.

- **Engagement with direct parametric generation approaches.** Beyond the pipeline paradigm (discrete → segmentation → reconstruction), there are emerging approaches that directly generate CAD construction sequences. The paper mentions some (CAD-SIGNet, CAD-MLLM) but mainly to note their limitations. Deeper analysis of whether *any* parametric generation approach can handle complex geometries would strengthen the discussion.

- **Clarification of "directly manufacturable" vs. "useful starting point for a human expert."** The paper assumes the bar is directly manufacturable output, but in practice, engineers often iterate. A brief discussion of whether approximate decomposition-quality outputs could still accelerate human CAD workflows would contextualize the practical stakes.

## Novel Insights

The paper makes an underappreciated observation that the ML community's evaluation practices for 3D generation systematically obscure physical manufacturing feasibility. Chamfer distances measured at unit-sphere scale look acceptable but translate to errors orders of magnitude beyond real manufacturing tolerances. This reframing—from a computer graphics evaluation paradigm (visual fidelity) to a manufacturing evaluation paradigm (parametric precision, manifold topology)—exposes a fundamental misalignment in how 3D generation is currently assessed and benchmarked. The dataset bias argument (that existing benchmarks actively exclude the features—fillets, chamfers, complex extrusions—that make CAD challenging) is also a genuine and important contribution to community awareness.

## Suggestions

- Reframe the position as "geometric decomposition is necessary but not sufficient" rather than "the key missing ingredient," explicitly acknowledging that downstream reconstruction also needs fundamental improvement. This honesty would strengthen, not weaken, the argument.

- Clearly distinguish the three claims in the introduction (discrete representations inadequate, decomposition is the bottleneck, manufacturing constraints needed) and state which is the primary position and how the others support it.

- In Section 5, expand the engagement with the implicit/parametric counterargument to at least a paragraph, acknowledging what would need to be true for neural representations to bridge the gap and why current evidence suggests this hasn't happened yet.
