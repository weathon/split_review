## Summary

IncentRL augments standard RL with a KL divergence term KL(p(o|s,a)∥q(o|s)) that penalizes misalignment between predicted and preferred outcomes, and claims to adapt the incentive weight β as a Bayesian random variable to eliminate manual tuning. Empirical results on three sparse-reward environments (toy MDP, MountainCar, MiniGrid) show that adding the KL shaping term with appropriately chosen fixed β improves sample efficiency and final performance over standard RL (β=0).

## Strengths

- **KL-based shaping consistently improves performance across multiple sparse-reward benchmarks.** The fixed-β experiments show genuine gains: 69–74% higher reward in a toy MDP (Table 1), 18% more goal reaches in MountainCar at β=0.1 (Table 2), and 98% vs. 90.5% success rate plus 61% shorter episodes in MiniGrid at β=0.01 (Figure 2). These results are replicated across three qualitatively different environments.

- **The problem is well-motivated.** The paper identifies a genuine open challenge—balancing intrinsic and extrinsic rewards without manual tuning—and connects it to the cognitive science literature (dopamine RPE, Free Energy Principle) in a way that is clearly written and easy to follow.

- **Reproducibility practices.** The paper reports results over multiple seeds (3–7), provides standard deviations, and makes code publicly available.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

1. **The Bayesian adaptation mechanism—the paper's central claimed novelty—is never described.** The abstract and introduction (Section 1) prominently advertise "treating the incentive weight β as a Bayesian random variable, updated online to balance exploration and exploitation without manual tuning" as the primary contribution. However, Section 3 (Methods) contains no description whatsoever of this mechanism—no prior distribution, no likelihood model, no inference procedure, no update rule. The algorithm outline (Section 5.1, steps 1–5) does not mention β at all; β is treated as a fixed parameter in the shaped reward equation. The Bayesian adaptation appears only in results (Figures 3, 4, Table 3) without any accompanying algorithmic formalization. A reader cannot determine what the method actually is, making the core contribution unverifiable and unreproducible. This is a structural failure: even extensive additional experiments cannot compensate for a missing method specification.

### Major

2. **No comparison against standard intrinsic motivation baselines.** The paper compares IncentRL only against β=0 (standard RL). It does not compare against curiosity-driven exploration (Pathak et al. 2017, ICM), RND (Burda et al. 2018), or any other exploration method. The claim of "improves sample efficiency and final performance over standard RL and fixed-regularization baselines" is hollow when no exploration baselines are included. The fixed-β KL shaping is itself a form of intrinsic motivation; comparing against existing intrinsic motivation methods is essential.

3. **Bayesian adaptation results lack task-performance evaluation.** The Bayesian β results (Figures 3, 4, Table 3) report only posterior statistics of β itself. No task-performance metrics (success rate, episode length, cumulative reward) are shown for the Bayesian adaptation agent. The paper states "In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of β achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value" — this is hearsay, not evidence. Without task-performance curves comparing the Bayesian adaptation agent against fixed-β baselines, the headline claim that "Bayesian adaptation removes the need for manual trade-off tuning" is unsubstantiated.

4. **Inconsistency between Figure 3 caption and Table 3 data.** Figure 3's caption states that the posterior mean "quickly concentrates near the effective region (β≈0.1)." However, Table 3 reports mean β values of 0.1057 → 0.0454 → 0.0194 → 0.0173 across rounds—monotonically decreasing to ≈0.02 by Round 4, not ≈0.1. For the MiniGrid experiment where the best fixed β was 0.01, converging to 0.017 makes sense and supports the adaptation claim, but the caption's stated value (0.1) is incorrect and creates an apparent contradiction.

5. **Theoretical analysis is trivial.** Proposition 1 (small β preserves optimal policy) is a basic continuity argument that assumes bounded KL divergence, which is not guaranteed when q has narrow support. Proposition 2 (β→∞ ignores external rewards) is definitional. Neither proposition provides actionable insight into why or when IncentRL improves sample efficiency, nor do they connect to the claimed Bayesian adaptation.

### Minor

6. **Limited statistical rigor.** Only 3 seeds are used for MountainCar and MiniGrid experiments, with no confidence intervals or learning-curve variance shown. The toy MDP uses 7 seeds but reports only the final reward at episode 100 without showing that performance is stable.

7. **The KL shaping term reduces to a simple goal-conditioned reward shaping scheme.** When q(o|s) is a delta at the goal state (as in both MountainCar and MiniGrid experiments), the shaping term becomes essentially a log-probability-of-goal bonus: −β·log p(goal|s,a) (modulo handling of infinite KL when p has support outside the delta). This is a known form of potential-based reward shaping or goal-conditioned bonus. The paper's framing as a general "preference–prediction alignment" framework is substantially more ambitious than what is actually implemented.

8. **High sensitivity to β in MountainCar** (β=0.3 causes −52.76% degradation, β=1.0 causes −75.01%) contradicts the framing that Bayesian adaptation simply removes a minor tuning difficulty. If β is this brittle, a convincing demonstration of robust adaptation is especially critical—but the adaptation experiment was only run on MiniGrid, not MountainCar.

9. **Limited β sweep in MiniGrid.** Only β∈{0.0, 0.01} are tested, unlike MountainCar which sweeps β∈{0.0, 0.1, 0.3, 1.0}. The choice of 0.01 as the "best fixed value" lacks justification from a sweep.

### Trivial

10. Figure 3 caption mentions "β≈0.1" where it should likely reference β≈0.01 (matching the MiniGrid effective value) for consistency with Table 3.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Adding comparisons to curiosity-driven exploration methods (ICM, RND) would substantially strengthen the paper's claims about improving over "fixed-regularization baselines."
- Showing task-performance curves for the Bayesian adaptation agent alongside fixed-β curves across all three environments would directly validate the core claim.
- Reporting confidence intervals and per-seed learning curves would improve statistical rigor.
- Ablating the KL formulation against alternative shaping signals (e.g., negative Euclidean distance to goal, simple goal-reaching bonus) would isolate the contribution of the KL framing.
- A sensitivity analysis of how the Bayesian β update's hyperparameters affect posterior concentration would aid reproducibility.
