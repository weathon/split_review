## Summary

This paper proposes IncentRL, a reinforcement learning framework that augments standard RL rewards with a KL-divergence penalty aligning predicted outcome distributions $p(o|s,a)$ with preferred outcome distributions $q(o|s)$. The paper's central claimed novelty is treating the incentive weight $\beta$ as a Bayesian random variable adapted online to eliminate manual trade-off tuning. The paper presents experiments on three sparse-reward domains (toy MDP, MountainCar, MiniGrid) comparing fixed-$\beta$ IncentRL against a standard RL baseline ($\beta=0$), along with posterior statistics from a Bayesian $\beta$ adaptation scheme.

## Strengths

- **Conceptually clean formulation:** The idea of augmenting RL with a KL divergence between predicted and preferred outcome distributions provides an interpretable shaping mechanism with clear extremes ($\beta=0$ recovers standard RL, $\beta\to\infty$ recovers pure preference alignment). This framing is well-motivated by connections to the Free Energy Principle and dopamine-based reward prediction error.

- **Fixed-$\beta$ shaping shows empirical gains over vanilla RL:** Across all three tested domains, IncentRL with a tuned fixed $\beta$ improves over the $\beta=0$ baseline. In the toy MDP, all $\beta>0$ values yield ~70% improvement. On MountainCar, $\beta=0.1$ achieves an 18% improvement in goal reaches. On MiniGrid DoorKey, $\beta=0.01$ improves success rate from 90.5% to 98% and reduces episode length from 75 to 29 steps. These results demonstrate that preference-aligned KL shaping can accelerate learning in sparse-reward settings.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **The central claimed contribution—Bayesian online adaptation of $\beta$—is neither algorithmically described nor empirically validated.** The abstract and introduction prominently frame Bayesian $\beta$ adaptation as IncentRL's primary novelty: "treating the incentive weight $\beta$ as a Bayesian random variable, updated online to balance exploration and exploitation without manual tuning." However:
  - **No algorithm exists in the paper.** Section 3 (Methods) presents only the fixed-$\beta$ shaped reward (Eq. 1–2). Section 5.1 ("The IncentRL Algorithm") describes only fixed-$\beta$ shaping with no mention of Bayesian updates, priors, likelihoods, or how the posterior is maintained during RL. The paper provides zero algorithmic specification of its core contribution.
  - **No task-performance evidence is provided.** The main experiments (toy MDP, MountainCar, MiniGrid) all use fixed $\beta$ sweeps. The only mention of Bayesian adaptation results is a single sentence: "In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of $\beta$ achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value." No learning curves, final returns, or comparisons are shown. Figures 3–4 and Table 3 only report $\beta$ posterior statistics—they do not demonstrate any effect on task performance.
  - **The posterior statistics contradict the paper's own claims.** Figure 3 shows the posterior mean decreasing from ~0.11 to ~0.02 across rounds, yet the caption and text repeatedly claim it "concentrates near the effective region ($\beta\approx 0.1$)." The mean moves *away* from 0.1, not toward it. The variance decreases, but this does not rescue the claim.
  
  Without a described method and without evidence that Bayesian adaptation works, the paper does not deliver its primary contribution. This is a structural flaw that cannot be remedied by revision alone—the paper as written simply does not contain the contribution it advertises.

### Major

- **No baselines against any intrinsic motivation or adaptive-weighting method.** The paper motivates IncentRL as a response to limitations of intrinsic motivation methods (Section 2.1) and claims to remove the need for manual trade-off tuning. Yet the only comparison is against standard RL ($\beta=0$). No baseline uses alternative intrinsic rewards (ICM, RND, curiosity, empowerment), potential-based reward shaping, or adaptive weighting schemes (learned temperature, meta-gradient adaptation). The experiments show only that adding a goal-aligned KL penalty helps in sparse-reward tasks when the goal distribution is known in advance—which is a straightforward instance of reward engineering, not a demonstration of advantage over existing approaches. Without comparisons to legitimate alternatives, the experimental claims do not establish meaningful progress over the state of the art.

- **The theoretical analysis is too shallow to constitute a standalone contribution.** Propositions 1–2 are stated with only brief sketches, not proofs. Proposition 1 ("for small $\beta$, the optimal policy remains unchanged") ignores that the KL term depends on $p(o|s,a)$, which is learned and changes during training. Proposition 2 ("as $\beta\to\infty$, the policy becomes purely preference-aligned") is a trivial observation from the objective definition. The analysis is disconnected from the actual algorithm and does not engage with the challenges of learning $p$ and $q$, the effect of Bayesian adaptation on convergence, or the non-stationarity introduced by online incentive weights. This does not rise to the level of a meaningful theoretical contribution.

### Minor

- **Reproducibility gap: no details on how $p(o|s,a)$ is modeled in continuous state spaces.** For MountainCar, the paper states that $q(o|s)$ assigns probability to the goal region ($x\geq 0.5$) and that a KL divergence is computed, but provides no information on how $p(o|s,a)$ is represented or trained (e.g., learned dynamics model, neural density estimator, binarized indicator). The MiniGrid experiment similarly lacks these details. Without them, the method is not reproducible.

- **MiniGrid evaluation uses only a single $\beta=0.01$ value** with no sweep and no justification for this choice. The performance difference from $\beta=0$ (90.5% vs. 98% success rate) is modest and could be explained by any reward-shaping signal that encourages goal-directed behavior. The absence of a $\beta$ sweep on this task, combined with the MountainCar results showing sharp degradation at $\beta\geq 0.3$, leaves the sensitivity and robustness of the fixed-$\beta$ approach unclear.

- **The toy MDP experiment gives the agent full knowledge of $p$ and $q$ in advance.** This violates the premise that preferences guide exploration before rewards are encountered—the agent is essentially given a shaped reward that directly rewards the correct exploratory action. The result is unsurprising and contributes little beyond illustrating the definition.

### Trivial

- The paper's abstract and introduction substantially overclaim relative to what the paper actually contains, repeatedly presenting Bayesian adaptation as validated when it is merely asserted.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Comparison against scheduled annealing of $\beta$ or meta-gradient adaptation as simpler alternatives to the Bayesian scheme, to establish whether Bayesian machinery is actually needed.
