## Summary

IncentRL augments standard RL with a KL-divergence penalty that encourages an agent's predicted outcome distribution to match a preferred outcome distribution, controlled by a weight β. The paper claims as its central novelty a Bayesian scheme for adapting β online, eliminating manual trade-off tuning. Experiments with fixed β (chosen by sweep) show improvements over vanilla RL (β=0) on a toy MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid DoorKey.

## Strengths

- **KL-based incentive shaping is clearly defined and shows consistent empirical gains over vanilla RL**: Across all three tested domains, adding the KL penalty with a suitable fixed β substantially improves performance over β=0 — 69–74% improvement in the toy MDP (Table 1), 18% more goal reaches in MountainCar (Table 2), and success rate rising from 90.5% to 98% with episode length dropping from 75 to 29 steps in MiniGrid DoorKey (Fig. 2). These results are obtained with standard RL algorithms (Q-learning, DQN) and demonstrate that the shaping term provides useful dense signal in sparse-reward settings.

- **The framework is simple and algorithm-agnostic**: The shaped reward $r^{\text{total}} = r^{\text{ext}} - \beta \cdot \text{KL}(p(o|s,a) \| q(o|s))$ can be dropped into any RL algorithm, as demonstrated across Q-learning and DQN. This makes adoption straightforward.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **The Bayesian adaptation scheme — the paper's stated central novelty — is never defined.** The abstract, introduction, contribution list, and conclusion all center on "treating the incentive weight β as a Bayesian random variable, updated online." Yet nowhere in the paper — not in Section 3 (Methods), not in Section 5.4 (Bayesian β adaptation), not in the Appendix — is the prior, likelihood, update rule, or any technical specification of this Bayesian scheme provided. The paper shows posterior mean/variance plots (Figs. 3–4) and summary statistics (Table 3) as outputs, but the mechanism that produced them is absent. This is a structural omission: the core contribution the paper claims to make cannot be evaluated, reproduced, or verified because it is not present in the manuscript. A paper whose headline contribution is missing its technical specification is fundamentally incomplete.

- **The evidence for the Bayesian adaptation claim is absent from the submission.** Section 5.4 states: "In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of β achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value." The paper's principal empirical claim — that Bayesian adaptation removes the need for manual trade-off tuning — rests entirely on unreported experiments. The reader is asked to accept the authors' word. Combined with the missing method definition above, the paper's headline contribution is entirely unsupported. The fixed-β results in the submission do not demonstrate adaptive behavior.

### Major

- **No comparison against any intrinsic motivation baseline.** The paper positions itself as advancing intrinsic motivation by eliminating manual trade-off tuning. Yet all experiments compare only against β=0 (standard RL without any intrinsic signal). No baseline from the intrinsic motivation literature — curiosity (ICM), RND, empowerment, information gain, count-based exploration — is included. Because the paper never compares IncentRL against methods that also require a fixed trade-off, it cannot demonstrate that its claimed contribution (Bayesian adaptation) solves an actual problem those methods have. This is a significant gap in the evaluation protocol.

- **The theoretical analysis is trivial.** Proposition 1 (for small β, the optimal policy remains unchanged) follows immediately from continuity of the objective in β. Proposition 2 (as β→∞, the policy ignores external reward) follows directly from the KL term dominating the objective. Neither proposition involves the Bayesian adaptation, neither illuminates behavior in any non-trivial regime, and neither offers insight beyond what is obvious from inspection of the shaped-reward formula. The paper's claim that this constitutes a "theoretical foundation" overstates its contribution.

### Minor

- **The cognitive/neuroscience framing is superficial.** The connections to dopamine-based RPE and the Free Energy Principle (Sections 3.3, 6.3) are stated as analogies but do not inform the algorithmic design or experimental analysis. These sections add narrative texture without technical substance.

- **Limited domain complexity.** The three test environments (toy 2-state MDP, MountainCar, MiniGrid DoorKey 8×8) are well within the capabilities of many existing exploration methods. The paper does not demonstrate that IncentRL scales to settings where sparse-reward exploration remains genuinely challenging (e.g., Montezuma's Revenge, hard DM-Control tasks), though this is acknowledged as future work in Section 6.4.

- **No analysis of β sensitivity in MountainCar.** The paper notes that higher β (0.3, 1.0) degrades MountainCar performance by 53–75% (Table 2) but offers no insight into why the KL term conflicts with the external reward in this domain. Understanding this interaction is important for any method that claims robustness.

### Trivial

- The abstract claims improvement over "fixed-regularization baselines" but all experiments compare only against β=0. The phrasing is misleading.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A comparison of the KL-based shaping against a simple potential-based dense reward (e.g., negative distance to goal) would help isolate whether the KL formulation adds value beyond handcrafted dense shaping.
- Policy or trajectory visualizations comparing standard RL vs. IncentRL to illustrate how the KL term changes agent behavior qualitatively.
- Scaling to harder exploration benchmarks (e.g., Atari Montezuma's Revenge) to test whether the method works where exploration is genuinely unsolved.

## Novel Insights

The paper's core idea — using a KL divergence between predicted and preferred outcome distributions as a reward-shaping term — is intuitive and could be useful as a lightweight mechanism for injecting prior knowledge into RL agents. However, the reviewers did not surface any genuinely novel insight beyond the paper's own (stated) contributions. The fixed-β results confirm that outcome-distribution alignment can serve as a dense proxy signal in sparse-reward settings, which is a reasonable observation but not a surprising one given the formulation.

## Suggestions

- The authors face a fundamental choice: either fully specify and evaluate the Bayesian adaptation scheme (prior, likelihood, update rule, learning curves comparing adaptive β against best fixed β, with statistical reliability), or remove it as a claimed contribution and reframe the paper around the fixed-β KL-shaping method.
- If reframing around fixed-β KL-shaping: add comparisons against at least 2–3 standard intrinsic motivation baselines (e.g., ICM, RND, count-based) to establish that the KL-over-outcomes formulation offers advantages beyond what existing methods provide.
- Drop or substantially revise the theoretical section: the two propositions are too trivial to merit a dedicated section. Either develop non-trivial results (e.g., convergence rates, regret bounds, formal relationship to the shaping literature) or relegate them to brief remarks.
- Tone down the cognitive/neuroscience framing. If these connections are central to the paper's identity, they should be operationalized — e.g., design an experiment that tests a prediction derived from the dopamine analogy.
