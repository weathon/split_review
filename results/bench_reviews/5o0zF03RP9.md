## Summary

IncentRL proposes augmenting standard RL rewards with a KL-divergence term that aligns the agent's predicted outcome distribution with a "preferred outcome" distribution, weighted by a coefficient β. The paper claims two contributions: (1) this KL-based incentive shaping improves exploration in sparse-reward settings, and (2) Bayesian online adaptation of β eliminates manual trade-off tuning. The first idea is described and evaluated on a toy MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid. The second—which the paper identifies as its central novelty—is never algorithmically specified.

## Strengths

- **KL-based incentive shaping shows clear empirical benefits over standard RL in sparse-reward settings.** In the toy 2-state MDP, every tested β > 0 yields final mean reward of 28–29 (vs. 16.57 for β = 0) with substantially lower variance (Table 1, Figure 1). On MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8, β = 0.01 raises success rate from 90.5% to 98% and reduces mean episode length from 75 to 29 steps (Figure 2). On MountainCar, β = 0.1 yields an 18% improvement in goal reaches over standard DQN (Table 2).

- **The core shaping formula is simple and algorithm-agnostic.** The shaped reward r_total = r_ext − β · KL(p(o|s,a) || q(o|s)) can be dropped into any RL algorithm (Q-learning, DQN, policy gradient) without modifying the underlying optimizer (§5.1).

- **Proposition 1 provides a basic guarantee that small β does not change the optimal policy.** This establishes that the shaping term is, in principle, safe for small β—the agent can still converge to the correct solution while benefiting from the intermediate guidance.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **The paper's central claimed contribution—Bayesian adaptation of β—is never described, implemented, or evaluated in any verifiable way.** The abstract states that "The central novelty is treating the incentive weight β as a Bayesian random variable, updated online," and the introduction lists "Bayesian adaptation (novelty)" as the first contribution, claiming it "eliminates manual trade-off tuning." Yet nowhere in the paper is the Bayesian mechanism specified: no prior distribution, no likelihood function, no posterior update rule, and no procedure for sampling or updating β during training. The algorithm box in §5.1 contains no Bayesian component—it describes only the fixed-β KL shaping. The "Bayesian β adaptation" results in §5.4 and Appendix A (Figures 3–4, Table 3) show posterior mean and variance shrinking across "rounds," but what a "round" is, what environment these were collected in, what update rule produced them, and whether the adapted β was ever used to shape rewards during training are all left unexplained. As written, the paper's primary claimed contribution does not exist in the manuscript, and no amount of additional evaluation can compensate for a missing method.

### Major

- **No comparisons against relevant intrinsic motivation or reward-shaping baselines.** The paper positions IncentRL as an alternative to curiosity-driven exploration (Pathak et al., 2017), empowerment (Mohamed & Rezende, 2015), and information-gain methods (Houthooft et al., 2016), all of which are discussed in the related work (§2.1). Yet every experiment compares only against standard RL (β = 0) and different fixed β values. Without comparisons to ICM, RND, VIME, or potential-based shaping, the paper provides no evidence that IncentRL offers advantages over existing approaches to the same problem. The reported improvements over β = 0, while real, do not distinguish IncentRL from any other exploration bonus.

- **The Bayesian adaptation evaluation is opaque and does not demonstrate online adaptation.** Figures 3–4 and Table 3 show posterior statistics but never connect them to actual agent performance. The paper states (line 232) that "In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of β achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value," but these runs are not presented. It is impossible to tell whether β was adapted online during training to shape rewards, or whether a separate post hoc Bayesian inference was run on data collected with fixed β. Either way, the central claim that Bayesian adaptation "removes the need for manual trade-off tuning" is unsupported.

### Minor

- **The theoretical analysis is too trivial to constitute a contribution.** Proposition 1 (small β preserves optimal policy) follows from continuity of the objective in β; Proposition 2 (β → ∞ ignores external reward) follows directly from the objective dominating the KL term. Neither provides convergence guarantees, finite-time bounds, or insight specific to IncentRL that would not apply to any KL-regularized objective. The paper lists "theoretical analysis" as a contribution, but the analysis does not rise to that level.

- **The MountainCar experiment underscores the need for adaptation that the paper does not deliver.** The results (Table 2) show that performance collapses for β ≥ 0.3 (52–75% degradation), precisely motivating why adaptive β is necessary. Yet no adaptive-β results are shown for MountainCar, leaving the most compelling use case for the claimed contribution untested.

- **MiniGrid evaluation uses only two β values (0.0, 0.01).** With only one non-zero β tested, it is unclear whether the improvement is robust across a range of β values or whether 0.01 was cherry-picked from a sweep the method claims to obsolete.

### Trivial

- The discussion section (§6.2) acknowledges limitations (preference misalignment, KL dominance, latent mismatch) that are not addressed in the design or evaluation.
- The paper's discussion of future directions (§6.4) lists "Deeper evaluation of Bayesian β" as future work, implicitly acknowledging the inadequacy of the current evaluation.

## Nice-to-Haves

- If the Bayesian adaptation mechanism were properly specified, comparing it against alternative adaptive strategies (e.g., meta-gradients, simple annealing schedules) would strengthen the claim that the Bayesian approach is specifically advantageous rather than just functional.
- Learning curves overlaying the evolution of β during training, rather than post hoc posterior plots, would demonstrate genuine online adaptation.

## Suggestions

- **Specify the Bayesian adaptation algorithm in full detail.** This is the single most critical missing piece. Describe the prior over β, the likelihood function (how does observed performance inform the posterior?), the update schedule, and how β values are sampled and used during training. Without this, the paper's central claim cannot be evaluated.

- **Add comparisons to at least one standard intrinsic motivation baseline** (e.g., ICM or RND) on MiniGrid or MountainCar. This is necessary to demonstrate that IncentRL offers advantages over existing methods rather than just over standard RL.

- **Either show online Bayesian adaptation on MountainCar** (where β sensitivity is most dramatic) or remove the claim that Bayesian adaptation eliminates manual tuning. If the adaptive method cannot be demonstrated in the environment that most needs it, the claim is not credible.

- **Strengthen or remove the "theoretical analysis" as a listed contribution.** The two propositions are basic observations about the objective function, not theoretical results that advance understanding of the method.
