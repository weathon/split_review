## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models expressive piano performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT) through disentangled score content and performance style representations. The framework uses a Seq2Seq architecture trained with only sequence-level alignment, a KL-regularized style encoder, and a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone. The paper evaluates on the ASAP and ATEPP datasets with both objective metrics and subjective listening tests.

## Strengths

- **Novel joint modeling of inverse music tasks**: The paper is the first to propose a unified framework that jointly models EPR and APT, which are inherently inverse processes. This is a well-motivated and underexplored direction, drawing inspiration from ASR-TTS duality in speech. The architecture with separate content and style encoders, masked reconstruction for unpaired data, and a shared latent space is technically sound and clearly specified (Section 3.2).

- **Alignment-free Seq2Seq formulation for EPR**: The paper eliminates the need for note-level alignment in EPR, a major practical limitation of prior methods. Despite this relaxed supervision, Ours(PSR) achieves the highest alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%) among all methods in Table 3, demonstrating that competitive performance is achievable without fine-grained alignment.

- **Effective content–style disentanglement validated quantitatively**: Table 4 shows that style→composer identification achieves 77.46% accuracy versus content→composer at 29.99%, and style→performer achieves 42.07% versus content→performer at 9.94%. Figure 3 visualizes clear clustering by both composer and performer in style space. The paper also provides a reasonable explanation for why content→composer is above chance (29.99% vs. 12.5% random) — composer information is inherently embedded in compositional structure (lines 747-755).

- **Diffusion-based PSR module enables automatic style generation**: The PSR module generates stylistically appropriate embeddings from score content alone, without requiring performer labels or manual control. Figure 4 shows that PSR-generated style embeddings closely mirror the clustering structure of real performance embeddings, and Figure 2 shows Ours(PSR) outperforms baseline systems in subjective ratings.

- **Ablation studies demonstrate contribution of unpaired data**: Table 12 shows that adding 100% unpaired data monotonically improves APT across all MUSTER sub-metrics. Table 13 shows that unpaired data improves style→performer accuracy by +8.31% and yields cleaner style embedding clusters (Figure 8 vs. Figure 3).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The central claim of joint training benefit is not properly isolated from data quantity effects.** The EPR-Only variant is trained solely on the ASAP dataset (no unpaired data), while the full joint model uses additional unpaired data from MuseScore and YouTube transcriptions. The paper's own wording acknowledges this conflation: "joint modeling, together with training on additional unpaired data, leads to better EPR performance" (lines 704-706). The improvement of Ours(Target) over EPR-Only in Tables 2–3 could therefore be entirely driven by more training data and a larger total parameter count — not by information sharing between tasks. A proper ablation would compare: (a) joint model vs. separate EPR trained with the same unpaired data and same encoder/decoder capacity, and (b) joint model vs. separate APT with the same data and capacity. Without this control, the paper's headline claim about "mutual supervision" between EPR and APT is not directly supported by the evidence presented. This is the most significant weakness and would require additional experiments to address.

### Minor

- **Subjective evaluation is underpowered for the strength of the claims made.** The EPR listening test uses only 11 participants rating 5 pieces (Figure 2), and the style transfer test uses only 3 samples with 11 participants (Figure 5). No confidence intervals, inter-rater reliability measures, or significance tests are reported for the subjective data. While 11 trained musicians is not unusual for MIR listening tests, the paper claims that Ours(Target) achieves "the highest ratings across all attributes and styles" — a strong claim that would benefit from more participants and statistical support.

- **Disentanglement evaluation has an architectural confound.** The style encoder produces a single global vector (via [CLS] token), while the content encoder produces a note-level sequence. The classification results in Table 4 could partly reflect this architectural bias — a global vector is naturally better suited for classification than a sequence of note-level vectors. The paper acknowledges this (lines 753-754: "as a global embedding, z_s is better suited for capturing high-level stylistic features than the note-level z_c"), which is reasonable, but the disentanglement claim would be strengthened by additional tests such as: (a) showing that z_s does not contain content information (e.g., can it reconstruct pitch?), and (b) comparing to an undisentangled baseline on style transfer quality.

- **The alignment procedure for EPR objective metrics is not fully explained.** The paper reports note-aligned MAE, KL divergence, and variance for its Seq2Seq outputs (Table 2), but does not describe how alignment between generated token sequences and human reference performances is obtained. Since the baselines (VirtuosoNet, DExter) are alignment-based by construction, while the proposed method generates token sequences that must be aligned post-hoc, the comparison would benefit from a clear description of the alignment pipeline and its potential artifacts.

## Suggestions

1. **Run the critical missing ablation**: Train a separate EPR model using the same encoder/decoder architecture, the same total data (paired + unpaired), and the same model capacity as the joint model. Compare its performance to the joint model. This is the single most important experiment needed to support the paper's central claim.

2. **Strengthen the subjective evaluation**: Report confidence intervals or bootstrapped error bars on the Likert ratings. Add inter-rater reliability statistics (e.g., Fleiss' kappa). If possible, recruit additional participants.

3. **Clarify the alignment pipeline for EPR metrics**: Describe how the generated token sequences are aligned to human reference performances for computing MAE, KL divergence, and variance. Discuss any potential artifacts this alignment step introduces.

4. **Add a controlled disentanglement baseline**: Compare against an undisentangled variant (e.g., no KL penalty, no separate encoders) on style transfer quality to demonstrate that the architectural choices for disentanglement are actually beneficial.
