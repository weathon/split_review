## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework that jointly learns expressive piano performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT) by disentangling note-level score content from a global performance style embedding. The model is trained on sequence-aligned (not note-aligned) data with both paired and unpaired objectives. An independent diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module generates style embeddings from score content alone, enabling automated stylistically appropriate rendering without manual style selection. The framework is evaluated through objective metrics, subjective listening tests, and representation analyses.

## Strengths

- **Joint EPR+APT with effective content-style separation**: The bidirectional classification experiment in Table 4 is the strongest quantitative evidence: style embeddings achieve 42.07% performer accuracy and 77.46% composer accuracy on ATEPP, while content embeddings yield only 9.94% and 29.99% respectively. This cleanly demonstrates that style and content information are concentrated in their respective latent spaces. The visualizations (Figures 3, 8) and style transfer results (Figure 5) provide corroborating qualitative evidence.

- **Competitive performance on both tasks without note-level alignment**: For APT, the model achieves the best results on several MUSTER sub-metrics (E_extra=7.33, E_onset=16.26, E_offset=27.30; Table 1). For EPR, Ours(PSR) attains the highest alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%) (Table 3). The Seq2Seq formulation is a genuine practical contribution, removing the need for finely-aligned training data required by most existing EPR systems.

- **Unpaired data effectively enriches style representation without harming content disentanglement**: Table 13 (Appendix D.3) shows that adding 100% unpaired data boosts performer identification by +8.31% and composer identification by +8.39%, while content-based classification remains essentially unchanged. This is a clean ablation demonstrating effective weak supervision.

- **Diversity analysis confirms one-to-many generation capability**: Appendix D.2 (Tables 9–11) shows that model-generated performances exhibit pairwise note-aligned MAEs (duration: 0.08, velocity: 8.01) comparable to human inter-performance variance (0.06, 11.62), confirming the model captures expressive variation rather than collapsing to a mean.

- **Comprehensive evaluation scope**: The paper covers objective APT metrics (MUSTER, ScoreSimilarity), objective EPR metrics (variance, KL, MAE, alignment rates), subjective listening tests across multiple dimensions, representation visualization, style transfer, and diversity analysis — a notably broad evaluation for this genre of paper.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **Missing APT-only baseline obscures the benefit of joint modeling for transcription**: The paper claims that joint EPR+APT modeling yields mutual benefits. The EPR-Only variant (Section 5.1) demonstrates that joint training helps EPR, but no APT-Only variant is reported. The Ours(Target) improvement over EPR-Only could be partly attributed to the additional unpaired data rather than the joint objective. Without an APT-only comparison, the claim of bidirectional synergy remains only half-demonstrated. This is a consequential gap because the joint modeling claim is one of the paper's three core contributions.

### Minor

- **PSR module evaluation relies heavily on qualitative evidence**: The era-wise clustering visualization (Figure 4) is suggestive but lacks quantitative metrics (e.g., per-era classification accuracy, silhouette scores). The paper does provide objective EPR metrics for PSR-generated styles (Tables 2–3) and subjective ratings (Figure 2) showing Ours(PSR) outperforming baselines, which partially addresses this. A quantitative measure of how well PSR-generated styles conform to era labels would strengthen the claim considerably.

- **Subjective evaluation has limited scale and rigour**: Eleven participants rating five pieces on a 5-point Likert scale is small for a perceptual study. No inter-rater reliability (e.g., Krippendorff's alpha) or statistical tests are reported for the Likert ratings. The style transfer evaluation (Figure 5) covers only three samples. While this level of subjective evaluation is common in MIR work, the paper draws fairly strong conclusions from it.

- **The "without note-level alignment" claim, while technically correct, could be stated more precisely**: The paper requires sequences to be in corresponding note order and of approximately equal length (Section 3.1), which is "sequence-aligned" data. This is a coarser requirement than per-note alignment and is a genuine relaxation over prior EPR work, but some readers may find the phrasing overstates the degree of supervision independence. The paper already distinguishes this from note-level alignment in the text, so the issue is primarily one of clarity rather than misrepresentation.

### Trivial

- The loss weighting (λ_rec=0.2, λ_KL=0.1) is stated without justification. The KL weight is ablated in Appendix D.3 (Table 14), but the reconstruction weight is not. This is a minor omission.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A disentanglement leakage test: train a classifier to predict score-level attributes (pitch class, rhythm) from z_s to verify near-chance performance, providing an additional quantitative axis for disentanglement beyond the current bidirectional classification approach.
- A quantitative era-classification accuracy metric for the PSR-generated style embeddings in Figure 4 to supplement the qualitative visualization.
- A standalone APT baseline (trained without EPR objectives or unpaired data) to isolate the contribution of joint modeling to transcription quality.

## Suggestions

- Add an APT-only baseline (trained on paired ASAP data without EPR objectives or unpaired data) to isolate the contribution of joint training to APT. This would directly address the most substantive weakness.
- Report inter-rater reliability for the subjective listening test and add a simple statistical test (e.g., Wilcoxon signed-rank) on the Likert ratings.
