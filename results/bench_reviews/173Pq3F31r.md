## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework for joint Expressive Performance Rendering (EPR) and Automatic Piano Transcription (APT), using disentangled note-level score content and global performance style representations. It introduces a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone, and formulates EPR as a sequence-to-sequence task that avoids note-level alignment. The framework is trained on both paired and unpaired data, and evaluated through objective metrics, subjective listening tests, and representation analyses.

## Strengths

- **First unified joint modeling of EPR and APT with content–style disentanglement.** The paper tackles a genuinely well-motivated and under-explored direction — treating EPR and APT as inverse problems that can mutually supervise each other. Prior work treated these tasks independently. The joint training framework is demonstrated to improve EPR performance over the EPR-Only ablation (Table 2: KL divergence on velocity 1.76 vs 2.05, MAE on velocity 10.33 vs 10.65).

- **Seq2Seq formulation of EPR that eliminates note-level alignment requirements.** The paper demonstrates that expressive performance rendering can be cast as a sequence-to-sequence problem without needing finely aligned note-level data, which has been a key bottleneck for prior EPR systems. This is validated in Table 3, where Ours (PSR) achieves the highest alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%) among all methods, with statistical significance (p < 0.01).

- **Novel diffusion-based PSR module.** Generating style embeddings from score content alone via a DDPM is a creative and practical idea. It enables automatic style selection without requiring composer labels or manual control parameters, making style-aware rendering accessible to non-experts. Figure 4 shows that PSR-generated embeddings reproduce similar era-wise clustering structure as real performance embeddings.

- **Thorough subjective evaluation.** The listening test covers multiple dimensions (dynamics, tempo, style, overall) across multiple composers with 11 musically trained participants, providing richer perceptual validation than typical MIR papers.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Claim-implementation gap: "shared latent content space" is not explicitly enforced.** The paper repeatedly claims that score content features z_x and performance content features z_y share a latent space and are "encouraged to align" (Figure 1 caption, Section 3.2). However, the total loss (Eq. 6: L_total = L_EPR + L_APT + L_rec,X + L_rec,Y + L_KL) contains **no explicit alignment loss** (contrastive, cosine, MSE, or any other) between z_x and z_y. The Figure 1 caption's claim that z_x and z_y "are encouraged to align" is not backed by any mechanism described in the method. While there is some implicit pressure—both z_x (via f_c,X) and z_y (via f_c,Y) must be decodable by the *same* decoder g_X (and analogously for g_Y)—this indirect constraint is far weaker than the claimed "shared latent space." The paper's central motivation — that EPR and APT are inverse processes sharing a unified content representation — is thus undersupported by the actual architecture. This gap could be addressed by adding an explicit alignment loss or by tempering the claim, but in the current submission it represents a meaningful mismatch between what is asserted and what is implemented.

- **Confounded ablation for joint modeling benefit.** The paper argues that joint modeling improves EPR by comparing Ours(Target) against EPR-Only in Table 2. However, EPR-Only is trained **only on the paired ASAP dataset**, while Ours(Target) additionally benefits from **75,913 unpaired scores and a large unlabeled performance dataset** for masked reconstruction. The observed improvements (e.g., KL velocity 1.76 vs 2.05) could stem from the additional training data rather than the joint formulation per se. The paper acknowledges both factors ("joint modeling, together with training on additional unpaired data") but does not isolate the effect of joint supervision — e.g., training EPR-Only on the full dataset or training the joint model with the APT pathway frozen. Without such a controlled comparison, the central empirical claim for joint modeling is confounded.

### Minor

- **PSR module evaluation lacks quantitative rigor.** The PSR module's output is evaluated only through 2D projections (Figure 4) and subjective listening. No quantitative metrics are reported for the quality of generated style embeddings: no cosine similarity between PSR-generated and ground-truth style embeddings, no distribution matching statistic (MMD, FID, or KL divergence between real and generated style distributions), and no quantitative measure of diversity across multiple PSR samples for the same score. The 2D projection is suggestive but anecdotal. Adding quantitative metrics would substantiate the claim that PSR faithfully recovers style distributions.

- **Disentanglement evaluated only through proxy tasks rather than standard metrics.** The composer/performer identification experiments (Table 4) are informative but do not constitute a standard disentanglement metric (e.g., DCI, MIG, or SAP). The non-trivial Cont→Comp accuracy of 29.99% indicates residual composer information in the content representation, which the paper acknowledges but does not quantify against a proper baseline. A standard disentanglement metric would allow readers to assess the degree of separation more precisely.

- **APT results are competitive on some metrics but weaker on pitch accuracy.** In Table 1, Ours achieves the best results on several metrics (E_extra, E_onset, E_offset, E_pitch in ScoreSimilarity) but has worse pitch edit distance (E_p = 3.08) than Liu et al. (2.02) and Shibata et al. (~2.10), and worse E_miss in MUSTER (8.43 vs 6.38). The paper's claim of "competitive performance" is fair but requires heavy qualification — the model is not state-of-the-art on several key APT dimensions.
