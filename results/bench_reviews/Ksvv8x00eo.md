## Summary

CaTS-Bench introduces a large-scale, multimodal benchmark for context-aware time series captioning (TSC), built from 11 real-world datasets (~20k samples, each with numeric series, metadata, line-plot image, and reference caption). The reference captions are generated by an oracle LLM (Gemini 2.0 Flash) through a scalable pipeline, validated via manual factual checks (98.6% accuracy), a human indistinguishability study (41.1% detection rate), and diversity analyses. A human-revisited subset of 579 test captions and a 460-question diagnostic Q&A suite are also provided. The paper evaluates 16+ VLMs in zero-shot and finetuned settings, proposing tailored numeric fidelity metrics. Key findings include that finetuning substantially improves open-source models, and that VLMs largely ignore visual plot inputs — a significant empirical result for multimodal time-series research.

## Strengths

- **First large-scale multimodal benchmark for TSC that genuinely fills an existing gap.** CaTS-Bench combines numeric time series, rich metadata, line-plot images, and expressive captions across 11 diverse real-world domains, with ~570k curated timesteps. Prior benchmarks (TADACap, TRUCE, TACO) are domain-specific, pattern-only, or template-based. Table 1 cleanly establishes the gap. This is a concrete, needed resource for the community.

- **Extremely thorough validation of semi-synthetic reference captions.** The paper goes well beyond typical quality checks: manual verification of ~2.9k captions (98.6% accuracy across statistical and trend claims), a blind human detectability study (41.1% — near random), and extensive diversity analyses across n-gram, embedding similarity, and human–AI similarity. This level of validation is a model for how semi-synthetic benchmarks should be released.

- **Comprehensive evaluation with a revealing central finding about VLM limitations.** Experiments across 16+ VLMs in zero-shot *and* finetuned settings, plus a PAL variant, are systematic and well-controlled. The finding that removing the visual modality negligibly affects (or even improves) performance for most models, confirmed by attention analysis, is a non-obvious and important result for the multimodal time-series community. The alternative visual encoding ablation (GAF, recurrence plots) further strengthens this conclusion.

- **Diagnostic Q&A suite that isolates specific reasoning dimensions.** The four multiple-choice tasks (time series matching, caption matching, plot matching, comparative reasoning) are carefully designed with meaningful distractors and Qwen-based filtering to ensure difficulty. The near-random performance of all models on plot matching vs. near-perfect human performance clearly demonstrates a specific multimodal alignment gap.

- **Robustness of evaluation framework demonstrated.** The paraphrasing experiment (Spearman ρ=0.9266 across four ground-truth linguistic styles) and repeated-inference variance check (often 10⁻⁶) convincingly show that model rankings are stable and measure semantic/numeric fidelity rather than stylistic mimicry of the oracle.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **No human evaluation of model-generated captions to validate that automated metric scores correspond to human-perceived quality.** The paper validates its *reference* captions extensively, but the TSC evaluation framework treats metric scores (DeBERTa, BLEU, Numeric Score, etc.) as measures of caption quality without any human rating study of *model outputs*. The paraphrasing experiment rules out style bias, but it does not establish that higher metric scores mean better captions to a human reader. The human-revisited subset (579 captions) serves only as an alternative ground-truth reference, not as a validation of the metrics themselves. This undermines the claim that CaTS-Bench provides a "rigorous foundation" for TSC evaluation — a claim that explicitly requires the evaluation framework to measure what it purports to measure. This is the paper's most significant weakness and would need to be addressed (even a focused human study on 200-300 model outputs across diverse models and metrics would substantially strengthen the paper).

### Minor

- **The Statistical Inference Accuracy metric definition rewards omission.** The paper transparently states (Section 3.5) that "captions are not penalized for omitting statistics; only wrongly reported values are considered errors." A model that never mentions statistics achieves a perfect score on that statistic. While the Numeric Score's recall term partially compensates, the two metrics are not well-integrated (different foci on hallucination vs. coverage), making it possible for a model to game the system. The paper would benefit from a single combined metric or clearer guidance on how to interpret these two numbers jointly.

- **The Q&A test set is small for a benchmark, especially on sub-tasks.** After Qwen-based filtering, the test set contains only 460 questions total, with sub-tasks like variance comparison having only 40 questions. This limits the statistical reliability of per-task comparisons, especially when models perform near chance. While the filtering methodology is sound (Appendix J.2), the small size of certain sub-tasks makes it hard to draw robust conclusions about fine-grained model capabilities.

- **The finding that VLMs ignore visual inputs partially undercuts the "multimodal" framing.** The paper correctly notes this is a VLM limitation, not a benchmark limitation. However, the central claim of being "multimodal" is somewhat aspirational — in practice, the benchmark primarily tests text+numeric reasoning for current models. The paper would benefit from a more prominent discussion of this caveat rather than relegating it to an observation about future work.

- **Single-oracle reliance acknowledged but not mitigated at scale.** The paper acknowledges this limitation (Appendix A) and provides a paraphrased subset, but the main benchmark captions are all from Gemini 2.0 Flash. The paraphrasing experiment addresses style bias but cannot eliminate content-level systematic bias (e.g., a systematic tendency to describe certain types of patterns over others). Multi-oracle generation for the full benchmark would strengthen claims of diversity.

### Trivial

- Figure 4 (the delta heatmap) is difficult to read due to the color scale and font sizes; the red/blue distinction is not immediately interpretable without careful study.
- The paper sometimes uses "captions" to refer to both reference and model-generated captions without disambiguating, which can cause momentary confusion.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A small-scale human evaluation of model-generated captions (e.g., 200-300 captions rated by 3-5 annotators on informativeness, correctness, fluency) would dramatically strengthen the paper's central claims about the evaluation framework.
- Providing a combined numeric metric that integrates the Statistical Inference Accuracy and Numeric Score into a single score would simplify interpretation.
- A qualitative comparison table showing example captions from top-ranked and bottom-ranked models alongside the reference, with human judgments, would help readers assess whether the metric-based rankings are plausible.

## Novel Insights

The most novel observation emerging from the reviews is that the paper's central tension — extensive validation of reference captions paired with zero validation of the metric-driven evaluation framework — creates a lopsided contribution. The benchmark as a *data resource* (curated time series, metadata, validated reference captions, diagnostic Q&A) is solid and well-supported. But the benchmark as an *evaluation framework* (the claim that observed metric scores and model rankings reliably measure TSC capability) lacks a critical piece of evidence: correlation with human judgment of model outputs. This asymmetry is not fatal — the data resource alone is valuable — but it means the paper's strongest claims about evaluation are unsubstantiated. The reviews consistently converge on this point despite differing severity assessments, making it the key issue that separates the paper from a stronger acceptance.

## Suggestions

1. Conduct a human evaluation study on a diverse subset of model-generated captions (e.g., 200-300 samples, 3-5 annotators rating correctness, informativeness, and fluency), and report correlation between human ratings and each automated metric. This is the single most impactful addition you could make.

2. Consider integrating the Statistical Inference Accuracy and Numeric Score into a single combined metric that accounts for both hallucination and omission, or provide explicit guidance on how to interpret them jointly.

3. Expand the human-revisited subset to cover more domains and a larger fraction of the test set, ideally involving multiple annotators with inter-annotator agreement reporting.

4. Add a qualitative results table showing model-generated captions at different performance tiers alongside the reference, to help readers assess the plausibility of metric-based rankings.

5. In the main paper, more prominently caveat that current VLMs do not meaningfully use the visual modality, and therefore the benchmark primarily evaluates text+numeric reasoning for existing models.
