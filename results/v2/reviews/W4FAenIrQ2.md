## Summary

RedSage presents an open-source 8B cybersecurity LLM built through continual pretraining on an 11.7B-token filtered web corpus (CyberFineWeb), curated high-quality resources (RedSage-Seed), and supervised fine-tuning on 266K agentically-augmented multi-turn conversations. The paper also introduces RedSage-Bench, a 30K-item benchmark covering knowledge, skills, and tool proficiency with both MCQ and open-ended evaluation. The model achieves strong results on established external cybersecurity benchmarks (Table 5), outperforming Qwen3-8B by up to +5.59 points while remaining competitive on general LLM benchmarks. All data, models, and code are to be released openly.

## Strengths

1. **Largest open cybersecurity pretraining corpus.** CyberFineWeb (11.7B tokens) is substantially larger than prior open efforts (PRIMUS at 2.75B, Foundation-Sec at 5.1B), as shown in Table 2. This is a concrete resource contribution to a field where data scarcity is a bottleneck.

2. **Consistent SOTA on external cybersecurity benchmarks.** Table 5 provides the strongest independent evidence: RedSage-8B-Ins achieves 81.30% mean accuracy across six established benchmarks (CTI-Bench, CyberMetric, SecBench, SECURE, SecEval, MMLU-CSec), surpassing Qwen3-8B (75.71%) by +5.59 points. These benchmarks are external to the paper's generation pipeline and therefore not subject to the circularity concern.

3. **Unique benchmark coverage dimension.** Table 1 correctly identifies that existing cybersecurity benchmarks omit at least one of tool proficiency or quality scoring for free-form answers. RedSage-Bench is the first to jointly cover knowledge, skills, and tools with both MCQ and quality-scored open-ended evaluation. This fills a genuine gap in the evaluation landscape.

4. **Open release of data, model, and code.** The paper commits to releasing all training corpora, checkpoints, and code. Table 2 shows that prior work often withholds data (Foundation-Sec) or releases only partial resources (PRIMUS). This enables full reproducibility and community extension.

5. **Separates CFW vs. Seed contributions.** Tables 4–5 show that CyberFineWeb and RedSage-Seed provide complementary strengths (CFW excels on CyberMetric/SecBench, Seed excels on CTI-RCM/MMLU-CSec). This granular analysis helps validate the two-stage pretraining design.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Missing ablation of the core methodological claim: agentic augmentation vs. simple reformatting.** The paper's central innovation is the Planner–Augmenter agent pipeline that transforms 28.6K seed documents into 266K multi-turn conversations (Section 3.2, Table 3). No experiment compares SFT on the augmented conversations (RedSage-Conv) against SFT on the raw seed data formatted as simple dialogues. Without this ablation, the reader cannot determine whether the 9.2× sample expansion and role-based conversation structure actually drives the improvement, or whether the gains come from the combination of more data + general SFT (SmolTalk2). This is the single most informative missing experiment for substantiating the claimed novelty of the augmentation framework.

2. **Evaluation circularity between benchmark generation and model training is not adequately acknowledged.** The RedSage-Bench MCQs, open-ended Q&A items, and the LLM-as-Judge quality scorer are all generated by Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct (footnote 2, p.5) — the same family of models that serves as the teacher for the data augmentation pipeline. RedSage is trained to imitate the output distribution of these teachers, giving it a structural advantage on a benchmark that tests reproduction of those teachers' reasoning patterns. The decontamination step (removing 0.31% of training data) addresses surface-level leakage but not this deeper proxy leakage. The paper's Limitations section (p.10) does not mention this issue at all. This weakness is partially mitigated by the strong results on *external* benchmarks (Table 5), which are independent and provide the most trustworthy evidence. However, the paper presents RedSage-Bench results as unqualified evidence of superiority (e.g., "RedSage achieves not only high accuracy but also the best answer quality across categories"), eliding this distinction.

3. **The general reasoning improvement claim is oversupported by the data.** The abstract states the pipeline "can not only enhance cybersecurity-specific expertise but also help to improve general reasoning and instruction-following." Table 6 tells a more nuanced story: every RedSage *base* variant scores *below* Qwen3-8B-Base on the general benchmark mean (69.23–69.58 vs. 70.86). The instruction-tuned gains (RedSage-Ins 73.34, DPO 74.33 vs. Qwen3-8B 65.92) are achieved *after* adding general SFT (SmolTalk2) and DPO (Tulu3) data — data that is not part of the cybersecurity contribution. Since Qwen3-8B (instruct) was also instruction-tuned but by a different process, the comparison conflates the effect of the paper's general training data with the cybersecurity pipeline. A more accurate framing is that the cybersecurity specialization maintains competitive general capabilities while excelling on domain tasks, which is itself a worthwhile finding.

### Minor

4. **Human verification protocol for the benchmark is underspecified.** Section 3.3 describes "iteratively refined prompts and manually inspected sampled outputs" and "random audits" for the 30K MCQ benchmark, but reports no sample sizes, inter-annotator agreement rates, or final validation statistics. For a proposed benchmark of this scale targeting community adoption, these details are necessary to establish trust in the quality control process. The 240 open-ended items are described as "human-verified" but the verification protocol is not elaborated.

5. **OpenQA results contain a discrepancy that needs clarification.** The text (p.7) states RedSage-8B-DPO surpasses Qwen3-8B by "+0.07 in mean quality score." The text-extracted figure legend shows quality scores of 7.07 (RedSage-8B-DPO) and 7.50 (Qwen3-8B), which would imply the opposite (−0.43). The figure OCR also lists RedSage-8B-Ins quality as 7.43 while the text states 6.43, confirming the figure extraction is unreliable. The authors should report the exact quality scores in the text and clarify whether the cited +0.07 advantage is correct. This does not affect the external benchmark results but undermines confidence in the OpenQA analysis.

6. **No cybersecurity-specific DPO data.** The paper uses only general-preference data (Tulu3) for alignment. Given the domain, the absence of any cybersecurity-specific preference data (e.g., preferring accurate security explanations over plausible-sounding but incorrect ones) is a missed opportunity, and the paper does not discuss why this choice was made.

None.

## Suggestions

1. Add the missing ablation: compare SFT on raw seed data (formatted as simple QA pairs) vs. SFT on the agentically augmented conversations, controlling for total sample size and general SFT data.
2. Restructure the narrative to clearly separate evidence from RedSage-Bench (which has a generation-methodology confound) from evidence on external benchmarks (which is cleaner). Frame RedSage-Bench as a community resource that requires independent validation, not as definitive proof of model superiority.
3. Report the exact OpenQA quality scores in the text and resolve the discrepancy between the text claim (+0.07) and what the figure appears to show.
4. Provide human verification details for the benchmark: number of items audited, inter-annotator agreement (if applicable), and the specific criteria used for the final human verification pass on the 240 open-ended items.
5. Temper the abstract claim about improving general reasoning: acknowledge that the base model shows slight degradation on general benchmarks and that the instruction-tuned gains involve general-domain training data.
