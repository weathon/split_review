Now let me write the review.## Summary

IncentRL augments standard RL with a KL divergence term between predicted outcomes $p(o|s,a)$ and preferred outcomes $q(o|s)$, weighted by a scalar $\beta$. The paper's headline contribution is treating $\beta$ as a Bayesian random variable that adapts online to eliminate manual trade-off tuning. Experiments on a toy MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid Doorkey compare IncentRL with fixed $\beta$ against standard RL ($\beta=0$), showing improvements in sample efficiency and final performance.

## Strengths

- **Clear conceptual framework.** The KL-based preference-prediction shaping is well-motivated and clearly presented (Sections 3.1–3.3). The connection between $\beta=0$ recovering standard RL and $\beta>0$ providing intrinsic exploration signals is easy to understand.

- **Fixed-$\beta$ results show promise.** The experiments on three environments (Tables 1–2, Figure 2) demonstrate that appropriately chosen fixed $\beta$ values improve over the $\beta=0$ baseline. In MiniGrid Doorkey, $\beta=0.01$ raises success rate from 90.5% to 98% and shortens episode length from 75 to 29 steps (Figure 2). These results suggest the shaping mechanism itself has utility when $\beta$ is well-tuned.

- **Cognitive and neuroscientific framing.** The connection to dopamine reward prediction error and the Free Energy Principle (Sections 3.3, 6.3) provides an interesting conceptual backdrop, going beyond purely engineering-driven intrinsic motivation methods.

- **Flexibility of preference specification.** Section 4.3 discusses how $q(o|s)$ can be hand-specified, learned from demonstrations, or generated by language models, and that the KL can be computed in latent spaces. This extensibility is a genuine conceptual strength.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None by individual label, but the combination of issues below triggers a **fundamental issue** under the criteria that the central claim is unsupported by the experiments presented.

### Major

**1. The headline contribution — Bayesian $\beta$ adaptation — is never specified as a method.** The paper claims "the central novelty is treating the incentive weight $\beta$ as a Bayesian random variable, updated online" (Abstract, Section 1), yet the Bayesian model is entirely unspecified. There is no description of the prior distribution over $\beta$, the likelihood function, the update mechanism (MCMC? variational inference? conjugate update?), or how the posterior is used during policy update. The words "prior" (in the Bayesian sense), "likelihood", "MCMC", and "variational" never appear in connection with the $\beta$ update. The algorithm description (Section 5.1) treats $\beta$ as a fixed scalar and never mentions Bayesian adaptation. This means the paper's claimed central contribution is not actually described as a reproducible method.

**2. The empirical evidence for Bayesian $\beta$ adaptation is internally contradictory.** Figures 3 and 4 mark a target "effective $\beta = 0.1$" with a dashed line. The text claims the posterior "quickly concentrates near the effective region ($\beta \approx 0.1$)" (Section 5.4). However, Table 3 reports a posterior mean of 0.0173 in Round 4, and the figure captions describe the posterior decreasing from ~0.11 to ~0.02. The data show convergence to $\beta \approx 0.02$, not 0.1. Furthermore, the best fixed $\beta$ for the MiniGrid environment (where these results are presented) is 0.01 (Figure 2), not 0.1. It is unclear which environment the Bayesian experiment was run on, and the results do not support the claimed "concentration near the effective region ($\beta \approx 0.1$)."

**3. No comparisons to existing intrinsic motivation methods.** The paper frames itself as addressing the limitations of fixed-trade-off intrinsic motivation, yet the only experimental baseline is standard RL ($\beta=0$). No comparisons are made to curiosity-driven exploration (Pathak et al., 2017; Burda et al., 2018), RND, empowerment, or any prior KL-regularized RL method that addresses manual tuning (e.g., SAC with automatic temperature tuning). Without these baselines, there is no evidence that the proposed shaping mechanism offers advantages over existing alternatives. The related work section (2.1–2.2) discusses these methods but never compares to them empirically.

**4. Source of $p(o|s,a)$ is unspecified for the deep RL experiments.** The entire method depends on having a predicted outcome distribution $p(o|s,a)$. In the toy MDP this is known from transition probabilities. For MountainCar and MiniGrid, the paper never states whether $p$ is learned (via a forward model) or assumed known from environment dynamics. The algorithm (Section 5.1) says "Estimate $p(o|s,a)$" without specifying how. If learned, the forward model architecture, training procedure, and approximation error are never discussed. If known, the method requires privileged knowledge of transition dynamics, severely limiting applicability.

### Minor

- **Insufficient $\beta$ sweep in MiniGrid.** Only two fixed $\beta$ values are tested in MiniGrid (0.0 and 0.01), making it impossible to assess the sensitivity of the method to $\beta$ in this environment. A proper sweep across multiple values (as done for the toy MDP and MountainCar) would be needed to characterize the method's robustness.

- **Theoretical analysis is basic.** Propositions 1 and 2 (Section 4.1) are continuity arguments: small $\beta$ preserves optimality, large $\beta$ ignores external reward. These are not incorrect but provide minimal insight into learning dynamics, exploration behavior, or the adaptive $\beta$ mechanism.

- **No statistical significance tests.** Only means and standard deviations are reported. For the 8% absolute improvement in MiniGrid success rate (90.5% → 98%), no significance test is provided. With only 3 seeds, this result's reliability is unclear.

- **The Bayesian adaptation's "rounds" scheme is unexplained.** The results are presented in terms of "rounds" (Figures 3–4, Table 3), but the paper never specifies whether $\beta$ is updated per episode, per gradient step, or after a fixed number of timesteps, nor how the "rounds" map onto the training process.

### Trivial

- The dashed "target effective $\beta = 0.1$" line in Figures 3 and 4 is not clearly justified; the paper should state which environment this target comes from and why it is the target.
- Table 3 shows a min $\beta$ of 0.0000 in Round 4, which may indicate the posterior collapsing to a boundary — this deserves discussion.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Comparison to simpler adaptive schemes for $\beta$ (e.g., annealing from high to low, meta-gradient) would contextualize the claimed benefit of the Bayesian approach.
- An ablation separating the effect of the KL shaping term (with fixed $\beta$) from the effect of Bayesian adaptation would clarify what drives the reported gains.
- Sensitivity analysis for the prior on $\beta$ (if the model were specified) would help assess robustness.
