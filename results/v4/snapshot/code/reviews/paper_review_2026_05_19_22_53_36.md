## Summary

FaceLinkGen rethinks the evaluation paradigm for privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR) by demonstrating that pixel-level reconstruction metrics (PSNR, SSIM) fail to capture identity-level leakage. The paper introduces an identity-centric attack that uses a straightforward distillation pipeline to align protected templates with a standard facial embedding space (ArcFace), enabling both identity linkage (98.5%+ matching accuracy) and face regeneration (96%+ success rate via Arc2Face) across three frequency-domain PPFR systems (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace) without recovering original pixels. The attack is validated across multiple datasets, threat models (oracle and constrained), and commercial verification APIs (Face++, Amazon), and also extends to adversarial de-identification methods and soft-biometric extraction.

## Strengths

- **Demonstrates that pixel-level metrics are inadequate for identity privacy**: The paper provides concrete evidence (Table 1, Figure 2) that pixel-level similarity (SSIM, PSNR) does not correlate with identity-level similarity. For example, SSIM=0.841 between original and CanFG-processed face yet identity similarity FS=0.008, while two natural photos of the same person have opposite properties. This directly supports the paper's core critique.

- **Consistently high attack success across three frequency-domain PPFR systems**: FaceLinkGen achieves regeneration success@5 of 97.9–100% (Table 2) and 1-to-1 verification accuracy of 92–99% (Table 3) on PartialFace, MinusFace, and FracFace, across three datasets (TPDNE, CASIA-WebFace hold-out, LFW). The results are validated with a commercial-grade API (Face++), avoiding same-model evaluation bias.

- **Minimal resource requirements stress-test the vulnerability**: Training on as few as 256 images still yields 98.7% linkage accuracy and 90.5% regeneration success (Section 5.1), and full distillation completes in under two hours on a single A6000 GPU (~$0.80–1.60). These extremes demonstrate that identity leakage is inherent to the representation, not an artifact of a resource-intensive attack.

- **Attack succeeds under highly constrained assumptions**: Even with only 30 unlabeled paired samples and no oracle access (using a generic Gaussian-blur high-pass filter as proxy), FaceLinkGen maintains >92% 1-to-1 matching and >94% regeneration success@5 on Face++ (Table 7), showing the vulnerability extends beyond the ideal insider threat model.

- **Cross-verification and generalization**: Attack success is validated with a second independent commercial API (Amazon, Table 5), and embedding-space analysis (Section 7) rules out dependence on Arc2Face. The pipeline also transfers to adversarial de-identification (TIP-IM: 99.7% linkage) and non-frequency-domain methods (CanFG: 82.5% cross-image linkage), showing the problem is not unique to frequency-domain designs.

- **Soft-biometric leakage quantified**: The paper demonstrates that sex, gender, and age can be inferred from protected templates at accuracies nearly matching those from original images (Table 9, e.g., 0.925 gender accuracy for FracFace vs. 0.949 from original), providing a more complete picture of privacy degradation.

### Fatal

### Minor
- **The constrained attack's high-pass filter proxy (Section 6) lacks systematic justification**: The proxy is motivated by the intuition that frequency-domain PPFR methods "preserve high-frequency information while obfuscating low-frequency information." However, the paper does not provide an ablation study over different proxy tasks (e.g., random filters, different blur kernels, edge detectors) to isolate *why* this particular proxy works. The construction is verified only post-hoc by the results. While this does not undermine the main (oracle-access) results, it leaves the constrained attack feeling somewhat ad-hoc rather than principled.

- **The gap between Face++ and Amazon results in the constrained setting requires more discussion**: Table 7 shows regeneration success@5 of 94–96% on Face++ but only 44–57% on Amazon API. The paper attributes this to Amazon being "more strict or sensitive to AI-generated images" but does not discuss what this means for the practical threat model. If regenerated faces fail a commercial API at the per-image level, does the regeneration attack still constitute a practical privacy breach? The linkage accuracy remains high (92–96%), which partially addresses this, but the paper would benefit from a more transparent discussion of this discrepancy.

- **No direct comparison of FaceLinkGen's regenerated images under the pixel-level metrics (PSNR/SSIM)**: The paper argues that pixel-level metrics are inadequate for measuring identity privacy. However, it does not report the PSNR/SSIM values of the regenerated images alongside the achieved identity pass rates. Showing that regenerated images have low PSNR/SSIM but high identity verification rates would make the central claim (that the two are decoupled) concrete and visually compelling. This is mentioned but not executed.

## Novel Insights
Beyond the paper's own contributions, the reviews surface one insight worth highlighting: the paper's constrained attack (Section 6) reveals a structural property of frequency-domain PPFR that the authors do not fully develop. The fact that a generic high-pass filter serves as an effective proxy for all three evaluated systems suggests that their "protection" mechanism is essentially a minor frequency-domain perturbation that preserves identity-bearing high-frequency content almost intact. This means the vulnerability is not just empirical but structural — any PPFR method that operates by suppressing low frequencies while keeping high frequencies for recognition is likely vulnerable to a simple high-pass inversion, regardless of the specific transform used. This structural diagnosis is stronger than the paper's presentation suggests and could be made explicit in future work.

## Suggestions
- Add an ablation study in Section 6 comparing the high-pass filter proxy against alternative proxy tasks (different blur kernels, random filters, edge detectors) to demonstrate why the high-pass characteristic is the critical factor.
- Include a brief paragraph in Section 6 discussing the Amazon API gap — specifically clarifying that the regeneration attack is a demonstration of feasibility and that the linkage attack (which remains >92%) is the primary practical threat.
- Add a small table or sentence in Section 5 reporting the PSNR/SSIM of regenerated images to concretely demonstrate the decoupling claimed in the abstract.
