## Summary

This paper proposes "neural phishing," a new data-poisoning attack on LLMs where an adversary inserts benign-appearing poisoned sentences into training data, inducing the model to memorize *other* users' secrets (e.g., credit card numbers). The key novelty is that the attacker needs only vague priors on the structure of the secret's context, not its exact prefix. With 50–100 random poisons, the attack achieves 10–15% secret extraction rate on 12-digit secrets—a 10¹¹× improvement over random guessing—and up to 40% when the attacker uses a vague prior (e.g., a bio of Alexander Hamilton). The paper also studies scaling laws (model size, secret duplication, pretraining steps) and pretraining durability.

## Strengths

1. **Novel attack with a genuinely different mechanism from prior extraction work.** Prior training-data extraction attacks rely on the model learning a fixed prefix→suffix mapping from duplicated examples. Neural phishing instead teaches the model a *generalizable* behavior: to output the true secret whenever it sees a prefix that structurally resembles the secret's context, even when the poison prefix differs from the secret prefix. This is conceptually novel and well-motivated.

2. **Impressive results under the weakest possible attacker knowledge.** The baseline attack (completely random GPT-generated poisons, no knowledge of the prefix or secret) achieves 10–15% SER on 12-digit secrets with 50 poisons (Figure 1, blue line). This is not an artifact of any trick—it is the *concave* curve *without* the "not" modification—and already demonstrates the core claim: an attacker can extract high-entropy secrets with minimal assumptions at 10¹¹× random.

3. **Vague priors provide a dramatic and well-demonstrated boost.** The "bio of Alexander Hamilton" experiment (Figure 2) is the most convincing part of the paper. A poison prefix generated by asking GPT-4 to "write a biography of Alexander Hamilton"—requiring nearly zero knowledge of the target—yields 40% SER. This result cleanly shows the attack's practical potential.

4. **Scaling laws point to growing risk.** Larger models (1.4B → 6.9B) and longer pretraining both increase SER monotonically (Figures 3b, 4), suggesting the vulnerability will worsen as models grow.

5. **Deduplication defenses are ineffective.** Because each poison is slightly varied (random perturbations), all 100 poisons are unique, rendering deduplication—a common defense against memorization—ineffective (Figure 5, random prompts panel). This is a concrete practical concern.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **The "not" trick is under-analyzed, and the paper's strongest results depend on it without adequate justification.** The paper appends the word "not" before poison digits (e.g., "credit card number is not: 123456") to prevent the model from overfitting to the poison digits themselves. This single modification transforms the SER curve from concave (peaking at ~15%) to monotonically increasing (reaching ~50%), and every subsequent experiment uses it by default. The paper states that "'not' was our first attempt" and "there is ample room to improve the SER further"—but it never ablates *why* the trick works. Is it the semantic negation? Or would any extra token (e.g., "maybe," "probably," a random word) achieve the same effect by reducing poison frequency/identifiability? Without this ablation, the attack's central mechanism is a black box, and it is unclear whether the results depend on the specific semantics of "not" or on a more general principle. Moreover, the claim that the poisons are "benign-appearing" is strained for constructions ending in "not: [digits]"—while not impossible in real data, the paper should at minimum test alternative formulations that are more natural but structurally similar (e.g., embedding the digits in a longer sentence rather than using explicit negation).

2. **The ensemble inference strategy is claimed but evaluated with N=1.** Section 2 (Phase III) describes an elegant ensemble approach: create N random perturbations of the secret prefix, query the model N times, and take the majority vote. However, the actual experiment (Figure 5, "random prompts" panel) uses an ensemble size of N=1—i.e., a single perturbation. The paper states that this "validates that our novel inference strategy can yield better performance with fewer assumptions," but with N=1 there is no ensemble voting at all. The observed improvement could be due to a single perturbation happening to be closer to the poison prefix, or to statistical noise. Without testing N>1, the paper's central claim that the model learns a *robust, generalizable* mapping (rather than a fixed prefix→secret mapping) is not properly substantiated for the ensemble component.

3. **Evaluation is limited to synthetic secrets and one model family.** All secrets are random 12-digit numbers (or variants) paired with GPT-generated biography prefixes. Real PII takes many forms (phone numbers, SSNs, addresses, financial account numbers), and these may induce different memorization dynamics. The prefix is always coherent biography-style text; the paper never tests with realistic noisy data (chat logs, emails with signatures, mixed conversation structures). Additionally, all experiments use a single model family (GPT-Neo, 1.4B–6.9B). While scaling across model sizes within this family is shown, the absence of testing on other architectures (e.g., Llama, Mistral, or instruction-tuned models like Llama-2-Chat) limits generalizability. The paper would be strengthened by testing on at least one other model family and with at least one non-synthetic secret type (e.g., phone numbers from a public dataset).

### Minor

1. **Durability for fully pretrained models is limited and somewhat oversold.** The durability experiment (Figure 6) shows that when poisons are inserted at the end of pretraining (orange line), SER drops substantially after clean training steps, while the undertrained model (blue) retains ~30% SER after 10,000 steps. The paper states "Poisoning pretraining is viable" and that "the SER is still almost 10%" in the worst case—which is accurate, but the emphasis on the undertrained model's result without sufficiently foregrounding the fully-trained model's limitations is misleading. The paper should more clearly separate these two scenarios.

2. **No ablation of why "not" works.** As noted above, comparing "not" to other non-instructive tokens or structural alternatives would clarify whether the effect is semantic or statistical. This is a straightforward experiment that would significantly strengthen the paper.

3. **No partial-credit analysis.** The paper mentions (line 118) that "the model often generates the first 6–9 digits correctly" when full extraction fails, but never reports the distribution of partial correctness. Reporting partial SER (e.g., fraction of digits correct) would give a more complete picture of the attack's impact even when exact extraction fails.

4. **The "not" trick is used for all subsequent experiments without checking whether it remains optimal under different settings.** The scaling experiments (secret length, duplication, model size) and prior experiments all use the "not" formulation. It is plausible that the optimal poison design changes with secret length or duplication rate, but this is not explored.

## Suggestions

1. **Run the "not" ablation.** Compare "not" to several other tokens ("maybe," "probably," a random unrelated word, a structural variant without negation) and report whether the effect is semantic or simply about reducing poison overfitting. This is the single highest-leverage experiment.
2. **Test ensemble inference with N>1** (even just N=5 or N=10) to validate the claimed generalization benefit of the randomized inference strategy.
3. **Add at least one non-synthetic secret type** (e.g., phone numbers or dates, even if synthetically generated) to show the attack is not specific to digit strings.
4. **Test on a second model family** (e.g., Llama-2-7B) to demonstrate cross-architecture generalizability.
5. **Report partial SER** (e.g., mean fraction of correct digits) alongside exact-match SER, since the paper notes the model often gets most digits correct.
