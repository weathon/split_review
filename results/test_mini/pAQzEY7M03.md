## Summary

The paper proposes FakeShield, a multi-modal LLM framework for explainable image forgery detection and localization (IFDL). It introduces three core components: (1) MMTD-Set, a dataset of image-mask-description triplets generated by GPT-4o across Photoshop, DeepFake, and AIGC-Editing tampering types; (2) DTE-FDM, a domain-tag-guided detection and explanation module that uses a predicted tamper-type tag to mitigate domain conflicts; and (3) MFLM, a localization module that combines an LLM-based Tamper Comprehension Module with SAM for mask generation. Experiments show strong detection accuracy across all three tampering domains and competitive localization results on Photoshop and AIGC-Editing benchmarks.

## Strengths

- **First unified framework for explainable IFDL across three tampering types.** The paper is the first to combine detection, localization, and textual explanation under one M-LLM framework that handles Photoshop, DeepFake, and AIGC-Editing. This goes beyond prior M-LLM-based forensics work (e.g., huang2024ffaa) which targets only DeepFake and lacks localization.

- **Consistent state-of-the-art detection performance.** In Table 1, FakeShield achieves the highest ACC and F1 across all 14 detection columns (5 Photoshop subsets, DeepFake, AIGC-Editing). On AIGC-Editing it scores 0.98/0.99 vs. the next best CAT-Net at 0.82/0.81. Even the "w/o DTG" ablation (Table 4) outperforms most baselines on CASIA1+ (0.92 ACC) and DeepFake (0.89 ACC), indicating the base M-LLM pipeline is already strong.

- **Decoupled architecture validated by ablation.** The paper demonstrates through a controlled 25-epoch experiment (Figure 2) that separating detection/explanation (DTE-FDM) from localization (MFLM) consistently outperforms joint training. This is a clean empirical finding that justifies the design choice.

- **MMTD-Set with tamper-type-specific prompts.** The dataset construction methodology uses distinct prompts per tamper type to guide GPT-4o toward relevant artifacts (edge artifacts for Photoshop, blurring for DeepFake, texture anomalies for AIGC), which is a reasonable approach for bootstrapping training data for this task.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Circular explanation evaluation invalidates the explanation-quality claims.** The paper uses GPT-4o-generated descriptions as ground truth (line 218: "employ the tampered area descriptions generated by GPT-4o as ground truth") and measures CSS against them. Since FakeShield is fine-tuned on outputs from the same GPT-4o distribution, high CSS scores (0.8758 vs. GPT-4o zero-shot at 0.5183 on CASIA1+) simply demonstrate that the fine-tuned model reproduces its training distribution — not that the explanations are factually correct or useful. No human evaluation, no ground-truth tamper analysis, no assessment of factual precision of artifact mentions. The paper's claim of "explainable" IFDL rests on this unevaluated component.

- **Unfair detection comparison due to domain tag advantage.** FakeShield receives a predicted domain tag (Photoshop/DeepFake/AIGC-Editing) that strongly correlates with tampering characteristics, while none of the 7 baselines (SPAN, ManTraNet, HiFi-Net, PSCC-Net, CAT-Net, MVSS-Net, OSN) receive comparable information, even though they were "retrained on the MMTD-Set for consistency" (line 184). The ablation shows that removing DTG causes a 0.26 ACC drop on AIGC-Editing (0.98→0.72), confirming the tag conveys significant signal. The paper does not report: (a) accuracy of the domain tag classifier on test sets, (b) how often a wrong tag misleads the model, or (c) whether baselines could benefit from a similar tag. This makes the headline detection comparisons not an apples-to-apples test of forgery detection capability.

### Minor

- **Missing localization evaluation on DeepFake.** The localization comparison (Table 5) explicitly restricts to "MMTD-Set (Photoshop, AIGC-Editing)" (line 245), with no DeepFake localization results. Since DeepFake is one of the three core tampering types the paper claims to handle, and the DTG is designed to handle all three domains including DeepFake, this omission weakens the generalizability claim.

- **Domain tag classifier details are absent.** The DTG is described as a classifier trained with cross-entropy on three classes (line 140), but no accuracy, confusion matrix, or failure-case analysis is reported. Without this, it is unclear whether detection gains come from genuine domain conflict resolution or from the tag leaking ground-truth labels. The ablation (Table 4) shows a large drop on AIGC-Editing (0.98→0.72) and DeepFake (0.98→0.89), leaving open the question: is the drop due to domain conflict or just misclassification?

- **MFLM is a direct application of LISA to IFDL.** The localization module follows the LISA architecture (lai2024lisa) closely: an LLM encoder with a <SEG> token whose embedding prompts SAM. While applying this to forgery localization is reasonable, the technical novelty in this component is incremental. The paper would benefit from clearly distinguishing architectural adaptations specific to the forgery domain.

### Trivial

- **Robustness study is limited to a single dataset (CASIA1+).** Testing JPEG compression and Gaussian noise on only one benchmark provides a narrow view of robustness across the diverse tampering types the method claims to handle.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A small-scale human evaluation of explanation quality (e.g., expert raters scoring factual correctness and usefulness of explanations) would substantially strengthen the explainability claims.
- Controlled experiments where baselines receive a comparable domain signal (or where the DTG is removed and its impact on baselines is measured) would make the detection comparison fairer.

## Removed Points

- **"Suspicious CSS scores: FakeShield outperforms GPT-4o on its own ground truth"** — Removed because this is not suspicious; it is the expected behavior. A model fine-tuned on samples from a distribution will naturally have higher similarity to held-out samples from that same distribution than a zero-shot model. This point is already covered by the circular evaluation weakness above, and framing it as "suspicious" or evidence of "memorization" is misleading without additional evidence (e.g., showing that explanations are verbatim copies or factually incorrect).

- **"Modest localization improvements" (harsh critic)** — Removed because the improvements are in fact clear: +0.07 IoU on CASIA1+ (0.54 vs. 0.47), +0.12 on IMD2020 (0.50 vs. 0.38), +0.16 on DSO (0.48 vs. 0.32). These are not "modest" relative to the field; most IFDL methods improve in small increments. The tie on AIGC-Editing (0.18) is a specific data point, not the general pattern.

- **"Limited novelty: applying LISA to a new task"** — Moved here because the paper's contribution is not the localization architecture per se but the overall framework combining DTG-guided detection, explanation generation, and localization. The paper explicitly cites LISA. Novelty in a systems paper can come from the combination and task framing, not just individual components.

## Novel Insights

The most informative weakness-strength tension in this paper is the domain tag mechanism. The DTG ablation shows that AIGC-Editing detection drops from 0.98 to 0.72 without the tag — a much larger drop than Photoshop (0.95→0.92) or DeepFake (0.98→0.89). This asymmetry is revealing: it suggests that AIGC-Editing images are harder to distinguish from other tampering types via visual features alone, and the tag provides critical prior knowledge. This is a genuinely interesting finding about the nature of AIGC forgeries — they may be less visually distinctive than hand-crafted Photoshop edits or DeepFake facial modifications. The paper does not discuss this pattern, but it is a potentially valuable observation for future IFDL research.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a human evaluation of explanations** — even a small-scale study with 50-100 samples where forensic experts rate factual correctness and usefulness would salvage the explainability claim.
2. **Report DTG classifier accuracy** on each test set, with confusion analysis. Without this, the ablation results are ambiguous.
3. **Add DeepFake localization results** — this is essential for the three-domain generalizability claim.
4. **Run controlled detection experiments** where at least one strong baseline (e.g., MVSS-Net) is given a comparable domain signal, or show that the DTG advantage disappears when baselines are allowed the same information.
5. **Expand the robustness study** to include at least one dataset per tampering domain.
