## Summary

The paper proposes DER-Solomon, a large set of CVRPTW instances generated by fitting a Beta distribution to time-window half-widths extracted from the 56-instance Solomon benchmark (via MLE + RSS model selection), then sampling new time windows from this distribution while keeping spatial coordinates, demands, and service times from the original series. The authors validate distribution consistency by running three traditional solvers (GA, LKH3, OR-Tools) on both benchmarks and reporting ≤5% mean and ≤3% std gaps. They further show that DRL algorithms (AM, MDAM, POMO) trained on DER-Solomon achieve solutions within 10% of traditional methods on the Solomon benchmark with a ~1000× inference speedup.

## Strengths

- **Addresses a genuine practical need**: The Solomon benchmark's 56 instances are indeed too few for training DRL methods effectively, and existing "Solomon-like" generated benchmarks lack systematic validation. The paper's goal of producing a larger, distribution-matched benchmark is well-motivated.

- **Principled distribution-fitting methodology**: The paper fits 11 candidate distributions to time-window half-widths via maximum likelihood estimation and selects the best via RSS goodness-of-fit. For C101, the Beta distribution achieves RSS = 8.18e-4, outperforming all other forms (Section 3.4). This is more rigorous than the ad-hoc Gaussian estimates used by prior Solomon-like datasets.

- **Distribution consistency validated through algorithm performance**: Table 1 shows that GA, LKH3, and OR-Tools produce mean gaps ≤5% and standard-deviation gaps ≤3% between Solomon and DER-Solomon. This cross-validation via solver behavior provides meaningful (if indirect) evidence that the two benchmarks pose similar difficulty to algorithms.

- **Demonstrates DRL can approach traditional methods on Solomon instances**: Figure 5 shows DRL algorithms (AM, MDAM, POMO) trained on DER-Solomon achieve solutions within ~10% of traditional optima on the original Solomon benchmark, with Table 2 showing ~1000× inference speedup (e.g., AM: 51s vs. LKH: 44,228s on 1024 instances). This is the paper's strongest empirical result.

- **Broad coverage of algorithms**: The paper includes three traditional solvers (GA, LKH3, OR-Tools) and four DRL methods (MARDAM, AM, MDAM, POMO), providing a reasonably thorough evaluation.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Generation procedure is incompletely specified, hampering reproducibility**. The paper derives a distribution for time-window *half-widths* (Section 3.3–3.4), but a complete time window requires both a *center* and a *width*. The paper explains how centers were generated in the *original* Solomon benchmark (line 130: uniformly random for R/RC; from a 3-opt CVRP solution for C series), but it never states how centers are determined when generating DER-Solomon instances. This matters because:
   - For C series: if centers are taken verbatim from the original Solomon instances, every DER-Solomon instance in a series uses the *same* centers, producing highly correlated instances.
   - For R/RC series: are centers freshly sampled from the uniform range, and if so, with what exact parameters?
   Without this information, the generation process cannot be independently reproduced, which undermines the paper's central contribution as a benchmark resource.

2. **Distribution fitting is demonstrated on only one instance (C101)**. Section 3.4 shows the full fitting pipeline (MLE + RSS) for C101 and concludes Beta is the best fit. However, the Solomon benchmark has six series (C1, C2, R1, R2, RC1, RC2) with markedly different time-window characteristics (clustered vs. random vs. mixed; narrow vs. wide windows; 25%–100% density). The paper provides **no per-series fitting results** — no RSS tables, no parameter estimates, no evidence that Beta is universally the best fit. The claim that the fitted distribution generalizes across all series is therefore unsubstantiated.

3. **The validation of distribution consistency is indirect and lacks statistical rigor**. Table 1 compares *algorithm performance* (mean and std of solution costs) rather than comparing the *instance distributions* directly. While this is a reasonable heuristic, different instance distributions can yield similar aggregate algorithm performance. No direct distributional comparison (e.g., KS test on time-window half-widths, comparison of spatial or temporal features) is performed. Additionally, no confidence intervals or statistical tests are reported for the gaps in Table 1, so it is unclear whether the ≤5% gaps are within expected noise from single runs.

4. **Ambiguous training setup in Section 4.2**. The paper states: "We compare the performance on the Solomon benchmark of the algorithm training with the **original data** and training with DER-Solomon benchmark" (line 174). It is unclear what "original data" refers to. If it means the original 56 Solomon instances, then training MARDAM on those instances and testing on the same 56 instances constitutes test-set contamination, invalidating the comparison. The paper must clarify this — either by defining "original data" precisely or by restructuring the experiment to use a proper train/test split.

### Minor

- **No per-series breakdown in Table 1**: Aggregate mean gaps of ≤5% and std gaps of ≤3% could mask large disparities within individual series (e.g., C1 vs. R2). A per-series breakdown would strengthen confidence in distribution matching.
- **"Backward derivation" is standard statistical practice**: The method (assume candidate distributions → MLE → RSS selection) is a routine application of MLE and goodness-of-fit testing. The paper's novelty lies in applying it to this specific benchmark and generating the dataset, not in developing a new methodology. The framing as a novel "backward derivation" method (Section 2) overclaims.
- **DRL training cost not factored into speed comparison**: Table 2 reports inference time only (AM: 51s vs. LKH: 44,228s), omitting the hours/days of GPU training required to obtain the DRL models. This is standard practice in the NCO literature, but a practical comparison should acknowledge this trade-off.
- **RSS bin width not specified**: The RSS goodness-of-fit is computed on histogram bin frequencies (line 145), but the bin width is not stated. Different binning choices can yield different RSS rankings.
- **Density handling not discussed for DER-Solomon**: The paper describes how the original Solomon benchmark handles time-window density (25%–100% in Section 3.2) but does not explain how DER-Solomon handles this dimension — are instances generated at all four density levels?

### Trivial
- Figure axis labels are not readable (images).
- Table 1 and Table 2 are embedded as images, making precise values hard to extract.

2. **Report per-series distribution fitting**: Show RSS values and best-fitting distributions for all six series (C1, C2, R1, R2, RC1, RC2), not just C101.
3. **Clarify the Section 4.2 experiment**: Define "original data" explicitly. If it refers to the Solomon test instances, restructure the experiment using a proper train/test separation.
4. **Add direct distributional validation**: Include a statistical comparison (e.g., KS test, feature distribution plots) of time-window half-widths and other instance features between Solomon and DER-Solomon.
5. **Report per-series results in Table 1**: Show the breakdown by series to confirm consistency does not degrade in specific series.
