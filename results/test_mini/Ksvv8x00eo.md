## Summary

This paper introduces CaTS-Bench, the first large-scale multimodal benchmark for context-aware time series captioning (TSC). It draws from 11 real-world datasets (570k timestamps, 20k samples), pairing each numeric series with rich metadata, a line-plot image, and a validated caption. Most reference captions are generated by a Gemini 2.0 Flash oracle with extensive quality checks (manual validation of 72.5% of test captions at 98.6% factual accuracy, human-detectability study at 41.1%, diversity analysis), supplemented by a human-revisited subset of 579 captions. Beyond captioning, the benchmark includes 460 multiple-choice Q&A questions across four reasoning tasks. The paper evaluates a wide range of proprietary and open-source VLMs in both zero-shot and finetuned settings, finding that finetuning substantially improves performance while most models under-utilize visual inputs and perform near-random on plot matching.

## Strengths

1. **First multimodal benchmark unifying numeric series, metadata, visual plots, and expressive captions for TSC.** Table 1 shows that all prior TSC benchmarks (TADACap, TRUCE, TACO) lack at least two of these modalities. CaTS-Bench fills a genuine gap by providing a testbed that reflects the multimodal nature of real-world time series understanding.

2. **Scalable captioning pipeline with multi-faceted quality verification.** The paper reports manual validation of ~2.9k captions (72.5% of test set) achieving >98.6% factual accuracy, a blind human detectability study with only 41.1% accuracy (near chance), and embedding-based diversity analysis showing only 2.3% near-duplicate caption pairs. These checks provide concrete evidence that the synthetic captions are factual, human-like, and diverse.

3. **Human-revisited subset providing high-fidelity evaluation alongside the larger benchmark.** A curated set of 579 test captions from four domains, refined from multiple LLM candidates by human editors to eliminate inaccuracies and redundancy, strengthens the reliability of reported results and enables cross-reference with the larger synthetic set.

4. **Tailored evaluation metrics for numeric fidelity.** The Numeric Score (weighted accuracy/recall for extracted numbers, favoring recall) and Statistical Inference Accuracy (penalizing only hallucinated statistics, not omitted ones) are well-motivated for TSC and go beyond generic N-gram overlap metrics.

5. **Robustness checks confirming evaluation stability.** Triple-inference variance is vanishingly small (often 10⁻⁶), and rankings are consistent when ground truth captions are paraphrased (mean Spearman 0.9266), demonstrating that reported results are not brittle to stochasticity or stylistic variation.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Visual modality claim is confounded by redundant text input.** The ablation in Section 4.3 removes the plot while keeping the exact numeric values in text (e.g., `[25.3, 26.1, 26.8, ...]`). The conclusion that models "fail to effectively leverage visual cues" is overstated: a rational model should prefer precise numeric text over approximate visual patterns. The paper would need a condition where numeric values are withheld and the model must rely on the plot alone to cleanly test visual reasoning. The plot matching Q&A task does provide complementary evidence of visual grounding difficulty (models perform near-random while humans are near-perfect), partially mitigating this concern, but the core claim about visual under-utilization in the captioning task should be tempered.

2. **The primary test set relies on a single LLM (Gemini 2.0 Flash) as the reference oracle.** While the paper provides extensive validation (factual accuracy, human detectability, diversity), the reference captions for ~86% of the test set (all but the 579 human-revisited captions) are generated by one LLM. This creates a risk that models performing well on the benchmark may reflect alignment with Gemini's captioning style rather than genuine time series understanding. The paraphrasing robustness check (Spearman 0.9266) shows ranking stability but does not address whether the *content* of captions has systematic bias — a preference for certain statistical descriptors or narrative structures. The human-revisited subset is too small (579 samples, 14.5% of test, from only 4 of 11 domains) to serve as the primary validation benchmark. The paper would be substantially stronger by expanding this subset or by using multiple diverse oracles to generate references with independent verification.

### Minor

3. **Q&A filtering methodology and limited sample size.** The 460-question suite is filtered by removing questions that Qwen 2.5 Omni answers correctly, which introduces a model-specific definition of "difficulty." While Appendix J.2 checks for non-Qwen-specific artifacts, the final test set is small (100 questions per matching task, 40 per comparison task), making individual sub-task scores subject to noise. The paper reports no confidence intervals or significance tests for these results. This does not threaten the main benchmark (captioning) but limits the diagnostic Q&A as a standalone instrument.

4. **Numeric Score weight choice (λ_A=0.3, λ_R=0.7) is not justified with sensitivity analysis.** The weights favor recall over precision, which is defensible, but the paper does not show how results change under different weightings or provide a principled justification tied to a specific use case.

### Trivial

5. **The paper would benefit from reporting per-domain entity counts** (number of distinct entities per domain) to help assess whether benchmark diversity is driven by many entities or few, and whether finetuning could overfit to domain-specific patterns.

6. **Minor:** Section 2 frames TACO's "reliance on templates" as limiting, while CaTS-Bench also uses a fixed prompt template for the oracle. The paper analyzes diversity (Appendix H.4) but the framing slightly overstates the contrast.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Testing a plot-only condition (removing numeric values from the prompt) to cleanly assess visual reasoning in the captioning task.
- Expanding the human-revisited subset substantially (e.g., to ~2,000 samples across all 11 domains) and reporting sample-level correlations with the synthetic set.
- Testing cross-domain generalization (finetune on 8 domains, test on held-out domains) to complement the temporal split.
- Evaluating PAL (program-aided) models in finetuned settings, not just zero-shot.
- Direct comparison of benchmarks built from different oracle LLMs to measure oracle-induced variance in absolute scores.

- **Complaint about finetuning details being in Appendix D:** The paper provides these details; placing them in the appendix is standard practice for the venue.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The two most notable findings — that finetuning bridges the gap between open-source and proprietary VLMs on TSC, and that models heavily default to textual priors over visual plot information — are clearly stated in the paper. The reviews do not surface additional interpretations that the paper itself does not already articulate.

## Suggestions

1. **Acknowledge the visual ablation confound explicitly** in the main text. Add a condition that removes numeric text from the prompt to test genuine visual reasoning, or soften the claim to "models default to textual priors when precise numeric values are available."
2. **Expand the human-revisited subset** to cover all 11 domains (even at ~150 samples per domain) and report per-sample correlation between synthetic and human-revisited scores.
3. **Increase Q&A sample size** (at least 200 per task) and report confidence intervals. Consider replacing the Qwen-based filter with a multi-model ensemble filter or difficulty rating.
4. **Add sensitivity analysis** for the Numeric Score weighting and the 5% tolerance parameter.
