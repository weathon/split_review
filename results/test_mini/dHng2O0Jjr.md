## Weaknesses

### Major

- **ToolEval circularity and lack of independent evaluation**: ToolEval is built from ChatGPT (*gpt-3.5-turbo-16k*, lines 93, 203), which is the same model that generated the training data and served as the "teacher" for DFSDT-based annotation. While the paper reports 87.1%/80.3% human agreement, this validates the metric but does not rule out systematic evaluator bias favoring outputs that resemble ChatGPT's reasoning patterns. The central claim that ToolLLaMA matches ChatGPT rests entirely on an evaluator derived from ChatGPT itself. An independent evaluation (e.g., using a non-ChatGPT evaluator, GPT-4 with different criteria, or a human evaluation on a representative sample of 100-200 instructions comparing the two models directly) would substantially strengthen the core claim. The concern is amplified by the fact that multiple metrics (pass rate, win rate) rely on ChatGPT's judgment of success rather than objective criteria.

### Minor

- **OOD generalization claims are overstated for APIBench**: The paper claims "remarkable OOD generalization performance" and that ToolLLaMA+retriever "outperforms Gorilla+BM25." However, in Table 5, ToolLLaMA+Our Retriever achieves AST of 16.77% vs Gorilla-RS+BM25's 15.71% on HuggingFace (a +1.06% difference), 51.16% vs 50.00% on TorchHub (+1.16%), and actually *underperforms* on TensorHub (40.59% vs 41.90%). The oracle-retriever results (88-89% AST) are indeed strong but measure formatting ability rather than practical generalization.

- **No error bars or statistical significance**: All main results (pass rate, win rate, NDCG) are reported as point estimates without confidence intervals, bootstrap estimates, or significance tests. Many comparisons differ by only 1-4 percentage points (e.g., ChatGPT+DFSDT 64.8% vs ToolLLaMA+DFSDT 66.7%), and it is unclear whether these differences are meaningful. This is a presentation weakness common in LLM evaluation papers but worth noting.

- **DFSDT training data bias partially confounds inference comparisons**: The training data was generated using ChatGPT+DFSDT, meaning only instructions that DFSDT could solve were retained. This likely over-represents instruction types amenable to DFSDT's search strategy. While the paper does evaluate ToolLLaMA+ReACT (29.0%, Table 4), allowing partial disentanglement of training data vs. inference strategy, the training data itself may still bias toward DFSDT-favorable instructions. A controlled experiment where ReACT-generated trajectories are also used for training would strengthen the claim that DFSDT is a superior general reasoning method.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting pattern across the reviews is the tension between the paper's clearly substantial dataset contribution and the fragility of its evaluation framework. ToolBench is genuinely large and realistic — 16,464 APIs with 469K real calls — and the DFSDT improvement over ReACT on ChatGPT (63.8% vs 35.3%) is large and consistent. Yet the paper's headline claim ("comparable to ChatGPT") rests on an evaluator that is itself ChatGPT, creating a circularity that even high human correlation (87.1%/80.3%) doesn't fully resolve. Notably, ToolLLaMA+DFSDT *outperforms* ChatGPT+DFSDT (66.7% vs 64.8%) on this evaluator, which if anything argues against a simple self-preference bias — it's not that ChatGPT evaluator favors ChatGPT outputs. The more subtle concern is whether ToolEval's criteria for "pass" and "win" align with genuine task success in an unbiased way. The practical implication is that the paper's contributions (the dataset, DFSDT, the retriever) are stronger than the evaluation methodology used to assess them; future work building on ToolBench should invest in more rigorous, independent evaluation.
