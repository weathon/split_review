## Summary

This paper proposes VChangeCodec, a neural speech codec that integrates a voice changer directly into its encoding module, enabling seamless switching between original and customized timbre modes. The architecture uses scalar quantization (SQ) instead of RVQ for efficiency, and a lightweight causal projection network (Converter) for token-level timbre adaptation. The model achieves 0.231M parameters (70× reduction vs. DAC), strong objective quality scores (POLQA >4.0 at low bitrates), and streaming capability. The integrated VC mode is trained on pseudo-parallel data generated by an RVC system and evaluated against SOTA VC methods.

## Strengths

- **Novel integration of voice changer into the codec itself.** Figure 1 clearly contrasts the proposed unified pipeline (40ms) with cascaded VC+codec systems (e.g., AC-VC at 107.5ms). This architectural contribution — inserting a lightweight converter between encoder and decoder — is a genuine conceptual advance over pre-processing-based approaches.

- **Extremely lightweight codec with competitive objective quality.** At 0.231M total parameters (Table 1), VChangeCodec achieves a 70× reduction versus DAC (16.8M) while maintaining POLQA >4.0, outperforming OPUS, EVS, Lyra2, and EnCodec at comparable bitrates. This parameter efficiency is well-documented and independently meaningful.

- **Lightweight, streaming-capable Converter design.** The converter uses three grouped residual units with dilated causal convolutions and adds negligible compute overhead. The RTF of 0.053 on an Apple M1 Pro (Table 6) and 2ms processing per 20ms chunk on iPhone X demonstrate genuine deployability.

- **Operator-oriented design with explicit privacy safeguards.** Section 6 details a deployment model where encoder/decoder are immutable, target timbres are pre-defined, and a notification label is recommended — a practical and often-overlooked consideration for real-world VC deployments.

- **Comprehensive retraining experiments for VC comparison.** Table 4 retrains three SOTA VC methods (VQMIVC, QuickVC, DDDM-VC) on the same target timbre data and shows VChangeCodec still outperforms them, providing some controlled evidence beyond the primary comparison.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **40ms latency claim is not adequately supported.** The paper states "the encoder, converter and decoder" take 2ms per 20ms chunk on iPhone X (Section 3.2), then asserts "end-to-end latency ... is thus 40+ ms." The derivation of 40ms from these numbers is unclear — the gap between 2ms compute per chunk and 40ms end-to-end appears to come from algorithmic delay (framing, lookahead), but no breakdown is provided. More critically, the latency of openSMILE metadata extraction is never measured or even acknowledged in the latency computation; it is listed as part of the input pipeline but omitted from the 2ms profiling. Finally, the paper compares its 40ms claim (on iPhone X) against StreamVC's 124.3ms (on A100 GPU), which is not a controlled comparison. A proper latency breakdown and a same-hardware baseline comparison are needed.

- **The VC evaluation pipeline uses RVC-generated pseudo-parallel training data without adequate isolation.** The converter is trained to minimize token distance to the encoder output of RVC-converted speech. This means the model learns to reproduce the behavior of a separate VC system, not to perform voice conversion from first principles. While using teacher models to generate training data is common, the paper provides no ablation that measures how much of the reported VC performance (e.g., speaker similarity of 95.62% in Table 2) is attributable to genuine timbre adaptation versus convergence to the RVC output distribution. The retraining experiments (Table 4) partially mitigate this concern, but do not fully isolate it. A cleaner evaluation on true parallel data or a zero-shot setting without RVC pre-processing would substantially strengthen the VC claims.

- **Ablation study is narrow and omits key architectural choices.** The ablation (Table 5) only varies metadata presence, converter dimension, commitment loss weight, and encoder freezing. It does not ablate: (a) scalar quantization vs. RVQ — a central architectural choice that differentiates this work from most neural codecs but is never experimentally compared; (b) different metadata sources — openSMILE eGeMAPSv02 features are used but never compared against pre-trained speaker embeddings (the stated reason "computational costs" is not backed by measurements); (c) the impact of the RVC-based data generation pipeline itself. These gaps leave important design decisions unsubstantiated.

### Minor

- **Bitrate-matched comparison in Table 1 is not fully controlled.** The table compares VChangeCodec at 4.8–9.6 kbps against OPUS at 6 kbps, EVS at 9.6 kbps, EnCodec at 3–24 kbps, Lyra2 at 8 kbps, and DAC. The bitrates are not always aligned, and the comparison with DAC focuses on parameter count (70× reduction) despite DAC operating at a higher bitrate. A side-by-side comparison at matched bitrates across all methods would strengthen the codec quality claims.

- **The token commitment loss equation (Eq. 3) notation is confusing.** It writes $\|\hat{z}(x) - C(\hat{z}(\hat{x}))\|_{L_2}$ where $\hat{z}(x)$ is called "source" and $\hat{z}(\hat{x})$ "target," but the text and the variable naming ($\hat{x}$ for target) are reversed relative to expectations. The paper would benefit from clarifying which term comes from which branch.

- **Only two target timbres (one male, one female) are used for VC evaluation.** This is a small set for claiming general-purpose VC capability, though the paper is explicitly positioned as operator-oriented (pre-defined target timbres), which partially justifies this.

### Trivial

- Table 1 caption mentions "Underline: Optimal performance at low/medium bitrates is underlined" but the table does not appear to use underlining in the rendered text (likely a formatting issue).

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation emerging from the cross-review analysis is that the paper's core strength — the integrated codec+VC architecture — and its core weakness — the RVC-based training pipeline — are deeply linked. The integration is innovative precisely because it embeds VC at the token level inside the codec bottleneck, but this very design forces reliance on pseudo-parallel data (since token-level supervision requires aligned source/target token pairs). This tension suggests that the paper's approach would be significantly more impactful if paired with a truly zero-shot VC objective that does not require parallel token targets, or if the RVC-generated data were replaced with a generative data augmentation pipeline that could be independently verified.

## Suggestions

1. Provide a clear latency breakdown: algorithmic delay from framing, openSMILE extraction time, and neural compute time. Re-benchmark at least one streaming VC baseline on identical hardware (iPhone X or M1 Pro).
2. Add an ablation comparing SQ and RVQ on codec quality (POLQA/ViSQOL) and VC quality (speaker similarity, MCD) to justify the core quantization choice.
3. Either train the converter on true parallel data, evaluate in a zero-shot setting, or add an experiment that compares converter outputs against RVC outputs to bound the RVC contamination effect.
4. Include a controlled bitrate-matched comparison table with all codecs at identical bitrates.
5. Clarify Eq. (3) notation to avoid confusion about which term is source vs. target.
