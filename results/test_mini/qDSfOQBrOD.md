## Summary

The paper proposes VChangeCodec, a neural speech codec with an integrated voice changer that operates by inserting a lightweight causal projection network (Converter) between the encoder and quantizer, enabling timbre adaptation at the token level. The key architectural insight is that switching between original and customized voice modes happens entirely at the encoder side without modifying the decoder, achieving 40 ms latency and <1M parameters on smartphone CPU.

## Strengths

- **Genuinely novel integrated architecture**: Embedding a voice changer inside a speech codec so that timbre adaptation occurs at the token level in the encoder (without modifying the decoder or adding end-to-end latency) is a well-motivated, practically grounded architectural contribution (Section 3.2, Figure 1). The switch between original and customized voice modes is designed into the pipeline rather than bolted on as a pre-processor.

- **Impressive efficiency demonstrated concretely**: The system achieves 40 ms latency on a single CPU core of an iPhone X (Section 3.2), uses only 0.65M parameters — a 70× reduction versus DAC (46.9M) — and has an RTF of 0.01 on M1 Pro, faster than Lyra2 at 0.02 (Table 6, Section 4.4). These numbers are specific, well-measured, and support the RTC deployment claim.

- **Well-designed ablation study**: Table 5 systematically isolates the contributions of metadata, converter dimension, token commitment loss, and encoder freezing. The commitment loss improves speaker similarity from 88.58% to 90.06% and reduces MCD from 6.22 to 5.76. The finding that retraining the encoder hurts performance is non-obvious and practically useful.

- **Competitive subjective evaluation**: In N-MOS and S-MOS listening tests (Table 3), the method achieves the highest speaker similarity (S-MOS) among all compared systems and competitive naturalness (N-MOS). The retraining experiment (Table 4) shows that even when baselines are retrained on the same target timbre data, VChangeCodec maintains an advantage, particularly in MCD and speaker similarity.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Intelligibility is critically poor for the stated application (WER 26.3%)**: In Table 2, VChangeCodec's word error rate of 26.3% far exceeds QuickVC (11.8%) and FACodec (15.4%). The paper acknowledges this is due to the lack of text supervision (Section 4.3) but does not treat it as a serious limitation. For the claimed use case of "online conferencing" and "voice calls" (Abstract, Section 1), a voice changer that distorts roughly one in four words is not practically usable, regardless of how good the timbre similarity is. This is the single most important weakness in the paper: the method's central application claim is not supported by the evidence.

- **Voice changer training depends on an external VC system (RVC) for parallel data**: The converter is trained using near-parallel data generated by the open-source Retrieval-based-Voice-Conversion (RVC) tool (Section 3.3). This means the converter learns to match the output of a pre-existing strong VC system rather than performing timbre adaptation from first principles. While this is a form of knowledge distillation (a common technique), the paper presents it as an "integrated solution" — but the integration is only at inference time. During training, the method cannot function without an external VC pipeline. The paper should clearly reframe this as a distillation approach and discuss what performance would be achievable without this privileged training data.

- **Codec quality comparison with DAC is unsubstantiated in the main table**: Section 4.2 states that "the comparison with DAC proves that our VChangeCodec achieves similar performance with much lower parameters (70x reduction)," but DAC results do not appear in Table 1 — the paper only reports parameter counts. A subjective comparison with DAC is mentioned in the appendix (Table 9, visible to reviewers), but the lack of objective metrics (POLQA, ViSQOL, STOI) for DAC in the main table means the claim of "comparable quality" is not properly supported by the data presented in the main paper.

### Minor

- **No comparison against streaming VC baselines**: The Related Work section (Section 2) mentions StreamVoice (124.3ms on A100) and StreamVC (SoundStream + HuBERT) as directly relevant streaming VC systems, but the experiments compare only against non-streaming one-shot VC methods (Diff-VC, VQMIVC, QuickVC, DDDM-VC, FACodec). Since the paper's main advantage over cascaded approaches is latency, including at least one streaming VC baseline in the latency comparison would substantially strengthen the case.

- **Codec results that appear implausibly strong are not explained**: VChangeCodec at 9.6 kbps outperforms EnCodec at 24 kbps on POLQA, ViSQOL, and STOI (Table 1). While this is possible (VChangeCodec is speech-specific while EnCodec is general-purpose), the paper provides no analysis or ablation explaining why a 2.5× bitrate reduction yields better objective scores. This undercuts confidence and invites skepticism.

- **No error bars or significance tests**: None of the tables report confidence intervals, standard deviations, or statistical significance. Given the small test sets (68 utterances for codec evaluation, 42 utterances for VC), the stability of the reported numbers is unclear.

- **Subjective VC evaluation uses only two target timbres**: The N-MOS and S-MOS tests use one male and one female target speaker (Section 4.1). While subjective tests are expensive, the limited target set makes it unclear how well the method generalizes across diverse target voices.

### Trivial

- The paper says "40+ ms" for latency (Section 3.2) but the abstract and contributions claim "just 40 ms." The breakdown of this 40ms into algorithmic delay vs. compute is not provided.
- The equation for token commitment loss (Eq. 3) uses notation $\hat{z}(x)$ and $\hat{z}(\hat{x})$ that is confusingly similar; the distinction between source and target paths could be clarified.
- DNSMOS is used for VC naturalness evaluation but was designed for denoising, not specifically for VC artifacts (the paper does include subjective N-MOS to complement this).

## Nice-to-Haves

- Including a content-preservation loss (e.g., CTC or ASR embedding similarity) to bring the WER below 15% would dramatically strengthen the RTC applicability claim.
- A direct comparison between scalar quantization (SQ) and residual vector quantization (RVQ) at matched bitrates in the VC setting would clarify whether SQ introduces any degradation.
- The latency breakdown into per-module costs (encoder, SQ, converter, decoder) would help practitioners understand where the 40ms is spent.

## Removed Points

- Criticisms about "formatting issues," "typos," or "grammar" — these are parser artifacts, not author errors (per hard rules).
- The claim that the converter assumes "the token space is linearly related to timbre" — this is an overly strong characterization of an L2 token commitment loss, which is a standard training objective without that assumption being necessary.
- The criticism about openSMILE features vs. learned speaker embeddings — the paper's computational cost justification is reasonable for the stated efficiency goal.
- The claim about Figure 1's motivation not quantifying a "modern cascaded system" — the paper does quantify AC-VC + LPCNet + codec at 107.5ms, which is sufficient for illustrating the motivation.
- "Operator-oriented design" listed as a strength by the Strength Finder — this is an application framing, not a technical contribution.
- Strength Finder claims about "SQ reduces complexity without quality loss" — this is not directly benchmarked against RVQ in the paper, so weakened.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting finding from the ablation study is that retraining the encoder jointly with the converter *hurts* performance across all metrics (speaker similarity drops from 90.06% to 88.98%, WER increases from 26.28% to 27.33%). This suggests the pre-trained encoder's token space is already well-structured for the converter to learn a timbre transformation, and that fine-tuning the encoder may disrupt the token-level structure the converter relies on. This insight has practical value for future work on integrating modules into frozen codec backbones.

## Suggestions

1. Add a content-preservation mechanism (e.g., CTC loss, ASR feature matching) to reduce the WER from 26.3% to at most ~15%. Without addressing this, the paper's RTC claims are not credible.
2. Reframe the RVC dependency explicitly as knowledge distillation rather than claiming the converter is a fully "integrated" solution that learns timbre adaptation from scratch. Report an ablation training the converter without RVC-generated parallel data.
3. Include DAC objective metrics (POLQA, ViSQOL, STOI) in Table 1, or at minimum explain why they are omitted. Error bars on all tables would also help.
4. Compare latency and quality against at least one streaming VC system (StreamVoice or StreamVC) to substantiate the claimed advantage in the RTC setting.
