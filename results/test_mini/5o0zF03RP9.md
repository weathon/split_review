## Summary

The paper proposes IncentRL, a framework that augments standard RL with a KL divergence term between predicted outcomes p(o|s,a) and preferred outcomes q(o|s), forming an internal incentive signal. The headline contribution is treating the incentive weight β as a Bayesian random variable that adapts online, eliminating manual tuning. Experiments are conducted on a toy 2-state MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8.

## Strengths

- **KL-based incentive shaping produces measurable gains in sparse-reward settings.** The MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8 results (Section 5.4) show that a mild incentive (β=0.01) improves final success rate from 90.5% to 98% and reduces mean episode length from 75 to 29 steps compared to the β=0 baseline. The toy MDP (Table 1) similarly shows 69–74% improvement with reduced variance for all β>0 over 7 seeds. These results provide positive evidence that the shaped objective can accelerate learning.

- **The framework is conceptually clean and algorithm-agnostic.** The core idea of augmenting rewards with KL(p∥q) is straightforward to state (Section 3.2) and the algorithm description (Section 5.1) makes clear that it can wrap any base RL algorithm (Q-learning, DQN, policy gradient). The latent-space extension (Section 4.3) is a forward-looking design note.

- **Clear articulation of cognitive motivations.** The paper draws well-motivated connections to dopamine reward prediction error and the Free Energy Principle (Sections 2.3, 2.4, 6.3) while clearly distinguishing IncentRL from active inference (fixed preferences vs. adaptive beliefs).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **The central claimed novelty — Bayesian β adaptation — is never defined as a working procedure.** The paper describes Bayesian β adaptation as its headline contribution ("The central novelty is treating the incentive weight β as a Bayesian random variable, updated online" — Abstract), but the actual mechanism is completely unspecified. No prior distribution over β is stated. No likelihood function is given. No posterior update rule or inference algorithm is described. Appendix A and Figures 3–4 show posterior statistics and concentration plots, but the procedures that produced them are a black box. The algorithm description in Section 5.1 lists five steps for IncentRL but none involve any Bayesian update. The text in Section 5.4 says "we tracked the posterior evolution of β using our Bayesian adaptation scheme" without defining what that scheme is. The paper claims this adaptation "eliminates manual trade-off tuning" but does not provide the reader with a verifiable, reproducible method. This is not a missing ablation or a minor omission — it is the absence of the paper's core methodological contribution.

- **The predictive model p(o|s,a) is not specified for continuous environments.** In the toy MDP, p(o|s,a) is the known transition distribution (which is essentially cheating since a real agent would not know this). In MountainCar (continuous state space, discrete actions) and MiniGrid, the paper never explains how p(o|s,a) is instantiated, learned, or computed. No architecture, loss function, training procedure, or model class is described. The algorithm in Section 5.1 says "Predict Outcomes: Estimate p(o|s,a)" but provides no mechanism. Section 4.3 mentions modeling p and q as Gaussians "via variational encoders or diffusion priors" but this remains a high-level remark, not an implementation. Without this specification, the experimental results cannot be reproduced with confidence, and it is unclear whether the reported KL values correspond to what the authors claim.

- **No competitive baselines from the intrinsic motivation literature.** IncentRL is compared only against its own β=0 baseline in every experiment. There is no comparison to any existing intrinsic motivation method (curiosity [Pathak et al.], RND [Burda et al.], ICM, empowerment, count-based exploration) or to any alternative adaptive-β scheme (meta-gradient, PBT, simple annealing). Given that the paper explicitly situates itself relative to this literature and criticizes prior methods for requiring manual tuning, direct empirical comparison is essential to substantiate any claim of advantage. Without it, the experiments merely show that reward shaping can help — a point established since Ng et al. (1999).

- **Narrow and statistically weak empirical evaluation.** The experiments span three environments, with only 3 seeds for MountainCar and MiniGrid. The MountainCar results (Table 2) show high variance (e.g., β=0.3: mean 1654.67 ± 908.94) and degradation for 2 of 3 β values. The MiniGrid experiment tests only a single β=0.01 against β=0. The Bayesian β adaptation is tracked as side analysis but never actually tested as an online replacement for fixed β within the RL training loop — there is no learning curve comparing Bayesian β to the best fixed β. No confidence intervals, effect sizes, or significance tests are reported. The evidence base is too thin to support the paper's broad claims about general-purpose utility.

- **The theoretical analysis is trivial.** Proposition 1 (small β preserves optimality) and Proposition 2 (large β ignores rewards) are immediate from the definition of the shaped objective and require no substantive analysis. They provide no insight beyond what a reader would infer from the form of the objective function. The "Limit behavior" section (4.2) is descriptive rather than analytical. The paper promises theoretical grounding as a contribution, but the actual content does not constitute one.

### Minor

- The paper states that in MountainCar, q(o|s) "assigns all probability to the goal (x ≥ 0.5)" (Section 5.3) — a degenerate point-mass over a continuous region. How a KL divergence is computed against this distribution (which has infinite density at any point in the goal set) is not explained. This may be a tractable computation under certain parameterizations (e.g., Gaussian q with small variance centered at goal states) but is not clarified.

- The Bayesian adaptation results (Figures 3, 4, Table 3) appear to come from a separate multi-round meta-tuning process, not from single-run online adaptation within the RL training loop. The paper says "across rounds" and describes posterior concentration over rounds, but the relationship between these "rounds" and online training is never explained. This undermines the claim of "online" adaptation.

- The Bayesian posterior statistics (Table 3) show the mean converging to ~0.017 by Round 4, but the figure captions state the target effective β is 0.1. The MiniGrid best fixed β was 0.01. This inconsistency is confusing and suggests a scaling issue or labeling error.
