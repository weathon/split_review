## Summary

This paper frames lead-lag detection in financial markets as a temporal link prediction problem on dynamic graphs, where assets are nodes and directed edges represent one asset's return preceding another's. The authors construct a custom dataset of 37 assets (29 stocks + 8 commodities) over 5 years, adapt eight models (from LSTM to six TGNNs plus a novel GM-TNF variant), and benchmark them on predicting these threshold-defined lead-lag edges. GraphMixer (GM) achieves the best results (AP 0.79, MRR 0.47), significantly outperforming the LSTM baseline (AP 0.51), and statistical tests confirm the rankings are robust.

## Strengths

- **Novel problem formulation with clean formalization.** Casting lead-lag detection as temporal link prediction on dynamic graphs (Section 3.1, Equation 1) is a genuinely new perspective that makes the problem accessible to the TGNN toolkit. The formal distinction between lead-lag *relationships* and *effects*, and the explicit evaluation under two scenarios (positive-only and mixed-sign), addresses genuine ambiguity in the literature.

- **Comprehensive, well-executed TGNN benchmark.** Eight models are adapted, trained, and tested under a unified framework (TGL) with consistent hyperparameter tuning. Tables 1 and 2 show results across six metrics with standard deviations over 5 runs. Figure 2 provides rigorous statistical significance testing (Friedman + Conover). This provides a reproducible comparison of TGNNs on a novel financial task — prior work (Li et al., 2024) only considered static graphs.

- **Clear evidence that graph structure matters for this formulation.** Every TGNN substantially outperforms the sequential LSTM baseline (e.g., GM AP 0.79 vs. LSTM AP 0.51 in Table 1). The gap is large and consistent across both scenarios, convincingly showing that relational information is essential for this formulation of lead-lag detection.

- **Ablation study with non-trivial findings.** Table 3 reveals that adding price/financial features degrades performance for most models, while GM improves with full features. This nuanced result provides useful guidance for feature engineering in financial graph learning and shows the task is not trivially solvable by static description embeddings alone (GM does benefit from temporal features).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None. The paper's core claim — that TGNNs can effectively model the proposed threshold-based lead-lag formulation — is supported by the experimental results. The label validity concern (see below) is significant but not fatal because the paper is transparent about its formulation and the empirical comparison among models remains valid on its own terms.

### Major

- **The label construction (5% daily return threshold) is not validated against any external or economic criterion.** Labels are generated by checking whether two assets both move ≥5% on consecutive days (Equation 1). The paper cites Li et al. (2022) for robustness of ε and Sheth et al. (2023) for the 5% choice, but Li et al. uses a fundamentally different formulation (sign-agreement aggregated over time, not a hard daily threshold). The paper does not demonstrate that the resulting edges correspond to economically meaningful lead-lag patterns — e.g., that the leader's return Granger-causes the follower's, or that the predicted pairs match known sector relationships (oil → airlines, semiconductors → hardware). Since the models are trained and evaluated exclusively on these synthetic labels, the reader cannot assess whether the framework captures *real* lead-lag phenomena or merely a threshold-based artifact. This substantially weakens the claimed practical relevance for "informing trading strategies and risk management."

- **No comparison or validation against any traditional lead-lag detection method.** The paper states that direct comparison with Granger causality, cross-correlation, or LASSO-based methods is "precluded" due to the different formulation (Section 3.1). While this defense has some merit, the absence of even a simple sanity check — e.g., computing pairwise Granger causality on the same data and reporting overlap with the model's top predictions — leaves a critical gap. Without this, the paper cannot substantiate its central claim that the TGNN approach yields *better* detection than established methods, only that it yields *different* results.

### Minor

- **No static graph baseline to isolate the contribution of temporal dynamics.** The paper compares TGNNs against a non-graph LSTM, showing graph structure helps. But it never tests whether a *static* GNN (e.g., GCN on a graph constructed from average lead-lag patterns or return correlations over the full period) could match or exceed TGNN performance. Given that the ablation study (Table 3) shows most models perform best with static description embeddings only, it is possible that static graph structure drives the gains and temporal modeling adds little. This baseline is needed to support the paper's framing that *temporal* graph learning specifically is essential.

- **Small graph size limits the benchmark's value.** With only 37 nodes, the dataset provides a limited challenge for TGNNs, most of which are designed for graphs with thousands to millions of nodes. This constrains the generalizability of the findings and reduces the benchmark's utility for the TGNN community — one of the paper's stated contributions.

- **No qualitative analysis of learned relationships.** The paper never shows what GM actually learns. Are the top predicted lead-lag pairs sensible (e.g., oil → airlines, NVIDIA → semiconductor suppliers)? A few concrete examples would greatly strengthen the evidence that the model captures real economic structure.

### Trivial
- The paper claims six contributions but (i) and (ii) are essentially the same (formulation + benchmark), and (iv)-(vi) are standard experimental design. The core novelty is the problem formulation and dataset.
- The negative sampling strategy is mentioned only briefly ("sophisticated negative sampling through node corruption," Section 3.3); more detail would aid reproducibility.

## Nice-to-Haves
- A backtesting experiment (e.g., a long-short portfolio based on predicted edges) would demonstrate practical utility.
- A plot of graph statistics over time (edge count per month, topological changes) would illustrate the temporal dynamics that motivate TGNNs.
- Sensitivity analysis on the ε threshold (e.g., 3%, 5%, 7%) would address concerns about label construction robustness.

## Removed Points
These points were identified by the reviewers but are removed or demoted for the reasons given:

- **"5% threshold is arbitrary and produces 'extreme' events"** — The paper cites Li et al. (2022) (minimal outcome variation when ε is altered) and Sheth et al. (2023) (5% balances density). The concern about label validity is kept (above) but the characterization as purely "arbitrary" is removed since the choice is grounded in prior work.
- **"LSTM baseline is not a meaningful measure"** — The LSTM serves its intended purpose: a non-graph baseline to isolate the value of relational structure. The paper does not claim it is a SOTA baseline.
- **"Ablation study shows models rely on static descriptions, undermining temporal claim"** — This misinterprets the paper. Temporal information comes from the evolving edge topology, not just node features. The ablation shows that temporal *node features* (prices) are often redundant, not that temporal *graph structure* is irrelevant. GM, the best model, does use all features.
- **"Missing related works"** — Not verifiable, and the paper surveys relevant lead-lag and TGNN literature adequately for its scope.

## Novel Insights
None beyond the paper's own contributions. The reviews surface a central tension that the paper does not fully confront: the formulation is clean and the TGNN comparison is thorough, but the lack of external validation means the paper cannot distinguish between detecting genuine lead-lag and detecting a threshold-based construct. This is a gap the authors could address with relatively modest additional experiments (case studies, Granger causality overlap), and if resolved, would substantially strengthen the contribution.

## Suggestions

1. **Validate the label construction.** Show that the threshold-generated edges correspond to known economic patterns (e.g., sector-level clustering, commodity → producer relationships). A simple experiment: compute the overlap between top-GM-predicted lead-lag pairs and pairs identified by Granger causality tests on the same data.
2. **Add a static graph baseline.** Build a single graph from the full training period (e.g., using mutual information or return correlation) and run a standard GCN/GAT. If the static GNN approaches GM's performance, the paper's framing should shift from "temporal graph learning helps" to "graph learning helps."
3. **Include qualitative case studies.** Show specific asset pairs where GM detects lead-lag and explain why they make economic sense (or do not).
4. **Release the dataset and code upon submission**, not just upon acceptance, to enable validation during review.
5. **Discuss limitations explicitly**, especially regarding whether the 5% threshold label corresponds to economically meaningful lead-lag, and acknowledge that the framework as presented detects a correlation-based construct rather than causal lead-lag.
