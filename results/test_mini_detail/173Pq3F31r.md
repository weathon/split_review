## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models expressive piano performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT) by disentangling note-level score content from global performance style representations. It introduces a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone, and formulates EPR as a sequence-to-sequence task without requiring note-level alignment. The framework is evaluated on both paired ASAP data and large-scale unpaired score/performance data.

## Strengths

- **Novel joint formulation of EPR and APT as inverse tasks.** The paper is among the first to explicitly model EPR and APT in a unified Seq2Seq architecture with disentangled content/style representations. The framing of style as a global conditioning vector and content as note-level is architecturally well-motivated. This joint bidirectional perspective is a genuine conceptual advance over prior works that treat the tasks independently.

- **Seq2Seq EPR without note-level alignment achieves competitive accuracy.** Despite removing the note-level alignment requirement that most prior EPR systems depend on, Ours (PSR) achieves the highest alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%) in Table 3, outperforming alignment-dependent baselines DExter and VirtuosoNet. This validates the claim that strict note-level supervision is not necessary for high-quality rendering.

- **The PSR module produces stylistically coherent style embeddings from scores alone.** Figure 4 shows that PSR-generated style embeddings cluster by historical era (Baroque, Classical, Romantic, Modern) with centroid positions and era-wise separation closely matching those extracted from real performances, demonstrating that the module recovers plausible style distributions without any performance input.

- **Disentanglement is quantitatively verified.** Table 4 shows that style embeddings predict composer identity with 77.46% accuracy versus only 29.99% for content embeddings, and performer identity with 42.07% versus 9.94%. This provides solid evidence that the learned representations successfully separate content and style factors.

- **The PSR module enables controllable, style-aware rendering for non-expert users.** By generating style embeddings conditioned solely on score content, the PSR module removes the need for composer labels or manual expressive parameter tuning required by prior methods (Jeong et al., 2019; Rhyu et al., 2022), making style-aware performance accessible to users without musical expertise.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **The claimed benefit of joint modeling is not isolated from the effect of additional training data.** The paper's central claim is that joint modeling of EPR and APT yields mutual benefit. Yet the only EPR ablation (EPR-Only) is "trained solely on the ASAP dataset" (line 219), while the full joint model is jointly trained with an additional 75,913 unpaired scores and YouTube performances. The paper acknowledges this confound explicitly: "joint modeling, **together with training on additional unpaired data**, leads to better EPR performance" (line 245). Without an ablation that keeps the unpaired data constant and removes only the APT loss (or vice versa), the improvement could come entirely from more training data rather than from cross-task mutual supervision. This gap undermines one of the paper's most central claims.

- **The subjective evaluation reports that the model is preferred over real human performances without any discussion or interpretation.** The paper states that "Ours (Target) achieves the highest ratings across all attributes and styles" (line 247) — and Figure 2 includes human performances as a comparison condition, meaning the model outperforms actual human pianists on human-likeness. This is an extraordinary result that demands commentary: were the selected human performances atypical? Was the Likert scale properly anchored? Did listeners detect or favor the synthetic condition? The total absence of any discussion, combined with the small sample size (11 participants, 5 pieces), substantially weakens the credibility of the subjective results as evidence for "naturalness."

### Minor

- **Alignment, insertion, and missing rate metrics are underspecified.** Table 3 reports Align/Insert/Miss rates but the paper does not define what constitutes a correct alignment (onset tolerance, pitch-match requirement, etc.). While these metrics are adopted from prior work (Tang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024), a self-contained definition is needed for reproducibility. Similarly, the "note-aligned MAE" in Table 2 implies an external alignment tool is used during evaluation, which contrasts with the paper's emphasis on alignment-free training — this warrants a brief clarification.

- **Subjective evaluation is under-powered for the strength of the claims made.** The listening test uses only 5 pieces and 11 musically trained participants. While this is a reasonable pilot, the paper makes broad claims ("achieves the highest ratings across all attributes and styles") without statistical interpretation of the error bars shown in Figure 2. Per-participant analysis, detection of anchor effects, or a sanity-check baseline with obviously poor quality would strengthen confidence in the results.

- **The disentanglement strategy relies primarily on architectural priors without explicit separation regularization.** Content (note-level sequence) and style (single global vector with KL penalty) are separated architecturally, but there is no explicit regularization (e.g., mutual information minimization, cycle-consistency) to push the two representations apart. While the classifier results in Table 4 are convincing, the observed separation could partly reflect the different granularities of the representations rather than true factor disentanglement.

- **No limitations section.** The paper does not discuss limitations such as the small subjective test, reliance on classical music only (generality to pop/other genres untested), potential noise in the YouTube unpaired dataset from audio-to-MIDI transcription errors, or the PSR module's inability to recommend styles absent from the training data.

### Trivial

- The DExter baseline's higher variance is attributed to "unstable dynamics" (line 245) based on what listening tests "suggest" — this interpretive claim lacks direct evidence. It would be better to rephrase as an observation rather than an explanation.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Isolate the joint modeling benefit.** Train an ablation that keeps the joint architecture but removes the APT loss while using the **same** unpaired data. Compare to EPR-Only and the full model to test whether mutual supervision actually helps beyond the data quantity effect.
- **Quantify PSR fidelity more precisely.** Beyond the 2D visualization in Figure 4, compute a distribution-distance metric (e.g., Earth Mover's Distance) between real and PSR-generated style vectors across era clusters.
- **Improve subjective test robustness.** Increase the number of participants, include a low-quality sanity-check baseline, and report whether listeners identified the Human condition.
