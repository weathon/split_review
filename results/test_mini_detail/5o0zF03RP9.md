Let me write the final review now.## Summary

IncentRL proposes augmenting the reinforcement learning objective with a KL divergence term that penalizes misalignment between predicted outcome distributions p(o|s,a) and preferred outcome distributions q(o|s). The paper's headline contribution is a Bayesian scheme that treats the incentive weight β as a random variable, updated online to eliminate manual trade-off tuning. Fixed-β experiments on a toy MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid Doorkey show consistent improvements over β=0, and posterior concentration plots for β are reported.

## Strengths

1. **Novel and well-motivated shaping objective.** The KL-based preference-prediction alignment term (Eq. 1) is conceptually distinct from existing KL-regularized RL (which penalizes policy divergence) and from curiosity/empowerment signals (which lack explicit preferred outcomes). The cognitive grounding in dopamine RPE and the Free Energy Principle provides a principled motivation. This is a genuinely interesting way to inject task-relevant prior knowledge into the RL objective.

2. **Consistent improvements with fixed β across three benchmarks.** On the toy 2-state MDP (Table 1), all β>0 values yield 69–74% improvement over β=0 with substantially lower variance. On MountainCar (Table 2), β=0.1 gives an 18% improvement in goal reaches. On MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8 (Fig. 2), β=0.01 raises final success rate from 90.5% to 98% and reduces mean episode length from 75 to 29 steps. These results show the KL shaping signal can meaningfully improve exploration and learning in sparse-reward settings.

3. **Appropriate discussion of limitations.** Section 6.2 explicitly acknowledges preference misalignment, KL dominance, and latent mismatch as failure modes, and Section 6.4 identifies areas for future work including deeper evaluation of Bayesian β against meta-gradient or annealing baselines.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **The Bayesian β adaptation mechanism — the paper's central claimed novelty — is completely unspecified.** No prior distribution, likelihood function, posterior update rule, or algorithm pseudocode is provided anywhere in the paper. The abstract and introduction highlight this as the headline contribution ("treating β as a Bayesian random variable, updated online"), yet Section 3 (Methods) does not mention Bayesian adaptation at all. The only content is in Section 5.4, which merely shows posterior statistics (Figs. 3, 4; Table 3) without describing how they were computed. This is not a minor omission: the Bayesian update is the mechanism that the paper claims eliminates manual trade-off tuning, and without it the contribution cannot be evaluated, reproduced, or built upon. The GitHub link in the abstract does not remedy the absence of algorithmic description in the paper itself.

2. **No performance curves for the Bayesian agent are shown.** The paper states "In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of β achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value" (Section 5.4), but provides no learning curves, final returns, or success rates for the Bayesian β agent. For a claim that "Bayesian adaptation of preference gaps removes the need for manual trade-off tuning," the absence of any performance comparison against the fixed-β sweeps is a critical evidential gap. The posterior concentration plots (Figs. 3, 4) show that β concentrates, but do not show whether the resulting policy actually performs well. Without this evidence, the central claim of the paper is unsubstantiated.

3. **p(o|s,a) is not specified for the main experiments.** In the toy MDP (Section 5.2), p(o|s,a) is hand-specified as the transition probabilities. However, for MountainCar and MiniGrid, the paper says only "p(o|s,a) is the predicted outcome distribution" (Section 5.3) with no description of how this distribution is obtained, what architecture computes it, what objective trains it, or whether it is learned online or fixed. Since the KL penalty depends entirely on p(o|s,a), the experimental results could be driven by the quality of this predictive model rather than the incentive framework. This makes the main experiments uninterpretable and not reproducible.

4. **No comparison to any intrinsic motivation baseline.** The paper frames its contribution against the limitations of curiosity-driven exploration, RND, empowerment, and similar methods (Section 2.1), yet the only baseline in every experiment is the same algorithm with β=0 (standard RL without the KL term). All three experiments compare only β>0 vs β=0. Without comparisons to ICM, RND, or other intrinsic reward methods, the paper cannot support its claimed position in the literature — specifically, that IncentRL's approach improves upon or is competitive with existing intrinsic motivation methods while additionally removing the need for trade-off tuning.

### Minor

5. **Internal inconsistency in reported β values.** The text in Section 5.4 states that "the posterior mean of β quickly concentrates near the effective region (β ≈ 0.1)" (line 234), but Table 3 reports the Round 4 mean β as 0.0173, and the Figure 3 image caption describes the posterior mean "decreasing to about 0.02 by Round 4." These are different by a factor of ~5. While for the MiniGrid task the best fixed β was 0.01, so β≈0.02 is actually close to the effective region, the text claiming β≈0.1 is factually inconsistent with the reported data. This erodes confidence in the paper's reporting accuracy.

6. **Theoretical analysis (Propositions 1 and 2) is too basic to constitute a meaningful contribution.** Proposition 1 states that sufficiently small β preserves the original optimal policy (standard for any bounded reward perturbation in finite MDPs). Proposition 2 states that as β→∞ the policy ignores external rewards (follows directly from the definition). These are continuity observations that do not analyze learning dynamics, sample efficiency, convergence, or the Bayesian β update. The paper's contribution list claims "theoretical analysis" as a deliverable, but these propositions are classroom-level observations.

7. **Bayesian adaptation uses "rounds" rather than online updates.** Figures 3 and 4 and Table 3 report β statistics over 4 discrete rounds, but the abstract claims β is "updated online." Online updating (per episode or timestep) is meaningfully different from round-based adaptation. The paper does not clarify the relationship between rounds and training steps, nor whether the round-based procedure constitutes the "online" update claimed.

### Trivial

8. The y-axis labels and scales in Figures 3 and 4 are not fully described. What constitutes a "round" in terms of training steps is not stated. The x-axis labeling ("Seed42-R1", etc.) in Figure 4 is cryptic without explanation.
