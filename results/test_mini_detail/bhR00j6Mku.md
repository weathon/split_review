## Summary

This paper presents the first systematic study of benchmark contamination detection vulnerabilities in Large Reasoning Models (LRMs). It identifies two stages where contamination can occur: (I) when base models evolve into LRMs via SFT+RL, showing that GRPO training conceals previously detectable SFT contamination evidence, with PPO-style importance sampling/clipping identified as the root cause; and (II) when SFT contamination with CoT is applied to advanced LRMs, finding that detection methods perform near random. The paper provides strong empirical evidence across 10 detection methods, 6 benchmarks, and multiple model families.

## Strengths

- **First systematic study of LRM contamination detection with strong empirical evidence across diverse settings**: The paper evaluates 10 detection methods spanning generation-based, perturbation-based, reference-based, and reference-free approaches across 6 reasoning benchmarks, 2 base models (Stage I), and 4 advanced LRMs (Stage II). Table 2 shows systematic AUROC drops across all methods after GRPO, and Table 5 shows near-random detection for Stage II contamination — this breadth convincingly demonstrates the fragility of existing methods.

- **Clean ablation isolating the clipping/importance-sampling mechanism as the root cause**: Table 3 provides a controlled experiment where removing clipping from GRPO and RAFT++ restores detection performance to near-baseline levels (e.g., GRPO w/o clipping AUROC 73.28% vs. with clipping 61.26%), while plain RAFT (no clipping) shows no reduction. This cleanly isolates the mechanism independently of the theoretical analysis.

- **Demonstration that CoT contamination on advanced LRMs yields large performance gains yet near-random detection**: Table 4 shows average pass@1 inflation of 11.76% on AIME25 for DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, while Table 5 reports AUROC values clustered near 50% across all 10 detection methods and 4 LRM families. Figure 4 confirms that log-prob distributions of members and non-members overlap, undermining the memorization assumption underlying most detection methods.

- **Controlled experiments ruling out forgetting as an explanation**: The paper shows that (1) additional SFT on clean data does not conceal contamination, and (2) GRPO on both clean and contaminated data yields comparable concealment to GRPO on clean data alone. This definitively separates the concealment effect from simple forgetting.

## Weaknesses

### Major

### Minor

- **The theoretical analysis (Section 3.2) is presented as a plausibility argument rather than a rigorous proof, and the paper slightly overstates its definitiveness.** Theorem 3.1 relies on a "small natural gradient step" assumption, a tabular setting, and several unverified conditions (e.g., that the covariance gap $\beta_N - \beta_M$ is negative under clipping). The proof is deferred to the appendix, and the main text's reasoning about variance differences between members and non-members is stated without direct empirical verification. The empirical evidence in Table 3 independently validates the core claim, so the theory is not necessary for the paper's contribution to stand. However, phrases like "These two observations perfectly reflect our theoretical analysis, empirically validating that the PPO-style importance sampling/clipping term is the key driver" overstate the connection. The theory is a plausible mechanism consistent with the data, not a proven cause — the paper would be stronger if it acknowledged this more explicitly.

- **The Stage II contamination setup does not specify the origin of the CoT responses used for SFT in the main text.** The paper states "We simulate extensive contamination with CoT by applying SFT exclusively on the member data in this section" and references Appendix D.4 for details, but the main text does not clarify whether the CoT responses were generated by the teacher model (DeepSeek-R1), by the student model itself, or by some other procedure. This matters because the distribution of training CoT relative to the model's own rollouts during detection could affect the interpretation of the negative results. The appendix presumably contains this detail, but the main narrative would benefit from a brief clarification.

- **Limited to math/science reasoning benchmarks.** All six benchmarks (OlympiadBench, GPQA, AIME25/24, Minerva, AMC23) are in mathematical or scientific reasoning. The paper does not discuss whether the findings are expected to generalize to other domains (e.g., coding, general QA, open-ended generation) where CoT patterns and detection dynamics may differ.

- **No analysis of why non-members also see increased log-prob after Stage II contamination.** The paper attributes this to generalization to distributionally similar questions but does not test this claim empirically (e.g., by measuring similarity between member/non-member questions or showing that the effect diminishes for out-of-distribution questions). This is a plausible explanation but remains unverified.

### Trivial

- The claim about "a broad class of RL methods" in the conclusion could be more precise. The paper tests GRPO and RAFT++, which share PPO-style clipping/importance sampling. Qualifying this as "RL methods that include clipped importance sampling" would be more accurate, though the paper does use "may" as a hedge.
