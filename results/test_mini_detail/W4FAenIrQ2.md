## Summary
2-3 sentence summary of the paper's contribution.

The paper presents RedSage, an open-source 8B cybersecurity LLM built through a comprehensive data-centric pipeline: large-scale web filtering for continual pretraining (CyberFineWeb, 11.7B tokens), curated high-quality resources (RedSage-Seed), and agentic augmentation to generate 266K multi-turn SFT conversations. The authors release models, data, and code, and introduce RedSage-Bench (30K MCQs + 240 open-ended QA) covering knowledge, skills, and tool proficiency. Evaluations on external cybersecurity benchmarks (CTI-Bench, CyberMetric, SECURE, etc.) show consistent improvements over strong baselines, with RedSage-8B-Ins/DPO surpassing Qwen3-8B by up to +5.59 points.

## Strengths

1. **Comprehensive, open pipeline covering CPT, SFT, DPO, and evaluation.** Unlike prior work that focuses on a single training stage (Table 2), RedSage combines large-scale continual pretraining (CyberFineWeb, 11.7B tokens), curated seed data (850M tokens), agentically augmented multi-turn conversations (266K), and preference alignment (DPO). All models, data, and code will be released — the only cybersecurity LLM work to open all artifacts (Table 2). This is a significant practical contribution to the open-source cybersecurity community.

2. **Consistent gains on established external cybersecurity benchmarks.** Table 5 shows RedSage-8B-Ins and RedSage-8B-DPO achieve the best results among 8B models across six benchmarks (CTI-Bench MCQ/RCM, CyberMetric, MMLU-CSec, SecBench, SecEval, SECURE). The improvements over Qwen3-8B (+5.39 to +5.59 points for instruct models) are demonstrated on *independent* benchmarks that do not share the data provenance issue of RedSage-Bench, making this the strongest evidence in the paper.

3. **Agentic augmentation pipeline is a methodological advance.** The two-stage Planner + Augmenter agent framework (Section 3.2, Figure 4) goes beyond prior template-based or simple Q&A methods by generating diverse, role-based multi-turn dialogues grounded in seed documents. The 9.2× sample expansion (28,637 → 266,180 conversations) across knowledge, skills, and tools categories is non-trivial, and the pipeline is clearly described with examples.

4. **Competitive general benchmark performance despite domain specialization.** RedSage-8B-DPO achieves 74.33 mean on the Open LLM Leaderboard (Table 6), exceeding Qwen3-32B (73.17) despite being 4× smaller. This demonstrates that domain-specific training does not necessarily harm — and can even improve — general capabilities when combined with appropriate general SFT data.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Benchmark contamination between RedSage-Bench and the training corpus.** The RedSage-Bench MCQs and open-ended QA are generated from the same RedSage-Seed documents used in continual pretraining (CPT). The decontamination step (Section 3.3) only checks semantic overlap between *synthetic post-training queries* and benchmark questions — it does **not** check overlap between the seed documents used in CPT and the knowledge required to answer benchmark items. The model may therefore score higher on RedSage-Bench partly through memorization of source material rather than through generalizable domain competence. This issue does **not** affect the external benchmarks in Table 5 (CTI-Bench, CyberMetric, SECURE, etc.), which provide the strongest evidence for the paper's claims. However, the RedSage-Bench results (Table 4, Figure 6) must be interpreted with caution. The authors should either: (a) source benchmark questions from independent materials not present in the training corpus, or (b) explicitly reframe RedSage-Bench as a within-distribution diagnostic tool and rely primarily on the external benchmarks for claims of domain competence.

### Minor

- **LLM-as-Judge evaluator shares model family with data generation pipeline.** The open-ended QA evaluation (Section 4.1, Figure 6) uses Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct as judges. These same model families were used as the Teacher and Verifier for generating the benchmark items and SFT conversations (Section 3.3). The paper does not report correlation with human judgments or use an out-of-family judge (e.g., GPT-4o). The reported quality gaps could partly reflect evaluator familiarity with the response style of models trained on data generated by the same model family.

- **Claims about domain-aware augmentation improving general reasoning are weakly supported.** The paper states that "Seed boosts math reasoning (GSM8K), CFW strengthens general knowledge and reasoning" (Section 4.3). However, looking at Table 6, the differences are small and often within noise: RedSage-8B-Seed scores 82.34 on GSM8K vs. Qwen3-8B-Base's 81.73 (+0.61); RedSage-8B-CFW scores 78.63 on MMLU vs. Qwen3's 78.73 (slightly *lower*). The general improvements in Table 6 for instruct models likely reflect the injection of high-quality general SFT data (SmolLM3) rather than a synergistic effect from cybersecurity data. An ablation training only on RedSage-Conv without SmolLM3 would clarify this.

- **No structured quality analysis of the augmented conversations.** The agentic augmentation pipeline (Section 3.2) is described in detail, but the paper provides no manual quality evaluation of the generated 266K conversations (e.g., error rate, hallucination rate, diversity assessment). The "human quality control" in Section 3.3 refers to the benchmark, not the SFT data. For a dataset being released to the community, a structured expert evaluation of a random sample would strengthen quality claims.

### Trivial

- The "Qual." column in Table 1 is not clearly defined — it refers to open-ended QA quality scoring, but e.g., CyberBench also includes open-ended tasks. A footnote or clearer definition would help.
- The 30% replay ratio from FineWeb-Edu is stated but not justified or compared against alternatives (e.g., 10%, 50%).

## Nice-to-Haves

- Include a comparison of RedSage-Bench results against a version where benchmark questions are sourced from entirely independent materials, to quantify the contamination effect.

## Suggestions

1. **Address the contamination concern transparently.** Acknowledge that RedSage-Bench shares source documents with the pretraining corpus, reframe it as a within-distribution diagnostic tool, and rely on external benchmarks (CTI-Bench, CyberMetric, SECURE, MMLU-CSec) as the primary evidence for domain competence claims. Alternatively, construct a small independent evaluation set from disjoint sources and compare performance.

2. **Add an out-of-family LLM judge** (e.g., GPT-4o or Gemini 1.5) for the open-ended QA evaluation, and report correlation with human ratings on a subset.

3. **Add an ablation** that trains RedSage-Ins without SmolLM3 data, to isolate the effect of cybersecurity-specific SFT data on both cybersecurity and general benchmarks.

4. **Include a structured human evaluation** of a random sample of the augmented conversations, reporting error/hallucination rates across categories.

5. Make the benchmark contamination analysis clearer in a limitations section.
