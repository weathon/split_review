## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models expressive piano performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT) via disentangled score content and performance style representations. The framework uses a Seq2Seq formulation that avoids note-level alignment, and introduces a diffusion-based performance style recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone. Experiments on the ASAP dataset show competitive performance on both tasks, and subjective evaluations confirm the quality of rendered performances. Disentanglement is validated through classifier-based analysis of learned representations and style transfer experiments.

## Strengths

1. **Joint modeling demonstrably improves EPR over single-task baseline**: Table 2 shows Ours (Target) achieves lower KL divergence and MAE for durations and velocities compared to the EPR-Only variant (e.g., MAE(D): 0.37 vs 0.42; KL(V): 1.76 vs 2.05), directly supporting the claim that sharing representations across EPR and APT yields better rendering fidelity.

2. **Seq2Seq EPR formulation without note-level alignment achieves competitive results**: Table 3 shows Ours (PSR) attains the highest alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%) among all systems, including alignment-dependent baselines DExter and VirtuosoNet. This is a practically meaningful result — it demonstrates that competitive EPR is possible without the labor-intensive note-level alignment that prior work requires.

3. **Content-style disentanglement is convincingly validated**: Table 4 shows that a classifier on style embeddings achieves 77.46% composer accuracy and 42.07% performer accuracy, while the content embedding achieves only 29.99% and 9.94% respectively. The gap is substantial and clearly confirms that the representations have separated the intended factors. Figure 3's visual clustering further supports this.

4. **PSR module produces stylistically coherent embeddings from scores alone**: Figure 4 shows that PSR-generated style embeddings (projected into 2D) mirror the clustering structure of real performance styles, with similar era-wise separation and centroid locations. Subjectively, Ours (PSR) in Figure 2 achieves ratings close to Ours (Target), validating that the module can infer appropriate styles.

5. **Thorough subjective evaluation with trained musicians**: The listening test uses 11 musically trained participants and covers five composers across four perceptual dimensions, providing ecologically valid evidence for the quality of generated performances.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **No explicit alignment objective between content representations** despite the claim that they "are encouraged to align" (Figure 1 caption). Equation 6 contains no loss term that directly enforces similarity between \(z_x\) (from score) and \(z_y\) (from performance). The only supervision is indirect: both must be informative for their respective decoding tasks (EPR and APT). Since \(z_x\) receives the style vector \(z_s\) added to it during EPR decoding, the decoder could learn to compensate for misalignment, making the implicit alignment even less reliable. An explicit cosine similarity, contrastive, or cycle-consistency loss would substantiate the stated claim.

- **The EPR evaluation metrics (alignment rate, insertion rate, missing rate) rely on a note-alignment algorithm that is never specified.** Computing these metrics requires matching each predicted note to a reference note, but no alignment algorithm (e.g., dynamic time warping, greedy matching, thresholding criteria) is described. Since the absolute values of these metrics are sensitive to the alignment procedure, and since the claimed advantage is that the method avoids note-level alignment *in training*, the evaluation still depends on it for assessment. This omission is a reproducibility gap.

### Minor

- **Style transfer has an unexplained failure case.** In Figure 5, Sample 2 shows that the Target condition receives lower style similarity ratings (~2.4) than the Mean condition (~3.0). The paper claims "the Target condition achieves the highest style similarity ratings in Samples 1 and 3" — which is accurate — but does not discuss why Sample 2 fails. This is not fatal (3 samples is a small evaluation), but it tempers the claim of "reliable style transfer."

- **Statistical significance is not reported for subjective ratings.** While objective metrics in Tables 1–3 use Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, Figure 2 (subjective ratings) shows only error bars without pairwise significance tests. With 11 participants and multiple systems, the headline claims (Ours (Target) highest across all attributes) would be strengthened by statistical validation. That said, the consistent pattern across attributes and composers partially mitigates this concern.

- **APT results show a clear tradeoff on pitch errors that is acknowledged but under-discussed.** In Table 1, Ours achieves significantly *worse* pitch edit distance (\(E_p\): 3.08 vs 2.73 for Beyer & Dai) and missing note rate, while significantly better on other metrics. The paper notes that alignment-based methods have an advantage on pitch errors, but does not directly discuss the practical implications of this tradeoff for users who care about pitch accuracy.

- **Limited genre scope**: The evaluation is confined to classical music. The paper acknowledges this as future work, but claims about "style recommendation" and "flexible rendering" are bounded by this scope.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation that separates the effect of joint modeling from the effect of additional unpaired data (e.g., training the full joint model without unpaired data) would more clearly isolate the source of improvements in Table 2.
