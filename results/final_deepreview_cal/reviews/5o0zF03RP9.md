## Summary

The paper proposes IncentRL, a framework that augments standard RL with a KL-divergence term between predicted outcome distributions p(o|s,a) and preferred outcome distributions q(o|s). The stated central novelty is a Bayesian scheme to adapt the incentive weight β online. Empirical results on a 2-state MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8 show consistent improvements with fixed β > 0 over β = 0.

## Strengths

- **Consistent empirical improvement from the KL shaping term across three domains.** With fixed β > 0, the framework improves over β = 0 in all three tested environments: 69–74% higher reward in the 2-state MDP (Table 1, Fig. 1), 18% more goal reaches in MountainCar (Table 2), and 98% vs. 90.5% success rate on MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8 (Fig. 2). These results are averaged over multiple seeds and demonstrate that the KL(p‖q) bonus can provide useful dense signal in sparse-reward settings.

- **Clear differentiation from policy-KL and entropy regularization methods.** Section 2.2 correctly distinguishes IncentRL's KL-over-outcome-distributions from TRPO/PPO's policy-constrained KL and SAC's entropy regularization, and Section 2.3-2.4 draws principled connections to and contrasts with the Free Energy Principle and dopamine RPE. This framing is coherent and situates the contribution.

- **Algorithmic generality.** The framework is agnostic to the base RL algorithm (Q-learning in the toy MDP, DQN in MountainCar and MiniGrid). The algorithm template in Section 5.1 is straightforward and could be applied to other base methods.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **The claimed central novelty — Bayesian β adaptation — is never described as a method.** The abstract, introduction, and conclusion all state that treating β as a Bayesian random variable updated online is the paper's primary novelty. However, Section 3 (Methods) contains zero algorithmic description of this mechanism. There is no specification of the prior over β, the likelihood model (what data drives the update?), the inference procedure (Bayesian filtering, variational inference, MCMC?), or how the posterior is used during training (posterior mean? mode? sampling?). The Bayesian results appear only as post-hoc plots (Figures 3-4) and summary statistics (Table 3 in appendix) with the one-line statement "we tracked the posterior evolution of β using our Bayesian adaptation scheme" — but the scheme itself is absent. A reader cannot understand, implement, or evaluate the paper's central claim. This is not a missing detail; it is the absence of the method.

2. **The claim that Bayesian β adaptation "matches the best fixed choice without manual tuning" is unsupported.** The paper states, "In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of β achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value" (Section 5.4). No data, learning curves, or statistical tests are provided for this claim. The Bayesian adaptation plots (Figures 3-4, Table 3) only show posterior concentration, not a direct performance comparison between Bayesian β and optimally-tuned fixed β on the same task. On MiniGrid, the best fixed β was 0.01, while the Bayesian posterior converges to mean ≈0.017 (Round 4 of Table 3) — close but no head-to-head comparison is shown.

3. **No comparison against existing intrinsic motivation methods.** The paper frames IncentRL as addressing the same sparse-reward problems as curiosity, ICM, RND, count-based exploration, etc., but only compares β > 0 against β = 0 (standard RL). On MiniGrid, a standard benchmark for exploration methods, any comparison to ICM, RND, or simple count-based exploration is absent. Without this, it is unclear whether the improvement comes from the specific KL(p‖q) formulation or from adding any dense intrinsic bonus at all.

4. **Implementation of p(o|s,a) and q(o|s) in continuous and grid environments is critically underspecified.** In MountainCar (2D continuous state) and MiniGrid (RGB observations), the paper never specifies how the outcome distributions are represented, parameterized, or learned. Section 4.3 discusses latent-space Gaussian models as an option, but the experiments do not state which approach was used. Without this detail, the computed KL divergence — the core of the method — is uninterpretable and the results are not reproducible.

### Minor

1. **The theoretical analysis is trivial.** Propositions 1 and 2 state that for sufficiently small β the optimal policy is unchanged and for β → ∞ the KL term dominates. These are immediate consequences of the continuity of the objective in β and do not provide insight into dynamics, convergence rates, sample complexity, or the behavior of the Bayesian update. This section adds no substantive support to the paper's claims.

2. **Bayesian adaptation is tested on only 4 rounds with limited seeds.** Figures 3-4 show Bayesian adaptation over only 4 rounds with 2 seeds. This is a very short timescale for an online RL setting and does not demonstrate that the scheme works in a continual learning scenario.

3. **The choice of q(o|s) is not ablated.** All experiments use a hand-specified q that places all mass on the goal. Sensitivity to this choice — which is critical for practical deployment — is not explored.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Direct head-to-head comparison between Bayesian β and the best tuned fixed β on the same task, with learning curves and statistical tests.
- Comparison to existing intrinsic motivation methods (ICM, RND, count-based exploration) on MiniGrid.
- Ablation of the definition of q(o|s): how sensitive is performance to the preference distribution?
- Quantification of the computational overhead of maintaining p(o|s,a).
