## Summary
This paper conducts a systematic evaluation of nine open-source mid-sized LLMs on two reverse engineering tasks—name recovery and type inference—using 40,000 functions across four architectures and four compiler optimization levels. The study finds that off-the-shelf LLMs achieve very low F1 scores (<0.1) and that LoRA fine-tuning yields modest, inconsistent improvements. The paper addresses a genuine gap: no prior work has systematically benchmarked LLMs across both tasks at this scale, and the field has lacked a principled basis for model selection in reverse engineering.

## Strengths
- **Broad, systematic evaluation design**: The paper evaluates 9 LLMs on 40,000 unique functions spanning 4 architectures (x86-64, x86-32, ARM 32-bit, MIPS 32-bit) at 4 optimization levels, with both decompiled-code and assembly-code inputs. This breadth is unusual for the domain and gives the benchmark genuine coverage (Tables 2–6).
- **Real-world firmware validation**: Table 6 evaluates models on stripped firmware binaries using both Ghidra and IDA Pro decompilers, grounding the benchmark in a realistic reverse-engineering setting and showing that decompiler choice introduces measurable variation.
- **Insightful finding on model size vs. training quality**: Figure 2 (CodeLlama 7B–34B comparison) and the Qwen2.5 vs. DeepSeek-R1 comparison (identical architecture, different training) convincingly demonstrate that training data quality dominates parameter count for these tasks—a non-obvious result with practical implications.
- **CodeWordNet sensitivity analysis**: Figure 1 quantifies the large gap between exact-match and semantic-similarity metrics, making a clear case that name recovery evaluation requires semantic matching.

## Weaknesses

### Major
- **Unsupported quantitative claim in the introduction**: The introduction states that after fine-tuning, DeepSeek-R1 reaches an F1 score of 0.37 (line 127). No table in the visible paper reports this number—the highest fine-tuned DeepSeek-R1 F1 across all presented tables is 0.17 (Table 3, variable name recovery, O0). If the 0.37 comes from a non-x86-64 architecture in the stripped appendix, the introduction presents it without qualification, making the number unverifiable. This directly undermines reader confidence in the paper's quantitative reporting.
- **Metric ambiguity between Section 3.4 and the main tables**: Section 3.4 states that name recovery is evaluated using the SYMLM/CodeWordNet method with partial-credit semantic matching. However, the Table 2 values (e.g., CodeLlama O0 original = 0.04) align closely with the "without CodeWordNet" bars in Figure 1 (~0.05) rather than the "with CodeWordNet" bars (~0.15). This strongly suggests the main tables use exact-match scoring, contradicting Section 3.4's description. Readers cannot determine which metric the paper's headline results actually reflect, which undermines the quantitative basis for the paper's conclusions about LLM performance.

### Minor
- **Unvalidated output extraction pipeline**: The auxiliary LLM used for response normalization (Section 3.2) receives no accuracy validation. Given that some models produce chain-of-thought reasoning, refusals, or truncated outputs, errors at this stage could systematically bias results against certain models. A small manually labeled validation set would address this.
- **Thin fine-tuning protocol**: The paper reports only "200 steps" with LoRA and no learning rate, batch size, or convergence analysis. Several models show performance *decreases* after fine-tuning (e.g., DeepSeek-V2 Table 3, O1: 0.10→0.05), raising questions about whether the protocol is properly tuned. Without hyperparameter details or evidence of convergence, the fine-tuning results are harder to interpret.
- **No variance reporting**: All tables report point estimates without standard deviations or confidence intervals, despite differences between models often being ≤0.01–0.02 on F1. This makes it difficult to judge whether observed differences are meaningful.
- **Some findings are already well-known**: Findings 5 (training data quality matters more than architecture) and 8 (LLMs need post-processing to follow formats) are already established in the broader LLM literature, though they remain useful to confirm in this specific domain.

### Trivial
- Table 7 (fine-tuning wall-clock time) depends entirely on hardware and prompt-template implementation details. It is not informative about task difficulty or model quality and takes space that could be used for variance reporting or precision/recall breakdowns.

## Nice-to-Haves
- A systematic taxonomy of failure modes (e.g., which kinds of names are hardest to recover, whether decompiler-induced artifacts systematically confuse models) would add depth beyond the four qualitative examples in Table 8.
- Reporting precision and recall components separately (not just F1) would help readers understand whether low scores stem from incomplete predictions or incorrect ones.
- Type-name normalization (e.g., `unsigned int` vs. `unsigned`, architecture-dependent `long`) should be discussed, as exact-match type inference may artificially depress scores.

## Novel Insights
The paper's demonstration that training data quality strongly dominates model size for reverse-engineering tasks (Figure 2, Finding 4–6) is genuinely interesting and not obvious a priori—one might expect larger models to capture more assembly-code patterns. Additionally, the finding that fine-tuning can *decrease* performance in some cases (e.g., DeepSeek-V2 variable name recovery drops from 0.10 to 0.05 at O1) is a useful cautionary signal to practitioners who might assume fine-tuning is uniformly beneficial.

## Suggestions
- Either remove the 0.37 F1 claim from the introduction or present the supporting data (architecture, task, optimization level) in the body of the paper so readers can verify it.
- Clarify which metric (exact match vs. CodeWordNet) is used in Tables 2–3 and ensure consistency with Section 3.4. Ideally, report both metrics in the main tables so readers can see the impact.
- Validate the auxiliary LLM extraction step on a labeled subset (e.g., 200 manually checked outputs) and report its accuracy.
- Report standard deviations or confidence intervals, at minimum for the primary results.
