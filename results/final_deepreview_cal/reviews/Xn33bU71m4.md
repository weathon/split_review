## Summary

This paper evaluates nine open-source LLMs (7B–16B) on two reverse engineering tasks — name recovery (function and variable names) and type inference — using a dataset of ~40,000 decompiled functions across four architectures and multiple optimization levels. The experiments cover both zero-shot and LoRA fine-tuned settings, with additional evaluation on assembly code and real-world firmware. The main finding is that current LLMs, even after fine-tuning, perform poorly: F1 scores rarely exceed 0.1 on most tasks, and the best result (0.33 on variable name recovery at O0 with WizardCoder/Qwen2.5) is an isolated peak.

## Strengths

- **First systematic multi-model benchmark for LLMs on reverse engineering tasks.** The paper evaluates nine distinct LLMs (CodeLlama, Llama2, DeepSeek-R1, Qwen2.5/3, WizardCoder, Phi4, Gemma3) on two core RE tasks, filling the gap identified in the motivation where prior work selected models arbitrarily (Section 2). The dataset of 40K functions across 4 architectures and 4 optimization levels is large and diverse (Section 4.1).

- **Standardized evaluation pipeline enabling fair comparison.** The paper normalizes decompiler-specific symbols with placeholders (Section 3.1), uses an auxiliary LLM for consistent output extraction (Section 3.2), and adopts CodeWordNet for semantic name matching (Section 3.4). This addresses the real challenge of heterogeneous LLM outputs and makes the comparison reproducible.

- **Quantitative evidence that LLMs are not yet effective for RE.** Tables 2, 3, and 4 show consistently low F1 scores across nearly all models and settings. Even after fine-tuning, no model exceeds 0.33 on variable name recovery, most remain below 0.1 on function name recovery, and all are below 0.06 on type inference (Finding 1, Finding 2). This negative result is documented with sufficient breadth to be informative.

- **Controlled comparisons revealing that training quality trumps model size.** Figure 2 shows flat performance across CodeLlama 7B, 13B, and 34B, and the comparison between Qwen2.5 and DeepSeek-R1 (same architecture) shows that training data quality matters more than parameter count (Finding 4, Finding 5). These are concrete, evidence-backed insights.

- **Real-world firmware evaluation with two decompilers.** Table 6 tests LLMs on actual stripped firmware using both Ghidra and IDA Pro, providing practical deployment evidence. The finding that IDA Pro outputs yield slightly better results is a useful observation for practitioners.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **No baseline comparison to existing specialized reverse engineering models.** The paper cites SYMGEN, RESYM, Debin, SymLM, and VARBERT in the related work (Section 2) but provides no performance numbers for them. Without this anchor, the reader cannot determine whether the poor LLM performance (F1 < 0.1) is actually worse, comparable to, or better than the specialized state-of-the-art. A benchmarking paper that asks "how good are LLMs at RE?" must also answer "how good do you need to be?" to be informative. This is the single largest gap in the experimental design.

- **No measures of statistical uncertainty.** All experiments are reported as single-point F1 scores with no variance, confidence intervals, or significance tests (Tables 2–6). Given the random sampling of functions and the stochasticity of LLM outputs, it is impossible to distinguish genuine performance differences from noise. For example, DeepSeek-V2's F1 dropping from 0.10 (O1 original) to 0.05 (O1 fine-tuned) — is this a real degradation or random variation? The paper cannot say. This weakens every comparative claim.

### Minor

- **CodeWordNet metric is adopted without independent validation.** The paper relies on CodeWordNet (from SYMLM, Jin et al., 2022) for semantic name matching, and Figure 1 shows that removing it reduces F1 by 60–70%. While adopting an existing metric is defensible, the paper provides no evidence — human judgment correlation, edge-case analysis, or alternative metric comparison — that CodeWordNet scores correspond to semantically meaningful name recovery. The decisive role of this metric means the headline F1 numbers could overstate or understate true capability.

- **Fine-tuning improvements are concentrated at O0 and under-analyzed.** The largest fine-tuning gains (e.g., WizardCoder 0.01→0.33 at O0 on variable name recovery) collapse at O1–O3 (0.03→0.06–0.08). The paper notes this pattern but does not investigate why — is the fine-tuning data dominated by simple (unoptimized) patterns? Does optimization destroy the cues the model learned? This is a potentially important finding left as an observation.

- **Assembly code evaluation uses exact matching only (Table 5).** All models score 0.00 on assembly function name recovery. The paper does not apply CodeWordNet or any fuzzy metric here, even though a model predicting a semantically equivalent name (e.g., "main" for "_start") would get no credit. The floor effect could mask partial capability that the decompiled-code evaluation would capture.

- **Auxiliary LLM introduces an uncontrolled confound.** The post-processing pipeline relies on an auxiliary LLM to extract and standardize predictions (Section 3.2). No analysis is provided of this auxiliary model's accuracy or failure modes. If it misses predictions or misattributes them, the downstream F1 scores are affected in an unmeasured way.

- **Finding 7 (fine-tuning time sensitivity to prompt templates) rests on thin evidence.** The claim that prompt template design causes up to 4× training time differences is based on modifying one model's (CodeLlama) template and observing a similar increase. No controlled experiment varying templates for multiple models is presented. The observation is suggestive but not conclusive.

### Trivial

- The introduction highlights DeepSeek-R1 reaching "an F1 score of 0.37" after fine-tuning, but the main-body tables (x86-64 only) show a maximum of 0.17 for this model. Since results for other architectures are in the appendix (which was stripped by the parser), this is not an error — but citing an appendix-only number prominently in the main text is confusing for readers without access to the appendix.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Qualitative analysis of failure cases.** The paper shows only success cases (Table 8). Adding failure examples — e.g., why fine-tuning sometimes hurts performance, or why type inference is uniformly poor — would provide actionable insights beyond "LLMs are not good enough."

- **Analysis of data leakage potential.** The training and test sets are drawn from the same pool of open-source projects. While deduplication is performed on decompiled/assembly code, a discussion of whether functions from the same project could appear in both sets would strengthen the validity claims.

- **Study of why IDA Pro outputs lead to better performance than Ghidra** (Table 6). The paper notes the gap but does not investigate whether it stems from formatting, variable naming conventions, or decompiler quality differences.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation that emerges from the data but is not fully developed in the paper is the **sharp asymmetry in fine-tuning gains across optimization levels**. Fine-tuning produces substantial improvements at O0 (e.g., 0.01→0.33) but minimal gains at O1–O3. This suggests that either (a) the fine-tuning data is dominated by simple patterns that optimization destroys, or (b) the models overfit to decompiler artifacts that appear consistently only in unoptimized code. Either explanation has concrete implications for how future RE datasets should be constructed — namely, that optimization-level stratification in training data may be necessary to achieve robust gains. The paper identifies the pattern but does not pursue this diagnostic path.

## Suggestions

1. **Add specialized RE model baselines.** Even reproducing a single existing method (e.g., Debin or SymLM) on the same data would contextualize all LLM results and dramatically increase the paper's value.
2. **Report variance.** Run each experiment 3–5 times with different random seeds and report mean ± std. This is essential for distinguishing signal from noise in the fine-tuning comparisons.
3. **Validate CodeWordNet on a small human-annotated sample** or compare with an alternative semantic metric (e.g., BLEU, ChrF) to confirm trends are consistent.
4. **Investigate the O0 vs. O1–O3 fine-tuning gap** with a controlled experiment — e.g., train on O1 data only and test on O0/O1/O2/O3 — to determine whether the pattern is data-driven or optimization-driven.
5. **Apply CodeWordNet (or a fuzzy metric) to the assembly code evaluation** so that semantically equivalent predictions receive partial credit, removing the floor effect.
