## Summary
Blueprint-Bench proposes a benchmark that evaluates spatial reasoning in AI systems by asking them to reconstruct 2D floor plans from interior apartment photographs. The benchmark applies the same task and scoring protocol to LLMs (via SVG generation), image generation models, and coding agents, providing a cross-paradigm comparison. Results on 50 apartments show most models perform near a random baseline, substantially below human performance, leading the authors to conclude that spatial reconstruction from in-distribution images remains a blind spot for current generalist AI systems.

## Strengths
- **Genuinely novel cross-paradigm comparison**: Applying the identical task and metric to LLMs, image generation models, and agent systems is a creative and valuable contribution. No existing benchmark enables direct numerical comparison of spatial intelligence across these different model architectures on a single task.
- **Transparent discussion of design trade-offs**: Section 2.4 honestly acknowledges the limitations of the scoring method (room-type agnosticism, shape-ignorance, strict formatting rules) and explains why alternative approaches (LLM-based extraction, shape-based metrics) were rejected. This candor strengthens rather than weakens the paper.
- **Prevents overfitting through private test set and public leaderboard**: The paper open-sources the code and a data sample while keeping most apartments private, and welcomes community submissions. This is a responsible benchmark design.
- **Ecologically valid input**: Using real interior photographs rather than synthetic renderings or text descriptions makes the task more representative of real-world spatial reasoning demands.

## Weaknesses

### Major
- **Scoring extraction robustness is unvalidated**: The evaluation pipeline depends on generated floor plans following nine strict formatting rules (specific pixel widths, pure colors, enclosure) for the extraction algorithm to parse connectivity and room sizes. The paper acknowledges this trade-off (Section 2.4) but does not demonstrate that the extraction algorithm degrades gracefully under minor, rule-compliant deviations — such as slight anti-aliasing, JPEG compression artifacts, or lines of 2–4 pixels rather than exactly 3. Without a validation study (e.g., measuring extraction accuracy on a set of human-drawn or programmatically varied floor plans), the headline scores may partially reflect instruction-following compliance rather than spatial reasoning alone. This undermines confidence in the metric that all comparisons depend on.

- **Random baseline is misnamed and under-specified**: The "random" baseline is generated by prompting models to produce floor plans *without* seeing the apartment images, which captures models' priors about typical apartment layouts rather than a true chance level. Calling this a "random baseline" is misleading. Furthermore, which model(s) produced this baseline, whether results were averaged, and how many samples were drawn are not specified. The central claim that "most models perform at or below a random baseline" is therefore difficult to interpret — a model with a strong architectural prior about apartment layouts will have a higher "random" score, potentially making its image-conditioned improvement appear smaller by comparison.

- **Human baseline is too thin to support strong claims**: Human performance is evaluated on only 12 of the 50 apartments and appears to come from a single individual (Section 3 states the human "iteratively drew the map after viewing more images" in the singular). The paper uses the human–model gap to argue for a substantial spatial intelligence deficit, but a single person on a quarter of the dataset is anecdotal evidence. Additionally, the paper notes the human was penalized by the size-ranking component despite correct connectivity, yet does not report what the human score would be under alternate weightings or on the connectivity sub-component alone. This weakens the claim that the gap reliably reflects a spatial reasoning deficit rather than metric idiosyncrasies.

### Minor
- **Agent comparison partially uncontrolled**: The LLM results include GPT-5 (score ~0.42), while the Codex agent uses GPT-6 (Figure 5) or GPT-5 (Figure 7, text) — an inconsistency that makes it impossible to isolate the agent scaffold's contribution for the Codex system. For Claude (Opus 4.1 with and without Claude Code), a controlled comparison does exist, and it shows a modest improvement (0.32 → 0.38). The paper's conclusion that agent-based approaches "show no meaningful improvement over single-pass generation" is therefore partially supported but overstated given the evidence from only two agent setups.

- **Scoring weights lack justification**: The composite score uses 50% edge overlap, 20% degree correlation, 10% density, 10% room count, 5% door count, and 5% door orientation. No rationale is provided for these weights, nor is there a sensitivity analysis showing how rankings change under alternative weightings. Room count — arguably a critical spatial error — receives only 10%, making the overall score relatively insensitive to hallucinated or missing rooms.

- **Metric sub-scores not reported**: Only the composite score is presented. Reporting individual sub-scores (edge overlap, degree correlation, room count accuracy, etc.) would be far more informative, allowing readers to distinguish models that fail due to connectivity errors from those that fail due to size-ranking mistakes or instruction-following violations.

- **Random baseline shifts between figures without discussion**: The baseline is 0.279 in Figure 5 (all 50 apartments) and 0.322 in Figure 7 (the 12-apartment human subset). This ~15% shift suggests the baseline is sensitive to apartment subset, which calls its stability into question. The paper does not address this.

- **No statistical tests reported**: The text states that some models "statistically perform better than the random baseline" (Section 3) and that Claude Code's results are "not statistically better" (Section 3), but no test name, p-values, or effect sizes are reported anywhere in the paper or appendix.

- **Limited dataset characterization**: Beyond the aggregate count (50 apartments, ~20 images each), no summary statistics are provided about room count distributions, connectivity graph properties, apartment types (studio vs. multi-room), or geographic/architectural diversity. This makes it difficult to assess task difficulty and generalizability.

### Trivial
- **Figure 5 table miscategorizes models**: Claude Code and Codex are labeled as "Image model" in the extracted table (while the legend correctly identifies them as agents). This is a figure formatting issue.
- **Inconsistency in model naming**: Codex is labeled as using GPT-6 in Figure 5 and GPT-5 in Figure 7 and the text.
- **"Epochs" undefined**: The term is used in Figure 5's caption and axis without definition; from context it appears to mean repeated independent runs.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Validate the extraction algorithm on a set of synthetic or human-drawn floor plans that follow the rules but include minor artifacts (JPEG compression, slight color variation, lines of 2–4 pixels) to demonstrate graceful degradation.
- Replace the "no-image" baseline with a proper chance level (e.g., random connectivity graph with matched node count) alongside the unconditioned model prior.
- Collect human annotations from multiple participants on a larger (ideally full) dataset, and report how the metric correlates with qualitative human judgment of floor-plan accuracy.
- Design a controlled agent experiment that compares the same base LLM with and without the agent scaffold, recording whether and how the model inspects and corrects its own output.

## Novel Insights
The benchmark reveals an interesting asymmetry: image generation models (GPT Image, NanoBanana) and LLMs approach the floor-plan task very differently, yet both produce near-random results. The agent experiments further show that even when given tools for iterative refinement, one agent (Codex) never inspected its own output while another (Claude Code) did iterate but still failed to produce correct plans. This suggests that the bottleneck is not merely output modality or single-pass generation but something deeper about spatial reasoning capability — an insight worth pursuing in future work.

## Suggestions
- Report metric sub-scores (edge overlap, degree correlation, room count, door count, door orientation) separately for each model. This would let readers see whether, for example, GPT-5 gets the room count right but the connectivity wrong, or vice versa.
- Include a simple sensitivity analysis: vary the scoring weights and show whether model rankings are stable.
- Provide at least summary statistics for the dataset (distribution of room counts, connectivity graph properties) so readers can assess task difficulty.
- Clarify the number of runs ("epochs") per apartment and how variance is computed for the error bars.
