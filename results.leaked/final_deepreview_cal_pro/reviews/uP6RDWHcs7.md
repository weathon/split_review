## Summary

Marginal Flow proposes a density estimation framework that defines the model as a finite mixture \(q_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{N_c}\sum_i q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w}_{\theta,i})\) where the mixture parameters \(\mathbf{w}_{\theta,i}\) are generated by pushing base-distribution samples through an unconstrained neural network and are *resampled* at each iteration. The resampling approximates marginalization over \(\mathbf{w}\), which the authors argue avoids the capacity limits of a fixed GMM. The model supports efficient single-step sampling, exact density evaluation of the defined mixture, training via both forward and reverse KL, handling of multi-modal targets, and adaptable parametric families (Gaussian, Wishart, Dirichlet). Experiments span synthetic densities, simulation-based inference, Wishart matrix distributions, and image latent-space manifolds.

## Strengths

- **Novel framework with practical advantages**: The idea of resampling mixture parameters from a learned distribution via an unconstrained network is genuinely different from both standard MDNs (which learn fixed parameters) and normalizing flows (which require bijections). Figure 1 convincingly shows that resampling prevents collapse to a discrete GMM. The framework's ability to use any parametric family \(q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w})\) is demonstrated effectively on Wishart matrices (Section 4.3, Figure 9), where changing to a Wishart conditional enables domains that NFs cannot handle at high dimensions.

- **Efficient sampling and density evaluation**: Figure 3 demonstrates orders-of-magnitude speed advantages over NF, FM, and FFF for both operations up to dimension \(10^5\). The sampling procedure (feed-forward through \(f_\theta\), then sample from a mixture component) is genuinely single-step, unlike diffusion or flow-matching models.

- **Strong empirical results across diverse settings**: The method matches or exceeds baselines on synthetic densities (Figures 6–8), achieves competitive SBI results, learns Wishart mixture manifolds (Figure 9), and produces interpretable latent-space traversals on MNIST and JAFFE (Figures 10–11). The reverse-KL results (Figure 8) are particularly notable since few models can do this efficiently.

- **Unconstrained architecture**: Unlike normalizing flows, \(f_\theta\) has no bijectivity, invertibility, or Jacobian-determinant constraints, which enables modeling of multi-modal targets from few data points (Figure 5) and use of lower-dimensional base distributions.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **Stochastic density and unaddressed training bias**: The model defined in Eq. 2 is a stochastic density — each evaluation yields a different value depending on the random draw of \(\{\mathbf{w}_i\}\). The paper motivates the model through the marginalization integral (Eq. 1) but the actual model (Eq. 2) is a finite-sample approximation of that integral. The paper acknowledges this ("The resampling induces an approximation to the marginal distribution in Eq. 1," line 68), but then Table 1 and the abstract claim "exact likelihood" without qualification or comparison to other models' *deterministic* exact likelihoods. More importantly, training maximizes \(\log \frac{1}{N_c}\sum_i q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w}_i)\), which by Jensen's inequality is an optimistically biased surrogate for the log of the true marginal \(\log \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}}[q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w})]\). The paper provides no analysis of this bias, no discussion of its dependence on \(N_c\), and no justification for why this surrogate is preferred. This is a substantive methodological gap — pages 2–3 — that needs to be addressed for the framework to be considered principled.

- **No analysis of \(N_c\)'s role**: The number of mixture components \(N_c\) is central to both the model's quality and its computational cost, yet the paper provides no guidance on how to choose \(N_c\), no ablation study varying \(N_c\), and no discussion of the bias-variance tradeoff as a function of \(N_c\). The runtime comparison in Figure 3 is presented without stating \(N_c\), making it unclear whether speed comes at the cost of density-estimate quality. This is a significant practical gap.

### Minor

- **Imprecise manifold claims**: The paper states that Marginal Flow "enables learning distributions on lower-dimensional manifolds" (abstract, Section 2.3). Since \(q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w})\) is typically a Gaussian with full support on \(\mathbb{R}^d\), the model has full support and does not place mass *exactly* on a lower-dimensional manifold. What the model actually does — concentrate density near an \(m\)-dimensional structure when \(m < d\) — is practically useful and well-demonstrated, but the language should be precise (e.g., "learn densities concentrated near a low-dimensional manifold").

- **Missing MDN positioning**: The framework shares structural similarities with Mixture Density Networks (Bishop, 1994) and related infinite-mixture models, yet the related work section (Section 3) omits this literature entirely. The resampling strategy genuinely differentiates Marginal Flow from standard MDNs, but the paper should cite and contrast with this line of work.

- **SBI results relegated to appendix**: The paper claims "state-of-the-art results" on the SBI benchmark (Section 4.2) but the quantitative results appear only in Appendix Figure 14, with no numerical summary in the main text. This weakens the support for an important claim.

### Trivial

None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A theoretical statement about the expressiveness of the model (e.g., universality under the marginalization formulation) would strengthen the motivation.
- Discussion of potential failure modes: when \(q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w})\) is misspecified, or when the base distribution dimension \(m\) is chosen incorrectly.
- The runtime comparison (Figure 3) would benefit from also measuring density-estimate quality (e.g., variance across resampling runs) at each \(N_c\), to contextualize the speed gains.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed; treat them with caution.

- **"The model does not provide an exact, deterministic likelihood" (as framed as fatal)**: The critic claims the density is not exact because it's a random variable. However, Eq. 2 *defines* the model — the model IS the finite mixture of \(N_c\) drawn components. Given the sampled \(\mathbf{w}_i\), the density IS computed exactly (no approximations in the computation). The randomness is an inherent design choice, not a computational approximation. The paper explicitly states the resampling is done "at each iteration" and acknowledges the approximation relationship to Eq. 1. The real concern is the mismatch between the "exact likelihood" label in Table 1 and the stochastic nature of the model, which I've captured under Major as a precision/clarity issue rather than a fatal methodological error.

- **"Reverse KL experiments only use NF baselines"**: The critic suggests comparing with more methods for reverse KL. However, very few models support reverse KL training with exact density — essentially only NFs and this work. The paper does mention that score-matching was attempted and was unstable. Comparing only against NFs for reverse KL is reasonable given the constraints.

- **"The NF and FM implementations appear to use naive dense Jacobian computations that trigger out-of-memory errors"**: This is speculative — the critic has no evidence that the implementations are suboptimal. The paper states details are in the appendix; without access to it, this claim cannot be verified.

- **"The Wishart NF comparison is potentially unfair because no efficient NF architecture was tried"**: The paper does compare NFs at \(10 \times 10\) (where NFs work) and states NFs could not be trained at \(100 \times 100\) (which is credible given Jacobian costs at \(d = 5050\)). The critic's suggestion to try RealNVP/Glow is speculative and does not account for whether those architectures can handle positive-definite matrix constraints.

- **"The paper uses hyperbolic language ('overcome limitations altogether')"**: This is a tone preference, not a substantive weakness. The paper's claims are largely backed by experiments.

- **Strength removed: "The problem is important"**: This is a generic, non-specific strength. The paper's contribution should be evaluated on its own merits, not on the importance of density estimation as a field.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, the key insight crystallized by these reviews is that Marginal Flow occupies an unusual design point: it defines a *stochastic model* (the density depends on a random draw of parameters) but then treats that stochasticity as a feature rather than a bug — the resampling is what prevents GMM-like collapse. This is philosophically distinct from both fixed-parameter mixture models and from latent-variable models (like VAEs) where the latent is integrated out analytically or via bounds. The framework essentially proposes that we should be comfortable with a generative model whose density function is itself a random object, as long as the resampling strategy approximates the intended marginal. Whether this is a principled position or a hidden weakness is the central tension the paper needs to resolve.

## Suggestions

- **Reframe the model definition**: Clearly distinguish between the idealized model (Eq. 1, the true marginal) and the implemented estimator (Eq. 2, the finite-sample approximation). Claim "exact density evaluation" only for Eq. 2 conditional on the draw, and acknowledge the discrepancy with Eq. 1.
- **Analyze the training bias**: Provide a derivation of the Jensen's-inequality bias in the log-average training objective, discuss its dependence on \(N_c\), and empirically verify (e.g., by evaluating true marginal log-likelihood with a very large \(N_c\)) that the bias does not lead to degenerate solutions.
- **Add \(N_c\) ablation**: Include an experiment varying \(N_c\) and measuring both density quality and runtime, to guide practitioners.
- **Add MDN comparison**: Cite Bishop (1994) and other relevant mixture-density work; explain how resampling differentiates Marginal Flow.
- **Tone down manifold language**: Replace "learn distributions on lower-dimensional manifolds" with "learn densities concentrated near lower-dimensional structure."
