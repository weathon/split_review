## Summary

This paper challenges the prevailing sequence-centric paradigm in Scientific LLMs by arguing that existing models face a "tokenization dilemma" — treating sequences as language destroys functional motifs (weak representation), while treating them as a separate modality introduces semantic misalignment. The authors propose a context-driven approach: use established bioinformatics tools (InterProScan, BLASTp, ProTrek) to generate structured textual context from protein sequences, then feed only that context to LLMs. Through systematic experiments across 7 models (3 specialized Sci-LLMs, 4 general LLMs) on 3 protein biology tasks, they demonstrate that context-only input consistently and substantially outperforms both sequence-only and sequence+context configurations. Strikingly, adding raw sequence to context *degrades* performance, suggesting sequences act as informational noise. Supporting evidence includes representation analysis (t-SNE + ARI), temporal degradation analysis, efficiency comparisons, and wet-lab validation on novel proteins.

## Strengths

- **Clear, well-articulated conceptual framing**: The "tokenization dilemma" — decomposing the problem into the weak-representation and semantic-misalignment horns — provides an effective lens that structures the entire empirical investigation and makes the results interpretable. The paper argues its thesis coherently from motivation through experimental design to conclusions.

- **Systematic, multi-model empirical demonstration**: Table 1 tests 7 diverse models (Intern-S1, Evolla, NatureLM, Deepseek-v3, Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-5, Qwen3-235B-A22B) across 3 input configurations on 3 task types. The consistency is remarkable: for every single model, Context-Only achieves the highest or near-highest score, and Sequence+Context consistently underperforms Context-Only. This replication across model architectures and scales substantially strengthens the central claim that raw sequences act as informational noise.

- **Multiple converging lines of evidence beyond the benchmark**: The paper does not rely solely on Table 1. The representation analysis (Figures 2–3) quantifies the quality of functional clustering via ARI scores; the layer-wise decomposition of Evolla (Figure 3) traces representation degradation from SaProt encoder (ARI=0.945) through Q-Former (0.916) to final LLM embedding (0.809), directly substantiating the semantic misalignment claim. The temporal analysis (Figure 4) and wet-lab validation (Figures 5–6) provide orthogonal evidence of robustness on novel sequences.

- **Practical relevance grounded in efficiency**: Table 2 demonstrates that the context-driven pipeline is not only more accurate but also ~23× cheaper and 1.3× faster per query than the specialized Evolla model, making the approach genuinely practical for large-scale biological research.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None. The core finding (context-only outperforms sequence-based approaches across diverse models) is well-supported by consistent evidence, and none of the identified concerns invalidate this central result.

### Major

None. The paper's central empirical claims are substantiated by the evidence presented. The concerns below are important but addressable and do not threaten the paper's core contribution.

### Minor

- **LLM-Score metric lacks human validation**: The primary evaluation metric uses an automated LLM judge without reported validation against human judgments or inter-rater reliability analysis. While many papers in the field use LLM-as-judge, and the consistent degradation pattern (Sequence+Context < Context-Only across all 7 models) is difficult to explain as a pure artifact of judge bias, the absence of human calibration leaves some uncertainty about the absolute score magnitudes and the metric's sensitivity to answer verbosity or style. A small-scale human evaluation, even on a subset, would substantially strengthen confidence in the reported differences.

- **Data leakage quantification is absent**: The paper's anti-leakage design — reading GO annotations from homologs rather than the query protein's own record (Section 4, lines 146–152) — is a sound standard bioinformatics practice. However, the paper does not report how frequently the correct answer appears verbatim in the generated context or what fraction of test proteins have near-identity homologs in Swiss-Prot. Without such measurements, readers cannot assess the extent to which the Context-Only performance reflects reasoning versus information extraction from the provided context. A simple analysis measuring answer-context overlap would substantially address this.

- **Representation analysis uses different embedding methods**: The context-driven approach's embeddings (Figure 2d, ARI=0.958) are generated by Qwen-embedding, a general text-embedding model, while the Sci-LLM representations come from their internal final-layer states. Since the context is precisely the kind of human-readable text that a text-embedding model is designed to cluster, the comparison is not fully head-to-head. The layer-wise Evolla analysis (Figure 3) avoids this issue and remains internally valid, but the cross-model ARI comparison in Figure 2 should be interpreted with this caveat.

- **Temporal analysis confound is acknowledged but not controlled**: The paper notes (lines 234–238) that Evolla's training data cutoff (Swiss-Prot Release 202303) creates a temporal bias that partially explains its performance collapse on recent proteins. The analysis does not separate the effects of temporal novelty from homology sparsity, making it difficult to cleanly attribute Evolla's degradation to reliance on evolutionary context versus a simple train/test distribution shift.

- **Wet-lab validation is narrow in scope**: Binary classification of two protein families (Rhodopsin, PETase), while providing real-world validation with genuinely unpublished sequences, is a limited test of "reasoning" ability. The Evolla result on Rhodopsin (5.00% accuracy, essentially below chance for binary classification) raises questions about whether the experimental setup (prompt format, output parsing) was appropriate for that model, which the paper does not fully diagnose.

### Trivial

- The paper occasionally overstates its conclusions relative to the evidence, particularly in the abstract and conclusion where phrases like "lays the foundation for a new class of hybrid scientific AI agents" assume broader robustness than the current experiments support.
- The claim about a "154× speed-up in batch mode" (Section 5.5) depends on implementation details deferred to Appendix M; the main text would benefit from briefly explaining how tool runtime is amortized in the batch setting.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Ablation of context components**: Removing tools one at a time (no BLAST, no Pfam, only ProTrek) would reveal which annotation sources drive the performance and whether the LLM is genuinely synthesizing across sources versus reading off a single high-quality annotation.
- **Comparison with a simple retrieval baseline**: A baseline that directly returns the BLAST top-hit annotation would quantify how much the LLM adds beyond the bioinformatics tools themselves, clarifying the "reasoning vs. retrieval" question.
- **Error propagation analysis**: Testing what happens when bioinformatics tools produce erroneous or conflicting context would assess real-world robustness.
- **Validation of the LLM-judge metric** against human expert judgments on a held-out subset, as noted above.
- **Broader biomolecular coverage**: The paper focuses on proteins; extending to nucleic acids would strengthen the "biomolecular understanding" claim in the title.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel and surprising insight from this work is the *degradation* effect: adding raw sequence to rich context *reduces* performance across every tested model. This is not merely that context is better than sequence — it is that sequence is actively harmful when good context is available. This finding reframes the conversation around Sci-LLMs from "how can we better encode sequences?" to "should we be encoding sequences at all for reasoning tasks?" and suggests that current sequence integration strategies introduce noise that overwhelms whatever marginal signal the sequence might contribute. The paper's decomposition of *why* this happens — the two horns of weak representation and semantic misalignment, validated through layer-wise ARI analysis — provides a mechanistic explanation that goes beyond mere benchmarking.

## Suggestions

- **Add a small human evaluation of the LLM-judge**: Even 50–100 examples scored by a domain expert and correlated with LLM-Score would dramatically increase confidence in the metric and is feasible within a rebuttal period.
- **Report answer-context overlap statistics**: A simple analysis measuring how often ground-truth answers appear verbatim (or in close paraphrase) within the generated context would transparently bound the contribution of extraction vs. reasoning. This addresses the data leakage concern directly and quantitatively.
- **Include an ablation removing BLAST from the pipeline**: This would isolate whether performance is dominated by a single high-quality homology annotation or whether the LLM meaningfully synthesizes across InterProScan, BLAST, and ProTrek outputs. It would also clarify how the method degrades for orphan sequences.
- **Tone down overclaimed language**: Phrases like "lays the foundation for a new class of hybrid scientific AI agents" in the conclusion claim more generality than the current protein-focused experiments support. More measured language would match the evidence better.
