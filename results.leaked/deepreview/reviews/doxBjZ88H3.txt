# Review

## Summary
This paper tackles the problem of experimentally distinguishing two popular coding hypotheses in neuroscience: the likelihood coding hypothesis and the posterior sampling hypothesis. The authors propose to use an information gap, i.e., the difference in decoder performance when trying to decode the posterior vs. the likelihood from population activity, to distinguish the two hypotheses. The authors provide analytical expressions for the information gap under both hypotheses and show that the information gap can be used to optimize task parameters in a visual orientation discrimination task to maximize the difference between the information gap under the two hypotheses. The authors show that their analytical results match simulation experiments and provide a non-obvious prediction that can be tested with existing datasets.

## Strengths
- The paper is very well written and provides a good overview of the existing literature on the topic.
- The authors provide a novel theoretical framework for experimentally distinguishing the likelihood coding hypothesis from the posterior sampling hypothesis.
- The authors provide analytical expressions for their measure of distinguishability that they show match simulation experiments.
- The authors make a non-obvious prediction that can be tested with existing datasets.

## Weaknesses
- The paper only considers a very simple toy setup with two contexts. While this is a good starting point, it would be interesting to see how the information gap changes as a function of the number of contexts and the number of stimulus features that are relevant for the task.
- The paper does not consider the role of neural noise, e.g., what happens when the same neuron encoding orientation $\theta$ also encodes a different stimulus feature, e.g., location.
- The paper does not consider the role of finite sample size, e.g., what happens when there are not enough samples to reliably estimate the posterior and likelihood from population activity.

## Questions
- I was wondering whether the authors could comment on the role of finite sample size. In particular, I was wondering whether they could provide some intuition for how the information gap changes as a function of the number of samples. Does it converge quickly? Is it more difficult to distinguish the hypotheses for a given number of samples or does it get easier?
- I was also wondering whether the authors could comment on the role of neural noise, e.g., what happens when the same neuron encoding orientation $\theta$ also encodes location $\phi$. Intuitively, I would imagine that this reduces the information gap because the decoder has less information to go by. Is this correct? If so, could the authors provide some intuition for how the information gap changes as a function of the noise level?
- I was wondering whether the authors could comment on how the information gap changes as a function of the number of contexts and the number of stimulus features that are relevant for the task.
