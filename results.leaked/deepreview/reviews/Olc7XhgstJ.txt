# Review

## Summary
The paper introduces a method for improving reasoning in LLMs by reducing unnecessary thought-switching during reasoning. The approach consists of three stages: (1) segmentation of the reasoning chain into distinct thoughts, (2) completion of each thought to its final answer, and (3) preference optimization to encourage the model to stick with promising thoughts. The method is evaluated on four reasoning benchmarks: MATH500, AIME 2024, GSM8K, and LiveCode. The results show that the method can reduce the number of tokens generated while maintaining or improving accuracy.

## Strengths
The paper addresses an important problem of under-thinking in LLMs, where models often abandon promising reasoning paths too early. The proposed approach is novel and shows promising results in improving reasoning efficiency without sacrificing accuracy. The paper is well-written and easy to follow.

## Weaknesses
1. **Limited evaluation**: The evaluation is limited to four math/code reasoning datasets. Evaluating on a wider range of tasks, such as logical reasoning or commonsense reasoning, would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the method's effectiveness. The paper also lacks evaluation of the method's robustness to different types of questions or complexity.

2. **Limited baselines**: The paper compares the proposed method to only three baseline methods (NoThink, NoWait, SEAL). There are several other relevant methods for improving reasoning in LLMs that could be used as baselines, such as self-consistency, reflection, or chain-of-thought prompting. Additionally, the paper could benefit from comparing the method to stronger baselines that also aim to reduce thought-switching.

3. **Limited analysis of results**: While the paper presents accuracy and token count results, it lacks a deeper analysis of the method's behavior. For example, it would be useful to see examples of the generated reasoning chains and how they change after applying the proposed method. The paper could also benefit from an analysis of the computational savings achieved by reducing thought-switching.

4. **Limited analysis of failure cases**: The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the method's failure cases or limitations. Understanding when and why the method fails could provide valuable insights for future improvements.

## Questions
1. How does the method perform on different types of reasoning tasks beyond math/code reasoning? Have you tested it on logical reasoning or commonsense reasoning tasks?

2. How does the method compare to stronger baselines for improving LLM reasoning, such as self-consistency, reflection, or chain-of-thought prompting?

3. Can you provide examples of the generated reasoning chains and how they change after applying the proposed method?

4. How does the method affect the model's robustness to different types of questions or complexity? Have you tested it on questions with higher difficulty or ambiguity?

5. What are the computational savings achieved by reducing thought-switching? Can you provide an analysis of the computational cost vs. accuracy trade-off?

6. How does the method perform in different languages or cultural contexts?

7. How sensitive is the method to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the entropy threshold for thought segmentation?

8. Can the method be combined with other techniques for improving LLM reasoning, such as reward models or reinforcement learning?
