# Review

## Summary
This paper introduces a novel method for evaluating societal bias in Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMS), specifically addressing the challenge of refusals in guardrailed models. The authors propose a new evaluation paradigm that decouples the task from the depicted person by using person-irrelevant prompts and attaching images as provisional user information to provide demographic cues. The method is instantiated across three tasks: story generation, term explanation, and exam-style QA. The authors evaluate 20 recent LVLMS, both open-source and proprietary, and find that all models exhibit societal bias, although proprietary models show lower bias than open-source ones. The paper discusses potential factors behind this gap and emphasizes the importance of continuous model monitoring and improvement in reducing bias.

## Strengths
1. The paper proposes a novel evaluation method that addresses the issue of refusals in guardrailed models, which is a significant contribution to the field.
2. The authors provide a thorough evaluation of 20 recent LVLMS, offering valuable insights into the performance and biases of both open-source and proprietary models.
3. The discussion on potential factors behind the bias gap between proprietary and open-source models is interesting and provides valuable insights for future research.

## Weaknesses
1. The evaluation tasks are limited to three specific tasks, which may not fully capture the diversity of real-world applications of LVLMS.
2. The paper focuses on gender and racial biases, which are important, but does not explore other types of biases that may be relevant in different contexts.
3. The proposed method relies on the assumption that images can provide demographic cues, which may not always be accurate or appropriate.
4. The paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method and potential directions for future research.

## Questions
1. How generalizable is the proposed evaluation method to other types of biases beyond gender and race? Have you considered evaluating other attributes?
2. What are the potential limitations of using images as provisional user information, and how can these limitations be addressed?
3. How do you plan to extend the framework to other tasks beyond the three evaluated in this paper? What are your criteria for selecting tasks?
4. What are the potential ethical implications of using this evaluation method, particularly in terms of privacy and potential misuse of demographic information?
