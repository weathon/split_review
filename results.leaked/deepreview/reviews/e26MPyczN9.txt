# Review

## Summary
This paper revisits the claims that programmatic policies generalize better than neural networks in RL tasks. They argue that much of the reported advantage of programmatic policies in previous works arises from experimental confounds, such as more conservative reward functions, rather than fundamental representational differences. The paper argues that the ability of a representation to enable OOD generalization depends on two factors: (1) whether the policy space induced by the representation includes a generalizing policy (expressivity) and (2) whether a search algorithm can find the generalizing policy (discoverability). They show that neural policies can match or exceed the out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization of programmatic policies when trained with modifications such as sparse observations and cautious reward functions. The paper also identifies classes of problems, such as pathfinding and domains with nested subproblems, where commonly used neural architectures fail to satisfy expressivity due to their fixed-capacity design. Finally, they provide a proof-of-concept experiment demonstrating that programmatic representations can express solutions with instance-scaling memory that provably generalizes OOD.

## Strengths
1. The paper presents a novel perspective on the comparison between programmatic and neural policies, focusing on the factors of expressivity and discoverability. This framework helps in understanding the conditions under which each type of policy may succeed or fail in OOD generalization.
2. The paper provides a comprehensive re-evaluation of previous works, identifying experimental confounds that were overlooked in prior studies. By controlling for these factors, the paper demonstrates that neural policies can generalize as well as programmatic ones in several domains.
3. The paper identifies classes of problems, such as pathfinding and domains with nested subproblems, where commonly used neural architectures fail to satisfy expressivity due to their fixed-capacity design.

## Weaknesses
1. The paper focuses primarily on a few specific domains (TORCS, Karel, and Parking) to support its claims. While these domains are relevant, the paper could benefit from a broader range of experiments to strengthen its generalizability.
2. The paper does not provide a comprehensive comparison with the latest state-of-the-art methods in both programmatic and neural policy domains. Including such comparisons would help in understanding the current landscape and the relative performance of the proposed approach.
3. The paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity and efficiency of the proposed approach compared to existing methods. Understanding the scalability and resource requirements is crucial for practical applications.

## Questions
1. How do the findings of this paper compare with the latest state-of-the-art methods in programmatic and neural policy domains?
2. Are there any potential drawbacks or limitations of the proposed approach, particularly in terms of computational complexity and efficiency?
