## Summary
2-3 sentence summary of the paper's contribution.

The paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models expressive piano performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT) through disentangled representations of note-level score content and global performance style. It introduces a diffusion-based performance style recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone, enabling automated style-aware rendering. The model is trained without note-level alignment, using only sequence-level supervision, and achieves competitive performance on both tasks while enabling style transfer and interpretable disentanglement.

## Strengths

- **Joint modeling with disentangled representations is a novel and well-motivated framework for two inverse MIR tasks.** The paper is the first to explicitly connect EPR and APT under a unified transformer architecture with disentangled content and style spaces. The formulation is conceptually clean: APT maps performance→score by extracting content, while EPR maps score→performance by combining content with a style vector. This is a principled framing that goes beyond treating each task independently.

- **EPR performance is competitive or superior to strong baselines across objective and subjective metrics, despite relaxed (alignment-free) supervision.** Ours (Target) achieves the lowest KL-divergence for velocity (1.76) and MAE for both duration (0.37) and velocity (10.33) in Table 2, outperforming alignment-dependent methods like VirtuosoNet and DExter. The subjective listening test (Figure 2) shows Ours (Target) receiving the highest ratings across dynamics, tempo, style, and overall human-likeness.

- **The PSR module provides a practical, score-conditioned style generation mechanism that preserves stylistic coherence.** The PSR-generated style embeddings cluster by historical era similarly to real performance style embeddings (Figure 4), and Ours (PSR) achieves the highest alignment rate (92.27%, Table 3) among all evaluated methods. This is a genuinely useful component: it removes the need for manual style specification while producing appropriate renderings.

- **Disentanglement evidence is reasonably strong given the experimental design.** The performer/composer identification results (Table 4) show style→composer F1=52.45 vs. content→composer F1=3.03, and style→performer F1=25.82 vs. content→performer F1=0.74. While the comparison is not perfectly controlled (global vector vs. averaged sequence), the magnitude of the gap makes it unlikely that the result is purely an artifact of representation format.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **The claimed benefit of joint training is conflated with the use of additional unpaired data.** The EPR-Only variant is trained solely on the ASAP dataset (967 performances), while Ours (Target) is trained on ASAP + 75K unpaired scores + unlabeled YouTube performances. The paper (line 245) acknowledges "joint modeling, together with training on additional unpaired data" as a combined factor, but never separates them. The improvement of Ours-Target over EPR-Only could come entirely from the extra unlabeled data rather than from the joint APT supervision. Further, no APT-Only variant is presented, so there is no evidence that joint training benefits transcription either. Since "leverages the duality between the two tasks for mutual supervision" (line 39) is presented as a core contribution, this is a significant gap in the experimental evaluation.

- **The style transfer subjective evaluation lacks important methodological details.** The three-sample style transfer test (Figure 5) does not report the number of participants, the specific experimental design, or how the listeners were instructed. With only three samples and no stated participant count, it is difficult to assess the reliability of the claimed style transfer capability.

### Minor

- **The evidence for content–style disentanglement, while suggestive, leaves room for alternative explanations.** The classifier-based evaluation in Table 4 compares a global style vector against a sequence of content vectors that must be averaged across chunks. The content representation's low classification accuracy could partly reflect information loss from averaging rather than successful removal of style information. The paper acknowledges this (line 257) but does not provide complementary evidence from other methods (e.g., reconstruction under style swap, mutual information estimation).

- **APT statistical significance is only reported against one baseline.** In Table 1, significance annotations (†/‡) are provided only with respect to the "End-to-end" baseline (Beyer & Dai 2024). Without significance tests against other methods (e.g., Shibata et al., Liu et al.), the claim of "competitive performance" is weaker than it could be.

- **The note alignment algorithm used for computing EPR alignment/insertion/missing rates is not described.** The paper reports alignment rate as a key metric for EPR accuracy (Table 3) but does not specify how predicted notes are aligned to ground-truth notes. Since this metric is used to compare against alignment-dependent baselines, the lack of detail makes it harder to assess fairness.

- **The subjective evaluation for EPR (Figure 2) uses 11 participants.** While this is not unusual in MIR, the bar charts do not show confidence intervals or effect sizes, which would help readers assess the reliability of the observed differences.

- **No ablation of the KL regularization loss.** The paper introduces L_KL to promote smoothness and disentanglement in the style space (Eq. 5), but does not ablate its effect. A removal experiment or weight sweep would clarify its contribution.

- **The unpaired YouTube performance dataset is not analyzed for quality.** The paper mentions that a pilot study was used to select the transcription model, but does not report statistics on transcription accuracy, genre distribution, or filtering criteria for this dataset, which provides a substantial portion of the training signal.
