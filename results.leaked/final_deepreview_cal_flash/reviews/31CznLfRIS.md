## Summary

This paper introduces VideoJudge, a bootstrapping framework for training MLLM-based evaluators for video understanding. The key idea is an iterative generator–evaluator loop: a generator produces candidate responses at different quality levels (ratings 1–5), an evaluator scores them, and mismatches are refined. The resulting 103K bootstrapped examples are used to fine-tune Qwen2.5-VL (3B, 7B) into judge models that can assign pointwise ratings and pairwise preferences, optionally generating instance-specific rubrics. The trained judges are competitive with models 10× their size (32B, 72B) across pointwise and pairwise meta-evaluation benchmarks, and the rubric-trained variant (VideoJudgeR-3B) produces rubrics preferred by human evaluators over those from GPT-4o-mini and Qwen-72B.

## Strengths

1. **Novel bootstrapping pipeline for scalable supervision.** The generator–evaluator loop (Section 3.1) automatically produces 103,825 training examples from 20,765 video–instruction pairs without human annotation. Automatic metrics (BERTScore, BLEU) confirm monotonic quality degradation across rating levels (Figure 2), and a human evaluation on the hardest 2-vs-3 boundary achieves 94.8% annotator agreement and >92% correctness (Section 5.2), validating data reliability.

2. **Small fine-tuned judges compete with much larger models.** VideoJudge-3B achieves the highest Spearman correlation (0.82) on VideoJudgeLLaVA, surpassing Qwen2.5-VL-72B (0.80) and all other baselines (Table 1). VideoJudge-7B obtains the best Δ(C-D) score (1.16) on LongVideoBench. In pairwise evaluation, VideoJudge-7B reaches 98.6% accuracy on the VideoJudge benchmark, outperforming Qwen2.5-VL-72B (94.0%) (Table 3).

3. **Rubric-trained model yields interpretable, high-quality evaluations.** VideoJudgeR-3B (trained on only 10% of pointwise data) achieves MAE 0.59 and Spearman 74.16, comparable to Qwen2.5-VL-32B/72B (Table 2). Its instance-specific rubrics are preferred by human evaluators over those from GPT-4o-mini (53.4% win rate) and Qwen-72B (63.9% win rate) (Figure 3), demonstrating that rubric-guided training produces both accurate and interpretable judgments.

4. **Thorough analysis of practical design choices.** The ablation on maxframes (training benefits up to 240 frames, evaluation saturates around 120) provides actionable guidance. The temperature robustness study shows VideoJudge maintains stable performance (Spearman 0.66–0.73) across temperatures 0.0–1.0, while the base model degrades sharply (Figure 4).

5. **Open release of models, data, and benchmarks.** The paper will release two trained judge model series, four meta-evaluation benchmarks (VideoJudgeLLaVA, VideoJudgeVCG, VideoJudge-Pairwise, VideoJudge-Pairwise-H), and bootstrapped datasets, supporting reproducible research.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Closed-loop construction of pointwise meta-evaluation benchmarks.** Two of the four pointwise benchmarks (VideoJudgeLLaVA-MetaEval, VideoJudgeVCG-MetaEval) are themselves generated by the same bootstrapping pipeline that produces the training data (Section 4.2: "generating additional responses via our bootstrapping pipeline with threshold 0"). This creates a circularity: models trained on bootstrapped data are evaluated on data from the same process. While the paper also evaluates on independent human-annotated benchmarks (VateX, LongVideoBench, VideoAutoArena) where results are broadly consistent, the synthetic benchmarks dominate the headline quantitative claims. The evidence for generalization would be stronger if the synthetic benchmarks were treated as diagnostic checks rather than primary endpoints, or if their correlation with human judgment were explicitly measured.

2. **Significant overestimation bias and poor calibration.** The error analysis (Section 6.2) reveals that VideoJudge overestimates scores by ≥2 points in 14.8% of cases but underestimates by the same margin in only 1.5%. Only 36.9% of rating-3 responses receive the correct score — 46.6% are inflated to a perfect 5, and 81.3% of rating-4 responses are incorrectly rated as 5. This is a structural limitation for a model intended as a reliable evaluator. The paper acknowledges the issue but does not present mitigations (e.g., rebalancing training data with harder negatives, recalibration techniques), leaving its practical value for fine-grained quality assessment unclear.

### Minor

3. **Occasional overclaiming.** The conclusion states that "VideoJudge-7B consistently outperforms larger video-language models across multiple benchmarks" (Section 8). The data tell a more nuanced story: on VideoJudgeLLaVA, VideoJudge-7B has Spearman 0.78 vs. Qwen2.5-VL-72B's 0.80; on VATEx, PSUP is 0.66 vs. 0.71; on the human-validated pairwise subset VJ-H (w/ FB), it is 93.67 vs. 94.51. The models are *competitive with* and *sometimes surpass* much larger models — which is itself an impressive finding — but the phrasing should be precise.

4. **Limited human validation of pointwise ratings.** The human evaluation (Section 5.2) only validates pairwise preferences at the 2-vs-3 rating boundary (250 pairs). The quality of the 1–5 pointwise ratings — the primary supervision signal for the pointwise judge — is not directly validated against human judgments. A small-scale study asking annotators whether a generated rating-1 response is genuinely poor or a rating-5 response is genuinely good would strengthen the claim that the bootstrapped data provides reliable pointwise supervision.

5. **Generator/evaluator model identity deferred to appendix.** The main text refers to the generator G and evaluator E without naming which specific models serve these roles; the details are in Appendix A.2 (which exists in the original submission but is not accessible in the extracted text). While the appendix covers this, stating the models in the main text would improve transparency for readers who may not consult the supplementary material.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation varying the bootstrapping acceptance threshold α would clarify the sensitivity of data quality to this hyperparameter.
- Demonstrating the bootstrapping method on a different backbone architecture (e.g., LLaVA-NeXT) would strengthen the claim of generality beyond Qwen2.5-VL.
- A small-scale calibration experiment (e.g., upweighting hard negatives or adding synthetically perturbed good responses) to address the overestimation bias would substantially increase the paper's practical impact.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The insight that bootstrapped generator–evaluator loops can produce reliable supervision for video judge training, and that the resulting small models compete with 10× larger systems, is well captured by the paper itself.

## Suggestions

1. Recalibrate the strongest comparative claims (e.g., "consistently outperforms" → "is competitive with and in several cases surpasses") to match the experimental evidence precisely.
2. Add a small human validation study for pointwise ratings (e.g., 100 samples across the 1–5 scale) to verify that the bootstrapped ratings align with human judgment, not just pairwise preferences.
3. Include an analysis or experiment that addresses the overestimation bias — even a simple discussion of when the bias is likely to be harmful (e.g., ranking coarse quality buckets vs. fine-grained distinctions) would help practitioners calibrate their trust in VideoJudge.
