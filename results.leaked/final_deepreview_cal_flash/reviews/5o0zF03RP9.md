## Summary

IncentRL proposes augmenting standard RL with a KL-divergence penalty between predicted and preferred outcome distributions (a "preference-prediction shaping" term), and claims a Bayesian online adaptation scheme for the incentive weight β that eliminates manual tuning. The KL shaping idea is presented clearly and the paper demonstrates consistent improvements over standard RL (β=0) across a toy MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid. However, the central claimed contribution — the Bayesian β adaptation — is never described in the main text, the KL computation is underspecified for non-toy environments, the evaluation lacks any comparison to existing intrinsic-motivation methods, and the theoretical results are elementary. The paper's execution falls substantially short of its ambitions.

## Strengths

1. **Clean, well-motivated conceptual framework.** The KL-based preference-prediction shaping term is clearly introduced (Section 3, Eq. 1) and its connections to cognitive science (dopamine RPE, Free Energy Principle) provide a plausible motivation. The framework is flexible: q(o|s) can be hand-crafted, learned from demonstrations, or generated by LLMs (Sections 3.3, 4.3).

2. **Consistent empirical improvement over standard RL.** In the toy 2-state MDP (Table 1, Fig. 1), all β>0 improve final cumulative reward by 69–74% over β=0 with dramatically reduced variance. On MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8 (Fig. 2), β=0.01 reaches 98% success versus 90.5% for β=0, and mean episode length drops from 75 to 29 steps. On MountainCar (Table 2), β=0.1 yields an 18% improvement in goal reaches. These results show the KL shaping term can provide useful dense signal in sparse-reward settings.

3. **Posterior concentration evidence.** Figures 3 and 4 and Table 3 show that the posterior over β narrows across rounds and concentrates near task-relevant values (β≈0.1), suggesting the adaptation scheme can identify effective β regions.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Bayesian β adaptation — the central claimed contribution — is not described in the main text.** The abstract, introduction, and conclusion all present Bayesian β adaptation as the paper's primary novelty ("We introduce a Bayesian scheme for adapting the incentive weight β, eliminating manual trade-off tuning"). Yet the paper never specifies: what prior is placed over β? What likelihood function relates β to observed data? How is the posterior updated (exact inference, variational, MCMC)? How is β sampled or integrated into the policy update during training? What do "rounds" correspond to in Figures 3–4? The algorithm section (5.1) treats β as a fixed hyperparameter throughout. A central contribution must be described at least conceptually in the main text; the current description is limited to "using our Bayesian adaptation scheme" (Section 5.4) with no mechanism provided.

2. **KL computation is underspecified for the non-toy environments, threatening reproducibility.** For the toy MDP, p(o|s,a) is set to the true transition probabilities. For MountainCar (continuous state space) and MiniGrid, the paper states that p(o|s,a) is "the predicted outcome distribution" and can be "obtained from a forward model or from environment dynamics," but never specifies how it is actually computed. Is a forward model learned? If so, what architecture, loss, and training procedure? For MountainCar, q(o|s) assigns all probability to the goal region (x ≥ 0.5) — how is KL computed between a continuous predicted distribution and a point-mass-like preference? Without these details the experiments are not reproducible.

3. **No comparison to any existing intrinsic-motivation method.** The only baseline across all experiments is β=0 (standard RL). The paper claims "improved sample efficiency" and outperformance of existing approaches, yet includes no comparison to curiosity (Pathak et al., 2017), RND (Burda et al., 2018), ICM, empowerment, or any other intrinsic-motivation method discussed in Related Work. Claims about competitiveness with or superiority over existing methods are therefore unsupported.

4. **The Bayesian adaptation results lack comparative task-performance evaluation.** Figures 3–4 and Table 3 show posterior concentration of β, but the paper's central claim is that Bayesian adaptation *achieves task performance* comparable to the best manually-tuned fixed β. The only evidence for this is a single sentence: "In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of β achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value" (Section 5.4). No learning curves, success rates, or numerical task-performance results comparing the adapted β against fixed-β sweeps are presented. The claim that Bayesian adaptation "removes the need for manual trade-off tuning" is not directly validated.

5. **Potential numerical issue with the KL term in the toy MDP.** For action a₀ (which keeps the agent in s₀): the predicted outcome is p(o|s₀,a₀) = {s₁:0, s₀:1} and the preferred outcome is q(o|s₀) = {s₁:1, s₀:0}, yielding KL(p‖q) = 1·log(1/0) = ∞. The paper does not address this or describe any smoothing, clipping, or numerical stabilization used. With ε-greedy exploration, this KL divergence would be encountered during training.

### Minor

1. **Propositions 1 and 2 are elementary.** Proposition 1 (small β preserves optimality of the original optimal policy) and Proposition 2 (β→∞ gives a policy that minimizes only the KL term) are straightforward continuity observations for any bounded reward perturbation. They do not provide insight specific to KL-based shaping or Bayesian adaptation.

2. **MountainCar results lack learning curves.** Only final mean goal reaches over 5000 episodes are reported (Table 2), without showing dynamics over time. Given the high variance (e.g., std 472–909 across conditions), learning curves and per-seed trajectories would be informative.

3. **Limited statistical evidence.** Experiments use 3 seeds for MountainCar and MiniGrid, with no confidence intervals or significance tests reported.

4. **The dopamine/FEP connection is evoked but not formalized.** Sections 1 and 6.3 draw analogies to reward prediction error and the Free Energy Principle, but these remain at the level of metaphor; no formal mapping is established.
