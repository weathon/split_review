## Summary

This paper proposes ATF (Autoformalizer with Tool Feedback), a framework that integrates Lean 4 syntax checking and a multi-LLM-based semantic consistency judge as tools during the autoformalization process. The training pipeline has three stages: a cold-start phase on synthetic tool-calling trajectories, an expert iteration phase to improve formalization, and DPO to reduce ineffective revisions. ATF-32B achieves substantial improvements over existing formalizers (e.g., 29.13pp improvement on CombiBench Pass@1 consistency over Goedel-V2-Formalizer-32B), and the results are partially validated by human evaluation. The paper also releases Numina-ATF, a 750K formal statement dataset.

## Strengths

1. **Well-designed tool-integrated training pipeline.** The three-stage approach (cold-start → expert iteration → DPO) is carefully motivated and each stage addresses a specific challenge: teaching tool invocation syntax, improving formalization capabilities via iterative refinement, and pruning ineffective revision behaviors. The ablation study (Table 4) cleanly demonstrates that each component contributes, with the full pipeline dramatically outperforming the no-tools configuration (65.38% vs. 23.69% on CombiBench CC).

2. **Consistent and substantial empirical gains across all benchmarks.** ATF-32B outperforms every baseline (including Goedel-V2-Formalizer-32B, the previous SOTA) on all three evaluation sets, at all Pass@K levels, on both syntax and consistency metrics (Table 3). The gap is particularly striking on the out-of-distribution CombiBench, where ATF-32B achieves 65.38% Pass@1 consistency vs. 36.25% for the best baseline, demonstrating strong generalization.

3. **Human evaluation supports the core ranking.** The paper conducts human evaluation on 100 samples per benchmark with 3 independent expert judges. ATF-32B wins on all three benchmarks in human evaluation (e.g., CombiBench consistency 49% vs. next best 22%), confirming that the improvement over baselines is not purely an artifact of the tool-based metric. The Pearson correlation of 0.746 between tool and human judgments provides additional evidence for the tool's validity.

4. **Informative analysis beyond raw results.** The inference-time scaling analysis (Figure 4) shows that ATF continues to benefit from additional revision attempts beyond its training budget and that Pass@32 approaches near-100% on all benchmarks. The tool usage analysis (Figure 5) reveals sensible adaptation to problem difficulty and provides fine-grained behavioral insight.

5. **Open-source dataset release.** Numina-ATF (750K formal statements from Numina-v1.5) is a practical contribution that supports future work in autoformalization and theorem proving.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Evaluation contamination: the consistency judge used as the primary evaluation metric is the same tool used during training.** The multi-LLM-as-judge consistency check (Section 3.1.2) is used both to filter/validate training data in the cold-start and expert iteration phases and as the evaluation metric for the primary results (Table 3). Since ATF is explicitly trained to produce statements that pass this specific judge, its success on the same judge is likely inflated relative to both baselines and to true semantic alignment. This concern is compounded by the judge's high false negative rate (FNR = 40.33%, Table 1), meaning many inconsistent statements are systematically missed. The concern is not fully speculative: on two of three benchmarks (FormalMath-Lite and ProverBench), the tool-based advantage over Goedel-V2-32B is substantially larger than the human-judged advantage (9.1pp vs. 3pp, and 10.08pp vs. 4pp respectively), suggesting the tool overestimates ATF's relative improvement. While the human evaluation (100 samples/benchmark) does show ATF winning on all benchmarks, the sample is small and confidence intervals are not reported, making it difficult to assess whether the headline margins (especially the 29.13pp on CombiBench) are accurate.

2. **Data decontamination is described only in a single sentence with no details.** The paper states "we perform similarity-based decontamination on all training data against these evaluation sets" (Section 4.1), but provides no information about the similarity metric, threshold, manual verification protocol, or examples of detected near-duplicates. Given that FormalMath-Lite and ProverBench are described as in-distribution, this is essential for ruling out memorization as a confound. The lack of detail prevents the reader from assessing the reliability of the results on these benchmarks.

### Minor

1. **Cold-start data quality is not analyzed.** The synthetic tool-calling trajectories are generated by Claude-4-Sonnet, but no analysis of their quality, the success rate of tool invocations, or the upsampling ratio for multi-revision trajectories is provided. Since this phase is foundational to the model's ability to use tools, some basic quality metrics would be informative.

2. **The "NO TOOLS" ablation configuration is underspecified.** The paper reports a "NO TOOLS" configuration that drops both syntax and consistency checks, but it is unclear whether this means (a) the model was trained on the same cold-start data with tool traces removed, (b) trained on direct informal-formal pairs without any tool invocation format, or (c) something else. The comparison in Table 4 is harder to interpret without this clarification.

3. **Human evaluation sample size limits precision.** The human evaluation covers 100 samples per benchmark. While the procedure is otherwise sound, the resulting estimates have wide confidence intervals (e.g., the 49% vs. 22% CombiBench comparison). Bootstrap confidence intervals or Bayesian intervals for the main tool-based results would give the reader a better sense of variability.

## Novel Insights

The most striking observation that emerges from the reviews — beyond the paper's own contributions — is the systematic discrepancy between tool-based and human-based evaluation margins on two of the three benchmarks. On FormalMath-Lite and ProverBench, the tool-based gap between ATF and Goedel-V2-32B is roughly 2–3× the human-judged gap, while on CombiBench the gaps are more aligned. This pattern suggests that the consistency judge may overestimate ATF's advantage more on in-distribution data than on OOD data, which could be an artifact of training-data exposure to similar judge feedback. This is a testable hypothesis that the authors could explore in a revision, and it highlights the importance of independent validation for any training-as-evaluation pipeline.

## Suggestions

1. **Expand the human evaluation** to at least 200–300 samples per benchmark, or provide bootstrap confidence intervals for the existing 100-sample estimates, so readers can assess whether the headline margins are statistically reliable.
2. **Run the consistency evaluation with an independent judge** (e.g., GPT-4, Claude-4, or DeepSeek-R1 prompted with the same rubric) that was not part of the training loop, and report whether the ranking and approximate magnitudes hold.
3. **Report a calibration analysis:** what fraction of statements that passed the tool-based consistency check were flagged as inconsistent by humans, separately for ATF and baselines? If ATF has a higher false-acceptance rate, that is direct evidence of overfitting to the judge.
4. **Provide full decontamination details:** similarity metric, threshold, number of removed examples, and at least one concrete example per evaluation set.
5. **Clarify the "NO TOOLS" ablation** by specifying exactly what changes were made to the training data and format relative to the full pipeline.
