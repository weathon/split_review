# Review

## Summary
The paper proposes a novel method for offline preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL) that addresses the challenge of reward model extrapolation errors. These errors occur when the policy optimization trajectories deviate from the distribution of preference-labeled training data, leading to unreliable reward estimates and suboptimal policies. The authors introduce SPOT (Subgoal-based Preference Optimization Through Attention Weight), which mitigates these errors by leveraging attention-derived subgoals from the preference data. SPOT regularizes the policy towards these subgoals, constraining learning within the training distribution and reducing extrapolation errors. The paper presents comprehensive experiments demonstrating that SPOT outperforms existing methods while effectively mitigating extrapolation errors. The approach preserves fine-grained credit assignment information and enhances query efficiency, suggesting promising directions for reliable and practical offline preference-based learning.

## Strengths
The paper presents a novel approach, SPOT, which addresses the issue of reward model extrapolation errors in offline preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL). This is an innovative solution that leverages attention-derived subgoals from preference data to mitigate extrapolation errors, which is a significant contribution to the field.

## Weaknesses
1. The paper does not provide a comprehensive discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. It would be beneficial to include a detailed section on the limitations, potential drawbacks, and scenarios where the method may not perform optimally. This would provide a more balanced view of the approach and help readers understand the context better.

2. The paper could benefit from a more thorough comparison with existing methods, including both conventional and more recent approaches in offline preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL). While it mentions some baseline comparisons, a more extensive analysis would strengthen the paper's contributions and help readers understand the advantages and disadvantages of the proposed method in relation to other state-of-the-art techniques.

3. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity or resource requirements of the proposed method. This information is important for understanding the practical feasibility of implementing the approach, especially for large-scale or real-time applications.

4. The paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of the assumptions made in the proposed method. It would be helpful to clarify any underlying assumptions about the problem structure, data availability, or distribution of preferences that may limit the applicability of the approach in real-world scenarios.
