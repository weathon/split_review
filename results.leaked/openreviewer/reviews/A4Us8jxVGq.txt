# Review

## Summary
This paper focuses on the critical question of how semantic associations are learned in transformers. The authors present a theoretical framework to explain this process by analyzing the gradient leading terms for each set of weights. They reveal that transformer weights can be expressed as compositions of three basic functions: a bigram mapping, an interchangeability mapping, and a context mapping. These findings are supported by experiments on both toy transformers and real-world LLMs.

## Strengths
1. This paper addresses a highly significant problem: understanding how semantic associations are learned in transformers. The authors provide a clear and intuitive explanation of this process, supported by robust theoretical analysis and empirical evidence.
2. The theoretical framework is novel, and the authors make a compelling case that the leading terms of the gradients for each set of weights can be expressed as compositions of three basic functions: a bigram mapping, an interchangeability mapping, and a context mapping.
3. The paper is well-written and easy to follow.

## Weaknesses
1. The theoretical analysis is based on the assumption of using a learning rate of $\frac{1}{T}$. However, in practice, larger learning rates are often employed. It would be beneficial for the authors to discuss the implications of using larger learning rates and how they might affect the validity of their analysis.
2. The theoretical analysis is conducted on a 3-layer transformer with a vocabulary size of 3000. It would be helpful for the authors to explain how their analysis can be generalized to models with different numbers of layers and vocabulary sizes.

## Questions
1. The theoretical analysis is based on an attention-only transformer. How would the inclusion of MLP layers affect the validity of the analysis?
2. The authors mention that the basis functions are crucial to express token associations and language structure. Can these basis functions be considered as the building blocks of language? If so, what are the implications of this finding for our understanding of language learning and processing in transformers?
