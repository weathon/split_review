# Review

## Summary
This paper investigates the reliance of LLMs on chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning in physics problem-solving. The authors introduce a deletion-based probing method to evaluate the faithfulness of CoT usage, showing that while models can maintain accuracy despite significant deletion of CoT tokens (up to 40-60%), they often engage in "cramming" behavior, reconstructing deleted information in the final answer. This suggests that current accuracy-based evaluations may not adequately capture genuine reasoning dependence. The findings underscore the need for evaluations that prioritize reasoning faithfulness in AI applications to scientific domains.

## Strengths
- This paper introduces a novel deletion-based probing method that allows for precise investigation of CoT faithfulness in physics problem-solving. This approach provides a new lens on evaluating LLM reasoning, focusing on whether models genuinely rely on intermediate reasoning steps rather than merely using them as scaffolding.
- The study employs rigorous evaluation metrics, including domain-specific overlap measures, to assess whether deleted information reappears in final answers. This methodology enables quantifiable insights into the faithfulness of CoT usage.

## Weaknesses
- While the study focuses on physics reasoning as a representative scientific domain, the findings may not fully generalize to other reasoning-intensive tasks (e.g., math, logic). The conclusions are based on observable outputs, lacking analysis of internal mechanisms such as latent representations or attention patterns, which could provide more comprehensive insights into the models' reasoning processes.
- The study is limited to three open-source LLMs, and the findings may not hold for larger, more capable models. A broader analysis across different model architectures and scales could offer insights into how model size and training impact CoT faithfulness.
- The deletion experiments, while systematic, may not fully capture the complexity of real-world reasoning scenarios. The study would benefit from exploring more varied deletion strategies or combinations of deletions to better understand the models' robustness and limitations.

## Questions
- The paper uses Bag-of-Words metrics (Jaccard similarity and Manhattan distance) to measure information overlap. Could the authors elaborate on the limitations of these metrics, if any, and discuss potential biases in how they might over- or under-estimate the similarity between deleted and re-generated content?
- The paper shows that models often engage in "cramming" behavior, reconstructing deleted information in the final answer. Could the authors provide more insight into the mechanisms that drive this behavior? Is it possible that the models are not simply "cramming" but rather re-organizing and re-interpreting the deleted information in a way that supports the final answer?
- The study focuses on physics reasoning as a representative scientific domain. Could the authors discuss the potential limitations of this choice and how those limitations might affect the generalizability of the findings to other reasoning tasks?
